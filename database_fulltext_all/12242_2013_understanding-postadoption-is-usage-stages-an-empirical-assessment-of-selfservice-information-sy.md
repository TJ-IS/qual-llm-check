# Understanding post-adoption IS usage stages: an empirical assessment of self-service information systems

Khawaja Asjad Saeed\* & Sue Abdinnour<sup>†</sup>

W. Frank Barton School of Business, Wichita State University, Wichita, KS 67260-0077, USA, \*email: khawaja.saeed@wichita.edu, <sup>†</sup>email: sue.abdinnour@wichita.edu

Abstract. Organisations have invested in self-service information systems (IS) to provide a direct interface for service delivery. Enriching the usage of these systems can provide organisations with immense benefits. However, limited research has been directed towards understanding post-adoption IS usage behaviour in general and specifically in the context of self-service IS. This study proposes post-adoption IS usage behaviour as a broader concept constituting feature level usage of IS, integration of IS in the work system and exploration of new uses of IS. We evaluate how the new conceptualisation can be used to classify users at different stages of self-service IS usage. Further, we examine user perceptions that differentiate among the users situated at different selfservice IS usage stages. Data were collected in the context of a self-service Web-based IS to validate the post-adoption IS usage constructs and to examine the proposed thesis. The newly developed conceptual structure and measures for post-adoption IS usage behaviour exhibit strong psychometric properties. The analysis shows three distinct post-adoption IS usage stages and highlights that usefulness, user-initiated learning, ease of use, satisfaction and voluntariness of use differentiate users at the different stages of post-adoption IS usage. The results show that these variables aggregate into value confirmation and learning orientation as two higher-level concepts. Further, we evaluate the predictive efficacy of the research model in classifying users into different post-adoption self-service IS usage stages. Implications are drawn for future research.

Keywords: post-adoption IS usage, IS usage stages, IS acceptance, self-service IS, cluster analysis, discriminant analysis

## INTRODUCTION

Effective utilisation of information systems (IS) is a critical issue that is tied to the post-adoption usage of IS. As the maturity of IS in organisations has increased, interest in examining post-adoption IS usage phenomenon has also grown. An area where there has been tremendous growth is self-service IS. Proliferation of self-service IS such as online banking, student self-service systems and online brokerage systems supports this contention. This study focuses on a self-service IS context in which services are delivered through a Web site and users perform the services by themselves. Organisations are investing heavily in this area, but the examination of post-adoption usage of self-service IS has received limited attention. Effective utilisation of self-service IS by users offers great potential in terms of reducing resource requirements, improving service and building switching costs (Meuter et al., 2000; Curran & Meuter, 2005). According to Forrester Research, the cost of single customer service interaction drops from \$35 for telephone support to \$0.75 for online self-service support. Thus, a better understanding of user engagement with self-service IS at the post-adoption stage can be beneficial to organisations.

Multiple aspects make effective promotion and acceptance of self-service IS a challenge to an organisation. For example, an organisation has relatively less control over persuading customers to use self-service IS as compared with internal employees. Customers may not find it compelling to utilise self-service IS because of concerns regarding service delivery and/or effort required to learn a self-service IS. Further, significant resources need to be allocated towards developing and managing the self-service IS channel. Lack of self-service IS acceptance can adversely impact resources because it will require the organisation to maintain redundancy across service delivery channels. Thus, organisations can benefit immensely if the self-service IS is utilised extensively by the users. Achieving that state requires an understanding of how users interact with self-service IS and factors that explain the variations in interaction. This study examines the important issues of self-service IS usage behaviour and factors that impact it at the post-adoption stage.

Jasperson et al. (2005) defines post-adoptive behaviour as ‘myriad of feature adoption decisions, feature use behaviours and feature extension behaviours made by an individual user after an information technology application has been installed, made accessible to the user and applied by the user in accomplishing his/her work activities’. The definition highlights that expanding the scope of self-service IS usage to include feature use and feature extension behaviours is important. For example, a user of online banking system can choose to use bil pay and transfer across accounts among a host of services offered through the system. Users also have the option of expanding beyond the scope of services offered by the system and use it for budgeting, a service that may not be directly available through the system. Research suggests that different IS usage behaviours can be aggregated into a pattern that captures a post-adoption IS usage stage (Lassila & Brancheau, 1999). This perspective has a strong tradition in innovation diffusion research that offers insights on the different post-adoption stages such as routinisation and infusion (Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Agarwal, 2000). Postadoption stages represent a set of behaviours that captures a coherent scheme. We believe that linking IS usage behaviours and post-adoption IS usage stages offers an interesting approach that can be used to understand how users interact with a self-service IS. This approach will offer a viable measurement tool to organisations to gauge acceptance and utilisation of self-service IS.

A related question to ask is why users are situated at different self-service IS usage stages? Identification of perceptions that differ across users situated at different self-service IS usage stages can provide guidance on this issue. Prior research offers various factors such as sense making, post-adoption IS evaluation, control and learning that have been found to be important at the post-adoption stage (Beaudry & Pinnsonneault, 2005). Our study builds on this literature base to investigate factors that provide insights on how users interact with self-service IS at the post-adoption stage. We then evaluate the predictive value of these factors in delineating the users that fall into different IS usage stages. Thus, the study not only tests the significance of the factors but also evaluates the accuracy of how well these factors can predict classification of users into different IS usage stages. Understanding user perceptions that can be used to classify users across self-service IS usage stages offers an important step in identifying the best ways to design and promote self-service IS.

In summation, the study intends to achieve two objectives. First, there is a clear need to offer a coherent conceptualisation and measurement approach for self-service IS usage behaviours. Further, the study evaluates the linkage between self-service IS usage behaviours and self-service IS usage stages. Second, we investigate the factors that provide insights into the variation across the users that are situated at different self-service IS usage stages. Specifi cally, we examine the predictive value of factors in correctly classifying users positioned at different self-service IS usage stages. We believe that the results of the study will provide firms with an effective measurement tool and guidelines to augment utilisation of self-service IS.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

Better understanding of the IS usage phenomenon can help in promoting higher utilisation of IS applications. In the self-service IS context, most studies have focused on adoption- and implementation-related issues (Carter & Bélanger, 2005; Chan & Ngai, 2007). Prior research in this area does not offer guidance on conceptualisation and measurement of self-service IS usage behaviour. Considering that the focus of our study is on a context in which services are delivered through a Web site, we can draw on IS usage behaviour literature for relevant guidance. Review of prior research on IS usage behaviour shows a predominant focus on frequency, breadth of IS features and appropriateness of IS usage (Leidner & Elam, 1993; Massetti & Zmud, 1996; Devaraj & Kohli, 2003; Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). Adaptive structuration proposes the usage of IS features and alignment of IS usage with the initial spirit in which it was implemented as two important perspectives (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). However. Burton-Jones & Straub (2006) highlight that evaluation of an IS (appropriateness spirit) should be separated from its usage. Despite this difference, both perspectives agree on the validity of using IS feature usage as an appropriate approach. A detailed assessment of this approach requires an understanding of the users’ interaction with the IS features and the usage of these features in diverse task environments. According to Burton-Jones & Straub (2006), the progression in richness of conceptualisation and measurement of IS usage depends on the extent to which the measures progressively capture more elements (user, task and system) that are tied to the usage phenomenon.

Implicit in the approach outlined previously is the variation across users in their interaction with an IS. Some users may engage in using basic IS features, while other users may get more engaged, such that the IS becomes central to how they accomplish tasks. Thus, examining IS usage from a staged approach, wherein the users depict a pattern of interaction with the IS at different stages, offers an interesting line of inquiry for two reasons. First, only a limited number of studies have evaluated IS usage from a staged perspective. Second, assessment of the stage approach requires a systematic process for delineating the stages in which users are situated (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). Greenwood & Hinings (1993) propose that an appropriate approach in identifying stages is to first obtain an empirically derived pattern and then compare that pattern with a previously validated model. For example, Johnson & Rice (1987) in the context of word processing systems have put forward low-integration, standard adoption, expanding and high integration as four stages that can be linked to different patterns of word processing system usage behaviours. Beaudry & Pinnsonneault (2005) argue that users can be segmented into different post-adoption strategies based on the extent to which they feel in control and view the IS as an opportunity/threat. For example, they propose that users can be categorised into benefits maximisation, benefits satificing, disturbance handling and selfpreservation categories. By using interview data, they link these categories to patterns of IS usage behaviours. Overall, these studies propose that specific usage behaviours can be aggregated to develop patterns that represent IS usage stages. Empirically derived schemes can then be compared with IS usage stages that have been proposed in the literature.

Based on innovation diffusion literature, Cooper & Zmud (1990) propose and elaborate on six stages: initiation, adoption, adaptation, acceptance, routinisation and infusion that constitute the diffusion of IS. The last four stages fall within the post-adoption domain. Adaptation and acceptance capture the initial revision to the IS and have been discussed from an organisational diffusion perspective. As the focus of our study is at the user level, these two stages were excluded. Routinisation and infusion stages were included in our study because each relates well with a distinct pattern of IS usage behaviours at the user level. Routinisation reflects a stage after adoption, wherein the users view IS as a normal activity and develop standard IS usage routines. Infusion stage is characterised by a comprehensive and integrated approach in supporting higher-level aspects of organisational work through IS (Cooper & Zmud, 1990). Some recent studies highlight that a dominant pattern depicted by users is testing the boundaries of the IS to address emergent needs, a stage termed as extension (Jasperson et al., 2005). Based on synthesis of the literature, we propose routinisation, infusion and extension as three key post-adoption IS usage stages (Premkumar et al., 1994) (Figure 1).

The process through which users can be categorised into different IS usage stages is unclear. For example, what approach can be used to confirm that a user is situated in the routinisation stage vs. infusion stage? Prior studies show that the first step is to identify relevant behaviours/characteristics related to the subject (Thomas & Venkatraman, 1988). Subsequently, these behaviours/characteristics can be combined to develop a coherent theme that represents a stage. Thus, the stage is observable through the behaviours/characteristics of the subject. This approach has grounding in multiple literature streams such as marketing for customer profiling and strategic management for strategic group analysis (Sinha & DeSarbo, 1998; Grover & Saeed, 2004). In the context of our study, the relevant attributes selected to categorise the users are self-service IS usage behaviours. Strong evidence exists to show that IS usage phenomenon needs to be examined from multiple dimensions (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). Different dimensions of IS usage behaviour can be jointly used to discover patterns that correspond with self-service IS usage stages proposed in the prior literature. Thus, in order to gain a better understanding of self-service IS usage stages, a comprehensive assessment of self-service IS usage behaviours is imperative (Beaudry & Pinnsonneault, 2005).

Burton-Jones & Straub (2006) propose that studies examining IS usage behaviour should first clearly define the construct and then develop a measurement approach based on how the user leverages the IS functions that are tied to his/her task environment. We define post adoption IS usage behaviour as the interaction that a user develops with an IS in performing and managing his/her work after initial adoption of the IS. Table 1 offers a review of prio conceptualisations of IS usage behaviour. To structure the review, we use the framework developed by Saga & Zmud (1994). We propose expanded usage, integrative usage and exploratory usage as likely IS usage behaviours that users may pursue after initial adoption of the IS. We elaborate on the three IS usage behaviours next.

## Expanded usage

Most IS is task centric and the features are tied to performing certain actions and processes. Thus, it can be argued that capturing IS feature usage involves both the IS and the task. Leidner & Elam (1993) in the context of executive IS (EIS) measured usage as number of times executives use the EIS to make decisions. Devaraj & Kohli (2003) offer a broader perspective by expanding the set of features to include accessing reports and records. From a self-service IS perspective such as on online banking, using services such as bill payment, transfer across accounts and checking account balances are some examples. The user also has a choice in how extensively these features are used. Thus, the number of IS features and how extensively they are utilised depict the user’s IS usage preferences. We term this behaviour as expanded usage, which captures the extent to which the users are leveraging the different features of IS to perform tasks.

## Integrative usage

A user’s behaviour can range from stagnation in utilising IS features to total integration of the IS in his/her work environment (Agarwal, 2000; Jain & Kanungo, 2005). This measurement approach involves the user and the system. User involvement is an important characteristic in driving integration behaviour. Integrative usage captures the integration of IS into performing work practices. In other words, IS becomes an integral component of how users perform their tasks and view IS as critical in task accomplishment (Agarwal, 2000). For example, a user can utilise online brokerage services for executing trading strategies. The online brokerage service is viewed not only as a simple trading platform but also as a service that is integral to identifying a potential stock, conducting assessment of technical and fundamental indicators and execut ing the trading strategy. Thus, integrative usage captures the utilisation of IS by a user to undertake a process from initiation to completion. We define integrative usage as the extent to which the users are able to effectively integrate the IS into their work.

## Exploratory usage

Users differ on the extent to which they explore new and innovative ways to apply the IS in their work (Nambisan et al., 2000; Jain & Kanungo, 2005). Exploratory usage captures all the three elements (IS, user and task) because this behaviour subsumes active engagement of the user in exploring the efficacy of IS in new contexts/tasks. Ahuja & Thatcher (2005) propose the concept of trying to innovate with IS and define it as the user’s goal of finding new uses of existing workplace information technologies. Nambisan et al. (2000) propose intention to explore the IS, a concept rooted in user’s intentions towards learning the IS and exploring new ways of applying the IS in their work. Jasperson et al. (2005) emphasise exploration of new uses of the IS as a critical post-adoptive behaviour. Expounding on the online brokerage example, a user may explore the ability to configure the interface to have relevant screens visible while conducting trading activities. Further exploration of online services that can assist in tax preparation, assessment of investment goals over time and configuration of stock alerts are some othe examples. Based on these works, we define exploratory usage as the extent to which the user makes an active effort in finding new uses of the IS within their work environment.

We believe that systematic variation across these three IS usage behaviours can provide guidance on the stratification of users into routinisation, infusion and extension stages. It is important to note here that a user categorised in the extension stage will depict all three behaviours. Thus, all three IS usage behaviours will be used jointly in classifying the users. The unique pattern and the relative strength of these behaviours will provide the basis fo identifying categories from the data and then relating those categories to the three postadoption IS usage stages proposed in the literature. Next, we explore factors that enable us to distinguish between users situated at different post-adoption IS usage stages.

## HYPOTHESES

Jasperson et al. (2005) propose that change from one stage to the other is driven by an adjustment to align initial expectations with actual interaction experience. However, over time the adjusted cognitions become situated leading to a pattern of IS usage behaviours. This perspective highlights two interesting issues. First, the difference in user’s perceptions across the post-adoption IS usage stages can provide insights on adjustments. Second, it would be interesting to explore whether user’s perceptions depict similar adjustments across all three IS usage stages.

In identifying pertinent user perceptions, we rely on the technology acceptance model and the post-adoption IS usage model proposed by Jasperson et al. (2005). The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) shows that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use play an important role in influencing intention to use the IS after the initial adoption decision. Longitudinal studies based on TAM have shown that the impact of perceived usefulness persists after initial adoption, whereas the role of ease of use diminishes but remains significant (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Also, usefulness has the strongest influence on future intention to use the IS (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Therefore, usefulness perceptions can be one of the reasons why users fall in different post-adoption IS usage stages. Similarly, ease of use perceptions can either constrain or facilitate user’s interaction with the IS albeit at a smaller scale. Given that this thesis has held across multiple IS types, we argue that it will also hold true in the context of self-service IS

H1: Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use will differentiate among users at different stages of post-adoption self-service IS usage.

TAM focuses on future intention to use the IS but does not provide guidance on postadoption IS usage stages. The work by Jasperson et al. (2005) offers a detailed review on this topic. An important aspect of their model is the feedback loop through which users experience with the IS shapes where they are situated at the post-adoption stages. They argue for the existence of two different feedback loops. First involves reflective assessment of the IS. As users interact with the IS, it prompts an evaluation of pre-usage cognitions. The users engage in sense making and reflectively examine the role of IS in their work (Griffith, 1999). Moving beyond routinisation stage requires revision or confirmation of initial cognitions as users gain more experience with the IS. Sense making and user-initiated learning are proposed as two mechanisms that can assist in this process (Jasperson et al., 2005). The second feedback loop is based on the premise that users have a tendency to engage in habitual usage pattern due to stabilisation of cognitions after the initial acceptance decision. In this case, users tend to become inert within a post-adoption IS usage stage, show tendency to non-reflectively use the

IS and thus depict habitual usage pattern. Although both types of feedback loops are possible, we focus our attention on the one involving reflective assessment.

Jasperson et al. (2005) define technology sense making as ‘an evaluative cognitive process that transpires when an individual contrasts the outcome of a post-adoptive episode with those expected from pre-episode cognitions’. A likely outcome of the sense making process is satisfaction that results from confirmation of pre-adoption cognitions at the post-adoption stage. Many prior studies have shown that confirmation is an important aspect that drives user’s satisfaction with IS and future IS usage intentions (Bhattacherjee, 2001). Satisfaction o dissatisfaction is an outcome of the assessment that the user goes through when they use the IS. It captures the gap or lack thereof between pre-adoption and post-adoption cognitions about the IS (Griffith, 1999). Extrapolating this argument to the self-service IS context, it can be argued that an experience that is consistent with the expectations of the user is likely to indicate to the user that the self-service IS is capable of supporting his or her service needs in future service encounters (Johnson et al., 2008). Once the user establishes a belief in the capability of the self-service IS, it can lead to using a broader set of features or exploration of how the self-service IS can assist in fulfilling emergent service needs.

It is important to recognise that satisfaction with self-service IS can drive actions to better utilise the IS but can also lock in the users into routine usage pattern. It can be argued that disruptions such as system failures or lack of met expectations provide the impetus for change rather than satisfaction. We recognise that this thesis has merit. However, lack of met expectation can also trigger abandonment of the self-service IS. If the users are not satisfied with the self-service IS, it is more likely that they will only use it for necessary tasks. However, a satisfied user has a higher likelihood of utilising the self-service IS to its fullest potential. If the users believe that the self-service IS delivers on its promise, it is more likely that they wil engage in using it and will be situated in either infusion or extension stage. On the contrary, a dissatisfied user will have a higher tendency to restrict usage to basic features on a repeated basis. Thus we argue:

H2: User’s satisfaction will differentiate among users at different stages of post-adoption self-service IS usage.

Another critical aspect that is closely related to sense making is the nature of IS usage (Venkatesh et al., 2003). In the self-service IS context, there are numerous examples where organisations have directed their customer to use self-service IS by eliminating alternative modes of service delivery. For example, many state governments in the USA have mandated that businesses file their withholding taxes electronically. Companies engage actively in channel deflection strategies by diverting customer from physical/telephone channel to the self-service channel. When IS usage is mandated, users are left with no other choice but to use it. Thus, the use environment either constraints or facilitates the sense making process and subsequently shapes the positioning of the user at a post-adoption IS usage stage.

In a mandatory context, the incentive to migrate to a different IS usage stage is constrained. However, if the users deem IS usage to be voluntary, it gives them a sense of autonomy (Beaudry & Pinnsonneault, 2005). When users have the freedom to make a choice, they are more intrinsically motivated about engaging in those activities (Barczak et al., 1997). Thus, intrinsically motivated users are more likely to be situated in either infusion or extension stage. According to psychological reactance theory, an individual becomes more attracted to the option that is taken away and is less attracted to the option that is forced upon him or her (Reinders et al., 2008). The basic premise behind the theory is that individuals react to the loss of freedom by becoming indifferent to forced behaviour, even if it offers more value. Thus, a user working in a mandatory usage environment will have a higher tendency to restrict usage to basic features. Thus, we argue:

H3: User’s perception about voluntariness of use will differentiate among users at differen stages of post-adoption self-service IS usage.

User-initiated learning is an important factor that can explain why users may be situated at different post-adoption IS usage stages (Gioia & Manz, 1985; Orlikowski et al., 1995; Venkatesh & Speier, 1999). Users engage in a variety of learning modes. Learning avenues include users, observation, formal training, manuals and system-related help features (Bandura, 1986; Brancheau & Wetherbe, 1990). In the context of self-service IS, organisations offer online tutorials, information about new system features, online help desk and forums for the users to interact with each other. Relevant learning mechanisms include those that disrupt standard IS usage patterns and steer the users towards expanding their usage of self-service IS. Jasperson et al. (2005) highlights user-initiated learning from sources provided by the organisation as an important aspect. They propose that users and technology experts can challenge standard usage routines and provide guidance on how to integrate the IS in the work environment.

Users generally tend to seek assistance from other users when they confront novel situations or simply when they believe that other users possess more knowledge about the IS capability and features. However, the influence of such options to a large extent depends on the user’s discretion in utilising them. Prior research shows that social influence impacts intention to use an IS (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Users can choose to leverage the resources to gain a better understanding of the IS in general and enhance their knowledge of its specific features and capabilities. Thus, users who engage in active learning from other users and experts are more likely to be classified into a different post-adoption IS usage stage than users who do not solicit such assistance. Thus, we argue that:

H4: User-initiated learning differentiates among users at different stages of post-adoption self-service IS usage.

As highlighted previously, it is also interesting to explore if all the five factors will differentiate across users at routinisation stage, infusion stage and extension stage. Literature does no provide any detailed guidance on this issue. Some of the prior studies show that the influence of usefulness perceptions persists at the post-adoption stage, while the impact of ease of use wears off over time (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). Empirical assessment of satisfaction, voluntariness of use and user-initiated learning at the post-adoption stage is limited. Thus, we approach this question from an exploratory perspective by proposing that all the five factors will differentiate the users across the three post-adoption self-service IS usage stages (see Figure 2).

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

The study was conducted in the context of a Web-based student information system (SIS) that has been developed by a university to offer students various functionalities to support their academic work. SIS is introduced to students when they enrol at the university. SIS usage for the students, although encouraged, is completely voluntary. Thus, all the tasks that are available through the SIS could be completed through alternative means at the time this study was conducted. The university did not take any specific initiatives to convert the students from one option to the other. Efforts were only made to make the students aware about the options that they had in completing the various tasks when they joined the university.

The main focus of SIS is to assist the students in their academic work. SIS includes an extensive list of features out of which we selected 10 features to include in our study (Table 2). The selection was done based on input from the system administrators and a focus group that we conducted with six students from different colleges within the university. SIS can be categorised as a self-service IS that falls within the operational customer support system. It depicts similarity to IS such as online banking, online brokerage and self-service benefits management. Other systems include the ones that predominately focus on providing online access to customers to conduct transactions and get customer service. This offers an interesting context because organisations have made substantial investment in this area. The higher the extent to which the users engage in these systems, the more benefits accrue to the firms through reduced resource requirements, higher level of customer service satisfaction and greater switching costs. The scope of self-service IS also includes self-service kiosks and interactive voice response systems. In this study, we limit the focus to Web-based IS that supports transactions and online service delivery.

## Scale development

New instruments were developed and validated for measuring post-adoption usage of IS behaviours and user-initiated learning. Other variables were measured using existing scales from the literature. Table 2 provides the list of items for the constructs. The development of the instrument for post-adoption usage of IS behaviours involved four steps. First, we reviewed the literature to comprehend the conceptual structure of the constructs and subsequent item’s creation. Second, six student users of SIS participated in a focus group on examining the use of the SIS in supporting their academic work. The main objective of the focus group was to conduct an initial refinement of the items and further comprehend the concept of post-adoption IS usage behaviour. The inputs provided by the participants of the focus group were used to modify the items. Next, we conducted a q-sorting exercise wherein six judges sorted the items based on construct definitions provided to them. All judges were able to correctly categorise the items. Table 3 provides examples of different IS usage behaviours to support the practical relevance of the scheme developed through the literature review. ## CLUSTER ANALYSIS

After validating the measures for self-service IS usage behaviours, the next step was to formulate post-adoption self-service IS usage stages. As recommended by earlier research, the appropriate approach is to investigate discernable patterns visible in the data keeping into consideration routinisation, infusion and extension stages proposed in the literature. We used cluster analysis to address this issue. k-means cluster approach was used because sufficient theoretical support exists on the three stages of post-adoption self-service IS usage. k-means cluster approach uses the Euclidean distance to classify cases into homogenous groups, although a priori specification of the number of groups is required. It is a good approach for large datasets. The clustering algorithm offers the option of using fixed cluster centres or using running cluster centre means. In the fixed option, the initial cluster centres are not updated, while in the running means option, the cluster centres are updated after each case is assigned to a cluster. However, the running means option is impacted by case order. In order to ascertain the stability of the results, we used both approaches iteratively and found marginal difference in the results. The commonality across the three groups was 98% for group 1, 99% for group 2 and 100% for group 3. Based on these results, we decided to use the algorithm based on fixed cluster centres because it is not impacted by the ordering of the observations.

## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Multi-group discriminant analysis (MDA) was used to evaluate the efficacy of the variables in distinguishing the users situated at different post-adoption SIS usage stages. In conducting MDA, a three-step process was followed. The first step was to determine which variables significantly discriminate among the users in different post-adoption SIS usage stages. Similar to regression, MDA offers stepwise procedure that helps in identifying the variables that are the best differentiators. The second step involves the identification of optimal number of discriminant functions that represents aggregation of variables into higher-level factors. The third step includes classifying observations based on the discriminant functions and evaluating the efficacy of the classification rate. At this stage, an important issue is evaluating external validity that relates to the accuracy of the discriminant functions in classifying cases that are from a different sample. The large dataset gave us the opportunity to use the holdout method to assess external validity.

We segmented the sample into two groups. Data from one group were used to estimate the discriminant function. The function was then used to classify the observations in the second group. In order to segment the sample, we used a random number function based on uniform distribution to generate and assign a value between zero and one to each observation. The values were than rounded off. The first group that was used for the estimation of the discrimi nant functions had 513 observations, while the second group that was used for classification had 495 observations.

The important issue in evaluating this question is to examine the efficacy of the classification rate. We used the approach proposed by Huberty (1984), which provides test statistics to evaluate the statistical significance of the overall classification rate and classification rate for each group.

The test statistics evaluates the value of the classification rate against classification rate due to chance. The assessment shows that the overall classification rate comes out to be 65%, which is significantly greater than due to chance (z-value = 14.81). Thus, we believe that the predictive efficacy of the functions has room for improvement and further studies specially focused on the infusion stage can help.

## IMPLICATIONS AND CONCLUSION

The conceptualisation and measurement approach in this study offers a comprehensive understanding of post-adoption self-service IS usage phenomenon. Some users tend to remain in their comfort zone, while others may test the boundaries of what the IS has to offer. The study models three post-adoption IS usage behaviours and aggregates them to uncover three post-adoption IS usage stages referred as routinisation, infusion and extension, which align well with prior literature on innovation diffusion. Although, the study does not explicitly capture the transition across these stages, it does support the existence of three distinct stages that are manifested through specific pattern of IS usage behaviours. Without a clear understanding of what post-adoption IS usage constitutes, it is difficult to devise strategies to promote it. Thus, our work contributes to the literature by highlighting IS usage behaviours visible in the three post-adoption IS usage stages in self-service IS context. The measuremen approach offered in the study can be used by firms to evaluate how their customers are interacting with self-service IS and their distribution across the IS usage stages. This information can be used for monitoring, as well as for developing strategies to promote desirable usage behaviour, if required. Higher engagement of the customers with self-service IS can substantially increase switching costs and at the same time lead to reduced resource requirements in providing services.

The study also evaluates key user perceptions that may differ across users situated at different self-service IS usage stages. Specifically, the relationship among voluntariness of use, satisfaction, user-initiated learning and post-adoption IS usage stages offers interesting theoretical and practical contributions. Understanding mechanics that invokes sense making on the part of the users is an important issue. Reactance theory links forced adoption with negative user reactions. A likely consequence can be development of habitual usage pattern, which has been documented in earlier research (Jasperson et al., 2005). Although such behaviour in general is not detrimental, it does influence the effective utilisation of the opportunities that self-service IS offers. Furthermore, users are more receptive to self-service IS that meets their expectations. To further investigate this thesis, we compared the percentage of users at different post-adoption IS usage stages across satisfaction and voluntariness of use. Forty-five per cent of the users were in the extension stage that had a satisfaction rating of fou and above as compared with 42% in the infusion stage and 13% in the routinisation stage. Further, a similar assessment in the context of voluntariness of use shows that 43% of the users were in the extension stage that viewed usage as voluntary as compared with 32% in the infusion stage and 25% in the routinisation stage. Prior research has documented forced usage of self-service IS with negative attitude towards self-service IS usage and the organisation (Reinders et al., 2008). Thus, offering self-service IS as part of a multiple channe strategy that provides alternative service options can foster positive user perceptions, leading to enhanced utilisation of self-service IS. Further, a consistent assessment of user satisfaction with the self-service IS should be an integral part of the assessment process. Evaluating user’s met expectation can help in consistently offering a service experience that users enjoy and enhance the likelihood of creating a favourable impression about the firm among the users.

User-initiated learning offers the most interesting pattern among the factors examined. Prior studies have not adequately examined the role of such learning in different stages of post adoption IS usage, so it is an important contribution that this study offers. User-initiated learning has the most impact in the initial stages after adoption and in the advanced stages of IS usage. Reliance on this mechanism diminishes once the IS becomes integrated in the work practices. The results thus offer initial guidance on the effectiveness of learning from outside sources at different stages of post-adoption IS usage. For example, a firm may engage in developing targeted learning mechanisms for users at different stages of the self-service IS utilisation. Mechanisms such as expert chat, feature of the day and customer discussion forums are interesting options to explore. However, our research only establishes the impor tance of user-initiated learning at the aggregate level; more research is needed in further exploring the effectiveness of different learning mechanisms at different stages of self-service IS usage. Such an effort can help in developing guidelines on how to effectively use learning approaches at the post-adoption IS usage stages.

Finally, the aggregation of variables based on the discriminant functions into value confirmation and learning orientation offers two higher-level factors that help in explaining why users are situated at different post-adoption IS usage stages. Value confirmation shows a consistent increase, suggesting that users situated at the three different stages show divergence across this dimension. These results are in agreement with what other researchers have found in the context of different types of IS (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Assessment of value, interaction experience and met expectations play a pivotal role in differentiating users of self-service IS across post-adoption usage stages. However, learning orientation shows a much higher divergence across users in the infusion and extension stages. The aggregation of user-initiated learning and voluntariness of use into a single factor has some support in the literature According to Reinders et al. (2008), when self-service IS is forced on the users, help desk and system support options can mitigate negative user attitudes. Thus, negative user reactions towards self-service IS can be eased by instituting programmes to support user-initiated learning. This area also presents an interesting avenue for future research. As companies aggressively move into the self-service IS domain, developing an effective multi-channe strategy and managing the transition from one channel to another pose important challenges that future studies can examine.

The study has some limitations that should be noted. The generalisability of the results is limited to the domain of self-service IS such as Web-based SIS, online banking system, online brokerage system and Web-based benefits management system. Further testing of the theses proposed in this study in other contexts and IS types can help in addressing this issue. The measurement of expanded usage is specific to the Web-based SIS. This approach has limited applicability in the context of other IS. However, features available in other IS can be used to capture the breadth of feature level usage. Although using the feature level approach provides a more focused and better measurement approach, it will put the onus on the researchers to develop and validate measures that are specific to an IS. Identification of post-adoption IS usage stages was predominately based on the patterns visible in the data. To address this issue, we followed the guidelines offered in the literature on conducting k-means cluster analysis and we mapped the identified stages onto stages proposed in prior literature. Data collection at a single point in time only enables us to evaluate our thesis based on the variance approach. We first identify how the users are situated across the stages and evaluate if their perceptions are different. The causality between user’s perceptions and progression across stages, if examined through longitudinal assessment, can offer further insights. Voluntariness of use was measured as a dichotomous variable. MDA analysis requires that independen variables conform to normal distribution constraint. To address this issue, we ran the model using logistic regression. The results were identical to what were observed from the MDA analysis, so MDA analysis was used because it is a more robust technique. Finally, the classification rate has room for improvement. We believe that exploring additional factors offers a viable approach in identifying comprehensive discriminant functions, which can improve the classification rate. Factors that promote reflective assessment of IS and factors that promote the development of habitual behaviour offer two counter veiling forces that if examined together may help in improving the classification rate.
