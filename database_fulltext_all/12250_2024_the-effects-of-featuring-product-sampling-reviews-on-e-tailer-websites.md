# The Effects of Featuring Product Sampling Reviews on E-Tailer Websites

Xianghua Lu,<sup>1</sup> Chee Wei Phang,<sup>2</sup> Sulin Ba,<sup>3</sup> Xinlin Yao<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Fudan University, Shanghai, China, lxhua@fudan.edu.cn <sup>2</sup>University of Nottingham Ningbo China, Ningbo, China, cheewei.phang@nottingham.edu.cn <sup>3</sup>Depaul University, USA, sulin.ba@depaul.edu <sup>4</sup>Naikai University, Tianjin, China, xinlinyao@nankai.edu.cn

## Abstract

Given the significant influence of online product reviews on consumer purchases, firms are trying to be more proactive in leveraging the influence of reviews. One way of doing this is to offer free product samples to consumers to incentivize product reviews, displaying them along with a disclosure in the existing pool of organic reviews in the hopes of stimulating sales. However, it is not clear whether such product sampling reviews are indeed beneficial to sales, considering that they may have a nontrivial influence on the generation of organic reviews. Based on data from a leading e-commerce platform in China, we found that sponsored product sampling reviews promoted product sales but also reduced the volume of organic reviews for the targeted product. However, the net effect on sales remained positive, suggesting that e-tailers may wish to utilize sponsored product sampling reviews. We also conducted a scenario-based experiment to reveal how the nature of product sampling reviews affects consumers’ brand quality and fairness perceptions. Based on our findings we offer future research directions to improve the understanding of how to better harness the power of sponsored reviews.

Keywords: Sponsored Reviews; E-Tailer, Product Sampling, Organic Reviews, Word-of-Mouth (WOM)

## 1 Introduction

Given that online product reviews play a major role in consumers’ purchasing decisions (Ceran et al., 2016; Chen & Xie, 2008; Dellarocas et al., 2007; Godes et al., 2005; Goes et al., 2014; Sun & Xu, 2018), firms have been trying to play a more active role in harnessing their influence (Chae et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2016; Petrescu et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2019; Jabr & Rahman, 2022). Sponsored reviews have emerged as a potentially viable option for increasing the efficacy of online product reviews and have attracted increasing attention from practitioners (e.g., e-tailers) and researchers in recent years. Sponsored reviews refer to “online product and service reviews posted on e-tailers or review websites as a result of an incentive received by the reviewer.” (Petrescu et al., 2018, p. 2) In contrast to organic reviews, firms can proactively facilitate the production of sponsored reviews and ensure they are ethically acceptable by disclosing their solicited status when displaying them to consumers.<sup>1</sup>

Incentives offered to consumers may take the form of cash, coupons, discounts, raffles, or free product samples (Chae et al., 2017; Lu et al., 2014; Kim et al., 2019).

Due to the controversial nature of cash-based incentives (Lacetera & Macis, 2010), sponsored reviews solicited through the use of non-cash-based incentives such as free product samples are becoming increasingly common (Kim et al., 2019; Petrescu et al., 2018; Lin et al., 2019). For example, Amazon’s Vine Program reviews are reviews generated from consumers who tried out a free product. They are displayed alongside organic reviews of the product.

Research has shown that consumers perceive sponsored reviews tied to noncash-based incentives to be more acceptable than those solicited through cash payments (Lu et al., 2014). If a firm offers cash payments in exchange for reviews, consumers often perceive such firms as “buying” product recommendations (Hsu, 2010). In contrast, if a firm offers free product samples, consumers may see this as an opportunity for consumers to test and evaluate the product and may thus be more positive toward such reviews (Hsu, 2010; Lu et al., 2014).

Likewise, research has shown that offering product sampling can serve as a quality signal (Hu et al., 2012). Thus, given that sampling may signal a firm’s confidence in a product it makes available for scrutiny and review by consumers, displaying such reviews can serve as a further signal of quality and help promote product sales. However, a potential downside of displaying sponsored product sampling reviews alongside organic reviews is that introducing sponsored reviews could impact the generation of reviews by non-incentivized consumers. For instance, in the context of Amazon, Petrescu et al. (2018) found that a sponsored review campaign reduced the number of verified reviews, i.e., reviews from consumers with verified purchases who did not receive an incentive. They attribute this to the possibility that “product buyers were less motivated to post a review due to negative influences from the campaign or the hundreds of free products offered through this campaign” (p. 7). However, their study did not investigate the specific factor underlying the negative influence of sponsored reviews. We propose that displaying product sampling reviews may create a feeling of unfairness among consumers who did not receive a product sample. From an equity theory perspective, if consumers feel that they are being unfairly treated because they had to pay for the product, their motivation to invest the time and effort to contribute a review of the product may be dampened (Wu & Huberman, 2010).

Since organic reviews are known to influence sales, any effect on organic reviews could have implications for product sales. If the presence of product sampling reviews leads to fewer organic reviews, this could hurt sales. Therefore, is is both interesting and important to investigate the net sales effect of sponsored reviews after taking their potential effect on organic review generation into account. To the best of our knowledge, there has been little examination of the net sales effect of sponsored reviews and the specific factors that underlie both the positive and negative influence mechanisms of such reviews.

To fill this research gap, we conducted two empirical studies. In Study 1, we collaborated with a leading etailer in China, who was interested in testing whether using product sampling reviews could stimulate product sales. The actual sales and review data obtained from this collaboration allowed us to better examine the effects of product sampling reviews on the volume of subsequent organic reviews and on product sales as a whole. Our findings indicate that featuring sponsored product sampling reviews can increase product sales (compared to the absence of such reviews). However, incorporating these reviews on the product page also resulted in a reduction in organic reviews for the product. Nevertheless, after accounting for the negative effect on sales due to the reduction in organic reviews, the net effect of the sponsored reviews on sales remained positive.

In Study 2, we conducted an online scenario-based experiment with responses from 389 online shoppers to investigate how featuring product sampling reviews affects consumers’ brand quality and unfairness perceptions and, in turn, their subsequent intention to purchase the product and their intention to provide a product review. The results reveal that product sampling reviews are better than organic reviews at promoting consumers’ brand quality perceptions, thus leading to higher purchase intentions. However, we also found that product sampling reviews increased consumers’ perceptions of unfairness, resulting in lower intentions to contribute organic reviews.

In terms of research contributions, we demonstrate the impact of featuring sponsored product sampling reviews in the context of an e-tailer website, taking into account how this may affect the volume of organic reviews and sales. This context is important as this is where the sales effect of sponsored reviews can be directly tracked and analyzed. While earlier research has focused primarily on sponsored reviews in the context of blog websites (e.g., Carr & Hayes, 2014; Colliander & Erlandsson, 2015; Hwang & Jeong, 2016), recent research has begun to investigate the etailer context (Kim et al., 2019; Petrescu et al., 2018).

However, this research did not consider how introducing sponsored reviews impacts the generation of organic reviews and product sales as a whole. It is important to account for the effects of sponsored reviews on organic reviews to obtain a more accurate and comprehensive understanding of their impact on sales.

Further, our study reveals the underlying influence mechanisms of sponsored product sampling reviews by explicating two mediating factors, i.e., brand quality perception and perceived unfairness. This offers the theoretical explanations for why sampling reviews can result in both positive (increased intention to purchase) and negative outcomes (reduced intention to contribute an organic review), which future research could further evaluate. Third, this study also enriches the understanding of product sampling reviews by comparing them to other types of product reviews, i.e., organic and paid reviews, in terms of consumer perceptions and behavioral intentions.

For practitioners, specifically e-tailers, our study suggests that introducing sponsored product sampling reviews is a viable strategy for taking a more proactive role in leveraging product reviews. Explicitly disclosing that such reviews are sponsored, i.e., produced as a result of consumer product sampling, also satisfies ethical concerns related to transparency, deception, and the potential of misleading consumers (Han et al., 2018; van der Goot et al., 2021). In other words, sponsored product sampling reviews do not try to pass themselves off as organic reviews and thus do not deceive consumers. While our study revealed a positive net sales effect of sponsored product sampling reviews, it also highlighted a potential caveat for firms leveraging this review type, i.e., such reviews could hurt subsequent organic review contributions for the targeted product. Therefore, to the extent that organic reviews are important for brands, e-tailers should exercise caution in the use of these reviews and should carefully weigh the costs and benefits of using sponsored product sampling reviews.

In the following sections, we first provide a review of the extant literature on the effects of sponsored reviews (of which product sampling reviews are a type), and the gaps that our study aims to address. We then develop our research hypotheses in Section 3. Sections 4 and 5 introduce the research design and results of Studies 1 and 2, respectively. Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper with a discussion of implications for research and practice.

## 2 Conceptual Background: Literature on the Effects of Sponsored Reviews

While sponsored reviews are becoming increasingly common, in the past, their legitimacy was often questioned because some firms chose not to disclose that they were sponsored, disguising them instead as organic product reviews. Since disclosure has the potential to influence consumer perceptions and the sales effect of sponsored reviews, a large amount of research has focused on comparing the disclosure and non-disclosure of sponsored reviews (Carr & Hayes, 2014; Colliander & Erlandsson, 2015; Du Plessis et al., 2016; Hwang & Jeong, 2016). For example, such studies have compared simple disclosure and nondisclosure (Colliander & Erlandsson, 2015; Du Plessis et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2012) as well as different levels of disclosure explicitness, e.g., whether the reviews included explicit or ambiguous mentions of incentives (Carr & Hayes, 2014).

The findings from this line of research are mixed. For instance, Colliander and Erlandsson (2015) found that while disclosure may damage review authors’ credibility, it has no impact on readers’ purchase intentions. However, Du Plessis et al. (2016) found that disclosure led consumers to discount product reviews and altered their perceptions of product quality. Other studies showed that by explicitly disclosing sponsorship (Carr & Hayes, 2014) or emphasizing honesty in writing the review (Hwang & Jeong, 2016), sponsored reviews were able to alleviate the negative impacts of disclosure on, for example, source credibility and attitudes toward the review. Other research has investigated whether disclosure affects review quality, finding that disclosing a material connection motivated review authors to write higherquality sponsored reviews. We note that previous studies on sponsored reviews have primarily focused on monetary incentives.

Given the increasing consensus that the disclosure of sponsored reviews should be standard ethical practice,<sup>2</sup> there has been a shift toward research that directly addresses the effects of sponsored reviews whose nature is disclosed. An early study conducted by Lu et al. (2014) showed that the effects of disclosed sponsored reviews on consumer attitudes and purchase intentions are more salient and positive for search products and for brands with high visibility. Chae et al. (2017) found that seeded marketing campaigns may stimulate more sponsored review generation for the targeted product. We note that most of these studies were conducted in the context of blog websites (Carr & Hayes, 2014; Colliander & Erlandsson, 2015; Hwang & Jeong, 2016; Lu et al., 2014) or online communities (Chae et al., 2017), which makes it challenging to directly relate the effects of sponsored reviews to sales given that these venues are not where actual purchases occur. One study that was conducted in the e-tailer context is Du Plessis et al. (2016), which used data on Amazon’s sponsored Vine review program, to obtain initial insights about consumer ratings of the helpfulness of sponsored reviews compared to organic reviews. However, their study did not investigate how sponsored reviews affect sales.

Recent research has started to examine the influence of sponsored reviews on sales in the context of e-tailer websites. For instance, Pretrescu et al. (2018) found that sponsored reviews on Amazon.com may lead to a higher purchase potential (measured by the number of verified reviews as a proxy for the approximate number of purchases). They posited that sponsored reviews can lead to increased consumer interest in finding out more about the product through search engines, potentially resulting in higher sales. Kim et al. (2019) found that compared to organic reviews, sponsored reviews (with sponsorship disclosure) provide more elaborate and evaluative content but are perceived as less helpful and evoke lower purchase intentions compared to organic reviews. Table A1 in the Appendix presents a summary of the prior studies that have investigated the effect of sponsored reviews.

Although the studies above offer valuable insights into the nature and effect of sponsored reviews, there are several gaps in the extant research that our study aims to address. First, the extant research has focused on comparing the relative effects of sponsored review disclosure on consumer purchases (e.g., Carr & Hayes, 2014; Colliander & Erlandsson, 2015; Hwang & Jeong, 2016) or comparing sponsored reviews to organic reviews (Kim et al., 2019). For instance, Kim et al. (2019) showed that sponsored reviews may be inferior to organic reviews in terms of their perceived helpfulness and their effect on consumers’ purchase intentions (Kim et al., 2019). However, the question remains whether the use of sponsored reviews itself is a worthwhile strategy for firms seeking to take a more proactive role in leveraging reviews. In this paper, we evaluate whether featuring sponsored reviews (with disclosure per ethical requirements) promotes higher sales.

Second, most previous studies have focused on sponsored reviews solicited via cash incentives (i.e., paid reviews). There has been little research attention devoted to sponsored reviews motivated by free product samples, which could induce different perceptions and potentially different sales effects compared to paid reviews. While the use of cash incentives is likely to invoke clear-cut market exchange norms (Heyman & Ariely, 2004), i.e., the review is produced as a pure monetary exchange (Jin & Huang, 2014), we empirically explore how the sponsored product sampling reviews, in particular, impact consumers perceptions of the targeted product and thus their purchase and review contribution intentions.

Third, while recent research has begun to pay attention to sponsored reviews such as product sampling reviews on e-tailer websites where direct purchases occur (Kim et al., 2019; Pretrescu et al., 2018; Jabr & Rahman, 2022), more research on this context is needed. Specifically, on e-tailer websites, organic reviews are typically provided to aid consumers’ purchase decisions. Thus, if sponsored product sampling reviews are used, they will likely appear alongside existing organic reviews. Given their nature, i.e., they are produced by consumers after sampling a free product, we seek to evaluate whether the use of such sponsored reviews affects the production of voluntary organic reviews. This possible effect is important to account for, given that any negative effect on organic review production could lead to reduced sales (Clemons et al., 2006; Duan et al., 2008; Lau et al., 2018). However, few extant studies have examined the net sales effect of sponsored product sampling reviews. We do so by tracking both sales and organic review volume, enabling us to more accurately assess the effect of sponsored product sampling reviews on sales as a whole, which is of crucial importance for research on sponsored reviews and for etailers considering the use of sponsored product sampling reviews.

Lastly, there remains a lack of understanding and empirical evidence concerning the theoretical mechanisms through which product sampling reviews may exert their positive and negative influences. We seek to alleviate this research gap in order to pave the way for more systematic research focusing, in particular, on improving the design and implementation of sponsored reviews. This could also help practitioners better harness the power of sponsored product sampling reviews while minimizing potential pitfalls.

## 3 Research Hypotheses

This study examines sponsored product sampling reviews, which are reviews motivated by providing consumers with a free product sample. Research has shown that consumers view sponsored reviews motivated by non-cash-based incentives more favorably than those motivated by cash (Lu et al., 2014). In the context of product recommendations on blogs, Petty and Andrews (2008) also argued that consumers may consider bloggers who receive cash payments to be less credible than consumers receiving free product samples because consumers are likely to see cash payments as a firm “buying” the product recommendation and the blogger as “selling” the trust of consumers (Hsu, 2010). This is consistent with the view that cash compensation tends to be perceived as a straightforward market transaction (Lacetera & Macis, 2010). In contrast, consumers may view free product samples as a trial opportunity and the ensuing reviews as an evaluation of the product trial (Hsu, 2010; Lu et al., 2014). Previous research has also shown that compared to cash incentives, noncash incentives are associated with perceptions of altruism (Heyman & Ariely, 2004; Lacetera & Macis, 2010).

Research has also noted that when a brand offers free product samples, it signals the brand’s confidence in the quality of its product (Lu et al., 2018; Wright & Lynch, 1995). For instance, Hu et al. (2012) showed that providing a sampling option sends out a strong signal of product quality that could attract interested shoppers, thus promoting better sales than products without a sampling option. Thus, product sampling reviews may also be perceived as a signal of a brand’s confidence in the quality of its product. Based on signaling theory, this may increase consumer confidence in the product by reducing the uncertainty of decision-making (Spence, 1973). Accordingly, we hypothesize:

H1a: Ceteris paribus, featuring sponsored product sampling reviews as a source of WOM will promote sales more than not featuring such reviews.

H1b: Featuring sponsored product sampling reviews promotes sales by raising consumers’ quality perceptions of targeted products.

While we expect sponsored reviews generated from product sampling to have a positive effect on sales, we note the caveat of featuring these reviews alongside existing organic product reviews on e-tailer websites. Specifically, featuring sponsored reviews alongside organic reviews may affect the subsequent generation of organic reviews for the targeted product. This effect is important to consider—sponsored reviews may not only affect consumers considering purchasing the product but may also impact consumers considering writing an organic review. The perpetual nature of online review information (versus its transient nature offline) also makes the latter effect potentially salient. Thus, we need to understand how sponsored product sampling reviews affect organic review production to obtain a more comprehensive and balanced understanding of the effect of such reviews in the context of e-tailer websites, where both sponsored and organic reviews are available to consumers.

In contrast to the expected positive effect of sponsored product sampling reviews on sales, based primarily on equity theory (Huppertz et al., 1978), we expect a negative impact on organic review contribution. According to equity theory, equity is perceived by individuals as something that is distributed fairly (Oliver,

1997). In the context of consumer behavior, consumers may form perceptions of equity or fairness in relation to their inputs (e.g., time, effort) and outputs (e.g., tangible and intangible benefits) in an exchange, along three aspects, i.e., distributive, procedural, and interactional.

Distributive equity refers to the extent to which consumers perceive that the reward for their input is fair by comparing inputs to outcomes (Oliver & Swan, 1989). It is present when the perceived inputs and/or outcomes received by a consumer are psychologically consistent with those of similar consumers (Lacey & Sneath, 2006). Procedural equity concerns the perceived fairness of the process through which reward allocation decisions are made, including the consistency of application in policies, procedures, and other criteria used to determine outcomes (Blodgett et al., 1997). For example, Shugan (2005) found that consumers can be discouraged from participating in an activity if it is not implemented in a standard and fair manner among different participants. Interactional equity is associated with interpersonal treatment and focuses on two-way communications between consumers and firms, including whether consumers are treated with respect, interest, friendliness, honesty, and politeness (Lacey & Sneath, 2006; Maxham & Netemeyer, 2003). Of particular relevance to this study are the distributive and procedural aspects of equity, as discussed below.

When an e-tailer features a sponsored product sampling review on its website, other consumers become aware that some consumers contributed a review after receiving a free product sample. For consumers considering contributing an organic review, this may create a feeling of inequity. Specifically, as writing a review is costly in terms of time and effort (Wu & Huberman, 2010), individuals may feel that it would be unfair if their effort were not equally rewarded with a product sample, i.e., distributive equity (Lacey & Sneath, 2006; Oliver & Swan, 1989). Consumers may also perceive unfairness due to the inconsistent process used by firms to acquire product reviews—i.e., procedural equity (Blodgett et al., 1997). While most consumers are expected to contribute voluntary reviews, some consumers are incentivized by product sampling. Thus, product sampling reviews are likely to dampen the willingness of some consumers to contribute organic reviews, which may then lead to a lower overall volume of organic reviews. Thus we hypothesize:

H2a: Featuring sponsored product sampling reviews as a source of WOM will result in a reduced volume of subsequent organic reviews.

H2b: Featuring sponsored product sampling reviews reduces the volume of organic reviews by increasing perceived unfairness among consumers.

While we expect a generally positive effect of product sampling reviews on sales, we also need to consider whether sponsored product sampling reviews, after accounting for their negative effect on the volume of organic reviews, still lead to a net positive effect on sales. We expect the net positive effect to hold based on a twofold rationale. Specifically, a meta-analysis study by Floyd et al. (2014) highlighted that higher review volumes can lead to higher sales primarily by (1) promoting consumer awareness of the targeted product and (2) increasing diagnosticity through conveying product popularity information.

Regarding the first point, although reduced review volumes may diminish product awareness to some extent (Chen et al., 2004; Maeyer, 2012), featuring sponsored product sampling reviews may compensate for this effect since such reviews can increase the visibility of the product among consumers. Indeed, product sampling itself has been noted as an effective means of bolstering product awareness (Heilman et al., 2011; McGuiness et al., 1995). Coupled with the review information based on product sampling, sponsored product sampling reviews can also help reduce consumer uncertainty about whether to purchase the product, thus promoting sales.

Regarding the second point, a high volume of organic reviews can be used as an indication of product popularity because it suggests that many people have bought the product (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006; Filieri et al., 2018). This can facilitate quick decision-making among consumers since large numbers of people taking an action tends to convey the correctness of the action (Salganik & Watts, 2008). However, since sponsored product sampling reviews may reduce the volume of organic reviews, the popularity effects of organic reviews may be attenuated. If the sponsored product sampling reviews do not fully compensate for this effect, lower product sales may follow.

To evaluate the strength of these competing rationales, we propose two competing hypotheses, which will be tested through a mediation analysis that explicitly accounts for the negative effect of a reduced volume of organic reviews on sales. This will allow for a more complete assessment of the net sales effect resulting from featuring sponsored product sampling reviews. Thus, we hypothesize:

H3a: After accounting for the effect of the reduced volume of organic reviews, the net sales effect of featuring sponsored product sampling reviews remains positive.

H3b: After accounting for the effect of the reduced volume of organic reviews, the net sales effect of featuring sponsored product sampling reviews becomes negative.

The overall research framework is depicted in Figure 1. To test the hypotheses, we conducted two empirical studies. Study 1 used real sales data to examine the effect of featuring sponsored product sampling reviews on product sales, the volume of subsequent organic reviews, and net product sales. Study 2 used a scenariobased experiment to examine the psychological mechanisms through which sponsored product sampling reviews may affect sales and the volume of organic reviews. In particular, comparisons to organic reviews and cash-incentivized sponsored reviews were used to evaluate consumers’ brand quality perceptions and perceptions of unfairness.

## 4 Study 1: Effects of Featuring Product Sampling Review on Product Sales and Subsequent Organic Review Volume

## 4.1 Data Description

We obtained our dataset from a leading e-tailer platform in China that experimented with the use of sponsored product sampling reviews to promote sales. The platform sells more than 500,000 products through its online channel, including grocery and household products, clothing, electrical appliances, toys, and cosmetic and healthcare products. In addition, similar to Amazon, the platform not only sells its own products but also serves as a transaction platform for third-party vendors. To obtain sponsored reviews, product samples were used as an incentive. The product samples were provided by their respective brands, and the platform acted as a coordinator by running the campaigns for the brands, selecting who would receive a product sample, and soliciting sponsored reviews from the sample receivers. As the product samples were full-size products, only a limited number were offered and consumers needed to apply to receive the samples.<sup>3</sup> The selection of the recipients was random.

To implement the sponsored review program, a specialized section was set up on the e-tailer website in October 2012, allowing different brands/manufacturers to offer product samples to customers. A dedicated webpage was created for each product for which samples were available and displayed information about the brand, product, and campaign, including the number of samples available, the time period to apply for the product sample, and the current number of applicants.

Customers could apply for free product samples on the platform when the campaign began. Campaigns typically ran for one week. Platform customers could apply for the product samples by filling out a simple form to indicate their interest and provide basic information such as their age, gender, and mailing address. After the sampling campaign ended, the product samples were mailed to the recipients and the sample recipients were encouraged to write a review detailing their experience of using the product. On average, 4% of customers in our dataset obtained a sample and 48% of the recipients submitted a review.<sup>4</sup>

On September 6, 2013, the e-tailer began featuring a single sponsored product sampling review<sup>5</sup> alongside the existing organic reviews on the product page (see Figure 2 for an example). Consumers who checked the reviews of this product could see the sponsored product sampling review on the product page. They were clearly informed that this review was not a regular/organic review and there was an explicit icon indicating that it was a sponsored review (see Figure 2 below).

We collected campaign information on all products (217 in total) featured in sampling campaigns before July 1, 2013 (per agreement with the platform). We then captured sales data on the products from August 6, 2013 to January 6, 2014 (the maximum time length of data the platform agreed to) in order to examine the change in sales before and after September 6, 2013, when the sponsored product sampling reviews began to be featured on the product information page (see Figure 3 for a depiction of the timeline). We also collected the organic reviews of these products during this time period.

We first empirically examined whether the sponsored product sampling reviews were different from the organic reviews in terms of their comprehensiveness and positivity. We ranked the organic reviews of the 217 products by length (i.e., number of words), and randomly selected 1,000 from the first 100,000 organic reviews (the total number of organic reviews for all 217 products was more than 1 million) to make the two types of reviews more comparable. We coded three variables for the reviews (sponsored and organic): the number of positive points about the product, the number of negative points about the product, and the number of product features mentioned. Examples of positive points were favorable descriptions such as “a good experience” and “easy to use”; examples of negative points were negative descriptions such as “bad smell” and “difficult to operate.” Product features were product-relevant features mentioned in the review, such as the appearance, size, and functionalities. Table 1 presents a comparison between sponsored product sampling reviews and organic reviews in terms of positivity and comprehensiveness.

## 4.2 Tests of H1a and H2a

To test the effects of sponsored product sampling reviews on the two dependent variables identified in H1a and H2a (i.e., sales and organic review volume, respectively), we designed a difference-in-differences (DID) model to tease out possible time and sample biases. DID models are commonly used in social sciences to determine treatment effects (Shadish et al., 2002). DID models construct a “treatment group” that is exposed to a treatment and a “control group” that is not and compare the difference in the differences of the dependent variables before and after the treatment for the two groups. This approach removes biases that could result from permanent differences between the groups as well as biases due to changes over time in factors that are common to the treatment and control group. In this study, featuring sponsored product sampling reviews imposed an intervention on the sales of the products involved. However, there could have been a natural change in product sales on the e-tailer platform due to its business development in recent years. Also, the evolution of online reviews may have followed a stable pattern (Godes & Silva, 2012; Moe & Schweidel, 2012). We used a DID model to control for systematic temporal changes and to more precisely measure the effect of sponsored product sampling reviews.

We used time = 1 to indicate that the date was after September 6, 2013, when sponsored product sampling reviews began to be featured on the product page. In our dataset, some products involved in sampling campaigns did not have sponsored reviews featured on their product page during the observation period.<sup>6</sup> The platform manager stated during an interview that because they were unsure whether sponsored reviews would indeed help drive sales compared to displaying only organic reviews, they arbitrarily displayed sponsored reviews on some product pages but not others. For the excluded product pages, consumers could not read sponsored reviews even if time = 1. This naturally created a control group for our study. We defined this group as treatment = 0. There were 67 products in the control group. The generation of the control group was exogenous given that the decision was not related to the effects of the sponsored product sampling reviews. Nonetheless, as we could not be certain that the assignment of the treatment group was completely random (even though the e-tailer platform claimed it was arbitrary, there could still have been unconscious or human-inherent biases during the execution), we needed to consider whether there could be any potential sampling bias between the two groups.

To alleviate this potential bias and thereby increase confidence in our results, we conducted propensity score matching (PSM). We calculated the propensity scores of sponsored product sampling reviews for each product by using a logit function with observed explanatory variables, including the number of offered samples, the number of sponsored reviews collected, and a product category dummy, as well as product-specific factors including their average sales before September 6, 2013, price, and average rating before September 6, 2013 (to rule out alternative explanations related to product characteristics, e.g., the effects of sponsored product sampling reviews could have been due to product-related factors, such as popularity, price, or review volume or rating). We also included a variable “established brand” to denote products from large, well-known brands (1 = yes, 0 otherwise). We note that the e-tailer platform classified all products into 24 categories, including food, cosmetics, clothing, toys, appliances, etc.; however, the 217 products in our dataset only spanned 16 categories. We present the category distribution for the control group and treatment group in Table A2 in the Appendix.

After the application of PSM, we ended up with 31,978 observations for 211 products with a better-balanced control and treatment group. We conducted balancing tests per Rosenbaum and Rubin’s (1985) recommendation. Table 3 shows that none of the t-tests of the variables between the control and treatment groups was significant. We also present the kernel density graph of PSM in Figure 4. As shown in Figure 4, after the matching, the difference in the probability density of the propensity score value between the treatment and the control groups was significantly reduced. Both results indicate no bias in the dataset following PSM.

## 4.4 Mediation Analysis to Test H3a and H3b

The tests above show that the effect of sponsored product sampling reviews remained positive after controlling for the effect of other variables (including organic review volume). We also tested their net effect on sales with explicit consideration of their effect on the volume of organic reviews, which could in turn affect sales. This allowed us to clearly depict the structure of the relationships among the variables involved and assess the extent to which sponsored product sampling reviews still have a positive effect on sales in the relationship structure (i.e., our competing hypotheses H3a vs. H3b).

We set up a mediation model (Hayes, 2012) to depict the relationships among sponsored product sampling reviews, organic reviews, and sales. The model enabled comparisons of the direct and indirect effects of sponsored product sampling reviews on sales while at the same time accounting for the impacts of these reviews on organic reviews. Although we did not hypothesize the direct effect of sponsored product sampling reviews on subsequent organic review valence, since the relationship between organic review valence (dailyrating) and sales has been noted in the literature (Duan et al., 2008; Lu et al., 2013), we also included the valence dimension of organic reviews in the mediation analysis.

The dynamic relationships among the different variables are illustrated in Figure 5. In particular, we were interested in the net effect of sponsored product sampling reviews on sales after accounting for the effects of review volume and review valence on sales, i.e., total effects of Path A-B-C in Figure 5. It is also important to account for the potential effect of the product sampling campaign itself on sales (i.e., running a campaign inviting consumers to apply for a product sample could have affected sales; see Path D in Figure 5) in the estimations; otherwise, the net effect of sponsored reviews could be overestimated.

Therefore, we first compared sales that occurred after September 6, 2013 between the treatment group and the control group. Although the control group was exposed to the campaigns, no sponsored product sampling reviews were featured. Thus, while the sales of both groups were affected by organic review volume and valence, they differed in that the sales in the treatment group were also affected by sponsored product sampling reviews (i.e., rather than the campaign per se). Second, we controlled for the average daily sales from August 6 to September 6, 2013 (avgsale\_before) to capture the effect of the product sampling campaigns per se on sales before September 6, 2013 when the sponsored reviews began to be featured on the product pages.

## 5 Study 2: How Product Sampling Reviews Influence Consumers’ Perceptions and Purchase Intention

## 5.1 Data Collection

Following the research framework in Figure 1, we conducted a scenario-based online experiment to assess and understand how consumers perceive sponsored product sampling reviews compared to organic reviews and sponsored reviews motivated by cash incentives (i.e., paid reviews). The primary objectives are twofold: (1) to clarify sponsored product sampling reviews as a unique form of product reviews deserving dedicated attention; (2) to understand the mechanisms through which sponsored product sampling reviews may have an influence on consumer purchases and the contribution of organic reviews (i.e., our H1b and H2b).

The online experiment was conducted with the help of a professional market research company that maintains a large panel of general consumers from diverse backgrounds. The company also employs quality assurance mechanisms, including consumer demographic information verification, data quality checks, and fraud control. The market research company provided a sample spanning the general consumer population (versus student samples), which is important as this study targets the e-commerce shopping experience and thus the general population.

As shown in Figure 6, we prepared different review page presentation scenarios with different featured review types shown at the top of the page, including a featured sponsored product sampling review, an organic review, and a paid review. For sponsored product sampling reviews, we used two types: either a sponsored product sampling review with generally positive review comments or a sponsored product sampling review that had some positive and some negative comments. We did this to evaluate whether the effect of sponsored product sampling reviews is only present when the review contains positive information only. Altogether, we administered four versions of the survey (see the Appendix for details), each with the same description provided at the beginning of the survey:

Imagine you are considering buying an entry-level robotic vacuum cleaner. When you are browsing a reputed e-tailer website, the following featured review about a robotic vacuum cleaner (priced at \$65.90), placed at the top position in the review section, comes into view.

We chose a robotic vacuum cleaner as the product context to minimize the product preference effect because we expected that most consumers are not very familiar with this emerging product in the market. Also, the brand information was mocked up to avoid a brand preference effect. In addition, the review content was kept constant across all the versions, except for the version of the product sampling review that included some negative comments in the review content.

The market research firm sent out an invitation to panel respondents with experience in e-commerce shopping. In total, 389 valid responses were collected. The respondents were randomly assigned into one of the experimental conditions to evaluate a robotic vacuum cleaner and its product reviews and were asked questions about their perceptions and evaluations. The respondents provided ratings based on their evaluations on purchase intention (PI), intention to contribute new organic review (ICNR), brand quality perception (BQP), and perceived unfairness (PU). As we used a between-subject design, each respondent viewed one scenario only. Table A5 presents the demographic information of the participants and the detailed items are listed in Table A6. We focused on the following constructs based on the research framework in Figure 1: purchase intention (PI), intention to contribute new organic review (ICNR), brand quality perception (BQP), and perceived unfairness (PU) (see Table A6 in the Appendix for the measurement items). Assessments of the psychometric properties show that they satisfy the required thresholds for reliability, convergence validity, and discriminant validity, i.e., Cronbach’s alpha (CA) > 0.70, composite reliability (CR) > 0.70, average variance extracted (AVE) > 0.50, and the square root of AVE of each construct was larger than its correlations with other constructs (see Table A7 and Table A8 in the Appendix). Thus, we proceeded with further analyses.

## 5.2 Comparisons of Product Sampling Review with Paid Review and Organic Review

We first performed comparisons between how consumers perceived featured sponsored product sampling reviews, organic reviews, and paid reviews, in terms of brand quality perception and perceived unfairness. As can be seen in Figure 7, brand quality perception of featured sponsored product sampling reviews was slightly higher than that of featured organic reviews (mean $\mathrm { s a m p l i n g } = 4 . 8 9 0$ vs. mean <sub>organic</sub> = $4 . 6 4 1 , p < 0 . 1 )$ and significantly higher than that of featured paid reviews (mean $\mathsf { s a m p l i n g } = 4 . 8 9 0 ~ \mathrm { v s }$ . mean $\mathrm { p a i d } = 4 . 2 4 8 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). This suggests that featuring a product sampling review may indeed signal the brand’s confidence in its product quality. In addition, product sampling reviews also had higher perceived unfairness compared to organic reviews (mean <sub>sampling</sub> $= 4 . 3 5 8$ vs. mean $\mathrm { o r g a n i c } = 3 . 3 5 1 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ but had significantly lower perceived unfairness compared to paid reviews $( \mathrm { m e a n _ { \ s a m p l i n g } = 4 . 3 5 8 \ v s . \ m e a n _ { \ p a i d } = 4 . 8 7 8 }$ $p < 0 . 0 5 )$

We then compared how participants responded to featured sponsored product sampling reviews in comparison to featured organic and paid reviews in terms of two focal outcome variables, i.e., purchase intention and intention to contribute a new organic review. The analysis of the data (see Figure 8) shows that featuring a sponsored product sampling review elicited a comparable level of purchase intention compared to organic reviews (mean $\mathrm { s a m p l i n g } = 4 . 3 7 2$ vs. mean $\mathrm { o r g a n i c } = 4 . 4 5 7 , p > 0 . 1 )$ , and a significantly higher level compared to paid reviews (mean $\mathrm { s a m p l i n g } = 4 . 3 7 2$ vs. mean $\mathrm { p a i d } = 3 . 6 8 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ .

In addition, featuring sponsored product sampling reviews led to a lower intention to contribute a new organic review compared to featuring an organic review (mean $\mathrm { \ s a m p l i n g } = 3 . 7 9 9 \ \mathrm { v s }$ . mean $\mathrm { o r g a n i c } = 4 . 8 9 7 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ which is expected given the sponsored nature of the review. Nonetheless, the intention to contribute a new organic review was still significantly higher than that when a paid review was featured $( \mathrm { m e a n } _ { \mathrm { \ s a m p l i n g } } = 3 . 7 9 9$ vs. mean $\mathrm { p a i d } = 3 . 1 2 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , which supports our argument that providing a product sample to obtain a review is better than providing a cash incentive since the latter is likely to invoke pure market exchange norms (Heyman & Ariely, 2004; Jin & Huang, 2014).

Taken together, these findings support the value of sponsored product sampling reviews in helping consumers make purchase decisions and also highlight their unique nature compared to sponsored reviews motivated through cash incentives. In addition, our findings indicate that featuring sponsored product sampling reviews is effective in stimulating purchase intentions but lowers consumer intentions to contribute new organic reviews. However, sponsored product sampling reviews are superior to paid reviews for both outcomes.

As indicated above, we also included a version of the sponsored product sampling review that mixed positive comments with some negative comments. As presented in Figure 9, our analysis shows that this type of review is perceived to be similar to its counterpart that only includes positive comments in terms of purchase intention (mean $\mathrm { \ a l l { \_ p o s i t i v e } } = 4 . 3 7 2$ vs. mean $\mathrm { \ m i x e d } = 4 . 4 6 0 \mathrm { \Omega }$ $p > 0 . 1 )$ and brand quality perception (mean <sub>all\_positive</sub> = 4.890 vs. mean $\operatorname { m i x e d } = 4 . 8 9 6 , p > 0 . 1 )$ . We believe that competing mechanisms could have been at play in this finding. Previous research has indicated that reviews comprising both positive and negative comments are likely to be perceived as unbiased (Tang et al., 2014) and relied upon for purchase decision-making. When the negative comments are of a nondetrimental nature (i.e., not disparaging the product as a whole, as in our setting), this may promote purchase intentions, given the greater influence of the review. However, the presence of some negative comments may still reduce purchase intention to some extent given that these comments may alert certain consumers who are particularly sensitive to the criticized aspects of the product even if they are relatively peripheral (e.g., in the review that we designed, we used “looks a bit cumbersome…”, “noise generated…” as negative comments; see the Appendix).

Similarly, when a brand features a product sampling review mixed with some negative comments, it could further signal the brand’s confidence in its product quality as they are willing to subject its product to stringent scrutiny and willing to acknowledge that some limitations of its product may exist. Research has shown that consumers are also generally tolerant toward some negative aspects of a product and accept that no products are “perfect” (Pfeiffer et al., 2014). However, the presence of some negative comments may unavoidably raise alarms in consumers who are sensitive to those aspects of the product. Taken together, the two mechanisms may nullify each other.

The results also show that the product sampling review with relatively mixed comments led to a higher level of intention to contribute a new organic review (mean all $_ \mathrm { \ p o s i t i v e } = 3 . 7 9 9$ vs. mean $\mathrm { m i x e d } = 4 . 6 8 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and a lower level of perceived unfairness (mean <sub>all\_positive</sub> = 4.358 vs. mean $\mathrm { m i x e d } = 3 . 8 7 1 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . We believe that this may also be related to the finding that mixed reviews are likely to be perceived as unbiased (Tang et al., 2014). Specifically, when consumers see that the review contributor tried to be unbiased in writing the sampling review by diligently pointing out problems with the product, they may see the review contributor as a serious review contributor and not solely motivated by the incentive. Such reviews may thus reduce consumers’ unfairness perceptions and even increase purchase intentions.

## 5.3 How Product Sampling Review Affects Purchase Intention and Intention to Contribute New Organic Review

We next discuss the mediation analysis used to assess how and why sponsored product sampling reviews could affect purchase intentions and intentions to contribute new organic reviews. We employed the partial least squares (PLS) method in SmartPLS 3 to conduct the mediation analysis. We selected PLS, a variance-based SEM method because PLS is well suited for testing complex relationships, as it avoids inadmissible solutions and factor indeterminacy (Xu et al., 2011). PLS also allows for the simultaneous estimation of both measurement and structural models, which leads to a more complete analysis. The significance of paths was estimated based on the 5,000 bootstrap samples, and participants’ demographic variables were controlled for in the model.

We first estimated the main effect model without mediators and then the mediation model. respectively.

As reported in Model 1a of Panel A in Table 9, featuring product sampling reviews led to a similar level of purchase intention as featuring organic reviews $( \beta = - 0 . 0 0 9 , p > 0 . 1 )$ , while featuring paid reviews significantly reduced purchase intentions compared to featuring product sampling reviews (ꞵ = - 0.278, p < 0.01). When including the mediator brand quality perception in Model 1b, featuring product sampling reviews produced higher levels of brand quality perception compared to featuring organic reviews (ꞵ = 0.088, p < 0.1), and brand quality perception was positively related to purchase intention (ꞵ = 0.309, p < 0.01).

In Model 2a, featuring product sampling reviews significantly reduced the intention to contribute new organic reviews compared to featuring organic reviews (ꞵ = -0.330, p < 0.01), but featuring paid reviews further reduced the intention to contribute new reviews (ꞵ = -0.556, p < 0.01). These findings are consistent with the aforementioned ANOVA findings. In Model 2b, we observed the potential mediating effect of perceived unfairness because (1) featuring product sampling reviews increased the level of perceived unfairness compared to featuring organic reviews (ꞵ = 0.329, p < 0.01) and this factor exerted a significant negative effect on the intention to contribute new organic reviews $( \beta = - 0 . 5 8 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$

## 6 Discussion and Conclusion

Although the practice of sponsored reviews has gained legitimacy and popularity among e-tailers in recent years, our knowledge about their actual effects on product sales and their influence mechanisms remains limited (Kim et al., 2019; Petrescu et al., 2018). Our study contributes to this body of knowledge by investigating the actual sales effects of a specific type of sponsored review, i.e., product sampling reviews, through collaboration with an e-tailer. The e-tailer experimented with featuring such reviews on its platform and provided us access to actual sales data. Furthermore, our research considers an important contextual condition for featuring sponsored product sampling reviews on e-tailer websites, i.e., they are likely to be featured along with existing organic reviews, which are typically present on e-tailer websites to influence sales. Therefore, it is important to account for how the presence of sponsored product sampling reviews may impact the volume of organic reviews to more accurately understand their net sales effect.

Our study shows that while featuring product sampling reviews is positively associated with product sales, doing so may also decrease the volume of organic reviews (i.e., fewer subsequent organic reviews). However, our further analyses indicate that the net sales effect of sponsored product sampling reviews remains positive. Overall, our research responds to the call of Kim et al. (2019) to conduct an objective investigation to investigate the pros and cons of sponsored reviews and offers nontrivial implications for both researchers and practitioners.

## 6.1 Implications for Research

Despite the rich literature on online product reviews (e.g., Ceran et al., 2016; Clemons et al., 2006; Dellarocas et al., 2007; Duan et al., 2008; Forman et al., 2008; Gopinath et al., 2014; Lu et al., 2013; Sun & Xu, 2018; Yao et al., 2017; Zhu & Zhang, 2010), there is a need to recognize an increasingly popular variant of such reviews—namely sponsored product sampling reviews, which are motivated by firms’ desire to leverage product reviews in a more proactive but ethical way (Kim et al., 2019; Petrescu et al., 2018). However, in contrast to previous research, which has been unable to provide conclusive findings about the effect of sponsored reviews, our research contributes to this emerging field of research in three ways.

First, our research differentiates and focuses on sponsored reviews that are incentivized by offering consumers free product samples. Research has shown that consumers perceive sponsored reviews motivated by non-cash-based incentives, such as free product samples, to be more acceptable than those motivated by cash incentives (Lu et al., 2014). However, previous research has seldom taken this into account, either failing to distinguish this special type of review from sponsored reviews in general (e.g., Kim et al., 2019; Petrescu et al., 2018) or ignoring them altogether in favor of cash-incentivized sponsored reviews (e.g., Du Plessis et al., 2016; Hwang & Jeong, 2016; Colliander & Erlandsson, 2015). Our research reveals that incentivizing reviews through free product samples instead of cash leads to unique positive and negative effects.

Second, there is a lack of research that uses empirical evidence to explicate the mechanisms through which sponsored reviews affect sales. Without this understanding, the effect of sponsored reviews on sales remains a black box and it is difficult to make conclusions on the viability of this approach to leveraging consumer reviews. Our multimethod research, employing both secondary sales data and survey-based experimental data, shows that sponsored product sampling reviews can improve brand quality perceptions. Building on this finding, future research could investigate how to use sponsored product sampling reviews to further enhance brand quality perceptions. Our study also demonstrates that product sampling reviews may lead to feelings of unfairness among consumers. Future research could investigate how to minimize such feelings to further reduce the negative impact of utilizing this approach.

Both of these effects can be traced to the nature of sponsored product sampling reviews. On the one hand, our findings suggest that product sampling reviews tend to lead to favorable evaluations among consumers due to the quality signaling effect, which in turn promotes purchase intentions. On the other hand, these reviews can also lead to unfairness perceptions due to some but not all reviewers being compensated with a free product sample, which then undermines consumers’ intentions to contribute new organic reviews. This dual effect suggests that using product sampling reviews is associated with specific impacts that need to be considered. Our comparison of different types of reviews also demonstrates the need for further research on the specific types of sponsored reviews in terms of their effects and influence mechanisms.

Third, previous research has mostly relied on proxies to investigate the sales effect of sponsored reviews, such as purchase intention (e.g., Kim et al., 2019; Lu et al., 2014), or purchase potential, such as the number of verified reviews (e.g., Petrescu et al., 2018). However, such proxies may introduce subjective biases and lead to inconclusive findings with regard to actual sales (Furnham, 1986; Kim et al., 2019), especially since the sales effects of sponsored reviews are likely to be nonstraightforward, given their potential negative impacts (e.g., undermining organic review generation). Through our analysis of longitudinal back-end sales data from an e-tailer platform and organic review data, our research was able to address these limitations by focusing on sponsored product sampling reviews. By showing that the net sales effect of such sponsored reviews is positive even though they may undermine organic review generation, our study can serve as a solid foundation for future research investigating other more nuanced aspects of sponsored reviews (e.g., research investigating what kind of sponsored review content is more influential) and research examining how this promising form of review could be better harnessed.

## 6.2 Implications for Practice

For practitioners—in particular, e-tailers—our research shows that featuring reviews that are incentivized by free product samples is a viable strategy for firms seeking to leverage product reviews in an effective and ethical way. Brands may wish to work with e-tailer platforms to sponsor product sampling review campaigns to enjoy the benefits of featuring such reviews more directly, as these reviews can increase direct platform purchases. Brands may also try to enhance the signaling effect of using product sampling—for example, by communicating what makes them confident in their product e.g., innovative product design, commitment to quality, unique manufacturing process, etc.).

Nevertheless, although our empirical analysis shows that the net sales effect of product sampling reviews remains positive, we caution that the use of such reviews may reduce organic review contributions. This can be an especially salient issue to consider for products with a small existing volume of reviews. Managers may wish to set a threshold of product review volume decreases, especially if this leads to negative consequences beyond sales, e.g., reducing brand presence in the market. Managers should also seek to reduce the unfairness perception associated with product sampling reviews. For example, they could emphasize that they care about all customers and that the free product samples are distributed randomly. They might also remind their customers to pay attention to opportunities to obtain free product samples in the future.

## 6.3 Limitations and Research Opportunities

It is important to recognize the limitations of this research, which also provide opportunities for future research. First, our study focuses on a featured review approach adopted by e-tailers, i.e., sponsored product sampling reviews, and examines how such featured reviews may affect product sales. Owing to their nature, the increased product sales could be the joint effects of being featured on a product page and the content of the product sampling reviews. Given that these effects are inherent to featured reviews, we were unable to completely separate these two effects in this study; therefore, we combined them to investigate the effects of featuring product sampling reviews on etailer websites. If future research can tease out the effects of being featured from the review content, the independent effects of product sampling reviews could be measured more rigorously.

Second, since the featured product sampling review was selected by the e-tailer in our study, consumers might not have been aware of how the review was selected, which may have impacted its trustworthiness. There are at least two possible future research directions that could build on this. E-tailers could feature a few rather than one sponsored review. Increasing the number of such reviews could add to the information consumers can obtain to help them make purchase decisions. However, this could potentially also exacerbate the negative effect on the consumer intention to contribute organic reviews given the larger presence of sponsored reviews. This could be investigated in future research. There could also be other ways of displaying sponsored reviews to consumers. For example, e-tailers could set up a dedicated section to display sponsored reviews. While our collaborating e-tailer tried this but later changed to a featured review approach (after observing that the dedicated section received little attention from consumers), it is possible that consumers may not yet be accustomed to paying attention to a different review section other than the existing organic review section. With enough marketing efforts, this could potentially change. A separate section displaying sponsored reviews could help to minimize the undermining of organic reviews. Future research could employ an experimental method to test whether setting up a dedicated sponsored review section would yield more optimal outcomes and inform e-tailers which approach they should adopt to better harness the power of sponsored product sampling reviews.

Third, while we found that sponsored reviews could undermine the volume of organic reviews, we do not know to what extent this may affect the qualitative content of the organic reviews contributed by consumers beyond volume. Future research could conduct sentiment analysis and deeper content analysis to delve into this issue.

Lastly, while we performed PSM to ensure that the treatment group and the control group were comparable for analyses, there could still be unobserved variations in some variables, e.g., those related to specific product types that are difficult to capture. Future research could employ an experimental method to rule out such potential influences, e.g., by keeping the product types in the treatment group and the control group the same.

In summary, our research provides timely and valuable insights for firms contemplating harnessing the power of online product reviews other than organic reviews in an ethically acceptable manner, which is quickly becoming an important consideration for the ecommerce landscape.

## Details of Study 2

The scenarios for the different types of reviews vary in terms of what is stated before the review content. In the sponsored product sampling review version, the following is stated before the review content:

“The featured review below comes from a consumer who sampled a product given for free from the manufacturer. The manufacturer of this product has administered a product sampling campaign and offered a limited quantity of samples to consumers who applied for it based on a lottery draw. After sampling the product, consumers decide whether they want to share their review.”

In the paid review version, the following is stated before the review content:

“The featured review below comes from a consumer who has been verified to have purchased the product. He/she was given a \$3 voucher for sharing the review.”

In the organic review version, the following is stated before the review context:

“The featured review below comes from a consumer who is verified to have purchase the product.”

The content of the review (adapted from existing reviews and consistent across all versions except the sponsored product sampling review that has a mixture of positive and negative comments) reads:

“After unpacking, the robotic vacuum cleaner looks bigger than I thought. It is able to clear a lot of dust. It is easy to use, and the design is user-friendly. It even comes with two batteries, so I can just install and start using it. The color is black, which is nice. There is a hidden handle on top of the machine, which makes it easy to be carried around. The cleaner can be set up to automatically move back to the base station to charge itself when the battery is flat. There are three operating modes that can be selected, depending on how long you want it to do the cleaning. There is a remote control provided. The level of noise generated is low. After the cleaning is done, you will find a lot of dust and debris in the container. It is even able to work when there is water on the floor. I recommended it to my colleagues, and they too think it’s not bad.”

For the sponsored product sampling review mixed with some negative comments, the following negative points were added:
