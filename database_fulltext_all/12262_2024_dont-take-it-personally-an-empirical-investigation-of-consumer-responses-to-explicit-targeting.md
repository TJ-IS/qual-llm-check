# Don’t Take It Personally: An Empirical Investigation of Consumer Responses to Explicit Targeting

Dimitrios Tsekouras,<sup>1</sup> Ting Li,<sup>2</sup> Thomas W. Frick<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University, Netherlands, dtsekouras@rsm.nl <sup>2</sup>Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University, Netherlands, tli@rsm.n <sup>3</sup>Secomea A/S, thomas.frick.academia@gmail.com

## Abstract

Firms increasingly use consumer information to target and personalize communication with consumers. This paper focuses on explicit targeting, a practice where firms saliently (yet subtly) reveal the information used to target consumers in their advertisement text. How to craft ad messages to convey product benefits without triggering privacy concerns is a nontrivial task. Results from a field experiment reveal that consumers respond negatively to explicit targeting, yet such a negative effect can be partially alleviated by using hedonic instead of utilitarian information framing. In a controlled online experiment, we show that explicit targeting increases consumers’ privacy concerns relative to the perceived benefits of personalization, leading to less positive consumer responses. Interestingly, an extension of the online experiment suggests that providing consumers with a clear description of why they are targeted seems to offset the negative effects of explicit targeting. This study offers important academic implications for the personalization literature and valuable practical insights for firms and policymakers.

Keywords: Advertising Targeting, Information Framing, Mobile, Privacy, Elaboration Likelihood Model

## 1 Introduction

In a world with predicted spending on digital advertising of more than 526 billion US\$ in 2024 (Statista, 2021), firms are struggling to compete for consumers’ attention. They have come up with increasingly innovative and sophisticated ways to target consumers with personalized communication in advertising (Bleier et al., 2020). In such targeting, firms use information about consumers, such as their demographics, location, and online behavior, to decide which consumers to address and how to personalize the information presented in the ads (Chen & Stallaert, 2014). Customers benefit from receiving targeted ads based on their personal information, as the advertised products are more likely to match their preferences (Iyer et al., 2005). Yet, these ads may trigger consumers’ privacy concerns, especially when there is substantial information asymmetry regarding the collection and use of personal data by companies (Acquisti et al., 2013; Sutanto et al., 2013; Tucker, 2012).

To alleviate consumers’ privacy concerns, there is increasing pressure for firms to provide transparency in their ad messages to better inform consumers about the use of their personal data when targeting them (Acquisti et al., 2013; European Commission, 2018; Ramirez et al., 2014). This is a nontrivial task. Firms need to carefully craft their ad messages, which are much shorter compared to other types of marketing communication, to convey product benefits and to describe the reasons why consumers are targeted to improve product relevance without triggering privacy concerns (Ullah et al., 2020). The most frequently used practice involves using subtle yet explicit ad messages, saliently referring to the reason why consumers are targeted, which we refer to as explicit targeting.

Figure 1 presents two real-life examples of explicitly targeted ads. In these examples, the ads were shown to consumers who have searched for a vacation package or a hotel room. Each ad contains both some texts and an image. The text messages above the image mention why a consumer is receiving the ad (e.g. “Planning a vacation?” / “Want to get away tonight?”) and product information about the advertised product. Being more explicit about why consumers are targeted seems like a double-edged sword—while it increases perceived product relevance, it also triggers privacy concerns. Though often seen in practice, how effective these subtle yet explicit ad messages are remains unclear. Does such explicit targeting sufficiently alleviate consumer privacy concerns while providing consumers with relevant product information?

More specifically, in this paper, we answer the following questions: (1) How do consumers respond to explicit targeting? (2) How can firms design ad messages to better alleviate the negative effects of explicit targeting? (3) What are the mechanisms underlying the effectiveness of such explicit targeting designs?

While the direct impact of information personalization on consumer behavior (Bleier & Eisenbeiss, 2015a; De Keyzer et al., 2015; Komiak & Benbasat, 2006; Tam & Ho, 2006) and the extent to which consumers respond to privacy concerns (Acquisti et al., 2013;

Sutanto et al., 2013) have been extensively studied in prior literature, the combined effect is not straightforward. On the one hand, prior literature on personalization suggests that explicit targeting is effective because it allows consumers to more easily recognize the relevance of the presented information through an increase in consumers’ perceived personalization (Kim et al., 2019; Li, 2016; Summers et al., 2016). On the other hand, literature on consumer privacy suggests that when consumers become aware that their personal information is being used, they may feel their privacy has been violated (Sutanto et al., 2013; Tucker, 2016) and experience reactance to the targeted ad (Wattal et al., 2012; White et al., 2008). To study how consumers respond to explicit targeting, we draw upon the elaboration likelihood model to understand how individuals allocate cognitive effort to assess persuasive product information and the related evaluation of personalization benefits and privacy costs (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). Prior literature refers to such a tension between the benefits of personalization and its costs in the form of privacy concerns as the personalization-privacy paradox (Awad & Krishnan, 2006; Sutanto et al., 2013). Revealing the information used for targeting potentially increases the salience of both the relevance of the ad message (personalization) and the privacy concerns stemming from the use of personal information. Yet there is limited research on how such explicit targeting affects consumers’ reactions to targeted ads in view of the relative importance of personalization vis-à-vis privacy and how firms can design ad messages to be more effective in their communication.

Another important design choice is how to frame the ad message in terms of product benefits to alleviate the potential negative effect of explicit targeting. Prior research has shown that consumers are influenced by the framing of product information which also largely affects their privacy-related decisions (Acquisti et al., 2013; Dinev, 2014). While utilitarian information framing encourages consumers to assess information rationally and triggers increased elaboration (Couwenberg et al., 2017), hedonic information framing nudges consumers toward an affective assessment of information with less cognitive elaboration (Finucane et al., 2000; Slovic et al., 2007). We suggest that hedonic information framing, compared to utilitarian information framing, encourages consumers to assess ads from an emotional-experiential perspective rather than a functional perspective and could potentially alleviate the negative effect of explicit targeting.

In this paper, we report a randomized field experiment in collaboration with a mobile app company and two online experiments on Prolific to identify the causal effects and underlying mechanisms of explicit targeting designs on consumer responses. In particular, in the randomized field experiment, we vary explicit targeting (with vs. without) and message framing (utilitarian vs. hedonic) of ad message designs to investigate their effects on consumer click and download behavior. We found evidence that explicit targeting decreases clicks on the targeted ad as well as downloads of the advertised app. However, when hedonic information framing instead of utilitarian framing is used, the negative effect of explicit targeting is partially alleviated. We replicate the main findings in online experiments and explore the potential mechanisms. Our findings reveal that the negative effect of explicit targeting is mediated (1) by consumers weighing privacy concerns relatively higher than personalization benefits and (2) by an increase in consumer reactance towards the targeted ad. Further, we demonstrate that providing a clearer description of the reasons why consumers are targeted—making their targeting more explicit rather than subtle—seems to offset the negative effects of explicit targeting.

This study makes three main academic contributions. First, we add to the extensive body of prior literature in IS and marketing on the customer response to personalized information (Aguirre et al., 2015; De Keyzer et al., 2015; Komiak & Benbasat, 2006; Tam & Ho, 2006) and targeting (Bleier & Eisenbeiss, 2015a; Chen & Stallaert, 2014; Schumann et al., 2014). Whereas prior work has considered behavioral targeting to be an effective way to approach customers, our work focuses on the information aspect of targeting and adds to the literature by empirically examining the impact of explicitly revealing the information used when targeting consumers based on their clicks and download responses. We show that despite the subtle approach often used by firms to reveal targeting information to consumers, explicit targeting is, in fact, less effective.

Second, we contribute to IS literature by exploring the optimal design of explicitly targeted ad messages, which introduces challenges and opportunities regarding firms’ online advertising. Whereas prior research has focused on identifying the effectiveness of behavioral targeting across the different sources used for user profiling (Andrews et al., 2016; Ho & Bodoff, 2014; Lambrecht & Tucker, 2013; Tucker, 2014), we examine how firms could design ad messages differently to impact consumer responses. On the one hand, we show that clearly stating why consumers are seeing an ad can alleviate the negative effects of the subtle approach. On the other hand, we focus on the role of information framing and examine the extent to which displaying hedonic product benefits in ad messages alleviates the negative effect of explicit targeting. This expands prior research on the communication of product benefits into personalized information and targeting.

Third, we extend prior research on the personalizationprivacy paradox in the IS literature, which has focused on the balance between providing relevant information and reducing consumer privacy concerns (Aguirre et al., 2015; Aguirre & Roggeveen, 2016; Awad & Krishnan, 2006; Macha et al., 2023; Sutanto et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2011). We provide theoretical explanations and supporting empirical evidence demonstrating how and why explicit targeting reduces ad effectiveness: targeting increases consumers’ concerns about privacy, which is relatively more important to consumers than the personalization benefits that targeting affords and increases consumers’ reactance towards targeted ads. In addition, we contribute to the personalization-privacyrelated literature by showing how information framing regarding product benefits in ad messages can influence the impact of the respective trade-off on consumers. More precisely, hedonic information framing (compared to utilitarian information framing) can alleviate the negative effect of the reactance caused by the relative importance of privacy (compared to personalization) on consumers’ intention to click on an ad.

From a practical perspective, our work offers actionable implications for practitioners using explicit targeting to approach consumers. The majority of prior studies have focused on the positive effects of personalization and targeting (Aguirre et al., 2015; De Keyzer et al., 2015; Komiak & Benbasat, 2006; Tam & Ho, 2006). In line with increasing concerns about the potential privacy risks of information personalization, our findings suggest that firms should be cautious when in engaging in explicit targeting, as it can negatively affect consumers’ responses to targeted ads. Nonetheless, our empirical evidence suggests that firms can partially alleviate this negative effect if they design ad messages using hedonic information framing. Our study further demonstrates empirical support for the benefits of providing greater information transparency in ad targeting (Acquisti et al., 2013; European Commission, 2018; Ramirez et al., 2014). Clearly and explicitly describing the reasons why consumers are targeted is, in fact, beneficial and can offset the negative effect of explicit targeting. Explaining to consumers why they are targeted alleviates consumers’ privacy concerns and thus also benefits advertisers.

In the remainder of the paper, we provide an overview of relevant literature and develop a set of hypotheses. We then present our empirical studies and findings. We conclude with a discussion of the academic and practical implications of our findings and present limitations and opportunities for future research.

## 2 Theoretical Background and Hypotheses Development

## 2.1 Targeting

The proliferation of online tracking and profiling technologies and the reduction in data processing costs have created opportunities for firms to efficiently target consumers with personalized product information (Bleier et al., 2020; Kox et al., 2015; Ullah et al., 2020). Online firms can target individuals based on their past purchases (Ho & Bodoff, 2014), location (Andrews et al., 2016; Li & Du, 2012), social networks (Tucker, 2014), context (Goldfarb & Tucker, 2011), and browsing behavior (Bleier & Eisenbeiss, 2015a; Lambrecht & Tucker, 2013), both within or beyond their websites.

Research suggests that targeting based on personal information leads to an increase in the relevance of information and positive consumer reactions (Bleier & Eisenbeiss, 2015a; Chen & Stallaert, 2014; Schumann et al., 2014). Consumers benefit from receiving personalized product information that matches their preferences in that it reduces the effort needed to identify relevant products and are presumably more likely to positively respond to firms’ targeting attempts. <sup>1</sup> At the same time, targeting may also increase consumers’ privacy concerns related to the collection and use of their personal data (Sutanto et al., 2013). Individuals develop their own boundaries around personal information that they deem sensitive, and when these boundaries are crossed by third parties, it induces privacy concerns and reactance (Petronio, 2002; Xu et al., 2011). Such an increase in privacy concerns is especially pertinent when personal data are used by third parties (Aguirre et al., 2015; Bleier &

Eisenbeiss, 2015b) and when there is substantial information asymmetry about personal data collection and use (Acquisti et al., 2013; Sutanto et al., 2013).

Due to violations in the use of personal customer data by firms, policymakers and regulators have proposed ways to protect customers, either by offering full control of the use of data, better informing consumers about the risks of sharing personal data, or increasing transparency in firms’ targeting practices (Acquisti et al., 2013). In order to increase the perceived personalization of ads for consumers while also increasing transparency in consumer data use, advertisers often make the targeting of ads explicit. Explicit targeting makes the information that is used for targeting salient to consumers by referring to that information in ad messages. On the one hand, salient information influences consumers’ assessment and choice more strongly than implicit information (Dijksterhuis et al., 2005), increasing the perceived relevance of the product (Komiak & Benbasat, 2006). At the same time, making personal consumer information explicit may highlight the fact that personal information is being used and cause reactance among consumers due to privacy concerns (Summers et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2011). To understand the effectiveness of explicit targeting, we focus on consumers’ trade-offs between privacy and personalization.

## 2.2 Consumer Privacy and Targeting

The proliferation of consumer data use practices has brought privacy into the limelight of e-commerce research and practice (Acquisti et al., 2015; Dinev, 2014; Lowry et al., 2012). Privacy is a fundamental human right related to the ability to control personal information (Stone et al., 1983). A large body of research across multiple fields (e.g., information systems, human-computer interaction, management, and marketing) has focused on understanding the antecedents and consequences of privacy decisionmaking (Acquisti et al., 2015; Bélanger & Crossler, 2011; Kobsa et al., 2016; Smith et al., 2011). Earlier work has focused on the rational perspective of the privacy calculus, suggesting that consumers aim to maximize their utility by considering all positive and negative consequences (benefits vs. risks) of personal data disclosure (i.e. performing a cost-benefit analysis) and responding based on what action will maximize their expected outcome (Dinev & Hart, 2006; Stone et al., 1983). Following this perspective, research has suggested that individuals have stable yet heterogeneous preferences for privacy when assessing data disclosure benefits and risks (Adjerid et al., 2018; Smith et al., 1996).

Privacy calculus has been criticized for its lack of realism (i.e., individuals lack the capacity to conduct an actual cost-benefit analysis when assessing their privacy), leading to inconsistencies in observed privacy behavior (Acquisti et al., 2020; Acquisti & Grossklags, 2005; Hoffman et al., 1999). A growing body of research has suggested that the assessment of privacy is highly context specific (Adjerid et al., 2018; Cichy et al., 2021) and is susceptible to information framing and behavioral biases (Acquisti & Grossklags, 2005; Al Natour et al., 2020).

In order to provide customers with more relevant personalized information, firms need to use personal consumer data. Therefore, there is a trade-off between the benefits of a personalized service or product that matches the individuals’ preferences more closely and the disutility of privacy concerns, as personal data is exploited by third parties to offer these personalized services (Acquisti et al., 2015; Awad & Krishnan, 2006; Sutanto et al., 2013). The trade-off between the benefits of increased personalization and the costs in the form of privacy concerns is referred to as the personalization-privacy paradox. The personalization privacy trade-off is especially pronounced in the context of online advertising (Awad & Krishnan, 2006; Sutanto et al., 2013). Research shows that the context of personalization (Sheng et al., 2008) and the individual’s characteristics (Hann et al., 2007; Lee & Rha, 2016) influence how an individual will weigh the personalization benefits against their privacy concerns. Further, the design of the technology artifact affects the assessment of the perceived benefits and costs of personalization. For example, the level of overtness in information collection, the information delivery mechanism, and the priming of personalization benefits can outweigh privacy concerns (Li & Unger, 2012, Xu et al., 2011). Since individuals do not pay much attention to online advertising (due to the sheer amount of ads they are exposed to daily), the overall trade-off assessment is prone to contextual factors and behavioral biases (Acquisti et al., 2020; Acquisti & Grossklags, 2005). Therefore, the amount of cognitive effort that an individual expends when assessing a targeted advertisement is central to the understanding of the effectiveness of explicit targeting.

## 2.3 The Elaboration Likelihood Model

We draw upon the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) to theorize how individuals decide how much cognitive effort to expend to assess product information and how that is related to the evaluation of personalization benefits and privacy costs. ELM is a psychological theory that explains how individuals process information (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986; Petty & Cacioppo, 1981). The theory models how individuals’ attitudes and response decisions are affected by characteristics of persuasive information. To model the processing of persuasive information, the ELM framework distinguishes between two routes of persuasion: (1) the central route, and (2) the peripheral route (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). The central route of persuasion is characterized by individuals’ extensive cognitive efforts. In this route of persuasion, individuals carefully consider information to weigh the costs and benefits of a problem in order to inform their response decisions. This route of persuasion is further characterized by individuals’ careful assessment of the logic of presented arguments and the scrutiny of presented information (Ho & Bodoff, 2014). In the peripheral route, individuals allocate limited cognitive effort to the processing of information and rely on choice heuristics. In this route, information is processed according to associations and rules of thumb (Ho & Bodoff, 2014).

Which route an individual uses to process information depends on whether the individual possesses the ability and the motivation to process the information. The motivation to process information is tightly linked to individuals’ knowledge and need for cognitions and to the information’s relevance (Petty et al., 2002). When individuals possess the ability and the motivation to evaluate the information, they take a central route, devoting more cognitive effort into scrutinizing the information in a rational manner. In contrast, if individuals lack the motivation or ability to process the information, they assess the information via the peripheral route, which implies low cognitive effort and higher proneness to contextual and behavioral heuristics (Lowry et al., 2012). Importantly, more elaboration does not necessarily guarantee a positive response to the product information; this depends on the content of the communicated information.

ELM has helped to explain individuals’ processing of information in information systems research in a plethora of contexts, such as recommendation agents (Kretzer & Maedche, 2018), mobile app stores (Gu et al., 2017), online reviews (Cheung et al., 2012), knowledge management (Nah & Benbasat, 2004; Sussman & Siegal, 2003), gamification (Kwak et al., 2019), and electronic health records (Angst & Agarwal, 2009). Related to the context of explicit targeting, ELM has been implemented to explain how personalized content is perceived as more relevant by individuals, increasing the motivation to carefully process the message and the cognitive effort via the central route (Bhattacherjee & Sanford, 2006; Ho & Bodoff, 2014; Ho & Tam, 2005; Tam & Ho, 2006). Relatedly, ELM has been used to explain how individuals process ad messages, depending on their level of perceived involvement with the product (Goh & Ping, 2014; Rosengren et al., 2020). Furthermore, ELM has been proposed as a theoretical lens to explain privacy-related behaviors, where the central route with high cognitive effort leads to better-informed privacyrelated behaviors compared to the peripheral route (Dinev, 2014; Kobsa et al., 2016; Lowry et al., 2012). Finally, ELM has been used to explain how individuals experience privacy concerns and overcome them through the use of privacy assurance mechanisms on websites (Bansal et al., 2015; Gu et al., 2017). We suggest that ELM is an appropriate theory to assess the effects of explicit targeting, as firms use targeting to increase consumers’ cognitive processing of product information and convince them to adopt the product while attempting to minimize the elaboration around potential privacy risks.

## 2.4 The Effect of Explicit Targeting

In line with ELM, we expect that explicit targeting acts as a motivating factor and increases individuals’ elaboration (Ho & Bodoff, 2014; Ho & Tam, 2005; Tam & Ho, 2006). Explicit targeting increases the perceived personalization and relevance of the message, further increasing the motivation of consumers to carefully process the message (Summers et al., 2016). This leads to elaboration via the central route (Bhattacherjee & Sanford, 2006; Ho & Bodoff, 2014; Ho & Tam, 2005). An increased level of elaboration can increase targeting effectiveness, as it can make the message more memorable (Tam & Ho, 2006).

On the other hand, making the targeting mechanisms explicit may trigger privacy concerns, especially when the information used in targeting is considered unacceptable (Kim et al., 2019). Whereas the benefits of personalization are generally visible, privacy costs are intangible and emerge only through an elaborate assessment of the personalization-privacy trade-off (Acquisti et al., 2015). As a result, triggering a more elaborate assessment of the targeted message may steer the focus toward privacy risks from the use of personal data for targeting (Dinev, 2014). Previous empirical evidence has suggested that providing salient information related to the trade-off between privacy and personalization may trigger people to pay more attention to the information (Tsai et al., 2011). Further, privacy protection may sensitize consumers to disclosure risks and may trigger attention to privacy (Miller & Tucker, 2018). In the context of data collection via surveys, an explicit presentation of the confidentiality of collected data steers respondents to elaborate more on the risks of data collection (Singer et al., 1992). Increased privacy concerns originating from targeting can lead consumers to respond negatively to personalized product information (Tsai et al., 2011; Tucker, 2014; White et al., 2008), or emails with personalized greetings (Wattal et al., 2012).

Based on the arguments presented above and due to the inflation in the psychological value of risks compared to benefits (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), we expect that the negative response to targeting due to privacy concerns is stronger than the positive response to targeting based on personalized and relevant information. Thus, we expect the negative effects of explicit targeting to outweigh its positive effects.

H1: Explicit targeting negatively influences consumers’ responses to targeted product information.

Firms need to balance the benefits of personalization of product information with the costs of privacy concerns perceived by consumers when they feel that their boundaries of information flows have been breached. As previously argued, we expect explicit targeting to increase both the perceived relevance of a targeted ad and the privacy concerns emanating from the use of personal information. Privacy concerns shift the focus of individuals to the prevention of a negative event (Higgins et al., 1994). This prevention focus makes individuals less likely to engage with the advertisement, similar to how prevention reduces consumer interaction with firms (Tsai et al., 2011; White et al., 2008).

Therefore, we propose that explicit targeting negatively influences consumers’ responses to targeted product information, by increasing their privacy concerns relative to their interest in personalization (Kim et al., 2019). Privacy concerns outweigh the expected benefits generated by personalized advertising (White et al., 2008). Particularly when consumers consider the information used in targeting to be unacceptable, they will tend to weigh privacy concerns higher than personalization benefits (Kim et al., 2019). Therefore, we expect the increased importance of privacy concerns relative to personalization benefits to play a mediating role in the reduced effectiveness of explicit targeting.

H2: The negative effect of explicit targeting on consumers’ responses to targeted product information is mediated by consumers prioritization of privacy concerns over personalization benefits.

Individuals who feel that their freedom of choice is threatened may experience psychological reactance (Brehm, 1966; Fitzsimons & Lehmann, 2004). Psychological reactance is linked to the feeling of intrusiveness and leads individuals to attempt to restore their freedom of choice by responding counter to the original goal of the threatening trigger (Brehm, 1966). Reactance has been documented in the context of consumer response to firm recommendations (Fitzsimons & Lehmann, 2004) and promotional tactics (Kivetz, 2005).

In the context of advertising targeting, we expect that individuals’ perceived ability to avoid being observed by firms will be threatened when they receive ads that explicitly contain personal information about them and anticipate that they will react negatively, especially when they have increased privacy concerns (Aguirre et al., 2015; Bleier & Eisenbeis, 2015b; Tucker, 2014; White et al., 2008). In that case, we predict that they will react counter to the original goal of the advertisement and will be less likely to click on it. Therefore, we posit that individuals’ reactance to targeted product messaging plays a mediating role in the effect of explicit targeting.

H3: The negative effect of explicit targeting on consumers’ responses to targeted product information is mediated by consumers’ reactance to targeted product information.

## 2.5 The Moderating Role of Information Framing

Information framing influences how consumers assess product information (Higgins et al., 1994; Johar & Sirgy, 1991) and has been found to heavily influence individual preferences related to privacy (Acquisti et al., 2013; Dinev, 2014). Hedonic product information framing focuses on the affective and experiential benefits of a product, leading consumers to affectively assess product information (Finucane et al., 2000; Slovic et al., 2007). Past research has established that when consumers are nudged toward a hedonic focus, they will place more emphasis on the experiential benefits of a product (Lin & Shen, 2012) and likely take the peripheral route in assessing product information (Rosengren et al., 2020). In contrast, utilitarian product information framing emphasizes the functional benefits of products and fosters the rational assessment of products, which is linked to the central route of product information processing (Batra & Ahtola, 1990; Couwenberg et al., 2017).

According to the regulatory focus theory (Higgins et al., 1994), individuals may have distinct goals regarding certain tasks. Prevention goals are those whose fulfillment minimizes a potential negative experience proceeding from a choice. Prevention goals are tightly linked to the feeling of security (Chitturi et al., 2008). Prior research has found that product information focusing on utilitarian product attributes leads consumers to focus on the prevention of costs (Higgins et al., 1994). In contrast to prevention goals, which are goals that must be attained, promotion goals are goals that would be nice to have, and their fulfillment increases the chances of realizing a positive experience. In this context, hedonic information framing nudges consumers toward a promotion focus and an affective assessment of information (Finucane et al., 2000; Slovic et al., 2007).

We expect that information framing will affect how consumers perceive and respond to explicit targeting. That is, if explicit targeting triggers a negative response from a consumer, this negative response can be alleviated with hedonic information framing, as a promotion focus is likely to be triggered when consumers are confronted with hedonic information framing. On the contrary, utilitarian information framing is likely to amplify a negative consumer response to explicit targeting, originating in the consumer’s cost prevention focus induced by utilitarian information framing.

H4: Hedonic information framing (utilitarian information framing) attenuates (increases) the negative effect of explicit targeting on consumers’ responses to targeted product information.

## 3 Study 1: The Effect of Explicit Targeting and Ad Message Framing in The Field

In Study 1, we examined the impact of explicit targeting and ad message framing on consumers responses to ads. We conducted a field experiment in collaboration with a corporate partner in Europe. For the experiment, we ran an ad targeting campaign for a mobile app on Facebook. The mobile app allowed users to digitally store their loyalty cards from various retailers and use their smartphones at the point of purchase instead of carrying physical loyalty cards in their wallets. At the time of the experiment, the app had more than 100 loyalty cards in its portfolio and offered exclusive promotions to its users.

## 3.1 Experiment Design

In our experiment, we targeted users who were interested in product categories for which our partner firm carried loyalty cards in its portfolio. The ad targeting was based on users’ profile information and/or the product pages they liked. Facebook likes have been shown to predict consumer preferences (Kosinski et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2015). Consumers who liked a product page or expressed an interest in a certain product category are more likely to possess these products loyalty cards and to consider the targeted ads relevant. We targeted consumers interested in sports, fashion, and utility products (i.e., hardware stores). In the ads, we included the name of the app and a short description across all conditions. Figure 2 presents an illustration of the ad. During the two weeks of the experiment, the company did not run any other campaigns. Ads targeted users of Apple’s iOS operating system. We excluded users who had already liked or downloaded the app from the experiment.

We used a 2 × 2 experimental design and manipulated explicit targeting (1 = explicit / 0 = not explicit) and ad message framing (1 = hedonic / 0 = utilitarian). In the explicit targeting condition, we used the ad message “Do you like [sports/fashion/utility]?,” referring to the user information used for targeting. In the nonexplicit condition, there was no information cue. Using such a question to operationalize explicit targeting is in line with advertisers’ common practice of implementing a subtle form of explicit targeting. This operationalization of explicit targeting is also in line with our partner firm’s approach to explicit targeting and its aversion to implementing a more direct form of explicit targeting due to the fear of consumers’ reactance, and is also in line with common advertising practices (see Figure 1).

For ad message framing, ads with utilitarian framing promoted the usefulness of keeping all the consumer’s loyalty cards in one app, stating: “All your [sports/fashion/utility] loyalty cards directly available in [app name]!” Ads with hedonic framing highlighted the emotional value of using the advertised app, stating: “Shopping for [sports/fashion/utility] can be more fun with [app name]!” Table 1 presents an overview of ad messages used in the experiment.

To test our manipulations, we conducted a pretest. We asked participants, recruited on Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk),<sup>2</sup> to imagine that they had recently shown an interest in fashion and visited fashion websites and related social media pages. In the next step, participants were shown an ad for a mobile application on their Facebook newsfeed. We randomly allocated 117 respondents<sup>3</sup> to one of four ads (Explicit/nonexplicit × Utilitarian/hedonic) promoting a fictional app for loyalty cards for fashion stores. We used the same ad design and messages as in the field study. We asked participants to evaluate whether they felt they were being targeted with this ad (Aaker et al., 2000). <sup>4</sup> Participants exposed to explicit targeting felt, on average, more targeted than those who saw the nonexplicit ads $( \mathrm { { M } } _ { \mathrm { { e x p l i c i t } } } = 3 . 9 0 9 .$ $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { n o n e x p l i c i t } } = 3 . 1 1 0 , F _ { ( I , I I 5 ) } = 9 . 9 9 , p = 0 . 0 0 \dot { 2 } , \mathfrak { \eta } _ { \mathfrak { p } } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 8 )$ Further, we asked participants to evaluate the app based on attributes that reflect its utilitarian and hedonic value (Voss et al., 2003).<sup>5</sup> Respondents who saw an ad with hedonic framing evaluated the app as having more hedonic benefits $( \bf M _ { \mathrm { h e d o n i c } } = \delta 3 . 2 4 1 , \ M _ { u t i l i t a r i a n } = \delta 2 . 5 8 7$ $F _ { ( I , I I S ) } = 5 . 8 5 , p = 0 . 0 1 7 , \eta _ { \mathrm { p } } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 5 )$

To ensure that participants were not exposed to multiple treatments in our field experiment, we created mutually exclusive treatment groups based on geographical areas. We displayed ads to users in twelve cities in a Western European country, targeting each city with a particular experiment condition and a product category combination. The cities were geographically proximal, with an average distance of 42 kilometers between cities, and comparable in terms of demographics and ad relevance. This approach helped us avoid exposing the same consumer to different conditions but may have introduced endogeneity that could have biased our results. It is possible that consumers are different in different cities and the mobile app might have different attractiveness in different cities.

We took several steps to address these two concerns. First, we selected major cities that were homogeneous in terms of internet use, Facebook reach, gender and age distribution, and average income. Furthermore, the ads were only shown to consumers who had indicated an interest in the advertised product category on Facebook. Second, the advertised mobile app was new at the time of the experiment. Only a few consumers had downloaded the app before the start of our experiment, which made the difference in app popularity negligible. Nevertheless, it is possible that the loyalty card app was more attractive in cities with higher numbers of participating stores. Thus, we compared the cities by checking the number of participating stores per capita and the popularity of each product category (see

Appendix A). Our empirical comparison supports that consumers and app popularity were comparable across different cities.

## 3.2 Data

We collected daily Facebook campaign performance data during the experiment. Our ad campaigns were structured as unique combinations of an experiment condition and a product category for a given city. A unique campaign indicated (1) whether an ad was explicitly targeted or not, (2) whether we presented consumers with utilitarian or hedonic ad message framing, and (3) whether the ad text advertised the application referring to sports, fashion, or utility store loyalty cards. For each campaign, we had information on how many impressions were served on a specific day, how many consumers clicked on the ads, and how many times the mobile app was downloaded. We also had information on the number of actions directed toward an ad, which combined the number of likes, comments, and shares of an ad. The descriptive statistics of the main variables are shown in Table 2.

During the experiment, we provided 126,969 ad impressions to consumers, generating 1,211 clicks and 500 app downloads. <sup>6</sup> The number of ad impressions indicates how often the ads were displayed to consumers. When a consumer clicked on an ad, they were redirected to the app store. Ad impressions were evenly distributed across the product categories of the loyalty cards, with 31.3% of ad impressions referring to the sports category, 34.5% referring to the fashion category, and 34.2% to the utility category. Table 3 shows the summary statistics for campaign performance for each experiment condition. To protect user privacy, Facebook anonymizes and aggregates advertising data at the campaign level. Therefore, we were unable to trace impressions back to individual consumers. However, Facebook offers aggregate information about consumers’ gender and age: 63.7% of ad impressions were provided to females, and 70.1% of ad impressions were provided to consumers 25 years old or older. We disaggregated the dataset to analyze it on an ad impression level and linked ad impressions to user demographic information. This approach to analyzing data from Facebook’s ad platform has also been used in related studies (Tucker, 2014).

Next, we analyze our data at the ad impression level. The binary dependent variable in our model indicates whether a consumer clicked on an ad $( C l i c k _ { i } = 1 )$ or not $( C l i c k _ { i } =$ 0). We modeled the probability of clicking on an ad $i ,$ $P ( C l i c k _ { i } = 1 )$ , as a logit function of explicit targeting and hedonic framing:

$$
P (C l i c k _ {i} ^ {A d} = 1) = \frac {\exp {(U _ {i} ^ {C l i c k})}}{\exp {(U _ {i} ^ {C l i c k}) + 1}},
$$

where $U _ { i } ^ { C l i c k }$ is the latent utility of clicking on an ad impression i and is expressed as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r} U _ {i} ^ {C l i c k} = \alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {1} E x p l i c i t T a r g e t i n g _ {i} + \\ \beta_ {2} H e d o n i c F r a m i n g _ {i} + \beta_ {3} E x p l i c i t T a r g e t i n g _ {i} \times \\ H e d o n i c F r a m i n g _ {i} + \theta \times X _ {i} + \delta_ {i} + \gamma_ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i}, \end{array}
$$

where <sub>i</sub> is the constant. Explicit Targeting<sub>i</sub> represents a binary variable that equals 1 if an ad impression is explicitly targeted and 0 if the impression is not explicitly targeted. Hedonic Framing<sub>i</sub> is a binary variable that equals 1 if an ad impression contains an ad message emphasizing hedonic product benefits and 0 if the ad impression contains an ad message focusing on utilitarian product benefits. X<sub>i</sub> is a vector of consumer characteristics, including gender and age, as common for academic studies in the context of Facebook to account for consumer heterogeneity (Lee et al., 2018; Tucker, 2014). The vector θ represents the associated coefficients. To control for the timing of ad impressions, we included the vector $\delta _ { i } ,$ which represents fixed effects for the day of the week and the day of the campaign. <sub>i</sub> represents fixed effects for the product categories of the loyalty cards and ε<sub>i</sub> represents the idiosyncratic error term.

The coefficient for hedonic framing (compared to utilitarian framing) is negative and significant $( \beta _ { H e d o n i c } = - 0 . 2 8 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . ## 3.4 Robustness Checks and Additional Analysis

To assess the robustness of our empirical findings, we examined alternative dependent variables. First, it is possible that a consumer might click multiple times on the same ad. Although these cases are rare (6.0%), they may have inflated the number of clicks recorded. To address this concern, we ran a logit model using unique clicks as the dependent variable and found consistent results (Table 5, column 1). Second, beyond clicking, consumers might also “like” an ad, comment on it, or share it. We refer to the combination of these measures as unique actions. This variable took the value of 1 when a consumer responded to an ad impression with any one of the actions, and 0 otherwise. The results for this dependent variable are consistent with our main analysis (Table 5, Column 2). We also looked at conversions as an alternative dependent variable. We measured conversions as the number of app downloads. Consumers can view an ad and decide later to visit the app store without clicking on the ad. We first estimated the probability of downloading the app as a function of our treatment variables for each impression (Table 5, Column 3). We found that explicit targeting reduced the likelihood of downloading an app as did hedonic framing. The coefficient of their interaction is positive but not significant when using a logit model. Next, the decision to download the app could also be interpreted as contingent on clicking the ad in the first place. We found that the correlation between clicks and downloads is 0.623 $( p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . We analyzed app downloads contingent on ad clicks (Table 5, Column 4) and found that the treatment factors did not affect app downloads for consumers who clicked on the ad. This indicates that once consumers clicked on the ad, they had the same likelihood of downloading the app regardless of the ad treatment they received.

We further found that the effects of explicit targeting and message framing were consistent across different product categories (i.e. sports, fashion, utility) (Appendix B). Prior research suggests that consumers’ privacy concerns triggered by the use of personal information may differ by gender (Sheehan, 1999). Nonetheless, examining heterogeneous treatment effects for users of different genders provided no evidence for significant differences (Appendix B). To rule out the potential influence of media exposure on our observed effects, we assessed the search intensity of topics such as Facebook, privacyrelated issues, and general interest in the product categories. We collected Google trends data for six months before and after the experimental period and found no structural breaks around the time of the experiment (Appendix C).

The results of this field study offer us valuable insights into the effects of explicit targeting and ad message framing with high external validity. However, the field setting made it difficult to investigate the underlying mechanisms that drove the effect of explicit targeting. To investigate these mechanisms, we turned to a more controlled setting in which we replicated the design of the first experiment and supplemented it with a survey.

## 4 Study 2: The Mechanism Behind Explicit Targeting

Study 2 replicated the design of Study 1 in a different context and tested the mechanism that explained the effects observed in the field. We conducted the experiment on the online platform Prolific,<sup>7</sup> on which we targeted participants with ads for a job search mobile application.<sup>8</sup> Prior studies relied on developing fictional scenarios for which participants had to imagine they were experiencing the described scenario and then answer questions accordingly (Aguirre et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2019). Such an approach might serve as a sensible proxy for understanding behavioral responses to targeted advertising if participants can relate to the scenarios and act accordingly. In order to create a realistic setting in which participants actually perceive the advertisements as targeted, we only allowed Prolific users who indicated they were unemployed and actively looking for a job to participate in the experiment. These users provided this information when signing up on the platform (Palan & Schitter, 2018).

## 4.1 Experiment Design

We used a 2 (explicit targeting vs. no explicit targeting)  2 (utilitarian vs. hedonic ad message framing) between-subject experimental design. Participants were introduced to the task of the study and presented with an ad for a mobile job search application. Consistent with Study 1, we operationalized the explicit targeting by using the ad message “Are you looking for a job?” In the nonexplicit condition, there was no information cue. In terms of ad message framing, we advertised either the hedonic benefits of the application (“Finding a job can be fun”) or its utilitarian benefits (“Finding a job can be easy”) (see Figure 4 for an illustration).

After viewing the advertisement, participants indicated their click-through intention using the single item measure: “How likely are you to click on the advertisement about the JobSearch smartphone application?,” <sup>9</sup> and also indicated their intention to download the advertised app. To measure consumers’ relative activation of privacy concerns versus the perceived benefit of personalization, we used a construct called privacy over personalization developed by Kim et al. (2018) and asked participants: “In order to provide more personalized advertisements for you, marketers need to gather more information about you. In other words, when receiving an advertisement, there is a tradeoff between maintaining your privacy and enjoying the benefits of greater personalization. Upon seeing the above advertisement of JobSearch, which factor is more important to you when evaluating it?” (10-point scale; 1 = personalization to 10 = privacy). We measured reactance by asking participants to indicate whether they perceived the ad to be “disturbing,” “interfering,” “intrusive,” “forced upon me,” and “unwelcomed” on a

7-point rating scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.89 / AVE = 0.61 / CR = 0.88) (Bleier & Eisenbeiss, 2015b).

We also collected participants’ demographic information (age, gender, education) and their tendency to click on ads. To make sure the advertised product was relevant to the respondents, we asked for their current employment status, as well as their current involvement in the task of finding a job (five items: highly involved, of concern to me, important, relevant; Cronbach’s α = 0.80 / AVE = 0.80 / CR = 0.51) (Wang et al., 2012; Zaichkowsky, 1985). Finally, to account for common method bias,<sup>10</sup> we used a marker variable. We asked participants about their fashion leadership<sup>11</sup> (Goldsmith et al., 1993; Xu et al., 2012), an unrelated construct, to control for participants’ response styles. Since late respondents to a survey were assumed to be similar to nonrespondents, we accounted for nonresponse bias by comparing early to late respondents and found no significant differences in their responses (Armstrong & Overton, 1977; Mueller & Benlian, 2022).

## 4.2 Experiment Results

Our manipulation check indicates that participants in the explicit targeting condition were significantly more likely to agree with the statement: “I understand why I am being targeted with this ad” (7-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree) $( M _ { e x p l i c i t } = \ 4 . 5 5$ $M _ { n o n e x p l i c i t } = 4 . 1 1 { \mathrm { 1 } } , F _ { ( I , 2 4 5 ) } = 6 . 4 2 , p = 0 . 0 1 , \eta _ { \mathrm { p } } ^ { \mathrm { 2 } } = 0 . 0 3 )$ . To assess how participants perceived the ad message framing (hedonic vs. utilitarian), we used a two-item, 10-point bipolar scale ranging from fun to useful and from feeling (e.g. emotional) to thinking (e.g. rational) (Batra & Ahtola, 1990). We made use of this bipolar scale to disentangle the effect of the ad message framing from how participants perceived the advertised job search application, and found that participants perceived the utilitarian framing as significantly more utilitarian than hedonic $( M _ { u t i l i t a r i a n } = 4 . 4 4 , M _ { h e d o n i c } = 3 . 9 8 , F _ { ( I , 2 . 2 5 ) } = 7 . 4 2 , p$ $= 0 . 0 0 , \eta _ { \mathrm { p } } { } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 3 )$

To ensure that ads were perceived as targeted, we excluded participants who were not looking for a job and those who indicated low product involvement. Our final sample $( n = 2 4 7 )$ consisted of 46.2% female participants; 75.7% of participants were between 18-34 years old and 56.3% had at least an undergraduate degree. To investigate the mechanism through which explicit targeting affects consumers’ intention to click on ads, we estimated a serial mediation model following Hayes (2013). In our model, we controlled for ad message framing, the marker variable, and participants’ tendency to click on ads. The results are shown in Figure 5.

We found that the direct effect of explicit targeting on click intention was not significant $( B = 0 . 2 0 3 , S E = 0 . 2 1 2 ,$ $p = 0 . 3 3 9 )$ . To test for mediation, we used percentile bootstrapping to generate 95% confidence intervals around the indirect effects. If mediation is present, the generated confidence intervals do not include zero (Hayes, 2013). In our analysis, we made use of 10,000 bootstraps. <sup>12</sup> We found a significant indirect effect of explicit targeting on click intention through privacy over personalization (ab = -0.078, SE = 0.049, 95%LLCI = - 0.192, 95%ULCI = -0.001) and through reactance (ab = -0.136, SE = 0.073, 95%LLCI = -0.286, 95%ULCI = - 0.004). The indirect effect of serial mediation through both privacy over personalization and reactance was also significant $( a b = - 0 . 0 5 1 , S E = 0 . 0 2 5 , 9 5 \% L L C I = - 0 . 1 0 8 ,$ 95%ULCI = -0.011), supporting H2 and H3. As a robustness check, we replaced the dependent variable click intention with download intention, yielding consistent results (see Appendix E).

Next, we investigated whether ad message framing moderates the effect of explicit targeting on click intention in our conditional process model. Our empirical investigation helps to disentangle the mechanism through which this moderation effect takes place—i.e., by providing evidence for the phase of consumers’ assessment of explicit targeting at which the effect of information framing comes into play. This allows us to understand how information framing influences consumers’ focus on two routes to persuasion: a functional route, fostered by the communication of utilitarian benefits of a product, and an emotionalexperiential route, fostered by the communication of hedonic benefits. A moderation on an earlier path suggests that ad message framing moderates the effect of explicit targeting on consumers’ perceptions of targeted ads, i.e., the extent to which consumers perceive the tension between personalization benefits and privacy concerns and experience reactance. On the other hand, a moderation on a later path would suggest that ad message framing moderates the extent to which consumers translate their perceptions into behavior.

We found that hedonic framing moderated the path from reactance to click intention. We therefore estimated the most parsimonious model, excluding the nonsignificant interactions (see Figure 6). Consistent with the field study, we found that hedonic compared to utilitarian framing decreased participants’ click intention $( B { \bf \Phi } = { \bf \Phi } - { \bf \Phi }$ 1.405, SE = 0.529, p = 0.009). Moreover, hedonic framing reduced the negative effect of reactance on click intention $( B = 0 . 3 1 2 , S E = 0 . 1 4 9 , p = 0 . 0 3 7 )$ .

The indirect effect for the serial mediation was found to be significant for hedonic framing (ab = -0.032, SE = 0.020, 95%LLCI = -0.080, 95%ULCI = -0.003) and utilitarian framing (ab = - 0.070, SE = 0.034, 95%LLCI = -0.149, 95%ULCI = - 0.016). This suggests that hedonic framing reduces the negative effect of explicit targeting on click intention. As a robustness check, we tested all the models with traditional CB-SEM, which yielded qualitatively similar insights (see Appendix G).

Our results provide empirical evidence for the causal mechanism through which explicit targeting transmits its effect on ad effectiveness as well as the moderating role of ad message framing. Advertisers aim to exploit the benefits of increasing perceived personalization without raising too many privacy concerns with explicit targeting. Interestingly, the results from both

Studies 1 and 2 indicate that explicit targeting actually harms ad effectiveness. Study 2 provides evidence of the mechanism behind this negative effect of explicit targeting. The study demonstrates that explicit targeting leads consumers to focus more on privacy costs than on personalization benefits, leading to reactance and a decrease in ad effectiveness. Furthermore, we found that ad message framing moderates the effect of explicit targeting by influencing the serial mediation effect through privacy over personalization and reactance. More specifically, ad message framing moderates the path from reactance to click intention. It seems that hedonic ad message framing nudges consumers to assess ads from an emotional-experiential perspective rather than a functional perspective, decreasing the negative effect of privacy concerns on consumers’ response to explicit targeting. This is in line with evidence that suggests that in the context of a purchase decision, the effect of the perceived risk of a purchase (i.e. potential negative experience) may depend on the focus on the hedonic versus utilitarian attributes of a product (Chiu et al., 2014). Similarly, the alleviating role of hedonic information framing happens for those who experience a negative experience from explicit targeting (in the form of privacy concerns and reactance to an ad).

In Studies 1 and 2, we employed a subtle operationalization of explicit targeting by referring to personal information used for targeting in a question in the advertising copy. Advertisers are increasingly under pressure to provide more transparency regarding their targeting practices (European Commission, 2018; Ramirez et al., 2014). Policymakers are unlikely to be satisfied with advertisers’ practice of making the use of personal information explicit via questions as a means of transparency. Instead, policymakers are likely to require a clearer description of the targeting mechanism. In fact, ad platforms, for example Facebook, are beginning to allow consumers to access information related to why they are being targeted with specific ads.<sup>13</sup> While such measures may be perceived as increasing the pressure for transparency on advertisers (Hearn, 2019), it is unclear how consumers respond to targeted ads with a description of the targeting mechanism. To answer this question, we extended the experiment from Study 2 and examined consumers’ reactions to targeted ads when the targeting mechanism is clearly described.

## 6 Discussions and Conclusion

## 6.1 Discussion of Main Findings

Responding to the increasing demand for transparency in advertising targeting (Acquisti et al., 2013; Cram et al., 2022; European Commission, 2018; Ramirez et al., 2014), firms are increasingly using explicit targeting with the aim of enhancing the effectiveness of their online advertising messages. Explicit targeting means that firms reveal the information used to target individuals in the ad message. To limit the potential increase in privacy concerns triggered by revealing targeting information, firms generally use a rather subtle approach when revealing the reason consumers are targeted. However, the findings from our studies suggest that such explicit targeting is actually counterproductive. To gain further insights into advertisers’ strategies to increase consumers’ perception of personalization benefits relative to their perception of costs in the form of privacy concerns, we assessed how consumers weigh these competing considerations when confronted with explicit targeting. The results from an online experiment provide evidence that explicit targeting nudges consumers toward a focus on privacy concerns over personalization benefits, leading to reactance toward targeted ads.

We further found evidence for the presence of a moderation effect of product information framing in the ad message. We found that hedonic information framing (i.e., referring to hedonic product benefits) can partially alleviate the negative effect of explicit targeting. Promoting the hedonic product benefits seems to make consumers less sensitive to explicit targeting. Our online experiment reveals that ad message framing influences the effect of explicit targeting by moderating the serial mediation effect through privacy over personalization and reactance. This indicates that hedonic information framing, which nudges consumers to focus more on the hedonic aspects of product benefits, reduces the negative effect of explicit targeting on consumers’ responses to targeted ads. Specifically, the moderation effect takes place on the path between consumers’ reactance and consumers’ click intentions. This aligns with the alleviating role of hedonic product attributes for those with negative experiences from explicit targeting (i.e., increased privacy concerns leading to lower perceived personalization benefits and reactance to the ad) (Chiu et al., 2014). Nevertheless, we found that hedonic information framing cannot fully compensate for the negative effect caused by explicit targeting.

This result suggests that product information framing affects how consumers translate their perceptions into behavior instead of affecting how consumers perceive targeted advertising. According to the elaboration likelihood model, the functional route to persuasion is fostered by the communication of the utilitarian benefits of a product, whereas the emotionalexperiential route is fostered by the communication of hedonic benefits. Our study design provides empirical evidence allowing for an initial understanding of how product information framing influences consumers focus on these routes to persuasion. One might expect that product information framing directly influences the functional route of persuasion by shifting consumers’ focus toward personalization benefits versus privacy costs. Instead, our findings indicate that product information framing seems to shift consumers’ focus from a primarily functional route of assessing an ad toward a more emotional-experiential route. The hedonic ad message framing decreases the impact of consumers’ functional assessment of an ad on behavioral outcomes, as evidenced by a decrease in the serial mediation effect that represents part of the consumer’s functional assessment of the ad. This explanation is also supported by the fact that the relationships from explicit targeting to privacy over personalization and reactance are not moderated by product information framing and remain intact.

As an extension of our online experiment, we assessed how a regulatory intervention in which a clear description of the targeting mechanism becomes mandatory affects the effectiveness of targeted ads. In the past, advertisers have generally been opposed to such a clear articulation of the use of consumers’ personal data, as demonstrated by advertisers’ typically subtle approach to explicit targeting. Interestingly, our findings indicate that providing a description of the targeting mechanism actually positively affects ad effectiveness. This could potentially be attributed to increased transparency, which is valued by consumers. Furthermore, our findings suggest that the inclusion of a targeting description can work as a remedy, offsetting the negative effects induced by explicit targeting. These findings offer important insights for policymakers by indicating that the inclusion of a targeting description might not be as harmful as advertisers fear.

## 6.2 Theoretical Contribution and Practical Implications

This paper contributes to the literature on the consumer response to personalized information and targeting (Bleier & Eisenbeiss, 2015a; Chen & Stallaert, 2014; Lambrecht & Tucker, 2013; Tam & Ho, 2006). More specifically, this paper provides theoretical explanations and empirical evidence for the effects of explicit targeting. Contrary to previous research on the positive effects of personalization and targeting (Aguirre et al., 2015; De Keyzer et al., 2015; Komiak & Benbasat, 2006; Tam & Ho, 2006), our findings show that consumers respond negatively when advertisers explicitly yet subtly refer to the information used to target them with ads. In addition to identifying the effect of explicit targeting, our work provides insights into the mechanism underlying the negative effect of explicit targeting. Explicit targeting leads to a relative increase in privacy concerns compared to perceived personalization as well as an increase in reactance toward targeted ads. Our study extends the IS literature on the personalization-privacy paradox (Awad & Krishnan, 2006; Sutanto et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2011) by establishing the tension between privacy and personalization as a mechanism that explains the effect of explicit targeting on advertising effectiveness. Our study also demonstrates the importance of information framing in influencing consumers’ responses to targeted ads and examines the extent to which hedonic framing can alleviate the negative effects of explicit targeting.

Our results also have important practical implications for advertisers using explicit targeting. Despite evidence that targeting can improve the perceived relevance of advertising (Iyer et al., 2005; Liu et al., 2013), we provide evidence that indicates that revealing the use of personal information in targeting can backfire. Our results from the field indicate that the probability of clicking on an ad using nonexplicit targeting is over 70% higher than the probability of clicking on an ad using explicit targeting. Advertisers would be well advised to reevaluate their current explicit targeting practices. This might seem like bad news for advertisers but there is a silver lining: as privacy regulations become stricter, advertisers are under increasing pressure and may become legally obliged to clearly state why consumers are being targeted with their ads. Our research provides empirical evidence that including a description of the targeting mechanism is not harmful. In contrast, it has a positive effect on consumers’ reception of targeted advertising. This result is informative for both advertising strategy and regulators’ policy decisions. It provides evidence of the benefits of the ethical use of online profile information and shows how such use could and should be manifested in policies of appropriate use regarding this type of personal information. Consumers’ personal information can provide substantial benefits to consumers via personalized targeting, but firms need to responsibly use such information and, more importantly, offer all consumers the right to be informed.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Research

We have identified several future research opportunities that arise from our study’s limitations and results. First, as is common in the industry, our study implemented a subtle form of explicit targeting to limit consumer privacy concerns. Future research could explore consumer responses to varying degrees of targeting explicitness, which may have differing impacts. It would also be insightful to study how different consumer segments respond to explicit targeting—for instance, individuals with high privacy concerns versus those who are indifferent, different age groups, or people of different socioeconomic backgrounds or cultural contexts. This could provide advertisers with crucial knowledge on how to tailor their advertising strategies.

Second, it would be worthwhile to examine the type of user information utilized in explicit targeting and how that influences consumer responses. For example, demographic, browsing, location, and contextual information could have unique effects on consumers in the context of explicit targeting. In addition, our study revealed that hedonic information framing partially alleviates the negative effect of explicit targeting. It would be worthwhile to explore other types of framing techniques, such as fear-based, gain-based, or lossbased, and how these framing techniques interact with explicit targeting.

Third, in our field experiment, we assigned treatment conditions based on participants’ place of residence, which could potentially have introduced selection biases. We carefully selected cities that are comparable to minimize potential issues of consumer heterogeneity. Although we are confident that consumers in our different treatment groups can be considered homogeneous, a fully randomized field experiment could further alleviate such concerns. Our online experiments in Studies 2 and 3 helped us to further remedy these concerns, as we found consistent results in these fully randomized experiments.

Fourth, as we found that clear descriptions of targeting mechanisms can positively affect ad effectiveness, it would be interesting to explore the impact of other possible regulatory interventions, like stricter privacy rules or mandatory opt-in/opt-out options for consumers. Further, while our research largely focused on immediate responses to explicit targeting, future research could explore long-term effects. Do consumers’ attitudes change over time? Does sustained exposure to explicit targeting have any lasting impact on consumers’ online behavior or brand perceptions?

Finally, future research could build on our work and explore whether our findings apply similarly across different industries or if there are industry-specific factors that significantly influence the effectiveness of explicit targeting. Additional insights could allow firms to optimize their explicit targeting implementation and may reveal ways to leverage the transparency of explicit targeting without decreasing the effectiveness of such targeting.

## Appendix A: Homogeneity of Cities

To alleviate potential concerns regarding the experiment design of the study, we made sure the cities in our experimen were homogeneous across a range of demographic and economic characteristics. First, the cities were homogeneous in terms of internet penetration. In fact, internet use at a national level is 91%, with no differences reported across urban and rural areas (Eurostat: epp.eurostat.ec.europa.eu). Second, the cities were homogeneous in terms of gender and age distribution, and average income. We further compared the variation in demographic characteristics across all major cities in the country and the cities used in our experiment by using the coefficient of variation (CV) (ratio of the standard deviation to the mean). Our empirical evidence indicates that the experimental cities are homogenous since their CV is low (≤10%), and systematically lower than the CV of all cities in the country (Table A1). We also found a low CV regarding the potential reach of Facebook users within the experimental cities (overall and across categories) (Table A2). To alleviate potential concerns about heterogeneity in the attractiveness of the app across the cities, we looked into the number of related stores per capita, since the advertised app could be more attractive in cities with a larger number of retail stores and businesses (that offer loyalty cards). We collected data about the number of business locations and retail stores in all major cities in the country. The empirical evidence indicates that the experimental cities are homogenous since they have a low CV (also lower than that of the entire list of cities) regarding these market characteristics (Table A3). Further, we investigated the CV of the experimental cities for each product category used in the experiment. We collected data on a number of fashion stores (clothing and shoes), sport stores (retail stores and gyms), and utility stores (Do-It-Yourself stores). We found small variations for the experimental cities, which comprise a rather homogeneous subset of cities compared to the entire set of cities (Table A4).

## Appendix B: Additional Analysis—Field Study (Study 1)

Prior research indicates that consumer responses may depend on the product category that is advertised (Kronrod et al., 2012). In our study, we designed different ad campaigns for the use of the mobile app in different product categories: sports, fashion, and utility stores. To rule out that our findings are driven by the congruence between the nature (hedonic/utilitarian) of the product category and ad framing (hedonic/utilitarian), we examined the heterogeneous effects of hedonic ad message framing for different product categories. For example, utility stores are mostly considered utilitarian, whereas fashion stores are associated with hedonic consumption (Voss et al., 2003). In Table B1, we included the interaction between product categories and ad message framing and found that, with respect to direction and significance, our focal coefficients remain consistent with our main analysis.<sup>17</sup> This finding suggests that the effects of ad message framing are not solely driven by the congruence with the advertised product category.

## Appendix C: Google Trends Around the Time of the Field Study (Study 1)

To rule out alternative explanations of the results during the specific sample observation period, we looked at Google Trends data on search intensity with regard to Facebook, privacy-related issues, as well as general interest in the product categories used in the study. The rationale is that if privacy-related issues received some unexpected exposure in the country (e.g. news), this unobserved structural break may contaminate the results. We examined one year of search data at a national level, but also across the major cities in the country (Figure C1).

The graphs show no structural breaks in the trends before and during the advertising campaigns. Further, we conducted a Chow test (Chow, 1960) to test for the presence of a structural break before and after the start of the ad campaign. The null hypothesis suggests that the trends are similar before and during the ad campaign. The Chow test across all search trends suggests that there were no structural breaks in the trends of the search intensity at the time of the implementation of the ad campaign. Similar results were found with structural breaks at later points of the campaign. We further show that the search trends were homogeneous across the experimental cities according to their low absolute and relative coefficient of variation (CV) (Table C1)

## Appendix E: Process Model for Download Intention (Study 2)

As a robustness check, we investigated the mechanism by which explicit targeting affects consumers’ intention to download the advertised smartphone app using a mediation model. Our results are depicted in Figure E1.

To test for mediation, we generate 95% confidence intervals around the indirect effects. In our analysis, we made use of 10,000 bootstraps. We found a significant indirect effect for privacy over personalization $( a b = - 0 . 0 7 5 , S E = 0 . 0 4 8 ,$ $9 5 \% L L C I = - 0 . 1 8 5 , 9 5 \% U L C I = - 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). We also found evidence for an indirect effect through reactance $( a b = - 0 . 1 4 6$ $S E = 0 . 0 7 7 , 9 5 \% L L C I = - 0 . 3 0 6 , 9 5 \% U L C I = - 0 . 0 0 5 )$ . Moreover, the indirect effect of the serial mediation was found to be significant (ab = -0.055, SE = 0.025, 95%LLCI = -0.111, $9 5 \% U L C I = - 0 . 0 1 3 )$ . The direct effect from explicit targeting to download intention is not significant in this model $( B = 0 . 0 4 4 , S E = 0 . 2 0 0 , p = 0 . 8 2 7 )$ . This indicates that effect of explicit targeting on download intention is fully mediated by the paths through privacy over personalization and reactance. The additional analysis supports the findings from the main analysis in the paper.

## Appendix F: Process Model for the Effects of Explicit Targeting in the Presence of the Targeting Description (Study 3)

To investigate the effects of explicit targeting in the presence of the targeting description, we conditioned all paths in our process model for explicit targeting on the presence of the targeting description (Figure F1). For easier interpretation, we depicted the results for the path coefficients separately for the targeted ads with and without targeting description. This approach is common for conditional process models (Bhattacharjee et al., 2014).

With no targeting description present, we found a significant indirect effect for privacy over personalization $\left( a b \pmb { \mathrm { \check { \tau } } } \right)$ 0.082, SE = 0.049, 95%LLCI = -0.195, 95%ULCI = -0.004). However, in the presence of targeting description, we found no significant indirect effect for privacy over personalization (ab = -0.004, SE = 0.020, 95%LLCI = -0.046, 95%ULCI = 0.040). The indirect effect for reactance was not significant in both cases, without a targeting description (ab = -0.125, SE = 0.071, 95%LLCI = -0.277, 95%ULCI = 0.005) and with a targeting description (ab = -0.021, SE = 0.054, 95%LLCI = -0.124, 95%ULCI = 0.090). The indirect effect for the serial mediation was significant when a targeting description was not provided (ab = -0.053, SE = 0.025, 95%LLCI = -0.110, 95%ULCI = -0.012) but not significant when a targeting description was provided (ab = -0.005, SE = 0.019, 95%LLCI = -0.045, 95%ULCI = 0.033). The results indicate that the targeting description seems to offset the negative effects of explicit targeting on the consumer response to targeted ads.
