## A B S T R A C T

Disaster management (DM) is a challenging domain to model because of the variety of dynamic characteristics attached to the domain. Metamodeling is a model-driven approach that describes how semantic domain models can be built into an artifact called a Metamodel. By collecting all the domain concepts and partitioning the domain problems into sub-domain-problems, a metamodel can produce a domain-specific language. This paper presents a Disaster Management Metamodel that can serve as a representational layer of DM expertise. This metamodel leads to better knowledge sharing and facilitates combining and matching different DM activities to best manage the disaster on hand.

## 1. Introduction

Disaster management (DM) is the management of the risks and consequences of a disaster. DM includes various aspects of planning and responses in all phases of a disaster event: mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery [3]. DM is a knowledge domain that can also be defined as the effective organization, direction and utilization of available counter-disaster resources [28]. Practitioners in this domain aim to reduce or to avoid potential losses from hazards and to assure prompt and appropriate assistance to victims of disasters and achieve a rapid and effective recovery. Many complex series of activities co-exist in the operationalization of this domain. These activities include risk assessments, preparedness actions, emergency responses, rescue operations, aid distribution, reconstruction tasks and many others. The DM knowledge domain is indeed quite complex, both to model and communicate. Moreover, it is continuously evolving. Rather than aim for a comprehensive and complete model, in this study we propose a metamodel that can pull together the various disparate and partial models that attempt to describe DM knowledge systematically.

Generally, models are used in many areas to share and communicate knowledge about the world [13] or manage complexities [23]. They can structure the theory of generic concepts that shape the way we conceive our phenomenon in our reality [17]. Ideally, models have a causal connection to the modeled part of reality [4]. They must form true or faithful representations so that queries of the model give reliable statements about the reality or that manipulations of the model result in reliable adaptations of reality. A metamodel is the artifact output of metamodeling that makes statements about what can be expressed in the valid models of the knowledge domain. It is often defined as a model about models. In our context, a model refers to the DM solution model that can show the coordination of DM activity and its elements (e.g., humans, resources, plans) and how these should be arranged for a specific disaster.

Failures in DM are often due to an accumulation of a complex chain of events and are often accompanied by changes in external environment factors [1]. A metamodel can help zoom in and identify the sources of inadequate DM practices. The management process itself is also contingent on existing organizational structures charged with the DM processes. It is common wisdom that no two disasters are exactly the same and that every disaster requires its own management process. However, the way disasters impact human lives and business processes exhibit similarities, and responses are often transferrable between disasters. The evacuation of personnel, for example, is a DM action that is applicable in many disaster situations. For this research, we structure the complexity of DM activities by developing a metamodel called a Disaster Management Metamodel (DM Metamodel). It is a precise definition of the constructs and rules needed for creating DM models. It will be a representation of how all DM models can be constructed (e.g., a mitigation model of bushfire, a response model of earthquake disasters, an aid distribution in nuclear disasters and many more). To develop the metamodel, we identify all generic concepts that appear in the DM domain (e.g., emergency teams, rescue resources, emergency plans, evacuation, disaster monitoring and alert warning). The aim of the DM Metamodel is to allow its users to easily create specific DM solution models based on their own disaster challenges. This will not only support users in making quicker decisions, but it will also provide DM knowledge sharing among varying DM communities. The research presented uncovers the potential of the modeldriven approach to support DM. To demonstrate the applicability of the model, we use two recent and real-world disaster scenarios: the 2011 Christchurch Earthquake disaster in New Zealand and the recent nuclear meltdown in the aftermath of the 2011 tsunami in Japan.

DM involves many decision-making activities. Weighing the amount of information needed before making a decision against the time available is a challenge. Timely decision-making to direct and coordinate the activities of other people is important to achieve the DM goals. Making rapid decisions in the chaos during disaster events is a very challenging endeavor. The process can become increasingly difficult when a decision includes considering the specific interests of victims, governments, NGOs and other emergency services teams. We believe if all DM processes, tasks and coordination are comprehensively and explicitly formulated, the potential to resolve many DM decision makers’ problems is improved. The DM knowledge domain is also dispersed. A DM Metamodel that describes other DM models can provide a clear representation of how various DM solution models can be generated. However, such a metamodel must not only provide the solutions for different disaster activities, but it must also be able to handle different types of disasters. A solution model derived from such a metamodel is expected to describe the roles and functions that need to be performed by the DM users in their specific scenarios. This approach can help many DM users, e.g., emergency managers, monitoring users, local and state government, emergency service teams and aid agencies. In essence, proposing this DM Metamodel provides a data modeling solution to unify access to extant data repositories. These repositories represent the observed world in a conceptual model and transform this representation into a logical structure in an operational database [30].

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: Section 2 describes related work to further highlight how the use of a metamodel can facilitate managing problems in DM. Section 3 briefly describes the development process of the DM Metamodel. Section 3.1 presents the process to assess the conformance of realworld DM solution models to our DM Metamodel. Section 3.2 presents the implementation of the conformance process in two disaster scenarios: (i) the earthquake disaster in Christchurch, New Zealand and the nuclear meltdown in Fukushima, Japan. Finally, Section 4 concludes the paper with a discussion on our findings and future research related to this subject.

## 2. Related work

By developing a metamodel for a specific domain, a language of the domain can be modeled efficiently. Metamodels have been used to configure extensible tools, processes and content to automate the development and maintenance of variant archetypical software products [15]. Our work aims to adapt the success of metamodeling in software engineering to disaster management (DM), supporting knowledge reuse. We adapt the previous use into the organization of DM domain knowledge to enable process engineering as unforeseen needs arise. We recognize that organizational knowledge is not only created within an organization but can also be acquired externally [21]. As such, this research aims to develop a DM language that can then be used to create repositories for sharing DM knowledge within and across organizations. The activities of transferring or disseminating knowledge from one person, group or organization to another could then be executed efficiently. Successful knowledge sharing depends on knowledge contributors populating the knowledge management system with content and knowledge seekers retrieving content for its reuse; the same individual may be a contributor or a seeker at different times [22]. This can, in turn, underpin the strategic management of organizational knowledge to help DM organizations sustain an effective operation in volatile environments [19].

Structuring information and maintaining it takes time and effort and requires a type of quality control [16] that often relies on the use of metadata [24]. Metadata is often seen as the cornerstone element for the development of information infrastructures because metadata facilitates the semantic description of content and services [24]. The conceptual frameworks of crisis management for knowledge repositories to strengthen the resilient capabilities of organizations and communities to face a crisis have been developed [8,20]. However, such frameworks currently need to be empirically validated by experts in the field. A metamodel not only provides the required metadata and describes the data relationships, it also provides a complete abstract view of the domain with guidelines for specializing this abstract view into specific contexts as need arise. As such, they can also be used to check the completeness of the knowledge repositories. The DM Metamodel generalizes all the concepts that appear in plausible DM models during its development. This set of concepts and their relationships can then be used to validate the completeness concepts used in a specific DM conceptual framework (aka model). Our metamodel introduced in this paper has the potential to be used as a standard guideline for DM knowledge modeling.

A model of a knowledge system is a description or specification of the system and its environment for a specific purpose [2]. The most important task of developing this specification is to gather all concepts that are important for the purpose of organizing the system [27]. In our context, the specification is the DM Metamodel that allows the DM users to derive the best disaster solution model from the context description provided. For example, if a regional government aims to create an effective flood evacuation process, all DM components concerning how the process needs to be executed must be presented clearly. This can be constructed by a model that can abstract the real evacuation processes. The processes could be a combination of the following activities: (i) the people at risk should evacuate from their disaster-hit location, (ii) the emergency services team should coordinate the evacuees, (iii) evacuation operation centers should be set up and (iv) evacuation centers and other evacuation processes should be organized. Constructing a solution model for specific disaster problems requires identifying the sub-processes and understanding sub-problems, solving them and combining them into new solutions [2]. This requires an understanding of the methods, procedures, protocols and strategies involved in DM [29]. Fig. 1 shows how a model-driven approach through a metamodel corresponds to such a problem-solving environment. Our work supports DM users with structured constructs and rules of DM knowledge (e.g., concepts, activities, stakeholders or resources). By developing a DM Metamodel, we uncover and make explicit key aspects of activities, organization and resources in DM. These are the reusable components of the domain (e.g., users, resources, procedures or plans) and are precisely identified by the metamodel. In addition to improving DM coordination, a unifying metamodel can ensure that the key components of problem solving in a domain are easily presented to newcomers to the domain [5,31].

As described in [31], inadequate access to domain knowledge can make the analysis process more time consuming, error prone, difficult, and costly. Moreover, it can also lead to overreliance on domain stakeholders beyond their availability. Having a metamodel can create better communication among DM practitioners and reduce the analysis effort. In essence, it will expedite the DM requirement analysis process. It has been argued that a weakness of traditional repositories is that they are limited to providing storage and search facilities for components, disregarding the barrier of process analysis required to enable reuse [31]. Indeed, the work in [31] argues that because repositories embody process domain knowledge, the time is ripe to deploy repositories for expediting domain analysis and speeding understanding. This speedier analysis is indeed facilitated in DM with the presence of a DM Metamodel. In fact, this has often motivated the use of metamodels, and they have been used to reduce the complexity and explore the behavior of a model against a large part of its domain [11].

Creating a metamodel requires attention to six criteria: (i) Purpose – why is it used, (ii) User – who should use it, (iii) Scope – what are the constructs (or entities) addressed, (iv) Formality – can it be easily interpreted by computers, (v) Independence – is it independent of system implementation, and (vi) Understand ability – can it be easily understood by a professional [12]. For the development of the DM Metamodel, we realized that the biggest challenge of this task is to identify its scope, that is, the general concepts used in all phases of DM. By identifying the DM concepts, the metamodel will partition all the DM problems into sub-DM problems. After the concept is identified, all elements hidden behind the concept (e.g., who performs the concept, how the concept should be performed, when it should be performed, what are the pre-requisites before performing the concept) must be obtained. The concept is then reconciled to ensure its generality. Particularly for DM, the domain contains a variety of elements in each of its domain operations. These elements include DM tasks, DM activities, DM roles, DM resources, DM decisions, DM users, DM tools and even the unpredicted DM environmental events. The accuracy of future DM models can be ensured when the models are developed to conform to an effective DM Metamodel. This process will eventually create good DM organization models in real-world domain applications. In an earlier work, we formulated an iterative metamodeling process to the DM domain [25]. The result from this process is the DM Metamodel we present here. Most significantly, we illustrate its use in actual disaster case studies.

## 3. Metamodel for the disaster management domain

In this section, an overview of the DM Metamodel development process and the resulting metamodel are discussed. It is developed based on the Four-layer metamodeling framework of the Meta Object Facility (MOF) offered by the Object Management Group [14]. In this framework, the four layers are labeled as M3-, M2-, M1- and M0-level. The details of each layer are as follows:

(i) The M3-level is reserved for Meta–metamodel elements modeling concepts.

(ii) The M2-level is a reserved for Metamodel elements (instance of meta–metamodel) that define a language for specifying models.

(iii) The M1-level is a layer for Model elements (instance of metamodel) that define a language that describes an information domain.

(iv) The lowest level, M0, is specifically dedicated for User Model elements (instance of model) that describe a specific informa tion domain.

The MOF framework can offer many advantages to the users of the metamodel. As described by [26], it can do the following: (a) support any kind of model and modeling paradigm imaginable, (b) allow different kinds of metadata to be related, (c) allow metamodels and new kinds of metadata to be added incrementally and (d) support the interchange of arbitrary models and metamodels between parties that use the same meta–metamodel (M3-level). Specifically for a metamodel positioned in the M2- level, the work of [10] discusses six criteria to gauge the goodness of a developed metamodel. These criteria include the following: (i) prediction of the metamodel reasonably consistent with those of the baseline model across the domain, (ii) having independent meaningful variables, (iii) ability to identify/highlight all input variables that are essential to success (critical components of a domain), (iv) reasonable depiction of relative ‘importance’ or a generation of statistical measures of the significance or importance of candidature variables and (v) provision of a good storyline (can tell the users why and how the model should behave as it does).

To create the DM Metamodel, a metamodeling creation process from the work used to develop a Framework for Agent Modeling Language (FAML) in [6] and [5] was adapted. Some of the steps we took for the DM Metamodel development are as follows: (i) extracting the general concepts relevant to all identified DM models, (ii) short-listing the candidate concepts, (iii) reconciling the differences between the concepts and (iv) identifying the relationships among the concepts. Before those four steps are executed, a preliminary step is first taken to identify DM models that are deemed influential. The extent of the influence of a given model is estimated using a heuristic function that combines the model acceptance (as indicated by citations to the model) and the effort that has been expended in developing it (the size of the organization behind it). The heuristic function was recently developed in [25]. The function normalizes the impact of the model to its longevity. Once a model is selected, disaster-specific expertise is deleted, and only general DM concepts are shortlisted for inclusion into our metamodel. A reconciliation process between the concepts from the different sourced models is then undertaken to produce the metamodel shown. In other words, the concepts of the metamodel are based on finding generalizations of consensus between various expert opinions encapsulated in the models. These models are chosen based on their initial acceptance.

In Fig. 2, we present the resultant DM Metamodel after all steps to create it were applied. The DM Metamodel contains the relationships among the concepts that can represent the semantic DM domain. The core class in this DM Metamodel is the Organization, which represents the loose ‘organization’ where DM concepts are operationalized. All key concepts in DM are grouped in the Organization concept. Other key DM concepts are aggregated within this class and include DMProcedure, DMRequirement, DMPolicy, Actor, ElementsAtRisk, DMTeam, DomainKnowledge, Resource, ActorRole and MessageCommunication. DMProcedure can represent the collections of implemented procedures of DM activities including Mitigation, Preparedness, Rescue, Response and Evacuation. DMTeam defines a collection of the ActorRole class that typically describes human roles that work towards a DMGoal. The ActorTask class in our metamodel is derived from a DMGoal class. Here, we also model a DisasterPreventionGoal as a class that can be achieved by DisasterPreventionTask. ElementsAtRisk includes elements that bear the brunt of a disaster impact. These elements include infrastructure, natural sites and people. Of these elements at risk, people can be active elements that also can play a role in the DM process. Specifically, the ‘victim’ victims can be active elements in DM and can have a direct impact on the process of DM. This impact can be positive as well as negative on the effectiveness of the DM process. There is a dynamic relation between victims and the unfolding of the DM process, and this is a recurring aspect of DM that warrants an explicit inclusion in the metamodel. As such, ‘Victim’ is an important subclass of ‘People’ at risk associated with both concepts, disaster ‘Indicators’ and ‘ActorTask’ (components of the DM goals).

After the metamodel is developed, the next stage is to determine how the metamodel can be used by the users of the metamodel. In our context, the DM Metamodel has the potential to instantiate a new DM model based on the problem requirements given by the DM users. In the next section, we detail how this process can be realized by showing its applicability in the real world of DM.

## 3.1. The conformance of the DM model from its DM Metamodel

In the MOF metamodeling framework, the derivation of a model from its metamodel is called a ‘conformance.’ Through the conformance process, a realization of concept in the DM Metamodel in a new instance (object) in the Model at the M1- level can be achieved. At the M0-level, all objects created by a Model at the M1-level can be used as an instance in the User Model (M0-level). The instance can then be stored in a DM Knowledge Repository for the purpose of future use. An instance created at the M0-level represents a sample of DM solution model obtained from the Model. The solution model is valuable because the artifact can be shared and be referred by other DM users at other times. It is an important model particularly to the DM users who may be dealing with the same kind of disaster problem. In addition, the time taken to solve the recurrent problem can be reduced as the same problem has been previously solved by other DM users.

The DM Metamodel is positioned at the M2-level in the MOF metamodeling framework. Therefore, a Model that is positioned at the M1-level can be modeled by the metamodel. All the Models (M1-level) are created based on model specification language described in its conformance Metamodel (M2-level). The same process form can also be inherited by the User Model (M0), which is positioned at a level lower than the M1-level. In MOF, the domain concept used in a metamodel is presented as a Class. The data for a Class is presented as an Object. As such, the data for the Object are in turn presented as an Instance in User Model. The User Model at the M0-level is the target model that is the aim of the DM Metamodel. The derived target model presents the model of real-world DM scenarios, and it contains ‘human and universal’ elements. Fig. 3 graphically illustrates these inter-level relationships.

During the metamodel creation process, we collected and reconciled all DM concepts appearing in the DM domain. The identification of all these concepts enables the DM Metamodel to handle the complexity of the domain. For example, a Disaster-ActionService is one of the DM concepts we identified (refer to Fig. 2 for the position of the concept). During the metamodel conformance process, concepts at the M2-level can be instantiated to be a new object in the DM Model at M1-level. For example, for the DisasterActionService, the concept is being instantiated to be the new object of EarthquakeActionService. This object is the instance of DisasterActionService concept that will be used in the Earthquake Emergency Response Model at the M1-level. The second example is the instantiation of the EarthquakeProcedure from the DMProcedure concept.

The DisasterActionService is created with a (0. . .\*) (zero or many) relationship to concept DMProcedure in the DM Metamodel. The ‘relationship statement’ sets a rule that allows the modeler to create more than one object from the DisasterActionService. For example, in Fig. 3, the DisasterActionService is instantiated into new objects of EvacuateVictims and RescueVictimsInRubble. Next, we inherit the relationship of Follows to be a new relationship to tie between the

EvacuateVictims and the StateEvacuationProcedure. In addition, Follows is a relationship that ties RescueVictimsInRubble and the StateRescueProcedure. These instantiations provide meaning that in a real DM scenario, disaster operation services can be implemented in different DM activities (evacuation and rescue). However, all these activities must follow their own specified procedure to perform the right DM action (refer ‘‘Follows’’ relationship in M2).

Other than its potential to offer solution for the disaster problems faced by the DM users, the DM Metamodel also allows them to create their own DM model based on the construct and rules provided by the metamodel. For example, in the preparedness against bushfires, the Department of Education and Early Childhood Development (DEECD) in Victoria, Australia has requested every school (government and non-government) and children’s service in the state to review their own emergency management plan [7]. Together with the task, they also need to address any priority maintenance works for that plan. This is an example of a situation where the DM Metamodel could help many DM users (school) through transforming new DM solution model specific to their own problem requirements. The process is possible as a repository built using the metamodel has stored various previous bushfire solution samples created by previous DM users. ## 3.2.2. Case study 2: nuclear meltdown (technological disaster)

For the second case study, we used a scenario of the nuclear meltdown disaster that recently occurred in Fukushima, Japan. The technological disaster happened as a consequence of two other devastating disasters, the 8.9 magnitude earthquake and the massive 10-meter tsunami that struck Japan in March 2011. The effects from the two natural catastrophes caused the failing of the nuclear cooling system in four Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plants owned by TEPCO (The Tokyo Electric Power Company) [18]. To prove the independence of our metamodel from any specific disaster model, we present the corresponding conformance of the Nuclear Disaster Model (M1-level) and its conformance to the DM Metamodel. ## 4. Conclusion and discussion

In this paper, we present the implementation of a model-driven approach through a metamodeling technique. The artifact output of the metamodeling is the DM Metamodel. It aims to provide a generic representational layer to provide a unified view of common concepts and actions applied in various disasters. A consensus developed from existing DM models and the literature is the basis of the knowledge encapsulated in the concepts that constitute the metamodel. In this way, the metamodel provides a set of generic concepts to guide DM knowledge reuse while not necessarily providing all the required details demanded by every specific disaster at hand. Some details are hidden behind the general concept we use, and we leave them to each individual user to extend it based on the specific disaster problem and the specific features of their organizational resources.

A metamodel that has the capability to identify the DM concepts can also specify the DM modeling language. It describes DM concepts and the way they are arranged, related and constrained. To achieve realization of these DM concepts as used in the metamodel, we demonstrated the conformance of various DM models to the metamodel. The DM Conformance Framework that underpins this is presented. Through the framework, we illustrated how the DM Metamodel that is positioned at the M2- level of the MOF Metamodeling Framework could be conformed to be the new DM Model at the M1-level. The results from the DM Model can then describe the User Model positioned at M0-level. The User Model is a target model of this approach that can describe the real scenarios of disaster management. In this paper, the 2011 Christchurch Earthquake disaster in New Zealand and the recent 2011 Nuclear Meltdown in the aftermath of the tsunami in Japan were chosen as two illustrative case studies. We believe that we demonstrated through model-driven disaster management that the complexities of this domain can be structurally modeled. From the language that is described by the DM Metamodel, users of the DM can practice and understand their domain more effectively.

Generally, the number of concepts from the metamodel that are reused depends on the context of the disaster and to what extent the DM expertise available needs to be complemented or supplemented. Even when it is partially reused, the details of the concepts or actions described by the metamodel may still need to be refined by its users. The metamodel as such serves as a guide for knowledge sharing, enabling the formulation of DM approaches as new situations arise.

In summary, a DM Metamodel offers these advantages: (a) facilitating global communication among different disaster emergency users, as the metamodel has generalized all the concepts that must exist in the DM domain, (b) providing guidelines for creating comprehensive DM models (e.g., Flood Emergency Response Model or Tsunami Risk Reduction Model), (c) enabling users to create new customized DM models based on choosing and combining a set of component concepts based on their disaster problems, (d) simplifying the instruction for new created solution models among DM users because a set of syntax and semantic rules of the domain is provided and (e) highlighting the scope for improvement in DM practice through the validation of metamodels compared with other DM models.
