# The dynamics of IT boundary objects, information infrastructures, and organisational identities: the introduction of 3D modelling technologies into the architecture, engineering, and construction industry

Uri Gal<sup>1</sup>, Kalle Lyytinen<sup>2,</sup><sub>w</sub> and Youngjin Yoo<sup>3,</sup><sub>w</sub>

## Abstract

In recent years, more companies engage in collaborative cross-organisational practices to achieve their business objectives. To cooperate effectively across boundaries requires organisations to overcome the tension between their distinct backgrounds and the need to create shared understandings with their partners for collaboration. This requires the creation of shared artefacts such as boundary objects. Whereas the past work on boundary objects has highlighted their role as translation devices, we examine them in relation to the information infrastructures within which they are embedded, and the identities of the organisations that use them. We propose a model that outlines the relationships among the three concepts and illustrate its dynamics by presenting two case studies that describe the introduction of three-dimensional modelling technologies into the architecture, engineering, and construction industry. Based on the case studies we suggest that boundary objects, in addition to facilitating cross-organisational communication, also help to form organisational identities. We further suggest the occurrence of a process whereby changes in boundary objects enable changes in information infrastructures and identities in one organisation. These changes, in turn, create the conditions for change in bordering organisations through shared boundary objects and boundary practices.

## Introduction

In recent years, more organisations engage in collaborative distributed practices that span across multiple locations, heterogeneous perspectives, and organisational contexts. Companies from multiple industries cultivate their relationships with their customers, suppliers, and other business partners to leverage each other’s distinct knowledge and integrate their resources. The composition of these networks is often fluid and fluctuates depending on the needs of the involved organisations. Such reconfigurable networks are typically supported by a myriad of information technologies (IT) that facilitate information sharing across heterogeneous contexts.

To cooperate effectively across boundaries and realise the potential benefits from these networks, organisations must overcome the tension between their distinct backgrounds and the need to create common understandings and a shared inter-organisational context with their partners (Turner et al., 2006). Some scholars have suggested that this requires the creation of shared artefacts such as boundary objects – conceptual or physical artefacts that are shared by multiple organisations and that help bridge cognitive and practical differences between them to facilitate common understandings (Star & Griesemer, 1989).

The past work on boundary objects has highlighted their role as translation devices that enable collaboration and knowledge sharing across diverse organisations (Henderson, 1991; Carlile, 2002). We expand this view by examining the dynamics of interacting organisations operating within heterogeneous networks and the ways these dynamics shape and are shaped by the use of IT as boundary objects. To this end, we analyse IT-based boundary objects in the context of the information infrastructures in which they are embedded and the identities of the organisations that use them. We define information infrastructure as a system of standardised practices that emerge in relation to a particular set of IT deployed within organisational boundaries. An organisational identity is defined as a symbolic representation of the organisation that is commonly and continually negotiated among organisational members. Examining boundary objects in this context is particularly relevant, given the recent proliferation of reconfigurable collaborative networks such as e-markets and supply chains. In these networks, organisations have to adjust to changing relationships, expectations, and demands. Therefore, organisational practices, patterns of interactions, self-perceptions, and use of technology are likely to be continually negotiated in a process that involves multiple relationships and that is mediated by boundary objects.

We propose a model that outlines the relationships among boundary objects, organisational identities, and information infrastructures, and the way they interact during a process of organisational change. We illustrate the dynamics of the model by analysing an IT-generated change process in the architecture engineering and construction (AEC) industry wherein two-dimensional (2D) modelling technologies – computer-aided design (CAD) tools and paper drawings – which have traditionally served as boundary objects, were replaced with 3D technologies. Additionally, we examine the implications of these changes for the information infrastructure and identity of one organisation in the industry.

The model we develop addresses the following aspects of IT-enabled organisational change processes: (1) the way boundary objects (in this case, IT-based boundary objects) shape and are shaped by organisational practices and patterns of communication; (2) the way IT-based boundary objects shape and are shaped by the identities of the organisations that use them; and (3) the implications of changes in IT-based boundary objects for organisational identities, practices, and interactions.

In what follows, we first introduce the theoretical constructs that inform our study. Next, we present our model, and describe the research setting, methodology, and results. This is followed by a discussion of the findings and their implications. We conclude by identifying this study’s limitations and suggesting directions for future research.

## Theoretical basis

## Information infrastructures

The term infrastructure refers to any substructure or underlying system. It denotes the ‘basic physical and organisational structures (e.g., buildings, roads, power supplies) needed for the operation of a society or enterprise’ (Oxford dictionary). Thus, an infrastructure signifies ‘something upon which something else runs or operates’ (Star & Ruhleder, 1996, p. 112). In the field of information systems, information infrastructures have received considerable attention in the context of largescale enterprise systems (Ciborra, 2000), networks, and databases (Bowker, 2005). Such infrastructures are conceived as large conglomerations of technological components and human skills that combine to serve the corporate needs of an organisation (Broadbent et al., 1999). This concept emphasises the standardisation of systems and platforms and focuses on the material features of technologies such as connectivity and interoperability (Duncan, 1995).

Alternatively, some researchers have argued that information infrastructures extend beyond materiality and human skills to encompass social, organisational, and moral elements (Star & Ruhleder, 1996). On the one hand, the development of information infrastructures involves multiple narratives and groups struggling to shape the standards and classification systems embedded in the infrastructure to reflect their values and interests (Star, 1999). On the other hand, information infrastructures in-use, by virtue of the standards that are embedded in them, have an impact on the nature, scope, and granularity of information available to their users (Bowker, 2005). As a consequence, infrastructures influence the way users communicate with one another and perceive their environments. Thus, a crucial point emerges: operating infrastructures involve the develop ment of standardisation and classification systems. However, such standards stretch beyond technological platforms to include users’ communicative behaviours and practices (Bowker, 2005).

Accordingly, we conceptualise information infrastructure as a system of standardised practices and modes of communication that emerge in relation to a particular set of IT artefacts within organisational boundaries. Such practices are acquired when actors are inducted into a community and undergo a process of socialisation whereby they internalise local knowledge, practices, and language (Berger & Luckmann, 1967). Over time, such artefacts and associated organisational practices become taken for granted, at which point they recede into the background and become part of the infrastructure (Star & Ruhleder, 1996).

Information infrastructures are contextual because they are meaningful only in relation to an organised system of artefacts and standards. Thus, what seems taken for granted for members of one community may acquire different meanings to members of other communities. However, despite their contextuality, information infrastructures are not restricted to the confines of a single community. Although they function in relation to communal standards, no community or organisation exists in isolation. Multiple organisations co-exist, interact, and do not have clear definitive boundaries (Star & Ruhleder, 1996). Hence, infrastructures always overlap to create interfacing areas. These areas depend on reciprocal recognition and evolution of practices, discourses, and artefacts across interacting organisations. Therefore, an important aspect in the constitution of information infrastructures concerns organising and generating practices, meanings, and roles that form and articulate organisational identities at the interfacing areas.

## Organisational identities

Organisational identity is typically understood to be organisational members’ collective understandings of the features that are presumed to be essential, distinctive, and relatively permanent about the organisation (Albert & Whetten, 1985). Central to most accounts is the recognition that identity is rooted in a deep cultural level of the organisation (Gioia et al., 2000). That is, identity resides in interpretive schemes that organisational members collectively construct to provide meaning to their history, experience, and activities (Merali, 2002; Ravasi & Schultz, 2006). Through continuous interactions, members negotiate a shared symbolic representation of their organisation that renders the organisation’s actions, objectives, and existence meaningful.

We emphasise the relational, practical, and dynamic nature of organisational identity. Identity is constructed not only against a backdrop of organisational members’ histories and experiences, but also in the context of multiple practices that interconnect the organisation with its customers, competitors, and suppliers (Gioia et al., 2000; Merali, 2002). Identity emerges in a dynamic context where multiple organisations engage in mutual practice and is reflective of the circumstances in which such practices are situated. Thus, the mode and scope of the practices, as well as the nature of parties involved, play a salient role in identity construction (Lamb & Davidson, 2005).

Because an organisation’s identity is tied to the environment from which it materialises, it will vary with changes in the practices in relation to which it is shaped, and with the context for which it is expressed (Gioia et al., 2000). Since organisations simultaneously engage in multiple common practices with different organisations, organisational identity is not singular but multiple across intersecting practices and discourses, and not stable but constantly changing (Hall, 1996). Accordingly, identity is best understood as a ‘work-in-progress’ rather than a finished product (Gioia et al., 2000): organisational identity is an ongoing, practice-based, enactment that unfolds as organisations interact with each other. Therefore the articulation of identities takes place in the interfaces among organisations. We next suggest that interfaces are populated by boundary objects, which form a critical element in the process of identity formation.

## Boundary objects

Boundary objects are conceptual or physical artefacts that reside in the interfaces among organisations. On the one hand, they are flexible enough to contain varying meanings that arise from multiple organisations. On the other hand, they are robust enough to serve as a common reference point to members of multiple organisations when they engage in mutual practice (Star & Griesemer, 1989). Boundary objects are part of the information infrastructure; they are used by people when they engage in standardised practices and communications at the boundary. However, they spread across two or more infrastructures and are shared by members of multiple organisations.

The notion of boundary objects was introduced by Star and Griesemer in their study of the Museum of Vertebrate Zoology at the University of California, Berkeley (1989). It has since been applied in various contexts such as design teams (Henderson, 1991), product development (Carlile, 2002; Bechky, 2003), knowledge brokering activities (Pawlowski & Robey, 2004), change in accounting systems (Briers & Chua, 2001), development and monitoring of production systems (Garrety & Badham, 2000), IS implementation (Yakura, 2002; Horton & Wood-Harper, 2006), boundary spanning activities (Levina & Vaast, 2005), and IS development projects (Sasped & Salter, 2004; Gasson, 2006).

These studies have expanded our understanding of boundary objects in multiple ways. For example, Levina (2005) and Levina & Vaast (2005) proposed that the usefulness of an object in enabling cross-boundary collaboration is not inherent in the object’s properties. Rather, it is associated with the ways in which the object is used in ‘collective-reflection-in-action’, and with the extent to which it acquires a common identity across contexts. Wenger (2000) suggested that boundary objects can bridge different communities of practice to enhance inter-communal learning. Garrety & Badham (2000) distinguished between primary and secondary boundary objects depending on their criticality in supporting interactions.

Some authors demonstrated the variability of boundary objects and outlined patterns of their evolution. Carlile (2002, 2004) argued that knowledge sharing across different types of boundaries requires different types of boundary objects. He anticipated that boundary objects may change, but did not elaborate on the change process or on how boundary objects relate to identities and infrastructures. Subrahmanian et al. (2003) analysed changes in boundary objects following changes in design and manufacturing teams. They claimed that such changes disrupt common grounds among organisations but fell short of exploring the process by which boundary objects relate to information infrastructures and organisational identities.

In sum, although the types and use of boundary objects have been examined in multiple settings, only a few studies hint at the potential for change in boundary objects, but do not explore this process and its possible implications. Also, previous research has not looked at the social dynamics within interacting organisations, namely, the changing identities and infrastructures associated with dynamic boundary objects. In short, the relationship between information infrastructures, organisational identities and the creation, maintenance, and change of boundary objects has remained largely unexplored. To address this gap, we next outline a theoretical model that juxtaposes boundary objects, organisational identities, and information infrastructures, and captures their relationships.

## A Model of boundary objects, organisational identities, and information infrastructures

The proposed model complements earlier studies on boundary objects by conceptualising boundary objects, information infrastructures, and organisational identities as interrelated constructs whose nature and structuring are intricately interwoven (Figure 1). It further delineates the dynamics of their interrelationships as transformations occur in boundary objects that are shared by multiple organisations.

The model displays an ideal-type situation where two organisations engage in mutual practice that is mediated by one, or several, boundary objects. The practices and identity of each organisation are reciprocally shaped in an ongoing process that crosses organisational boundaries. In this process, organisation A’s identity is predicated not only on its members’ shared experiences and history, but also on the organisation’s interactions with organisation B, namely, on the boundary practices that interconnect the organisations (a similar argument can be made for organisation B’s identity). These practices involve the use of boundary objects that facilitate interorganisational collaboration and that carry different meanings and inform different standardised practices in each organisation. Therefore, the interactions, or rather the interface, between organisations A and B involves the engagement of the two organisations in mutual practice along with their common use of shared objects.<sup>1</sup> The interface, in turn, constitutes the context for the enactment of the two organisations’ identities.

For example, a contract is a boundary object that regulates inter-organisational activities, designates roles, and assigns responsibilities in an inter-organisational interaction. While it serves as a common reference point to both organisations and enables them to collaborate effectively, each organisation is likely to have a different interpretation of the contract, and rely on it to inform its own practices. Because the contract has a role in shaping boundary practices that are common to organisations A and B (e.g., coordination meetings, inter-organisational reporting mechanisms, channels for information sharing, etc.), it has an effect on the manner in which both organisations enact their identities during their interactions. The model further suggests that a change in boundary objects is likely to modify the conditions for the interactions of organisations A and B, and may therefore alter the conditions for structuring their identities. Thus, a change in the contract can reconfigure the interface between the two organisations, re-regulate their boundary practices, and enable the enactment of new identities.

None of the elements in the model outlined is new. Organisational identities, information infrastructures, and boundary objects has each been extensively researched. Furthermore, some of the relationships in the model have received some attention, although not in a way that connects all three constructs together (e.g., Star & Griesemer, 1989; Bowker & Star, 1999; Lamb & Davidson, 2005; Levina & Vaast, 2005). Thus, earlier studies have not systematically examined the relationships among boundary objects, information infrastructures, and organisational identities, or the implications of changes in boundary objects for the organisations that use them. Our model supplements these studies by suggesting that information infrastructures, boundary objects, and organisational identities constitute a dynamic system whose constituent elements cannot be independently examined. Furthermore, it provides a framework for examining the dynamic relationships among these elements and delineates the consequences of changes in boundary objects for the identities and infrastructures of the organisations that share them. To illustrate the dynamics of the model, we next present examples from our research on the AEC industry, which depict an ongoing transformation process in an IT-based boundary object, and the changes in organisational practices and identities associated with it.

## Empirical case: changes in modelling technologies in the AEC industry

## Research setting: the AEC industry and Hoffman construction company

The AEC industry is one of the largest in the world. In the U.S.A. alone, it encompasses tens of thousands of organisations such as architects, engineers, and contractors. These organisations’ main undertaking is the construction of civic structures. The AEC industry is highly distributed in that the successful completion of construction projects requires extensive cooperation among a diverse set of organisations. Such organisations may have different professional backgrounds and technical expertise, unique business practices, and distinctive ways of representing their work and themselves – in other words, they may have different information infrastructures and identities. Given this diversity, boundary objects are an important means for maintaining effective collaboration during construction projects.

A prominent boundary object that has been traditionally used in the AEC industry comes in the form of 2D representations such as CAD models and paper drawings. The capacity of CAD models and paper drawings to accommodate different views originating from various organisations qualifies them as boundary objects. While the models are shared by multiple organisations and used by them in common practice, different organisations typically have their own specified language and practices in light of which they understand and use the models. Modelling technologies are a central element in the coordination efforts during construction projects and play a critical role in structuring construction activities (Beck, 2001). As Henderson notes, they ‘constitute the basic component of communicationy [they] shape the structure of the work, who may participate in the work, and the final products of design engineering.’ (Henderson, 1991, p. 449).

2D modelling technologies have been extensively used in the AEC industry since the 1960s and established as the absolute consensus and norm. However, since the beginning of the 1990s, a growing number of organisations have started using 3D modelling tools.<sup>2</sup> Today, while working with 2D models is still considered the norm, the use of 3D tools is continually spreading. Given the importance of visual models, as boundary objects, to the organisation of construction projects, a move to using 3D models can have vast repercussions to the way construction projects are organised, and to the manner in which organisations interact and construct their identities.

In this study we focus on one general contracting company, Hoffman Construction Company (hereafter Hoffman). Widely regarded as a leading general contractor in the AEC industry, Hoffman has been operating for over 80 years and taken part in numerous construction projects that span multiple industries, such as healthcare, transportation, and hi-tech. It has a rich experience in using 2D technologies and, as of the late 1990s, it has used 3D technologies in its high-end construction projects. As a general contractor, Hoffman is responsible for ensuring effective collaboration among the various stakeholders during construction projects and for managing communications between subcontractors and the architectural team. Overall, Hoffman’s example presents a good revelatory case (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007) of an organisation that has experienced the transition from using 2D technologies to 3D technologies, and the accompanying social and organisational implications.

## Study design

Our research focused on the replacement of 2D modelling technologies by 3D technologies by various organisations in the AEC industry, and the way these technologies were used as a new boundary object during construction projects. We additionally observed associated changes in infrastructural practices and identities in the participating organisations from the viewpoint of one organisation, Hoffman.

In the presentation of the case studies, we focus on the association of changes in modelling technologies with information infrastructures and organisational identities. Therefore these concepts only apply to those Hoffman employees that actively interact with the technology. Thus, our claims for established practices and identities, or for changes in practices and identities, only pertain to those people who participate in construction projects and engage with modelling technologies while doing so. It is likely that whereas the practices and identity of the people who use the technology will change when 3D tools replace 2D tools, the practices and identity of other Hoffman employees (e.g., accountants, human resource managers) will remain stable. This is consistent with our previous theorising according to which an organisation may exhibit multiple concurrent identities to reflect the multiple practices and relationships in which its different units engage.

We utilise data from two case studies that focus on Hoffman. We chose to focus on Hoffman rather than on multiple reciprocal inter-organisational relationships because the latter option would have required data that captures the mutuality of practices and identity construction between at least two organisations over time which, unfortunately, we did not have. However, prior to the two case studies we present a baseline case that describes Hoffman’s typical work practices, interactions, and identity during construction projects in which conventional 2D technologies are used. These interactions do not characterise any specific project in which Hoffman has been involved. Rather, they are reflective of typical practices and collaboration patterns that have matured as the use of 2D technologies in the AEC industry has become institutionalised. We use the baseline case as a benchmark against which we evaluate Hoffman’s work practices, interactions, and identity as the company started using 3D modelling technologies in its construction projects. This is done by focusing on Hoffman’s interactions with other actors during two other projects in which it was involved and in which 3D technologies were used to varying degrees. These cases demonstrate the transitions the company has experienced when it started to incorporate 3D technologies to replace 2D technologies in its construction practice.

## Sampling

We focused on interactions between Hoffman and other actors during two construction projects in which the company was involved in addition to one baseline case. Given our interest in the relationships between changes in the use of modelling technologies (conceptualised as boundary objects) and the information infrastructures and identities of technology-using organisations, we sampled cases that accounted for this dynamism and reflected a significant variance in the use of modelling technologies. We followed a theoretical replication (Yin, 2003) in that in each case a different type, or combination of types, of IT-based boundary objects was prevalent. In the baseline case 2D modelling technologies were used, in the first actual case 3D technologies were used, and in the second actual case both 2D and 3D technologies were used. At the same time, Hoffman’s project team remained largely unchanged and the project manager was the same in all the cases. Thus, observed differences in information infrastructures and identities could not be accounted for by variance in the composition of employees involved in these projects.

## Data collection and analysis

The data for this paper were drawn from a larger body of data that were collected during a longitudinal research project that focused on changes in the AEC industry as induced by the use of 3D technologies (Boland et al., 2007). Out of 93 semi-structured interviews that were conducted with 52 employees from 16 organisations, we largely relied on 18 interviews with five Hoffman employees, an executive, two project managers, a detailer, and a surveyor (the rest of the data were used to provide background information about the AEC industry and its typical infrastructural practices). These interviews formed a critical component of our data and were the focus of our analysis. The largest number of interviews (six) was conducted with one of the project managers (later referred to as ‘project manager 2’), whose insights and descriptions were central to our construction of the case studies. His role at the company places him at a prominent position from which he oversees the company’s operations during construction projects as well as its interactions with other organisations. Therefore, he is located at the boundary between Hoffman and other organisations and plays a crucial role in shaping Hoffman’s ongoing enactment of its interactions and identity. We conducted the interviews through three site visits and additional phone interviews between December 2003 and February 2007. During the visits, data collection included observations of meetings and work on construction sites, review of construction documents and models, and office and on-site interviews. Interviews lasted between 45 min to 2 h and were all recorded and transcribed. Our main interest in collecting the data was to understand Hoffman’s construction practice and explore how it played out during its interactions with other organisations. We were particularly keen to understand how the conditions for the enactment of Hoffman’s practices, interactions, and identities were reshaped with the introduction of 3D technologies into their practice.

Interviews were analysed by relying on established methods for handling qualitative data (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 2003) and followed these procedures: The first stage involved preparing detailed write-ups for each interview. Based on these transcripts, we constructed profiles of three projects in which Hoffman was involved (a baseline case and two actual projects). Within each case we instantiated the three theoretical constructs that initially shaped our inquiry, namely, boundary objects, information infrastructures, and organisational identities. To establish construct validity, we matched interviewees’ descriptions with the theoretical characterisations of each construct. For example, if a technological artefact was referred to by an interviewee as an enabler of communication between diverse organisations, this artefact would be labelled as a boundary object. Similarly, an interviewee’s reference to an organisational practice that had remained stable over time and become part of the company’s routine would warrant labelling this practice as part of that company’s information infrastructure. To gauge the symbolic nature of identities, we relied on interviewees’ metaphorical characterisations of their company’s involvement in construction projects and of its practices and interactions.

Next, we looked for concrete examples of the relationships among those constructs, specifically, for changes in practices and identities that were associated with the use of the new 3D boundary object. Consistent with our practice-based theorising of organisational identities, we examined changes in Hoffman’s identity mainly as they were reflected in the company’s changing tasks, roles, and interactions during different construction projects. We thus relied on concrete evidence of changing practices to make inferences concerning changes in the company’s identity. Thereafter, we mapped the relationships among the constructs onto a more consistent unified model (see Figure 1) and formulated the process models that show the main configurations of the interactions between actors and boundary objects in each case.

## Findings

The cases depict in chronological order an ongoing path of transformations in Hoffman’s information infrastructure and identity as the company starts using 3D modelling technologies. The baseline case describes Hoffman’s infrastructural practices and identity prior to using 3D technologies. The first actual case demonstrates how Hoffman’s infrastructure and identity start changing when it takes part in a project where the use of 3D modelling technologies was mandated by the architect. The second actual case shows how Hoffman’s identity and infrastructure continue to evolve when the company partakes in a project where a combination of 2D and 3D technologies is used.

## The poker dealer: Hoffman in typical 2D construction projects

Standardised information infrastructure and use of 2D boundary objects The use of 2D models in the AEC industry is a standardised aspect of the relationships among architects, general contractors, and subcontractors. The infrastructural practice in a typical construction project starts with an architectural team working to create a set of 2D drawings, which are called contract documents. These documents convey the architect’s design intent and indicate the performance and quality requirements of the building. Many of the systems that must be included – mechanical, electrical, and structural – are shown schematically, but not in detail. The architects then deliver the documents to Hoffman, which distributes them to the subcontractors. The subcontractors submit back to Hoffman specific work plans and shop drawings. The latter are detailed depictions of the manufactured items that go in the building and the systems that tie into them. These drawings, and not the contract documents, eventually become the blue prints for construction. Hoffman has to ascertain that the shop drawings are in compliance with the contract requirements and industry standards and then forwards them to the architects for approval. When the drawings are approved, they are sent back to the subcontractors that can begin their work. Since the contract documents do not specify in detail the construction process, it remains unclear which components of the building need to get built first. In principle, each subcontractor wants to be the first to lay its equipment and product so that they serve as a standard for other subcontractors. However, this is done strategically as different subcontractors try to estimate their potential risks and benefits. Establishing coordination for construction is a highly spontaneous and emergent process, which Hoffman tries to regulate. Project manager 2 provided the following example:

If the electrical contractor has a 4 inch conduit that he wants to route down a corridor and the duct work contractor has a 24 inch duct that he wants to route down a ceiling, and both of them are bringing their contract documents and showing how the engineers have told them to do that, we have to direct traffic and come up with a hierarchy of who goes first and who goes where.

The recurrent use of 2D contract documents and shop drawings has standardised the form and channels for inter-organisational communication and reduced the need for organisations to interact directly (Boland et $a l . ,$ 2007). Because the contract documents are subsequently translated into shop drawings that are individually created by different professional organisations, each organisation ends up using separate models to support its respective construction activities. Consequently, each organisation operates in a partially isolated silo with little interaction with, or visibility into, the processes of other organisations. This generates a loosely coupled system of communication flows among heterogeneous organisations that, nonetheless, have to maintain high level of coordination. Therefore, Hoffman’s role consists primarily of making sure that inter-organisational collaboration takes place. A Hoffman detailer commented:

We have a fragmented collection of data points that originate from different subcontractors’ shop drawings that have to be assimilated into a task. It is our responsibility to make sure that this is done accurately and efficiently.

Accordingly, Hoffman’s infrastructural practice relies, to a large extent, on the use of 2D models as boundary objects to coordinate construction efforts and reconcile various and, at times, conflicting perspectives that participating organisations have. Project manager 2 explained:

We do a lot of interdisciplinary coordination. We have these things called shop drawings. Those are used primarily by theyengineers and the general contractors. What happens is that everybody wants to take a straight shot from point A to point B of what they need to put in, but we need to work together to come up with a scheme that makes the most sense for everyone. We end up doing this in a more organised way. Everyone involved brings their drawings and we roll up our sleeves and work through the nuancesy and then people leave with that collective information and go revise their own drawings.

Figure 2 illustrates the established pattern of relationships among different organisations in 2D-based projects. It reflects Hoffman’s central position in this network of relationships and the extensive use that is made of 2D models (i.e., contract documents and shop drawings) to coordinate construction activities. The arrows in the figure denote the transfer of information across organisations through 2D models. These models act as immutable mobiles (Latour, 1987) in the sense that the information inscribed in them remains unchanged as they are transferred across organisational boundaries. This way they are used by different organisations in an attempt to influence the sequence of, and their own role in, the construction process.

Established organisational identity As we premised above, Hoffman’s identity is predicated upon the interactions in which it is involved. In 2D-based projects, the nature of these interactions noticeably resembles those that characterise a poker game. For instance, each actor’s actions are calculated based on the anticipated actions of other actors in an effort to increase its benefits and minimise its losses. Furthermore, actors are wary of the information that they share with others. Each subcontractor is reluctant to share their shop drawings with other subcontractors as they may contain proprietary knowledge. Similarly, architects are reluctant to release fully detailed information in their contract documents due to liability concerns and to avoid contradicting their own modelling and measurements. Project manager 2 provided the following anecdote:

Architects are taught, the term is called ‘do not double dimension’, and they are taught never to give the same information more than once in a set of drawings to avoid potential conflicts: reduce the information content to reduce the probability of contradictions.

In a system in which actors cautiously share information with each other, Hoffman is the only party that has access to everybody else’s information: it is the only one to receive all the architect’s contract documents and all the subcontractors’ shop drawings. Nonetheless, Hoffman too, is strategic about sharing information with others. It has to make sure that it does not reveal any of the other actors’ proprietary knowledge, and that it does not unnecessarily burden different subcontractors with overwhelming amounts of information. Thus, Hoffman is situated at a central node in a system composed of itself, the architects, and the subcontractors; it is the only party to have access to everybody else’s information and is in control of the information that everybody else receives. Much like a poker dealer, Hoffman sits at a table while each party lays out their cards for construction. Each player keeps their proprietary information to themselves and tries to negotiate the sequence of construction and the location of their components for their own benefit. Hoffman’s identity is reflected in the company’s practices and derives from its prominent position within this system of relationships. When prompted to describe Hoffman’s identity Project manager 2 provided this allegory:

Basically we’re more like dealers. Everybody is sitting around a table and we’re playing poker. We are the dealer. We are making the rules, we’re controlling the flow of the cards.

## The dispersed collaborator: using 3D modelling technologies with Gehry partners

Changing information infrastructure and use of 3D boundary objects In the summer of 1997 Hoffman became involved in the construction of the Experience Music Project (EMP) in Seattle, Washington, and its project team was managed by ‘project manager 2’. The building was designed by Frank Gehry who started using an advanced 3D modelling tool, CATIA, in his practice in the beginning of the 1990s (Boland et al., 2007). The EMP building was one of the first projects in which Gehry used CATIA models as contract documents, and insisted that all the main subcontractors adopt CATIA.

During the construction process, infrastructural industry practices were disrupted. The loosely coupled system associated with the established 2D construction process was reshaped into a more tightly coupled and interdependent system. In this system, new levels of knowledge sharing were reached among the participating organisations. One reason for these changes involved the overarching use of the CATIA technology. Since Gehry Partners imposed 3D CATIA models on Hoffman and on the prime subcontractors, they all had to learn how to use the software and integrate it into their practice:

‘CATIA was imposed on the team. In some ways it was an inefficient process because we had to inherit an entire learning curve at once. Structural engineer, mechanical engineer, prime subcontractors, Hoffman. A whole list of people had to learn how to use this tool.’ (Project manager 2)

3D CATIA models are extremely rich and accurate digital representations of the building that embed multiple layers of information that are meaningful to different professional organisations. Therefore, the same model can be used by most subcontractors to support their construction activities. Also, 3D models are transparent such that changes that are made in one portion of the model are visible to other users of the model. Additionally, any changes to the model are checked automatically and on the fly to verify that they do not clash with other elements in the model, thus enhancing inter-organisational coordination and interactivity.

Another reason for the emergence of a more tightly coupled system concerned the nature of the task. The high complexity of the building combined with the fact that most participants had not been previously involved in such a project or used CATIA, facilitated much tighter collaboration throughout the construction process. The traditional 2D contract language and associated practices of loose coupling did not provide actors with sufficient information to understand and negotiate their new roles and tasks (Boland et al., 2007). Therefore, they relied extensively on each other to inform one another about their designs and work during the construction process. Project manager 1 remarked:

During the construction of EMP we met much more frequently than we would on a typical [2D-based] project. Often there would be a problem on how to support a particular curved wing or something made up of warped metal sheets or something, and there was no definition of how to do that. And so we would meet and come up with ideas on how to do it. And those ideas would bounce from the structural engineer, to the architect, to us, and we would all have ideas and we would all contribute using the CATIA model.

In this newly formed interdependent system, Hoffman’s infrastructural practices and role markedly changed. Instead of managing a linear 2D-based communication process, as it would typically do, the company became more involved in the construction process and stepped out of its traditional organisational bounds. One example of this has to do with the use of CATIA by the subcontractors. During the construction of EMP, the 3D CATIA-generated contract documents that were created by the architectural team served as the central component around which construction efforts revolved. However, since most of the subcontractors were not familiar with CATIA, Hoffman hired CATIA operators and placed them in some of the major subcontractors. This was unusual as the general contractor now had its own employees located in the subcontractors whereas usually the parties remain separated.

Another way in which Hoffman became more involved in the construction process concerns the design of the 3D models. The initial models that were produced by Gehry Partners were often not sufficiently accurate to support construction. Therefore, Hoffman had to step in to further refine and develop these models, sometimes in collaboration with the subcontractors. Project manager 1 provided this example:

Gehry’s people didn’t have the surfaces of the building trimmed to fit the sidewalk in the CATIA model. Some of our first metal panels we had were hitting the ground. And we said, what’s going on here? Didn’t you have those trimmed? And they said, no, we had extended the surfaces too long and we figured you’d trim them off. We took the CATIA model and, with the metal fabricator, set the lines and gave them elevations and essentially built the surfaces, and made cut lines around the building where the sidewalks are.

Changing the architects’ contract documents is something general contractors normally do not do. Usually, due to liability issues, the general contractor prefers not to meddle with the contract documents and instead receives them from the architects and sends them to the subcontractors (similar to a poker dealer dealing cards). Thus, Hoffman’s practice during the construction of EMP was very unusual, as was illustrated by project manager 1:

The idea that we added information to the [3D] model is different than what a true general contractor does, because a true contractor doesn’t touch any of the product. They just want to shuffle the cards and deal them out and dictate the sequence. But we rolled up our sleeves and got involved because there was a lot of information that we wanted to make sure was done right. We are more involved in this process than we might be other wise.

Hoffman’s poker dealer identity drew on the distinct and central position that the company had in 2D-based project settings, and on the relationships with other organisations that were associated with this position. On EMP, Hoffman’s interactions substantially changed as the company became more involved in the construction process and as its boundaries with subcontractors blurred. These transformations were reflected in the metaphor that project manager 2 used to describe the company:

## The Air-traffic controller/knowledge broker: the Seattle library project

## Further changes in information infrastructure and use of 2D and 3D boundary objects

After the EMP project, Hoffman got involved in the construction of the Seattle Public Library in Seattle, Washington, which was completed in the winter of 2003. As in the previous case, the company’s project team was managed by ‘project manager 2’. To a considerable degree, Hoffman’s experiences during the construction of EMP played a part in shaping its practice and interactions during the construction of the library. The library building was structurally very complex and the architect, Rem Koolhaas, like Frank Gehry, used 3D modelling to design it. However, unlike Gehry, due to liability concerns, Koolhaas refused to share the 3D models with Hoffman or with the subcontractors. He was only willing to release a limited number of models, and even then it was done on a ‘use at your own risk’ basis. Given the enhanced collaboration that they experienced while previously working in a 3D environment, some of the people at Hoffman did not perceive this decision favourably. A surveyor at the company commented:

The architects hated giving us their 3D models because they knew they would keep changing stuff. If architects could get educated to feel more comfortable with their models and download them to the people that they work with, everybody’s life would be easier. Gehry is very much an exception that way.

Because of Koolhaas’ decision, all official communication between the architects and other participants in the project was 2D-based. However, large portions of the building were made of glass that was designed in highly complex shapes. This required accurate modelling and measurement, as was pointed out by a surveyor at Hoffman:

The library was a very complicated project. We had a lot of sloping slabs and weird angles, and no forgiveness – very high tolerances because of the glass and the complex geometries. There were warped surfaces, like sushi mats, where in some areas all the lines are straight but in others they are curved – a surveyor’s nightmare.

Having previously worked in a 3D environment on the EMP project, experienced the enhanced collaboration that it enabled, and in the absence of initiative from the architect, Hoffman decided to create its own 3D models. The aim of this initiative was to accommodate the complexity of the building, allow for accurate designs, and improve collaboration among subcontractors. However, this 3D environment was only used by Hoffman and the subcontractors. For the most part, the architects were using separate 3D models and relied on 2D models to communicate with the rest of the construction crew.

In this situation, Hoffman’s practices and role changed again. The company took some lessons it had learned from EMP and implemented them with its subcontractors. Compared to EMP, Hoffman was more proactive in creating a tightly coupled system to enhance collaboration. For instance, Hoffman made a strategic choice to recruit only 3D-savvy subcontractors. Also, it set up a 3D AutoCAD platform that served as a central translation device that mediated all communication between itself and the subcontractors and among the subcontractors. Project manager 2 explained:

On EMP, all the models came from Gehry, he was guiding everything, the structural engineers, everyone was onboard there. On the library project, the creation of the 3D models did not involve the architect. It was initiated by us and pushed by some of the subcontractors that collaborated with us.

The use of 3D models enabled Hoffman to create a collaborative project management context for subcontractors. Furthermore, to enhance communication with and among the subcontractors, Hoffman centralised surveying activities. Usually, the general contractor does some surveying to provide the subcontractors with basic information to produce their final measures. Each subcontractor applies this information to generate their own distinctive $< x , y , z >$ grids for the parts they place in the building. But on a complex project like the Seattle Library, there is a lot more detail to the layout, a large number of potential interactions between components, and therefore basic surveying practices become insufficient. Accordingly, Hoffman engaged in extensive surveying and distributed the information to the subcontractors helping them to set the layout to their installed components. Importantly, Hoffman used a centralised surveying system wherein all subcontractors shared the same $ { 3 \mathrm { D } } < x ,  { \gamma } , z >$ measurement space. This positioned Hoffman as a central provider of information that all the subcontractors had to rely on to accomplish their work. A surveyor at the company remarked:

The amount of surveying work we did on the library project was extraordinary. Typically the subcontractors rely on us to some extent for this information, but also have their own people who do surveying. Here we were the only ones feeding this information to them because without it they would virtually be lost.

The bold arrow denotes that the 3D environment was generated by Hoffman and the dashed arrows signify the surveying information that was provided by Hoffman to the subcontractors. The figure again reflects the centrality of the 3D AutoCad environment to the coordination of construction activities between Hoffman and the subcontractors.

Additional changes in organisational identity By playing a proactive role in the construction process, Hoffman brought more knowledge to bear than it did on its previous projects. These changes in the company’s practices challenged some aspects of its identity, as was reflected in the metaphor provided by project manager 2 to describe the company. Discussing the 3D AutoCAD environment that Hoffman set up and the surveying information that it provided to the subcontractors, he explained:

We become more like air-traffic controllers or knowledge brokers. We are still dealing with traffic, but we’re using this radar tied to a computer and a radio to be more effective. We are putting radios in all the cars and being able to call the guy coming down the street and say hey you need to change lanes before you get to the centre section.

## Discussion

Using a baseline case provides a benchmark on the basis of which changes in Hoffman’s information infrastructure, identity, and use of boundary objects can be explored (see Table 1). Prior to working with Gehry Partners, Hoffman’s poker dealer identity was rooted in the company’s use of 2D models and inter-organisational practices whose nature resembled that of a poker game. The use of 3D tools as boundary objects that was imposed by Gehry Partners and the associated changes in Hoffman’s infrastructural boundary practices during the construction of EMP, reconfigured the interface between Hoffman and its adjacent organisations, and enabled the enactment of its dispersed collaborator identity. On the Seattle Library project, Hoffman played a more proactive role than it previously did: the company provided surveying information to the subcontractors and created the shared AutoCAD platform. These newly formed relationships led to further changes in Hoffman’s identity.

The cases illustrate the validity of the model we outlined and of the relationships it depicted. First, with regard to boundary objects and organisational practices, the cases demonstrate the tight connection between the objects that are shared by multiple organisations and the practices that mediate their interactions. In each of the cases one would be hard pressed to describe the interface among multiple organisations without taking into account both the boundary practices and the objects that constitute it. For example, understanding the poker game environment requires more than analysing the features of 2D CAD and paper models and examining the linearity inter-organisational practices separately. It is the interlacing of 2D models and linear coordination, which makes up the interface that is shared by organisations. On the one hand, the immutable nature of 2D models and their restricted capacity to hold complex information limits the ability of organisations to be aware of the activities of other organisations. This, in turn, supports sequential interactions that are mediated by a ‘governing hand’, or rather, a ‘dealer’. On the other hand, such sequential interactions that are characterised by interdependency, mutual suspicion, and strategic sharing of information, encourage the use of technologies that limit inter-organisational visibility and enable organisations to keep private their proprietary knowledge.

Second, with regard to boundary objects and organisational identities, the cases demonstrate the importance of the technologies organisations use to communicate with each other and the mutual practices through which such communications unfold in the development of organisational symbolic representations. In each of the case studies, Hoffman’s identity was intimately intertwined with the nature of the company’s relationships with other organisations, which, in turn, were associated with the use of particular IT-based boundary objects. For example, Hoffman’s poker dealer identity was based on the nature of inter-organisational relations that resembled a poker game. However, as noted above, these relations were associated with the use of 2D CAD and paper models as boundary objects.

Third, concerning the consequences of changes in boundary objects, the cases illustrate that a change in technologies that are shared by multiple organisations is likely to be associated with changes in these organisations’ boundary practices, and therefore with modified conditions for enacting their identities. This was evident when Hoffman’s poker dealer identity, which was based on the use of 2D models and engagement in sequential construction processes, was going through transformations when the company started working on EMP. The use of a new technological boundary object in conjunction with the involvement in different boundary practices altered the interface between Hoffman and its neighbouring organisations and enabled the company’s dispersed collaborator identity to emerge.

In addition to illustrating the relationships depicted in the model, the cases highlight that organisational changes associated with dynamic boundary objects can cross organisational boundaries and have industry-wide repercussions. The introduction of 3D technologies by Gehry Partners and their use to facilitate inter-organisational communication instigated a cascade of changes in multiple AEC actors (Boland et al., 2007). Gehry Partners’ implementation of 3D tools disrupted the traditional 2Dbased pattern of inter-organisational relationships by enabling increased cross-organisational transparency and encouraging new interactions among architects, contractors, and subcontractors. These new interactions resembled tightly coupled systems, in which actors are more interdependent as their tasks affect each other continuously and directly (Boland et al., 2007).

Given that organisations in the AEC industry have mostly used the same technologies and relied on the same institutionalised construction practice since the 1960s, the significance of these changes should not be underestimated. Gehry Partners’ ability to challenge established industry structures therefore brings to the fore issues of power. Indeed, power relations are an inseparable part of the changes in the AEC industry as reflected through Hoffman’s story. Prior to the introduction of 3D tools. conventions for interaction and knowledge-sharing across organisations were heavily regulated through an elaborate web of industry standards. Official industry rule-books as well as informal norms specified and streamlined industry activities that have been accepted as the ‘normal’ way of doing things. The industry-wide acceptance of these standards has contributed to their reification and to the emergence of a stable and predictable inter-organisational environment. In this environment, tolerance for deviations from the standard way of operating is low and likely to be met with sanctions.

Against this backdrop, Gehry Partners’ ability to impose the use of 3D tools and implement a radically different construction process on EMP is notable. The use of 3D tools was backed by a significant use of power on the company’s part. Gehry’s reputation as one of the world’s leading architects had invested him with significant symbolic power within the field of the AEC industry (Bourdieu, 1991). The company leveraged this power to enforce its own agenda on the other participants during the EMP project.

During the construction of the Seattle library power issues were also at play. Koolhaas’ decision to use 2D models as an exclusive communication channel with the rest of the construction crew and withhold 3D information effectively established an obligatory passage point which reflected his efforts to enrol other actors into his actor-network (Callon & Law, 1989). Other actors had to comply with Koolhaas’ demand and convert their 3D models to 2D models when communicating with the architects thereby changing their pattern of activities and communication.

These examples highlight that the dynamics of boundary objects, infrastructures, and identities do not flow effortlessly. A change in boundary objects does not necessarily and immediately imply further changes in infrastructures and identities. The interdependence of these constructs is grounded in real-life situations that are often characterised by conflicts among different interests, struggle for power and prestige, and normalised practices.

The cases also indicate that organisational learning is involved in the relationships among boundary objects, infrastructures, and identities. For example, Hoffman’s use of 3D technologies during EMP opened the company’s eyes to new and more efficient construction practices. During the construction of the Seattle library, the company tried to implement some principles from its previous engagement: the decision to recruit 3D-savvy subcontractors, the initiative to develop its own 3D environment, and the use of centralised surveying were all attempts to create a similar socio-technical environment to the one that characterised EMP. Therefore, Hoffman’s infrastructure, identity, and use of 3D boundary objects during the construction of the Seattle library were grounded in the company’s effort to realise its recently acquired knowledge.

Concerning the generalisability of the proposed model, we believe that its applicability extends beyond the AEC context. The model can be applied to other interorganisational contexts that exhibit characteristics similar to those of the AEC industry. In such contexts, knowledge, resources, and expertise are distributed across multiple heterogeneous organisations that have to collaborate by means of shared artefacts. Some examples of such contexts are system development and outsourcing projects, product development projects, and IT consulting projects. Applying the proposed model in these situations may produce interesting insights concerning the processes whereby multiple organisations enact their practices and identities through the use of mutual artefacts and engagement in shared practices.

## Future research

Our findings and discussion suggest several paths for future research. First, to methodically pursuit our theoretical interests, future studies should broaden the scope of research both in terms of the period of time covered and the number of actors examined. Doing so would contribute to formulating a more valid theoretical model. Second, future research should allow for triangulation. Using multiple research methods would improve construct validity and reliability and offer a means to more richly illustrate the formation of organisational practices and identities. Third, modelling and representational technologies are just one IT tool that can be employed to mediate inter-organisational communication. There is a need to examine the role of other mediating devices, in different contexts, to assess the soundness of the model proposed here. Fourth, boundary objects are part of a system whose elements are connected in reciprocal relationships. When change occurs in one of the elements it will carry over to the others, spanning multiple organisations. In an increasingly inter-connected world, studies that follow the trajectory of such inter-organisational change processes, examine their patterns, and the conditions that may facilitate or inhabit them can be particularly relevant. Fifth, this work focused on one organisation and the changes it has gone through. Given the reciprocal nature of boundary objects, future work could look at joint inter-organisational influences and dynamics as organisations try to mutually form their identities and practices while using new boundary objects. Finally, efforts by organisational groups to retain their identities can play a significant role in shaping their adoption of new technologies. Future studies could illustrate how this process unfolds, and identify conditions that generate different patterns of this process.

## Implications

First, our study illustrates that whereas boundary objects play a significant role in cross-organisational interactions, they also form an essential resource for the symbolic structuring of organisations. This is due to the important role they play in the formation of organisational identities. Thus, adoption of new boundarycrossing IT may not only improve the efficiency of inter-organisational collaboration, but also facilitate changes in the identity and practices of the adopting organisations. Therefore, organisations that implement inter-organisational technologies need to be mindful about the consequences of such technologies, not only for their relationships with other actors, but also for their own identity and practices.

Second, the choice of boundary objects to mediate between organisations can be used to purposefully influence the shaping of practices and identities of adjacent organisations. Organisations can position specific types of objects at their boundaries with different actors to influence their pattern of relationships with these actors (i.e., their boundary practices) and consequently, these actors’ institutionalised practices and identities. For example, the decision to implement a new supply chain management system by a manufacturing firm may be intended not only to improve the efficiency of its interactions with its suppliers, but also to purposefully alter the suppliers’ institutionalised practices and identities.

Third, the use of IT artefacts goes beyond sheer instrumental considerations of efficiency or effectiveness. IT artefacts should be understood not only as physical objects that can enter an organisation and change it in any number of ways. IT artefacts also need to be construed as elements around which organisations develop institutional practices and interactions, which provide the context for the enactment of their identity. Therefore they have a significant symbolic importance. When dealing with resistance to IT-based change, managers need to be aware that organisational members’ reluctance to use new technologies may have to do not only with the necessity of having to change work routines or acquire new expertise, but also with the difficulties of having to change their established identities.

Finally, organisational change associated with new IT artefacts can be fruitfully understood in the context of reciprocal and dynamic inter-organisational relationships that are constituted through shared boundary objects and boundary practices. Some organisational change literature has suggested ways to improve the change process and its organisational consequences (Hsiao & Ormerod, 1998; Ward & Elvin, 1999; Labianca et al., 2000). Process-oriented studies have examined the actual process of organisational change (e.g., Van De Ven & Poole, 1995). Information systems researchers have examined the ensuing contradictory impacts of IT artefacts (e.g., Robey & Boudreau, 1999). What is striking in the extant literature is the scant attention paid to the fact that organisations are nested within dynamic and reciprocal social systems that are mediated and supported by boundary objects. Understanding organisational change in this context is particularly important given that more organisations are connected through a myriad of IT artefacts that serve as boundary objects. Explicitly incorporating such dynamic and reciprocal inter-organisational contexts provides a useful extension to existing theories of organisational change.

Attempts to initiate IT-enabled organisational changes are likely to instigate further changes in neighbouring organisations through mutually shared boundary objects, which could have unexpected consequences. Therefore, managers of change initiatives should take into consideration the broader organisational ecology that may be affected as a result of the initial change. Furthermore, mitigating resistance to change within the implementing organisation may be insufficient when a change in technology is likely to instigate a cascade of changes in neighbouring organisations, and when those organisations are not ready to embrace such changes. In such cases, an organisation’s efforts to minimise internal resistance are likely to be futile as the source of resistance may lie beyond that organisation’s reach.
