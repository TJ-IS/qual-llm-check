# Visualizing an information technology project: The role of powerpoint presentations over time

Elaine K. Yakura ⁎

Michigan State University, SLIR, East Lansing, MI 48824, United States

a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 15 March 2012 Received in revised form 21 July 2013 Accepted 26 July 2013 Available online 7 September 2013

Keywords: Visual representations over time Powerpoint presentations IT project visualization Visual media Conscription Inscription

## a b s t r a c t

Using longitudinal <sup>fi</sup>eld data, this paper examines the changing role of visual powerpoint media over the course of a large, multi-year information systems project in a regional U.S. bank. Existing research has generally focused on visual representation of relatively static objects in medical, scienti<sup>fi</sup>c and engineering contexts, but projects are an inherently dynamic collection of activities—distributed over time and space—that progress through a beginning, middle and end. This analysis of the visual presentation material used in an IT project highlights the abstract nature of the referent (the project). The dominant presentation technology in use in this project was the powerpoint presentation; this paper analyzes the changes in the presentations over time, and inscription and conscription at different stages of the project.

## 1. Introduction

The role of visual media in organizing continues to attract scholarly attention (Lilley, Lightfoot, & Amaral, 2004; Styhre, 2010, 2011). Perhaps because of its roots in the social studies of science (e.g., Latour & Woolgar, 1979), current theories have tended to focus on examples like medical imaging (Anderson, 1992), engineering drawings (Bucciarelli, 1988; Henderson, 1991, 1999), and other representations of things. While focusing on visualization of concrete objects serves the current interest in materiality (Leonardi, Nardi, & Kallinikos, 2012), it limits our inquiry in two important ways. First, the emphasis on visualizing things leads us to overlook images of more dynamic and abstract phenomena such as projects or processes (Sapsed & Salter, 2004; Yakura, 2002). Second, while visual media are theorized as part of the process of organizing (Styhre, 2010), existing research has been limited to relatively static, cross-sectional examples In particular, the use of visual media over time for inscription (Latour, 1990) and conscription (Henderson, 1991; Karsten, Lyytinen, Hurskainen, & Koskelainen, 2001) has seldom been examined.

In this paper, I extend existing theory by examining the use of powerpoint<sup>1</sup> over the course of a large information technology (IT) project, called the Bank Integration Project (BIP). I focus on powerpoint because it was the dominant visual technology in use during the project. The goal of BIP was to integrate computer systems and operations of eight banks into one. The project took place over three years, across four states, and affected millions of individual and institutional account holders. My data allow me to consider the practice of making the project visible over the entire lifecycle of the project. A project of this magnitude is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to describe, but with the help of powerpoint, it is easy to visualize.

The image in Fig. 1 represents the project: an interconnected web of activities that unfolded over a period of years. Unlike visuals with concrete referents, such as engineering blueprints (Henderson, 1991) or maps (Latour, 1990), powerpoint elements like the one in Fig. 1 have sometimes been labeled “eye candy” or “gratuitous graphics” (Levy, Zacks, Tversky, & Sciano, 1996): pure decoration, with no function except perhaps to distract or entertain viewers. While this criticism makes sense from a strict information processing/decision making perspective (Tractinsky & Meyer, 1999), my research suggests that such visual elements are far from gratuitous in large projects like BIP. The research question here is: How do visual media in information systems projects change over the course of a project?

The analysis starts from the observation that before a project gets under way, there is literally nothing to see except for the visual media itself. Engineering drawings (Henderson, 1991) or medical records (Anderson, 1992), while heavily stylized (Latour, 1990), usually refer to concrete things that can be independently observed. In contrast, the visual in Fig. 1 refers to a collection of interconnected, contingent activities that may or may not ever occur. Even when BIP was under way, it was distributed across dozens of locations and constantly undergoing revision. Powerpoint presentations played an essential role in making BIP into something that could be planned, discussed, and, for lack of a better word, visualized. The ethnographic study of BIP provides an opportunity to extend our understanding of the role of powerpoint as a visual media for organizing in several ways.

First, it draws attention to the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of powerpoint as a representational device within a project management context. Images like Fig. 1 are essential because abstract objects like BIP require abstract visual representations. For tangible objects, tangible representations such as prototypes or models are possible and often desirable (Carlile, 2002). For abstract objects, such as BIP, abstract representations are not only desirable but also necessary (Van Leeuwen & Jewitt, 2001).

Second, the analysis helps unravel the dual role of visuals as inscription devices (Latour, 1990) and conscription devices (Henderson, 1991; Karsten et al., 2001). Like any visual media, powerpoint can be used to display existing inscriptions or display new ones. But powerpoint stands apart from other visual media as a technology for conscription, particularly when used to represent projects like BIP. Unlike other visual media, powerpoint is most often used as part of a staged, ritualized performance (Biehl-Missal, 2011; Duarte, 2010; Gallo, 2010). The images in a powerpoint presentation generally do not speak for themselves. Furthermore, because they inscribe features of the organization, these media can include representations of members of the audience. For example, the names of departments and individuals may appear on organizational charts and task lists, and their presence (or absence) can be extremely salient for people who see the images. Thus, such images are deeply enmeshed in the social context of their use.

Finally, I argue that there are predictable differences over time in the relationship between visuals and their representations. In particular, the visuals used to represent BIP started as abstractions and became more concrete over time, in tandem with the project. This progression results from the distinctive properties of projects. Projects “evolve over time” (Strauss, 1988, p. 163), so visuals of projects tend to have a “short shelf life” (Sapsed & Salter, 2004, p. 1529). Unlike images of things (e.g., blueprints or maps), which may have archival value that persists for many years, the visual media of organizing are intended to become quickly obsolete. Through longitudinal <sup>fi</sup>eldwork, I was able to document this progression in the powerpoint visuals used to represent the project, which has not been the focus of prior research.

The paper begins by considering where powerpoint <sup>fi</sup>ts in the research literature on visual media in work organizations. Different research literatures on visuals are expanding as rapidly as visual media, but there is not yet a standard theoretical framework for the analysis of visual media in organizations (Styhre, 2010, p. 19–20). Because I am particularly concerned with the relationship between image and referent, I have adapted an analytical framework from the social studies of science (Pauwels, 2006) to analyze the visual data from BIP. After presenting and analyzing this data, implications of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings for research and practice are discussed.

## 2. Powerpoint as visual media in organizations

Visuals occupy a privileged position in human life. Neurologically, vision is our most highly developed sense (Hoffman, 1998). The phrase “seeing is believing” is more than just an aphorism; it re<sup>fl</sup>ects our deep-seated belief that visual evidence is the best evidence (Tufte, 2006). Visuals have more immediate impact and last longer in our memory: “The fact that pictures are generally better remembered than words has been known for a long time (Kirkpatrick, 1894)” (Stenberg, 2006, p. 813). Culturally, aesthetically and economically, visuals have enormous impact (Barry, 1997).

Thus it comes as no surprise that many diverse scholarly traditions have focused on visuals (Elkins, 2003; Mitchell, 2011; Spencer, 2011), from perspectives that vary from aesthetics to function: <sup>fi</sup>ne arts (Arnheim, 1954; Ruskin, 1973; Stafford, 1999), media and cultural studies (Barker, 2008; Bolter & Grusin, 1999; Horst & Miller, 2012; Mitchell, 2005), anthropology and sociology (Banks, 2007; Dicks, Soyinka, & Coffey, 2006; Harper, 2012; Pink, 2013), information science, decision making, and information visualization Kim, Hahn, & Hahn, 2000; Meyer, Shinar, & Leiser, 1997; Rosemann & Vessey, 2008; Zhang, 2012) and communications (Barnes, 2009; Barry, 1997). Each of these scholarly traditions brings a different set of disciplinary lenses and research methods to the analysis of visual media.

But powerpoint visuals do not readily <sup>fi</sup>t into any traditional category, and have escaped the attention of traditional visual scholarship in the past (Grady, 2006). This may be because a powerpoint “presentation” for project participants is both a set of visual images or “slides” and an action (someone “presenting” the slides to an audience). The presentation is more than just a viewing; there is typically a presenter who is reading from the slides, while the audience is reading into them. Plus, because powerpoint can display text as well as photos, drawings and movies, it cuts across traditional visual categories. As other media have become digitized. they have been incorporated into powerpoint, which transforms them from mere representations to performances.

When powerpoint has attracted scholarly attention, it has been subject to severe criticism. Critics warn of “death by powerpoint” (Eves & Davis, 2008), and decry its tendency to erode the quality of decision making (Simons, 2004; Tufte, 2003). Practitioners and researchers of teaching and learning have noted that powerpoint can be “a trap for bad teaching” (Klemm, 2007) and have a negative impact on student learning (Mahar, Yaylacicegi, & Janicki, 2009). In one of the U.S. literature reviews on powerpoint and higher education, Craig and Amernic (2006, pp. 147–148) have advocated a cautious approach in the absence of strong research <sup>fi</sup>ndings, and noted that powerpoint has become a “punching bag.” Management scholars such as Gabriel (2008) have referred to the “tyranny” of powerpoint.

Similarly, in the literature on decision making, powerpoint has generally been seen as an impediment. For example, Tractinsky and Meyer (1999, p. 398) noted that “there seems to be a gap between research-driven guidelines for information presentations and information presentation in practice, and this gap is widening with the introduction of new presentation technologies.” Tufte (1983, 1990, 1997) has been an articulate critic of powerpoint, focusing on the quality as well as the aesthetics of information displayed. In an oft-cited example of the negative effect of powerpoint on decision making, Tufte (2003) argued that powerpoint contributed to faulty decision making in the fatal launch of the Challenger space shuttle.

In spite of the criticism, the use of powerpoint has grown dramatically since its introduction in the mid-1980s (Gomes, 2007). It has become the dominant technology for “presenting” material in meetings in a very wide range of settings, from classrooms to boardrooms. Indeed, powerpoint was used in nearly every scheduled meeting that I observed in three years of following BIP. So, to understand the role of visual media in this project, we need to look at powerpoint.

## 2.1. Visuals and referents

In an overview of visual research in the social studies of science, Pauwels (2006) proposed a taxonomy of possible relationships between a visual representation and its referent. A “referent” is simply the object represented by the visual, e.g., the object to which the visual refers. In organizational research, this relationship has generally not been treated as problematic: visuals like engineering drawings are understood to represent concrete objects such as engines (Bucciarelli, 1988; Henderson, 1995).

Pauwels (2006) argues that the relationship of the visual to its referent can be quite complex because both the visual and the referent can vary in abstraction. First, consider the level of abstraction in the visual itself. The mixture of text and graphics ranges from highly abstract to highly concrete or “representational” as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Hamilton (1997). Photographs are considered the most purely representational kind of visual. In the theory of semiotics, similar distinctions can be traced back to the work of C. S. Peirce (1932), who noted the difference between “iconic” representation (which embody some likeness to the object being represented, as in the case of a photograph) and “symbolic” representations, which do not.

While this distinction is a helpful start, it fails to take into account that the referent—the object being represented—can also vary from concrete to abstract. This is critical to the argument here, because referents like “the BIP" are highly abstract. To account for this. Pauwels (2006, p. 4) offered a taxonomy of referents that range from “material existence” to “mental construction.” The material end of the continuum is anchored by examples from nature, technology and material culture, such as a transistor. The abstract end of the spectrum includes concepts such as “time” and “hierarchy.” The combination of these two dimensions creates a simple taxonomy, as shown in Fig. 2.

The nature of the referent has practical implications for how it can be represented. For referents with physical presence, such as engine parts, concrete or abstract visuals are both feasible and commonplace. For example, the technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations described by Karsten et al. (2001) consisted of 500 to 600 pages and included very abstract diagrams as well as very concrete and speci<sup>fi</sup>c requirements. With a concrete referent, the corresponding visual can be considered “representational” (Hamilton, 1997) or “iconic” (Peirce, 1932) and judged according to its accuracy and <sup>fi</sup>delity to the original object.

With an abstract referent, ideas like accuracy and <sup>fi</sup>delity do not make sense. At best, visuals can be used to signify an abstract referent, but literal representation is impossible (Van Leeuwen & Jewitt, 2001). When concrete visuals are used to represent abstractions, they must operate through metaphor (e.g., using a clock to represent time). Thus, even when the visual is apparently concrete (e.g., a photograph of a clock), the relationship between that visual and the referent (the concept of time) is not like the relationship between the photograph and the clock itself. In all four quadrants in Fig. 2, every representation involves some degree of translation (Pauwels, 2006), but for abstract referents, translation is more pronounced.

## 2.2. Applying the framework to BIP

With a complex project like BIP, the relationship between visual media and the referent is not so obvious. Unlike a building (Luck, 2007), it was never possible to directly view the “Bank Integration Project.” At <sup>fi</sup>rst, it did not exist. Later, once the project was in full swing, it was distributed in time and space (Strauss, 1988). Further, the label tends to confuse the part with the whole. It was possible to see some of the activities, but never the overall BIP. These qualities are not unique to BIP; they are common aspects of many organizational phenomena, and of organizations themselves (Morgan, 1997; Weick, 1979).

Visuals of abstract referents, such as BIP, have received very little attention in the organizational literature. This is rather ironic, of course, because abstract referents are the primary subject of the discipline. Even in Morgan's (1997) Images of Organization, there are relatively few images of organizations. Morgan's book is more about metaphors of organization, and the title is itself metaphorical. To see actual images of organization, one needs to look at the visuals used in organizational settings, like the project described in the following sections.

## 3. Research method

BIP data were collected during a multi-year ethnographic investigation of a major systems integration project at a large regional bank based in the Southeastern United States (“the Bank”). The data span the entire project, which lasted approximately 30 months from its genesis until completion. The identity of the organization and all individuals has been disguised to preserve con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality, pursuant to agreement with the organizations involved.

## 3.1. Research access

I negotiated access to the Bank through the Chief Information Of<sup>fi</sup>cer (CIO) of the Bank, who reported to the CEO. The CIO led the unit in charge of information technology at the Bank, called the Information Technology Group (ITG). The CIO and most members of the ITG were interested in research and readily accepted my role as a <sup>fi</sup>eld researcher at the Bank. During this project, I was granted access to the activities of the ITG, as well as the ITG members. They provided access throughout all levels of the IT function. For the project described here, I was on site from 2 to 4 days per week, and I kept in contact via email and phone on days when I was not on site. I attended project meetings, and spoke with Bank employees and outside consultants at various levels of the organization.

## 3.2. Data collection

During the period I was on site, I took extensive <sup>fi</sup>eld notes (Sanjek, 1990) on a daily basis, which I typed up later the same day or week. Copies of materials produced and used during this project were collected, including powerpoint presentation materials from all meetings I attended, as well as key meetings I did not attend (e.g., when BIP was presented to the Board of Directors) but that were prepared by the ITG. Given the proprietary nature of the material, audio or video recording was not permitted at any time.

During my stay in the <sup>fi</sup>eld, I was intrigued by the visual materials that described the project. It is important to note that the research method was inductive; the study was not designed speci<sup>fi</sup>cally to analyze the role of powerpoint. As often happens in qualitative <sup>fi</sup>eldwork (Charmaz, 2006; Strauss, 1978), this analysis emerged over time. I did not plan to study powerpoint, but my ethnographic stance as a “professional stranger” (Agar, 1996) led me to consider the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of this visual media. As in many projects, the use of powerpoint was ubiquitous, and carefully printed and bound handouts were provided to meeting attendees. While there are many research methodologies for studving visual materials such as photographs (Banks, 1998; Chaplin, 1994; Harper, 2005, 2012), anthropological videos (Chaplin, 1994; Hall, 1983), multi-modal artifacts (Van Leeuwen & Jewitt, 2001) and web sites (Delin, Bateman, & Allen, 2002; Hine, 2001, 2013), there are no methods designed speci<sup>fi</sup>cally for studying powerpoint visual materials. The lack of empirical or theoretical research on powerpoint was a sharp contrast to its ubiquitous presence.

As a <sup>fi</sup>eldworker, there is no fool-proof method for collection of all powerpoint materials used at every meeting in a large project spanning multiple locations; for instance, for the sake of equity, it was considered important for all regional banks to “see” the same powerpoint presentation that introduced the project to the users and IT groups at each regional bank. So those materials were carefully reviewed for uniformity, with changes only to select particulars (e.g., different regional bank names and different presentation dates). However, other presentations were not scrutinized for uniformity, and individual presenters often wrote and revised their slides at will and immediately prior to audience presentation. Consequently, there was no way to ensure an accurate collection of all visuals presented, or even a representative sample. However, I did collect all powerpoint materials used in the preliminary ITG meetings, the formal “rollout” meetings, and the periodic (approximately monthly) project review meetings. Within the limits of this ad hoc sample, the visuals presented here were selected to be representative of each analytical theme and time period.

For clarity, it is useful to de<sup>fi</sup>ne terms more precisely. The term “presentation” refers to the event where slides were shown to an audience by a presenter (or team of presenters) who talked about the slides and answered questions. The term “slide” refers to each page of a presentation, which might be projected onto a screen and/or provided to the audience on paper. The term “element” refers to speci<sup>fi</sup>c graphical or textual elements on a slide. The company logo and the visual in Fig. 1 were examples of elements that appeared on multiple slides.

## 3.3. Data analysis

As noted above, there is no visual methodology focused on powerpoint materials, and scant research on the use of visual presentation materials in IT projects. Moreover, the limitations of the data collection (e.g., the lack of audio or video recordings of the presentations) did not allow a situated discourse analysis (Sarkkinen & Karsten, 2005). Since much of the activity involved merging organizations and their multiple IT systems, there is good reason to expect that the various stakeholders brought widely different perspectives to the project (and the visuals to the participants), as will be evident from the sections that follow. Thus, a pure content analysis, based on the image itself, as one might <sup>fi</sup>nd in visual studies (e.g., Van Leeuwen & Jewitt, 2001), would also not be adequate for the research objective.

Instead, this paper provides a description of the overall context of the IT project, as well as an analysis of the unique meeting contexts in which the visuals were presented. The length of the <sup>fi</sup>eld study provides a longitudinal sweep of the entire project, and consequently, allows consideration of how the visuals changed over time.

Unlike some qualitative research, where analytical content themes are derived inductively from the data, the themes used here are taken from existing literature on project management, which generally adopts a practitioner perspective. Three interdependent themes—scope, resources, and time—are deemed so integral to the success of projects by researchers and practitioners alike that they are often referred to as the “Iron Triangle” (Agarwal & Rathod, 2006). Since these are central themes in any project, they recur endlessly in the data. Fig. 3 provides an overview of the analysis, which covers these three themes over time.

• Scope. This category refers to the way that the project and its boundaries are conceptualized. For example, which banking functions or software programs are to be included? And which banks are to be included?

• Resources (cost and people). Resources refer not only to the cost of the project, but also to its people. One of the largest cost categories in any IT budget is related to people working on the project. This category was considered “make or break” for the project's success or failure, so there was substantial discussion about people and work assignments at every meeting.

• Time. The time that a project will take is considered critical for any implementation—and time is often a proxy for cost (e.g., the more time it takes, the more it costs). Presentations often included estimated deadlines, including milestones and other indicators of progress, such as Gantt charts (Yakura, 2002). Moreover, the deadlines in a project are crucial for performance and planning. For instance, when installing a new automated teller machine (ATM) interface, holiday weekends (when ATM use is especially high) are avoided.

The following sections provide examples of the powerpoint slides at each stage and for each of the three Iron Triangle themes. The analysis here is focused on the use and interpretation of the visuals embedded in the meeting context.

## 4. Visualizing the Bank Integration Project

The Bank, already one of the largest in the region, had chosen a strategy of rapid growth through acquisitions, which created signi<sup>fi</sup>cant pressure on its information systems. Each of the acquired institutions had its own set of systems, many of which were not integrated. To simplify, the system for certi<sup>fi</sup>cates of deposit (CDs) might not be connected to the system for home loans. As a result, the Bank could not produce an integrated statement for customers showing the balance of their home loan with the balance of their CDs. Similarly, the systems of an acquired bank did not yet connect to those of other banks. Further, many of the smaller systems had been developed by different vendors or institutions, resulting in a patchwork of different interfaces and controls.

Thus, the ITG had to plan and implement a project that would integrate all of these diverse systems. Moreover, this challenging project would be highly visible, in that a problem with a system—such as an ATM system—would affect many customers immediately. And, IT projects are notorious for taking longer than expected and costing much more than originally budgeted, if completed at all.

## 4.1. Early stage: formulating a vision

Recognizing that they faced a challenge that was beyond their experience, ITG searched for a consulting partner for BIP. The ITG formed a steering committee consisting of seven senior IT managers at the Bank. They decided to begin by requesting a smaller “mini” consulting project to identify the best criteria for evaluating the complicated alternatives for the larger IT implementation. This would allow ITG to begin to fashion their approach to the project, as well as gather information about the consulting <sup>fi</sup>rm, since a project this size would require far more personnel than the Bank could muster. As a high-level ITG manager, Carl, stated, “This preliminary project is really to get an external perspective, and get details on how other banks have successfully done this.” A different ITG manager, interjected, “And what others did wrong!”

Ultimately, four consulting <sup>fi</sup>rms decided to compete for this job, and individuals from each of these consulting <sup>fi</sup>rms had several conversations (either by telephone or face-to-face) with individual members of the ITG steering committee before preparing their proposals. One <sup>fi</sup>rm dropped out immediately before their formal presentation; in the end, three <sup>fi</sup>rms made formal presentations to the steering committee. The <sup>fi</sup>rms were each allocated 90-minute time slots for their presentations.

Only one of the <sup>fi</sup>rms bidding on the job had done projects that were even close in size to the one the Bank was contemplating. Thus, one consulting <sup>fi</sup>rm had what everyone considered to be the advantage of experience. Two senior consulting partners for this <sup>fi</sup>rm made the presentation, and were dubbed “the ferret and the silver fox” by one of the members of the ITG steering committee. The appellations stuck because they were so apt. The “silver fox"—silver haired and silver tongued—knew less about the technical details of the banking systems implementation. He was the senior partner, intended to make the committee “feel comfortable,” as one of the committee members phrased it. In contrast, the technology partner was very knowledgeable about technical details. He was called “the ferret” because he spoke in rapid, staccato bursts.

The senior partner began the presentation by putting up the title slide, explaining that his <sup>fi</sup>rm “had experience with similar projects in multi-state bank consolidation.” He named one of the Bank's board of directors, saying that he could give the steering committee a “personal reference.” Three slides from their presentation are shown in Fig. 4.

## 4.1.1. Scope

The scope slide, Fig. 4a, included square boxes for “Information,” “Technology,” and “Architectures,” and circles for “Strategic Business Direction" and “Strategic Technology Assessment." The horizontal sections inside the <sup>fi</sup>rst triangle represented hierarchy. These geometric visuals and arrows provide an example of Lynch's (1991, p.6) “pictures of nothing:” “[l]ike many diagrams in theory texts, the <sup>fi</sup>gure is an assemblage of words, geometric shapes, and vectors.”

This slide was the <sup>fi</sup>rst in the presentation to reference the IT project. At this slide, the technology partner said, “Let's talk a little about scope—what areas are we really talking about? Is credit card included, for instance?” Carl responded with, “It depends on which satellites we're talking about.” The technology partner asked, “Can we talk to their business…” and before he was able to <sup>fi</sup>nish his sentence, several of the committee members said, “No!” Carl continued, “We need to gain their consensus once we formulate the project direction.”

But we can discuss the scope of the projects more generally.” At this, all of the steering committee nodded, and the next 20 minutes of discussion involved identifying past large-scale projects at other banks, the public outcomes of these projects, and the problems that arose during the projects.

## 4.1.2. Resources

Fig. 4b shows a slide that emphasized the relationships between the participants, including the consulting <sup>fi</sup>rm and various parts of the Bank. It is intended to illustrate some of the links (similar to those found in an organization chart) with categories of participants. It is not clear what the links between the boxes represent, but the vertical arrangement seems to imply hierarchy (note that the “engagement partners” are on the top) and responsibility, as well as accountability. During this part of the presentation, the senior partner talked about the “partnership” between the consulting <sup>fi</sup>rm and the Bank, and the need for “being on the same page.”

## 4.1.3. Time

In the early stage, there were many timelines of the proposed work, of the type shown in Fig. 4c. In the context of this proposal, the consultants included the timeline to demonstrate a clear, reliable commitment to project completion. Note that the list of tasks under the category “Phase” in this timeline is very broad, and could apply to almost anything. The complex tasks that must be completed, including adding acquired banking systems, are not even mentioned in this list. Also, only months are included, and the span of much of the project is a single year. Even at this early stage, the participants in this room knew that the project would take at least several years.

The important thing to note from this exchange is that the speci<sup>fi</sup>c content of the slides was not especially important, and none of the steering committee members was concerned by their vagueness. At this stage in the project, creating a valid, detailed or accurate representation would have been impossible; after all, the purpose of this preliminary work was to assess the scope and architecture of the overall effort. The consultants needed a way to talk about the proposed activity and make it more concrete. But the speci<sup>fi</sup>city of the visuals they used did not matter in the outcome. All discussion, even that touching on past projects at other banks, was broad and brief. References to scope served as representations or “placeholders” for the detailed activities which would ultimately take place.

Once this consulting <sup>fi</sup>rm was selected, the preliminary project took about six weeks. One of the Bank's senior managers described the <sup>fi</sup>nal product as “crisp, clear” and that it “told them what to do.” The CIO presented the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to the Bank's executive management team, who approved the project. The CEO reportedly said, “if you give me a number I multiply it by 2 or 3,” at which the CIO quipped, “at least it's a single digit.” At this stage, nothing (aside from this preliminary project) had been completed. The BIP had been approved, but it was not clear what would be included or how it would be accomplished.

## 4.2. Middle stage: elaborating the project

The next stage of the project involved planning the implementation strategy at each acquired bank. The acquired banks, while quite large, were smaller than the head bank, and thus sometimes called “satellites.” Ultimately, the ITG determined that having a single software platform for all of the systems of a satellite would be best (as opposed to tweaking existing systems of the various banks and thereafter linking them together). They would begin with the largest bank, create a stable platform, and then add the satellites to the platform one by one. Again, instead of tweaking each system incrementally, they determined that a “big bang” would work best, where the move to the common platform would take place at a speci<sup>fi</sup>c point in time. This plan required gaining the commitment of the satellites. Once this took place, the implementation at the main bank could begin.

Members of the ITG referred to this stage as a “roll-out,” when in fact no new software had been written or acquired and there was nothing technical to “roll-out.” Rather, they were rolling out the vision.

In particular, the “roll-out” consisted of a set of senior managers going to each satellite to make presentations and discuss the goal and details of the project with several distinct stakeholder groups at each of the satellites. These satellite groups consisted of the senior managers, the technology managers (and non-managers), and the users (non-senior managers) in various affected departments (operations, teller), each of which had very different interests. Some of these groups had a great deal of technology expertise, and others had none at all. Regardless of the level of their expertise, there was much consternation among members of all the groups. In the weeks leading up to these presentations, as one senior ITG member put it, “the rumor mill is churning furiously,” with people speculating about what teams would be formed and who would be on them. Questions ranged from “What's my role?” to “Will I have a job?”

The slides in Fig. 5 were taken from what came to be known as the ITG “road show.” The same slides (with individual names and related details changed as necessary) were used in each of the satellite locations. Communicating the project plan was a challenging problem. As one middle manager puts it, “It's not a secret, but then again, you want it to be consistent,” given the rumors that were spreading. For the presenters, there were many phone calls and meetings involved in preparing their presentations. Even deciding who would be invited, and who would “go <sup>fi</sup>rst” was political and problematic, because “Once they announce, you get two days to put this [the presentation] together.” Thus, this stage of the implementation began an extensive effort to gain the “hearts and minds” of project participants.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst and largest of these presentations was held offsite so people from branches all over the state could attend. The CIO delivered this presentation, and began: “We need to understand the enormous undertaking, and get buy-in from this group to get to the next level. My goal in sharing this developing process is to ask you in the room—who represent 100s outside it—to stretch.” He went on to talk for 20 min about the scope and timing of the project, and then answered questions from the <sup>fl</sup>oor.

Once the slides became available for all to see, it was “open season” for job opportunities, as one ITG member described it. Phones started ringing from people all over the Bank (and even some from outside the Bank), calling to get favorable placement, jockeying for higher-level or more interesting assignments. Because the visuals signi<sup>fi</sup>ed opportunity, they mobilized a great deal of opportunism.

## 4.2.1. Scope

The “bulls-eye” in Fig. 1 offers one representation of project scope that was widely used. By including the four states in the visual, behind a target, Fig. 1 signi<sup>fi</sup>ed the uni<sup>fi</sup>cation and integration of this geographic region. As the project moved into this stage, the slides still included many boxes (Fig. 5a), but the boxes were being used to represent specific sets of software and functionality such as “Deposits" or “Mortgage,” rather than Fig. 4a's “Technology” or “Architectures.” The visuals had become more concrete and specific, but were still fairly abstract because each of the boxes in Fig. 5a could include several different software applications, from different vendors, implemented and operated at different banks across the region. So the box labeled “Deposits” did not represent a single application, but a collection of applications and activities, with some still to-be-determined.

## 4.2.2. Resources

As the project progressed, the visual representation of resources also became more concrete. Fig. 5b shows speci<sup>fi</sup>c people assigned to different aspects of the “Business Conversion Team.” Note that some roles on the team are not yet decided (“TBD”). This representation is far more concrete than the early stage visuals, but it still contained gaps or placeholders for people viewing the visual to <sup>fi</sup>ll in. Because this project created a large number of opportunities for new and different roles, jockeying for slots became an ongoing process. In a very practical way, the positions “to be decided” functioned like non-speci<sup>fi</sup>c job postings for eligible members of the organization to consider. This illustrates the way that visuals can include references to the project viewer. As the CIO said, “I'm not planning to move people across state lines, and we won't do a lot of deal-making under the table.” But it was clear from the presentation that there were many opportunities for individuals to “stretch,” as the CIO mentioned in his opening sentences.

## 4.2.3. Time

As the project moved forward, timelines were still used as visuals, as shown in Fig. 5c. While many of these deadlines were still prospective, and some of them ultimately slipped, the set of tasks has narrowed in focus to speci<sup>fi</sup>c groups of applications (e.g., “ATM”). Moreover, these timelines were more realistic in that they stretched over several years. Further, while everyone realized that these timelines were not (and could not be) entirely predictive, these estimates at least gave the audience some sense of the relative timing. As one of the satellite managers stated, “now we can work in 2- or 3-year chunks—we don't know how much longer it will go, but who knows that far in advance anyway?”

## 4.3. Late stage: conversions of satellites

By the end of the second year, the bulk of the systems at the main bank was completed. Smaller systems were sometimes delayed when people working on them were “sucked into the vortex” of the implementation of the larger system. But this did not delay the start of the conversion of the satellites. The Bank's senior IT managers were far more comfortable with this late stage of the project for two reasons. First, the largest conversion, that of the main bank, was mostly complete. Smaller systems (e.g., safety deposit box systems) had yet to be completed, but their scope was far smaller, and they were not “core” to the main business of the Bank. Second, having a stable and operational platform allowed them to replace satellite systems with ones that they knew would “play well” with systems at the main bank. During this phase, a group of mid-level IT project managers met weekly to discuss the status of all IT projects. These meetings always included powerpoint slides, but the number of slides had decreased dramatically. Early slide sets would include more than 40 or 50 slides, but these sets averaged 5 or 6.

There are two striking things about the content of the slides at this point. First, abstract graphical elements had almost disappeared. Some slides still had the BIP element from Fig. 1 as a small logo, but others did not. Otherwise, the slides at this point consisted mainly of bulleted lists and similar text. Also, the themes of the corners of the Iron Triangle blended together, as shown in Fig. 6b. Unlike the early stages, where entire slides were devoted to issues like scope, resources and schedule, in the later stages it is impossible to <sup>fi</sup>nd a slide where a single theme is represented by one slide. At one of these meetings, Carl presided, and described the details of one of the few projects that were discussed at that meeting. This particular systems project had been lagging behind; others were on schedule and were neither mentioned nor even listed on the slides, since these were presumed to be on track. Carl explained that the gap was due to the management team delaying the start of the project to wait for one of the managers to become available. The meeting was brief, informational, and there was little discussion by other participants during the meeting.

## 4.3.1. Scope and resources

In the later stage, the representation of the project scope has shifted to text and has become even more concrete. Geography and strategic vision have given way to lists of particular systems, as shown in Fig. 6a. By analogy, it has become more like a shopping list and it was possible to list speci<sup>fi</sup>c names of speci<sup>fi</sup>c systems (rather than categories of functionality).

## 4.3.2. Resources and time

As the project progressed into the later stages of implementation, it became less and less necessary to present or discuss resource allocation. In fact, since scope was basically settled (and was treated like a checklist), the other two sides of the Iron Triangle tended to blend. The timelines disappeared and were replaced with more speci<sup>fi</sup>c lists of tasks, as shown in Fig. 6b. Furthermore, there was a tendency to focus on exceptions and problem areas. The bulk of the work was proceeding smoothly. It was the exceptions— deviations from the plan, rather than the plan itself—that became the focus of attention in presentations and meetings. As a result, the vast majority of the effort disappeared from view.

## 5. Discussion

Although the BIP is just one project, it can help shed light on the role of visual media in information systems projects. The data clearly corroborate the claim that powerpoint presentations have become “ubiquitous wherever business people meet to communicate” (Nunberg, 2001, p. 213). Powerpoint has become nearly synonymous with presenting or even “talking” (Grady, 2006). The analysis here suggests that powerpoint is ideally suited to the problem of representing an abstract, constantly changing object, such as BIP. As the examples in Figs. 4, 5, and 6 demonstrate, the representation of scope, resources and time changed over the course of the project, from abstract to concrete. While abstract visuals were common in the early stages of the project, they all but disappeared in the later stages.

## 5.1. Representing abstractions

Some of our most in<sup>fl</sup>uential theories of representation have been inspired by the application of information technology to concrete operations, such as paper mills and oil re<sup>fi</sup>neries. For example, Zuboff's (1988) idea of “informating” is focused on representing speci<sup>fi</sup>c things, processes and events, so that their “natural” presence is displaced and abbreviated (see also Cooper, 1992).

In contrast, in the case presented here, the referent is a collection of coordinated activities. It is inherently abstract. For this purpose, abstract images are particularly useful, because of the need to simplify (or abstract) away from the minutia and the complexity of the project. While some understandably view this tendency towards simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation as a shortcoming and evidence of sloppy thinking, it also means that members of an audiencecom can look at a powerpoint presentation and derive meaning from it (or read meaning into it). Given the nature of BIP as a referent, one could argue that abstraction is actually necessary.

Powerpoint is a useful and convenient vehicle for conveying such abstractions. Early in the project (while it was still being visualized by the ITG), a few boxes and arrows were enough. Another convenient quality of powerpoint is that presentation materials can be readily copied, edited, and re-used in multiple settings. In BIP, this revision process occurred throughout the “road-shows” in the middle phase, since the presentation needed to be quickly tailored to the details of each of the satellite banks. Generic slides are more valuable because they are more mobile; they can be moved from context to context. The visuals used in the early stage of the project were almost entirely generic, with only minor references to the particular client or project. And, through the use of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c imagery (e.g., boxes and arrows), the visuals created the impression of an orderly, rational process where no such process existed. Unlike scientists and engineers, who use these visuals to represent objects or events that are believed to have an independent, objective reality, the consultants in the initial meetings used powerpoint to represent something that lacked these qualities completely. The abstract visuals provided an anchor for discussion; they provided <sup>fl</sup>exibility as well as placeholders for participants during early discussions. Indeed, it seems reasonable to hypothesize that for representing and discussing abstract phenomena like IT projects, bad powerpoint—however that might be de<sup>fi</sup>ned—is better than no powerpoint. Conventional concerns about clarity of thought and accuracy of information, while important in decision-making (Tufte, 2003), do not apply in the case of developing projects because clarity and accuracy are not possible in the early stages of a complex project.

## 5.2. Conscription and inscription over time

The analytical framework presented here helps to clarify the multiple roles that visuals play, from inscription to conscription. Visual media have obvious functions as inscription devices (Latour, 1990) because they can represent and record information. The usual function of inscription technology, such as paper, printing, computers, databases, and networks, is to create visuals that are both “immutable” and “mobile” (Latour, 1990). Immutability refers to the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty involved in changing a representation. Especially in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c work, visuals represent facts: one cannot simply re-arrange the planets in the solar system or propose a triple-helix for DNA. Mobility refers to the ease with which visuals can move across different settings. When the identical (immutable) representation becomes more widely available, it becomes more “factual”—more solid and reliable as a form of knowledge. This corresponds to our common sense understandings that “seeing is believing.” In the BIP, the repeated, widespread use of common images helped to reinforce and lend substance to an inherently ephemeral collection of activities.

Prior research has suggested that visual media can also serve as conscription devices, mobilizing the participation and support of diverse constituencies (Henderson, 1991; Karsten et al., 2001). Karsten et al. (2001) argued that conscription devices could both encourage and also limit participation, depending on the community. In the BIP, conscription was clearly visible early on, and also in the rush of job-changing activity precipitated by the road shows.

Research from social studies of science suggests that the there is a natural progression from inscription, when the representation is produced in the laboratory, to conscription, when the representation helps to recruit adherents to a particular scienti<sup>fi</sup>c belief (Fujimura, 1992). Henderson (1991, p.455) contrasted this scienti<sup>fi</sup>c “assembly line” with the more recursive engineering process, where drawings are created, but thereafter put back into the production process:

Engineering designs are built up through a cascade of representations and re-representations to construct a <sup>fi</sup>nal design… However, at this point the directionality turns, and the constructed design in which “things” were turned into paper must now be used to turn paper back into things.

With visuals of IT projects, the situation differs in that the visuals are far more abstract, such as in Fig. 4, where the visuals preceded the initiation of actual work on BIP. If anything, the visuals created the referent, not vice versa. This is distinct both from referents in laboratory research as well as engineering. In the lab, ideally, the physical or biological phenomena is reproducible and, in a sense, static. Scientists produce visuals to document and further stabilize their results (Latour, 1990), but the results come <sup>fi</sup>rst. In engineering, the recursive process also ends with a concrete object. The visuals shown here seem to follow a different trajectory. Rather than inscribing the results of experiments, slides from the earlier phases function more as conscription devices than inscription devices. In the later stages, when there are speci<sup>fi</sup>c facts to report, their function includes inscription, as well. Disentangling these roles (conscription vs. inscription) requires locating the image in the broader context of its production and use, as suggested by Pauwels (2006).

Managers and consultants produce visuals to create new projects, each of which is unique in some respects. Not only is each organizational context unique, but information technology itself is changing over time. Systems for storage, processing and communication become obsolete; application software gains new functionality; new vendors replace the old ones. And on top of that, within the lifecycle of every project, there is constant change (Strauss, 1988). For these reasons, immutability is neither feasible nor desirable.

The practical necessity of visualizing abstract referents that are in constant <sup>fl</sup>ux has implications for the kinds of visuals we see in the workplace. In the daily lives of IT managers and consultants, inscriptions are increasingly mobile, but they are not immutable. Quite the contrary: multimodal artifacts such as powerpoint are highly mutable. Due to their interpretive <sup>fl</sup>exibility, they are more like empty vessels waiting to be <sup>fi</sup>lled with meaning in each new context. In the BIP, each new satellite bank required a different, contextually speci<sup>fi</sup>c version of the presentation. And in each context, there is a different interpretation of the presentation, as the local audience reads in their own meanings.

The image in Fig. 5b illustrates another distinctive feature of project visuals. As the project progressed, the visuals of resources began to refer to speci<sup>fi</sup>c individuals. Visuals of projects have a unique status in organizations because, unlike visuals of things (e.g., new products), an image of a project often represents the observer of the image as part of the referent. Project visuals are often used at meetings of project participants. In many cases, the visuals refer directly to the participants and actions they have taken, or will be expected to take, as part of the project. Thus, project visuals can in<sup>fl</sup>uence and interact with the social worlds of participants (Dougherty, 1992; Kling, 1978) more directly than visual images that are centered on technology or concrete objects.

The inclusion of audience members in the visual highlights the relevance of traditional topics in the communications literature. While it is beyond the scope of the analysis here, future studies will want to consider issues such as impression management (Westrup, 1996), persuasion (Barnes, 2009) and media richness (Daft & Lengel, 1986). These topics gain relevance when one realizes that the visuals are often created by one group (with particular interests and communicative intentions) for consumption by another group. For example, in the middle phase of BIP, the ITG was creating visuals to in<sup>fl</sup>uence members from the satellites (among others).

While this paper focuses on inscription and conscription, the role of representation in the social world has also been theorized in terms of performativity (MacKenzie, 2006). For example, in <sup>fi</sup>nancial markets, economic models do not merely represent the behavior of the market, but are also a causal factor that shapes the behavior of the market. I have suggested that a “presentation” has at least two dual roles: visual representation and social performance. But what kind of performance is it? And what is being performed? In terms of MacKenzie's (2006) dichotomy, powerpoint is neither an engine (causing events) nor a camera (merely representing). Rather, in the context of a large systems implementation, powerpoint is more like a midwife (or an OB/GYN). In principle, an organization could give birth to a major new system without powerpoint presentations. But in practice, it almost never does.

## 5.3. Predictable progression over time

Longitudinal <sup>fi</sup>eldwork allowed another interesting insight to emerge: there are predictable differences over time in the relationship between the image and its referent. In particular, the visuals are more abstract at <sup>fi</sup>rst and become more concrete as the project progresses. These differences have to do with the nature of the referent. Early in the project (at what is sometimes called the “vision” stage), the visuals are basically <sup>fi</sup>ctional; at best, the actions that they describe are contingent and may occur in the future. As the project progresses, the visuals become more concrete; they refer to speci<sup>fi</sup>c combinations of people, tasks and time. Eventually, when most of the work is done, the visuals refer only to exceptions. The ongoing project (in other words, the primary referent of the whole effort) is no longer represented at all. Only the exceptions need be represented. The same pattern occurred in all three analytic themes: scope, resources, and time. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings are especially striking because the abstract visuals disappeared completely by the later stage.

I argue that this progression from abstract to concrete is a necessary part of coordinating the work on large, transformative projects like the one described here. At <sup>fi</sup>rst, conscription is required (Henderson, 1991, 1995). There is a need to “get people on board.” My <sup>fi</sup>eldwork shows that in the early stage, they formulate the vision, and then sell that vision across the organization. Contrary to normative theories that suggest that presentation materials should be accurate and complete (e.g., Tractinsky & Meyer, 1999; Tufte, 2003), my data suggest that in the early stages, the visuals must be vague (abstract) to be effective. In later stages, as the project becomes better de<sup>fi</sup>ned, there is a need for inscription: solidifying roles and responsibilities through the creation of “immutable mobiles” (Latour, 1990). At this stage, vague visuals would be regarded as a liability. People need to know what to do. Eventually, if the project goes well, the ideas get embedded into the processes and routines of the organization, such that visual media are needed more for training newcomers. This shift from conscription to inscription is due, at least in part, to the unique nature of the project as a referent.

## 5.4. Limitations and future research

In this paper, I have not analyzed the process through which visuals were created, nor have I considered how they may have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced project outcomes. I have not addressed the question of what kinds of visuals are more effective. Some of the presentations described here occurred in small conference rooms, with dialog and discussion among the participants. Others occurred in auditoriums, more like a traditional lecture, with questions from the audience broadcast over loudspeakers. In future studies, these are fruitful areas to investigate because, as visual media, powerpoint does more than simply represent things (like a photograph, a blueprint or a drawing).

For example, powerpoint is sometimes used to replace more traditional means of managing meetings, such as agendas and minutes. The slides can be distributed in advance, and can serve as a reminder prior the meeting. Used for note-taking, copies of slides can also serve as a record of what was discussed. These can be annotated to indicate decisions, action items, and so on, much as technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations (Karsten et al., 2001) or other engineering drawings (Henderson, 1991).

Furthermore, when used in a presentation, powerpoint is interactive. The act of moving from the <sup>fi</sup>rst to the last slide of a presentation—a linear progression of visuals—appears important in its own right. As the presenter moves to the next slide, it signi<sup>fi</sup>es that issues raised on the current slide have been discussed, and consensus has been reached among the participants to move to the next slide. Each slide serves as an anchor for meaning; as the presentation moves from slide to slide, the presentation has a progression and even a sense of progress or completion. All of this is differs considerably from a blueprint.

## 6. Conclusion

It has been over 20 years since Alan Meyer (1991) called for more attention to the visual aspects of organizations. If anything, the need is even greater now, since digital technology is making new kinds of visual media widely available. I focus on powerpoint, but it is hardly the only such technology that deserves attention. Any artifact that functions in a medium that has a privileged perceptual and cognitive status for humans deserves our attention. This research calls attention, once again, to the need for visual literacy in our research and in managerial practice. Base disciplines such as linguistics, sociology and anthropology are taking advantage of analyzing visuals to garner greater insights into social worlds, particularly work worlds (Dougherty, 1992; Goodwin, 1994; Kling, 1978). Refocusing attention on a small subset of the larger visual media economy is a small but important step toward understanding this vital aspect of organizational reality.
