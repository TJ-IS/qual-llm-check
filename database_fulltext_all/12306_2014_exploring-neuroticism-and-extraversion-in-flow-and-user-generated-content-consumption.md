## A B S T R A C T

Using flow theory as our foundation, we empirically tested the impact of personality traits on the flow state experience and three user-generated content (UGC) usage types. The findings indicate that extraversion is positively related to UGC usage for entertainment and negatively related to communication and information, whereas neuroticism is positively related to UGC usage for entertainment and communication. Only extraversion has an influence on flow, which is positive, and flow, in turn, has a positive influence on UGC usage for entertainment and communication. This study extends flow theory by exploring antecedents and outcomes of the flow state experience.

## 1. Introduction

The Internet has been changing the ways its users communicate, learn, and research. Lately, it has expanded beyond its origins as a reading and writing tool and has increasingly become a more interactive and recursive tool. The rise of Web 2.0 has led to an interesting new phenomenon: user-generated content, or UGC.<sup>3</sup> UGC is defined as content made publicly available over the Internet that reflects a creative effort and is created outside of professional routines and practices [101]. Some examples of UGC sites (i.e., Web 2.0 applications) include Wikipedia, YouTube, and Flickr, to name a few. Research points toward a variety of ways in which consumption is being transformed by the participatory web culture of Web 2.0. UGC is created by ordinary, everyday people who make their content available for anyone to use, often for no monetary gain. However, if UGC users are not driven by monetary motives, why do they engage in this type of peer production (i.e., mass collaboration)? Previous literature has explored potential motivations, such as knowledge sharing [47], information search [13], and social networking [3,93]. While these studies have shed light on the phenomenon, a detailed exploration of the impact of personal factors on UGC participation is still lacking.

For decades, information system (IS) researchers have recognized how important users’ personal factors are in predicting technology adoption and use [4,62,84]. Personal factors in previous IS research can be classified into two broad categories: relatively mutable factors and dispositional factors [69]. More mutable factors include individual attitudes and perceptions, and less mutable dispositional factors include general personality factors, cognitive style, and self-efficacy. To date, dispositional factors such as personality have been largely ignored in the IS context. There has also been a lack of targeted research on the role of dispositional factors in IS adoption and use [69]. Although the role of user perceptions, such as perceived ease of use and usefulness, continues to dominate models of technology acceptance, personal factors affecting IT use also need to be acknowledged as important variables [2]. We explore two key personality traits, extraversion and neuroticism, because in all the three- and five-factor models of personality, two of the consistently identified traits are extraversion and neuroticism [22,29–31,73,88,96,111]. Recently, researchers have begun to understand flow as a way of understanding the nature of online human-computer interaction [75,94]. Computer users may experience a ‘‘flow state’’ when they feel a sense of exhilaration and enjoyment by controlling their own actions and fate [16]. Flow is defined in the literature as "the state occurring during network navigation which is: (1) characterized by a seamless sequence of responses facilitated by machine interactivity, (2) intrinsically enjoyable, (3) accompanied by a loss of self-consciousness, and (4) self-reinforcing’’ [44,p. 23]. Flow has been linked with the expected use of technology [20,25], and research has found that the content of websites can be the most defining influence on users’ flow [71,97]. Creating a compelling website depends on many factors, including facilitating a state of flow [43], and is thus an important objective for sponsors of UGC websites. Furthermore, users enjoy UGC sites for different purposes, such as chatting, sharing and downloading music, and reading and/or writing blogs, to name a few. Researchers have grouped Internet usage into three main categories: information acquisition, entertainment, and communication [5,42,59,107]. Because each type of UGC usage may be differently associated with a particular level of the flow state experience, an exploration of the different patterns of association between the level of the flow state experience and the types of UGC usage (e.g., information, entertainment, or communication) is needed. This manuscript, then, explores the effect of personality on flow in computer interactions, and in turn, the impact of flow on behavior (i.e., UGC usage). Specifically, this paper will investigate a proposed theoretical model that reflects the following general research questions: ‘‘How is individual personality related to the state of flow in UGC usage?’’ and ‘‘What is the impact of experiencing the flow state on UGC usage?’

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. User-generated content

User-generated content (UGC) websites are online environments where users contribute and retrieve content (e.g., photos, text, and video) that is created by users for users. These sites allow users to access content and use it for their own purposes. UGC represents a new type of web behavior that is reshaping online communication, collaboration patterns, and the ways in which information is produced, distributed, and consumed [5]. Wikis (a form of UGC) are web pages that can be easily edited by anyone who is allowed access [24] and have been found to support information sharing and acquisition [79]. Social bookmarking sites (another form of UGC), such as delicious, allow users to browse items based on a variety of categorizations (e.g., most popular, shopping, technology, or politics). In fact, social bookmarking sites are often being used as intelligent search engines for information acquisition [68]. Despite some differences behind the reasons why individuals operate personal blogs, they generally like to express their opinions in the style of an online journal [80]. Allowing users to create and post content gives blogs social power, in that they are not just one-way publishing platforms but a web of interlinked conversations [51]. Users interact with someone’s blog by reading the content and communicating with others by posting their own comments. Researchers have begun to explore issues such as the value of UGC usage by the elderly [87], the role of flow and personality-oriented trust in using a communitydriven UGC knowledge site [52], and the relationship between personality and collaboration in UGC [63].

## 2.2. Personality traits

Personality traits are a stable set of characteristics and tendencies that determine people’s commonalities and differences in thoughts, feelings, and actions [65]. Eysenck et al. [33,p. 17] insisted that these ‘‘traits are essentially dispositional factors that regularly and persistently determine our conduct in many different types and situations.’’ The Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ) has been prominently used in personality research [42,91,70,23,95] and has shown consistent results across a variety of samples (e.g., professionals, college students, children) in a number of countries. Barrett and Eysenck [8] and Lynn and Martin [64] even used the inventory to compare the personality of individuals from 25 to 37 different countries, respectively. Therefore, considering that research has shown the ability of personality traits to predict important behavioral variables [110,9], we have adopted personality as a forerunner to cognitive attitude and behavior formation.

While there have been several models of personality proposed over the years (see [26] or [86] for a review), generally, Eysenck’s three-factor model [33] and the Big Five Model [35] have been used to classify and study personality traits. Eysenck et al. [33] included extraversion (E), neuroticism (N), and psychoticism (P), while the Big Five model included extraversion, neuroticism, agreeableness, conscientiousness, and openness to experience. Eysenck et al. [33] suggested that other factors, such as agreeableness, conscientiousness, and openness to experience, are either components of the other three (E, N, or P) or some combination of these components. Some researchers highlight that in all the three- and five-factor models, two of the consistently identified traits are extraversion and neuroticism [73,96]. For example, Zuckerman et al. [112] compared Eysenck’s Big Three, Costa and McCrae’s Big Five, and Zuckerman and Kuhlman’s Alternative Five in a factor-analytic study. The first factor was clearly extraversion, and the second was neuroticism, with representative scales from all three questionnaires highly loading on their respective factors. Extraversion (as opposed to introversion) relates to an individual’s ‘‘ability to engage the environment’’ [18,p. 403]. Extraverts seek new opportunities and excitement. Neuroticism (as opposed to stability) represents a lack of psychological adjustment and emotional stability, and it illustrates the degree to which an individual perceives the world as threatening, problematic, and distressing [18][18, p. 403]. Highly neurotic people tend to be fearful, sad, embarrassed, and distrustful, and have difficulty managing stress [69]. Nemanick and Munz [73] examined the relationship among the Five Factor Model of personality and affectivity. Their results confirmed that the primary traits of extraversion and neuroticism are at a more fundamental level of personality, and positive and negative affectivity exist as a mediator between those personality traits and mood states.

Although the two models offer somewhat different definitions, in practice the instruments proposed by the two theories seem to tap similar aspects of personality [96]. We are interested, however, in casting personality as an antecedent to behavior, not in defining personality itself. Previous research has established the utilization of personality dimensions to explore flow [17,102] and online communication experiences [11,57]. In addition, it is not uncommon for researchers to focus on specific dimensions of personality that are particularly salient to the research questions. For example, Vittersø [102] looked only at the openness to experience personality dimensions with regard to its relationship to affect through flow experiences; Ross et al. [85] found that different personality dimensions were associated with various aspects of Facebook use; and the personality dimensions of extraversion (talkative, sociable, and confidently appearing in public) and neuroticism (timid, anxious, sensitive, and tendency of feeling guilty) have been found to be primary determinants of consumer behavior on the Internet [53]. Therefore, consistent with previous research, we utilize the extraversion and neuroticism traits by adopting Esyenck’s EPQ as the parsimonious choice for comparing the effects of two personality dimensions on UGC (e.g. [27–32]). It is important to note that we are not exploring all personality traits that may have an influence on flow and UGC usage within this study but rather two key traits. As such, this research is a necessary first step in the exploration of the connections among personality, UGC, and flow. Table 1 provides a summary of the EPQ traits under study.

Research has just begun to explore the connection between personality and behavior in computer interactions (i.e., UGC usage). In a recent study, Amiel and Sargent [4] examined the relationships among personality type, Internet use, and usage motives for undergraduate students. They found that people with different personality types showed distinctive patterns of Internet use and usage motives. Hamburger and Ben-Artzi [42] examined the relationship between personality traits and Internet services. They demonstrated that extraversion and neuroticism showed different patterns of relationships with the factors of the Internet-Services Scale, which was classified into social services, information services, and leisure services. In their study, Hamburger and Ben-Artzi [42] illustrated that extraversion had a significantly positive influence on the use of leisure services, while neuroticism had a negative influence on information services. Landers and Lounsbury [59] also found that a person who is normally conscientious and neurotic is less likely to use the Internet for what he or she sees as unproductive activities, such as watching YouTube clips. Thus, previous research indicates that personality traits may influence individual attitudes and behaviors, but an examination of personality, UGC usage, and flow has yet to be undertaken.

## 2.2.1. Flow

The concept of flow, originally proposed by Csikszentmihalyi [19], has been extensively researched in the fields of psychology, education, and even music. Flow is often described as an optimal mental state or a feeling of mental immersion, or being ‘‘in the zone’’ [15]. In his original work, Csikszentmihalyi [19] described flow as what a painter feels when ‘‘a new thing, a living form, takes shape in front of the astonished creator,’’ and ‘‘the holistic experience that people feel when they act with total involvement’’ Csikszentmihalyi [19,p. 36]. Novak, Hoffman, and Yung [75] defined flow on the Internet as a cognitive state experienced during navigation, and researchers have proposed flow as an important construct for recognizing user behavior on the web [94,20,98].

While Csikszentmihalyi originally suggested six flow dimensions (i.e., concentration, perceived control, mergence of action and awareness, transformation of time, transcendence of self, and autotelic experience), other researchers have proposed various models of flow and technology acceptance (see [41] for a review). Guo and Poole [41] tested a complete structure of the flow model, based on its original formulation, in an online shopping context. However, the goodness of fit of the full model was marginal, and only a few flow factors showed a strong relationship with the flow construct. MIS researchers frequently use the more parsimonious model developed by Trevino and Webster [98], who define four dimensions of flow as follows:

Flow represents the extent to which (a) the individual perceives a sense of control over the interaction with the technology; (b) the individual perceives that his or her attention is focused on the interaction; (c) the individual’s curiosity is aroused during the interaction; and (d) the individual finds the interaction intrinsically interesting.

This study adopts the four factor flow model of Trevino and Webster (attention focus, control, curiosity, and intrinsic interest) because it is more parsimonious and has received consistent support in the IS literature [72,48,60,49].

When participating in an engaging activity, individuals may experience a high level of focus and concentration [60,38,78,83]. When individuals have focused attention, they concentrate their full attention on the activity. Without focus, individuals might not be fully engaged in the activity, preventing them from entering into the flow state [82]. As an aspect of flow, control has been examined within the context of web use, software design, email, and online shopping, among others [41,38,78,83]. In a computermediated environment, individuals have the ability to control their interaction with web sites. People are in charge, choosing to progress, explore, or exit the content of the interaction [98], often without conscious effort. Another component of flow is curiosity, that is, a heightened arousal of sensory and cognitive curiosity [1]. When curious, the individual tries to achieve technological competence while engaged in an action [106]. Users can interact with the web site not only to accomplish something but also for their own pleasure. Finally, intrinsic interest, or satisfaction, regardless of personal expectations of any extrinsic rewards or outcomes, can contribute to individuals entering a flow state [1,106]. If individuals enjoy the activity they are participating in, they are more likely to continue with the activity [1].

In summary, this study focuses on exploring how and in what situations, individuals reach a level of the flow state experience with UGC. We also explore the idea that the level of the flow state experience may vary among the different personality trait types. The next section develops a model of personality and flow in UGC usage as well as associated hypotheses to test the proposed relationships in the model.

## 3. The research model and hypotheses

This paper proposes a model of the relationships among individual personality and flow in UGC usage. This model combines the Eysenck et al. personality model [33] and the theory of flow in computer interactions [43,98,106,36] to predict UGC usage, as shown in Fig. 1. We postulate that two different personality trait types are related differently with the levels of flow and UGC usage behavior, and we further propose that the level of flow state experience has a positive relationship with UGC usage. ## 3.1. Personality traits in UGC usage

How people behave in a particular situation is determined, at least in part, by their personality [33], and personality traits have been linked to IS adoption and use [4,62,84,69]. According to Eysenck et al. [33], one of the major components of differences in behavior is sociability. Extraverts report taking part in social interactions, indicate a greater preference for stimulating, active, and unusual situations, and are less likely to avoid stressful situations [33]. Extraverts prefer face-to-face interactions and typically spend less time on the Internet [59], especially for online social activities such as chat rooms [42]. In other words, extraverts do not rely on the Internet for their social interactions [36], and they do not use the Internet for information acquisition, such as viewing mainstream news [4]. On the other hand, extraverts will use the Internet for leisure activities and as a tool to acquire things to share with others, such as music downloads [4]. This study, therefore, expects that those individuals scoring high on the extraversion personality trait will use the Internet for entertainment purposes rather than for communication or information acquisition. In accordance with these considerations, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1a. Extraversion will positively influence UGC usage for the purpose of entertainment.

Hypothesis 1b. Extraversion will negatively influence UGC usage for the purpose of communication.

Hypothesis 1c. Extraversion will negatively influence UGC usage for the purpose of information acquisition.

Neuroticism is characterized by a lack of psychological adjustment and stability, and it is composed of a number of sub-elements, including anxiousness, depression, guilt, low selfesteem, tension, shyness, irrationality, moodiness, and emotional displays [31]. In other words, neurotics can be characterized by their high levels of rigidity, anxiety, and emotional instability. Neurotic individuals have been found to spend extensive time on the Internet seeking a sense of belonging [4,42], and they may use the Internet as a medium through which to communicate with the world. Therefore, neuroticism is expected to be associated with Internet usage for social activities, such as chatting, e-mail, and other interpersonal communication activities. In a study of online activity including chat rooms, discussion boards and instant messaging, Swickert et al. [95] found that individuals who were high on Neuroticism reported the lowest levels of perceived social support. While examining the relationship between neuroticism and media use, Zillmann and Bryant [109] found that those who were in a bad mood—a characteristic of neuroticism—preferred entertainment programming to news programming to balance their mood. In addition, one’s feelings of loneliness have been found to be positively associated with Internet usage and, more specifically entertainment programming on the Internet [12].

A few studies have found that those scoring high on neuroticism have a negative association with information services on the Internet [42,95,99]. It is likely that neurotic individuals will attend less to UGC usage for the purpose of information seeking [91], as exposure to such content may result in an emotionally negative experience for such viewers. Taken together, these findings paint a picture of an individual high in neuroticism who uses UGC sites to make connections with others and to escape the tensions of everyday life. Therefore, we expect to find the following:

Hypothesis 2a. Neuroticism will positively influence UGC usage for the purpose of entertainment.

Hypothesis 2b. Neuroticism will positively influence UGC usage for the purpose of communication.

Hypothesis 2c. Neuroticism will negatively influence UGC usage for the purpose of information acquisition.

## 3.2. Personality traits in flow formation

Recall that flow is an optimal mental state, a feeling of mental immersion, or being ‘in the zone.’ Csikszentmihalyi [20,19] used the term autotelic personality to explain in part the differences in people’s flow experiences. The autotelic personality notion refers to people who generally do things for their own sake rather than to achieve some future external goal [20]. Sharafi et al. [90] investigated the relationships among the engagement mode, flow, and personality in using information technology, and they found that users with different personality orientations show different degrees of positive and negative modes of engagement and different levels of the flow experience. This finding raises the question: ‘‘Which personality types are predisposed to experience higher levels of the flow state experience?’’

Online media, such as UGC sites, allow participants to actively engage in a wide variety of behaviors, but to experience greater flow, a computer interaction should be intrinsically enjoyable [99] and absorbing so that the user has little focus on anything else [43]. Considering extraverts’ active, venturesome qualities, people high in extraversion are expected to become thoroughly involved in online media, and this active engagement may allow them to attain a high level of the flow state experience. Anxiety is known to be a prominent predictor of the level of an individual’s flow state experience. In computer interactions, individuals who score low on emotional stability (i.e., neurotic) may be more likely to suffer from anxiety, characterized as fear, frustration, and nervousness. That is, people who are at ease and relatively free from anxiety may have an easier time experiencing the flow state [36,104]. We expect that individuals with higher neuroticism may become frustrated and nervous when interacting with UGC and may find it more difficult to become totally absorbed (i.e., enter the flow state experience). Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 3a. Extraversion will positively influence the level of the flow state experience.

Hypothesis 3b. Neuroticism will negatively influence the level of the flow state experience.

## 3.3. Flow influences on UGC usage

Woszczynski [106] found that the level of the flow state experience is significantly associated with users’ playful behaviors in computer usage. Because people experience pleasure when they are in the flow state, they may be more likely to continue prolonged engagements with those activities that offer them the chance to enter this state [20,19,98]. Ghani and Deshpande [38] and Novak et al. [75] found a significant and positive relationship between flow and exploratory usage (i.e., how users experiment with and try out new tasks). Users exhibiting exploratory behaviors generally enjoy unfamiliar websites for the sake of variety, novelty, and curiosity [75]. Because hundreds of thousands of people across the globe participate in creating web content in the form of videos or web blogs, UGC websites are continuously renewed and recreated. Users are able to experience emerging perspectives encompassing new technologies, content, and communities. These emerging perspectives, in turn, may motivate users’ focused attention, control, curiosity, and intrinsic interests in promoting their own usage. We propose that UGC usage is similar to the exploratory behaviors proposed in previous studies and that the level of the flow state will similarly have a positive influence on UGC usage. Therefore, this study hypothesizes the following:

Hypothesis 4a. The level of the flow state experience positively influences UGC usage for the purpose of entertainment.

Hypothesis 4b. The level of the flow state experience positively influences UGC usage for the purpose of information acquisition.

Hypothesis 4c. The level of the flow state experience positively influences UGC usage for the purpose of communication.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Measures

We used the Eysenck et al. [33] personality questionnaire (EPQ) to measure personality (see Appendix A for scale items). Following the suggestion of Amiel and Sargent [4], we slightly altered the language of the shortened version of the EPQ and adapted it to an American sample. The EPQ consists of 12 measures for each personality type on a seven-point Likert-type scale, ranging from ‘‘strongly disagree’’ (1) to ‘‘strongly agree’’ (7). Consistent with Eysenck et al. [33], responses were grouped and added to form interval level scales that identified extraversion (E: a = 0.87) and neuroticism (N: a = 0.83). The Pearson correlation coefficient computed between the variables revealed a significant, but weak, correlation between extraversion and neuroticism (r = -0.97, p < 0.05).

The level of the flow state experience, which is a second-order construct, is composed of attention focus, control, curiosity, and intrinsic interest. The flow state is a psychological variable within the person that is not directly observable [105]. The flow state measures were adapted from Trevino and Webster [98] and Webster et al. [105], which in turn were based on the research of Csikzentmihalyi [19]. The questionnaire consisted of 12 items covering the four flow components. These items were also rated using the same seven-point Likert-type scale.

As previously mentioned, a few studies have focused on categorizing UGC types. Kolbitsch and Maurer [54] suggested different types of UGC based on content and distribution platforms. With regard to content, UGC can be identified as text, photos, music, or video. With regard to distribution platforms, UGC can be classified into blogs, Wikis, sites allowing feedback on written works, group-based aggregation, podcasting, and social network sites. Similarly, Anderson [5] and Zhang etal. [107] suggested the UGC types blogs, Wikis, social bookmarking, and multimedia sharing. Thus, most studies have viewed UGC types based on functional distribution platforms from the perspective of UGC service providers. However, the center of UGC is the users themselves, and the key to UGC is participation. We suggest the need to consider UGC types from the perspective of the users: for what purposes do individuals use UGC websites? Labrogere [58] mentioned that Web 2.0 uses the web as a true convergent platform of communication, information, and entertainment in a way that turns the user into a communication service provider.

Using this perspective, researchers have categorized the types of Internet usage as: information acquisition, entertainment, and communication [42,59]. As a dependent variable, we define UGC usage as ‘‘the degree to which users utilize UGC’’ [21], and we similarly classified UGC usage based on the purposes of entertainment, communication, or information-acquisition. To measure the UGC usage type, the respondents were asked to selfreport their UGC usage type.

## 4.2. Sample

This study used a survey targeting UGC users from the United States over the course of three months, from March to May 2008. The research model was tested with data collected via a survey administered at a state university in the Southeast region of the U.S. To enhance the response rate, all respondents received a monetary incentive: a \$10 gift card or an equivalent gift. The respondents were undergraduate students, graduate students, and faculty members. Because the concepts under study apply to the general population, this sample is appropriate for this research. Seven undergraduate and graduate students majoring in business were trained and hired as data collectors. The data collectors approached students and faculty on campus, and from the outset they explained the concept of UGC and asked whether respondents had experienced UGC. If the participant responded in the affirmative, the collectors asked him or her to list the UGC sites visited within the past three months to confirm their eligibility. Those who met this criterion (visited appropriate UGC web sites) were given a copy of the questionnaire. This data collection procedure is consistent with previous research in the area of online activity and flow (e.g., [15,60,56]). The respondents filled out the questionnaire and, when completed, returned them directly to the data collectors (see Appendix B for general questionnaire information).

Through this process, a total of 387 responses out of 500 surveys were received, netting a response rate of 77.4%. After the elimination of 15 responses, which had missing values, 372 replies remained for statistical analysis. Table 2 summarizes the demographic profile of the respondents. As Table 2 shows, more than half the respondents were female (52.69%), more than half (55.91%) were between 20 and 30 years of age, and a majority of the respondents (70.16%) had more than seven years of Internet experience. A large portion of the respondents reported an average of 1–3 h per day of Internet use (41.13%), followed by 3–5 h (29.57%).

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Construct validity and reliability of instruments

Prior to the hypothesis testing, the reliability and validity of the measurement instrument was tested. All coefficient alphas of the measurement items reported in Table 3 surpassed Nunnally’s [76] 0.70 criteria for reliability acceptability. To evaluate the construct validity, we ran a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). To examine an acceptable fit of the proposed measurement model, each of the constructs was evaluated by examining the statistical significance of each estimated loading, and the overall model fit indices were evaluated (see Table 3). Two personality items with weak contributions to standardized loading were dropped [6]. The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ fit statistic was 491.368 with 284 degrees of freedom $\left( p < 0 . 0 0 1 \right)$ The RMSEA was 0.044 less than the recommended 0.08 threshold [74] and the recommended 0.05 cutoff [67]. The CFI and GFI values were, respectively, 0.964 and 0.908, which is quite respectable. Overall, given the number of indicators, all the statistics support the measurement quality.

## 5.2. Hypothesis testing

The hypotheses were tested with a structural equation path model, using AMOS version 7.0. The model was assessed using a partially aggregated approach (see Fig. 2), which involves the aggregation of the indicators of each dimension of the overall construct whereby each separate underlying factor is retained. A composite variable is created from the items of each separate dimension of the construct and becomes a single indicator of a single factor model [7]. Partial aggregation offers the benefits of being able to provide greater substantive content for each variable within a smaller matrix, less distraction from accumulated errors, and thereby, greater reliability [10,61]. The hypothesized model was tested across the sample (N = 372). The resulting x<sup>2</sup>(161) = 317.244, p < 0.01; GFI = 0.921; AGFI = 0.897; CFI = 0.972; RMSEA = 0.051 suggests that the hypothesized model fits the data. In Table 5, we present the resulting standardized parameter estimates. Within the overall model, the estimates of the structural coefficients provide the basic tests of the proposed theory. Although all the proposed hypotheses corresponded with the expected signs, three (Hypotheses 2c, 3b, and 4c) of the eleven were rejected.

Within the model, the estimates of the structural coefficients provided the basic tests of the hypothesized relationships. With regard to the relationship between extraversion and UGC usage, the expected relationship between extraversion and use for entertainment (Hypothesis 1a) is supported by the positive path coefficient (b = 0.528), which is statistically significant at the p < 0.001 level. Additionally, the expected negative effects of extraversion on use for communication (Hypothesis 1b), as well as for information acquisition (Hypothesis 1c), are significant (b = -0.622, p < 0.001 in Hypothesis 1b and $\beta = - 0 . 4 5 1 , p < 0 . 0 1$ in Hypothesis 1c). These results show that extraverts tend to use the Internet for entertainment but not to communicate their opinions to others or to acquire information.

The positive relationship between neuroticism and use for entertainment is supported (b = 0.233, p < 0.001), confirming Hypothesis 2a. The positive relationship between neuroticism and use for communication is also significant (b = 0.396, $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , confirming Hypothesis 2b. However, the effect of neuroticism on use for information (Hypothesis 2c) is not supported. The hypothesis tests demonstrate that neurotics show a particular interest in engaging in both enjoyable (i.e., entertainment) and communal activities (i.e., communication) on the Internet. In terms of the effect of personality on the flow state, the association between extraversion and flow (Hypothesis 3a) alone is confirmed (b = 0.676, p < 0.001). Finally, the flow state has a significant impact on UGC use for entertainment (Hypothesis 4a, $\beta = 0 . 1 9 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and UGC use for communication (Hypothesis 4b, b = 0.298, p < 0.001). The results are graphically presented in Fig. 2, and the hypothesis results are summarized in Table 5.

The conceptual model, which is reflected in Fig. 1, implies that flow partially mediates the effects of personality on UGC usage. Although the model is based on prior research, it is conceivable that flow fully mediates the effects of personality on UGC usage. Therefore, in addition to assessing the general goodness of fit of the partial-mediation model in Fig. 1, the fit of the two models was compared. The only difference between the alternative model and the original model is that the former does not include the direct paths from personality to UGC usage. Fitting the full-mediation model to the sample data, the resulting x<sup>2</sup>(35) = 167.59, p < 0.001; GFI = 0.91; AGFI = 0.86; CFI = 0.80; RMSEA = 0.10 suggests that the full-mediation model does not fit the data as well as the partialmediation model.

## 6. Discussion and implications

The present study explored the role of personality (extraversion and neuroticism) in users’ behavior regarding UGC web sites. The results of this study help answer our initial research question: ‘‘How is individual personality related to the state of flow in UGC usage?’’ We found that users high in extraversion use UGC for entertainment but not for social services, such as communication. Consistent with the findings of both Amiel and Sargent [4] and Weaver [103], we found that users high in extraversion also do not use UGC to acquire information (e.g., mainstream news). One may suggest from this finding that the venturesome quality of a person high in extraversion might encourage the use of UGC for entertainment, but the lively, talkative qualities still have the extrovert seeking social interaction outside UGCs.

We found that individuals high in neuroticism used UGC for entertainment and communication. The positive relationship between neuroticism and the use of UGC web sites for entertainment may indicate that neurotics are more prone to use UGC for entertainment when they feel anxious, lonely, nervous, and/or bad. The results also suggest that neurotics may use UGC for communication to establish virtual friendships online, to relieve feelings of loneliness and depression, and to avoid, instead of attending to, stressful matters at hand. The online environment allows individuals the opportunity to carefully craft their content or message, a fact that seems quite compatible with the neurotic personality type. It is possible that the anonymous online environment provides the platform in which neurotic people feel secure enough to engage in discussions and chats with other people.

Personality traits are correlated with the flow variable to different degrees. While previous research has shown a negative association between neuroticism and flow [104,14], this study did not find any significant influence (positive or negative). However, extraversion was positively related to the level of the flow state experience. Taken together, these findings suggest a pattern of differences between personality type and the state of flow. Further explanation of this finding could be a topic addressed by future research.

Finally, the present study showed significant relationships between flow and the use of UGC for both entertainment and communication purposes. Being in the flow state contributes to continued user UGC activities for entertainment, simply for the sheer pleasure experienced. Being in the flow states also led to higher usage of UGC for communication. In a UGC environment, communication activities are often performed to establish a social network through chat rooms, discussion spaces, and so on. Interestingly, the flow state did not influence the use of UGC for information acquisition. Taken together, these findings support and extend the work of van der Heijden [100], who found that perceived enjoyment was a stronger predictor of intention to use the Internet than perceived usefulness. While the web may serve both utilitarian and hedonic purposes, in the Web 2.0 environment, flow is clearly linked more closely to the hedonic functions.

## 6.1. Limitations

This investigation, of course, is not without its limitations. One possible limitation may be related to the narrow demographics of our sample. The respondents were students and faculty members at a university in the southern United States. Although college students and faculty members are representative users of UGC services, they may share too many educational and socioeconomic characteristics to be considered as a wide variety of respondents. Further studies should investigate a wider UGC user base to verify these results. However, the findings of this study may serve as a guide to future research that examines the effects of the interaction between UGC use and personality.

Additionally, this study measured three forms of UGC: entertainment, communication, and information acquisition. This pattern of usage may not fully reflect the actual range of UGC usage. For example, entertainment may represent a variety of genres, such as sports, movies, and games. According to the genre, the preferences of users with different personalities may show different levels of flow and usage. In addition, because the data were collected in 2008, and given the rapid developments in the UGC domain, there is a possibility that user perceptions may have changed. While the ‘age’ of the data may seem problematic on the surface, in this study we explored the effects of personality traits on flow and UGC usage and the relationship between flow and UGC usage. We believe personality traits are enduring (as opposed to state variables); therefore, these ideas are not time sensitive in regard to the flow experience. While the tools may have changed, we assert that the underlying factors and relationships are stable. To confirm this assertion, additional research is needed to identify at a more granular level the different UGC usage types and any changes in perception regarding usage. For example, future studies could conduct experiments to contrast the different types of UGC website activities. After browsing several UGC websites, the researcher could measure the subjects’ evaluations of each site.

Another limitation may be the use of retrospective measures.<sup>4</sup> While a plethora of studies in this area have used questionnaires and retrospective recall (e.g., [75,15,60,56,55,108]), some may argue that a survey is not an appropriate data collection method for studying flow behavior. A key facet of flow behavior is that users are not necessarily conscious of the activity [20]. Hence, employing a data gathering tool that depends on conscious recall and response formulation, such as a survey, may be less effective than other methods (e.g., think aloud protocol, experiment, or natural observation). Once again, future studies may utilize a variety of data collection methods to test the validity of the findings and the ‘best’ method to explore the relationships among personality, flow, and UGC.

Finally, the self reported usage measure could also be considered a limitation. Because the independent and dependent variables were measured by self-report methods, common method variance must be addressed [34]. Malhotra et al. [66,p. 1866] state the following: "IS research often deals with concepts that are relatively specific to an information technology (IT) artifact of interest. Accordingly, the scales used in IS research tend to be more concrete and less ambiguous when compared with those in psychology, sociology, or education. Therefore, to form answers to such IT-related questions, respondents are expected to feel less need to ‘‘guess’’ or rely on other situational cues.’’ While the threat is not as serious in IS-related research as in other domains, the definition of UGC and screening for participation were separated from the initial questionnaire in an effort to reduce the impact of this bias. Future research could include other procedures or mechanisms to further attenuate this bias.

## 6.2. Implications and future research

This study has implications for both research and practice. Flow theory is well-grounded in the psychology literature on human motivation and in the marketing literature on E-Commerce. We believe that understanding the impact of personality differences on the flow state can be a valuable addition to researchers in their efforts to understand why individuals consume and create web content. As Csikszentmihalyi [20] asserted, the differences among individuals are not merely in their skills but also may lie in their personality. In 2002, Finneran and Zhang [34,p. 1049] challenged researchers, claiming that more studies are needed ‘‘within the area of individual differences and autotelic personality to help flow and HCI researchers clarify which individual measures influence the flow experience and where they occur in the process.’’ This study has answered that call by being one of the few to explore the impact of two key personality dimensions on flow and UGC usage.

With regard to theoretical advancement, for researchers interested in extending this line of work, the first critical issue relates to the exploration of UGC usage types. Most of the traditional models of technology acceptance focus on its utilitarian aspects [40], but recent research on mobile computing has found that the usage of some types of computing is driven more by hedonic purposes [77]. These findings are consistent with our own, which demonstrates a positive relationship between personality and UGC usage for entertainment and the significance of the flow construct for this usage type. We encourage other scholars exploring flow theory to consider personality traits to help explain what motivates flow and UGC usage.

Second, the theory tested here is worthy of continued development and refinement. Our focus was on specific personality and UGC usage types. However, other personality types and UGC usage types may be relevant. For instance, the ‘‘Big 5’’ personality theory [35,39] or Holland’s ‘‘RIASEC vocational model’’ [45] could be utilized to further explore the personality traits. With regard to UGC usage, the categories could be further subdivided. For example, information could be subdivided into news, resources, and events, and communication could be subdivided into synchronous versus asynchronous modes, just to name a few possibilities. Of course the applicability of additional measures will need to be empirically validated for this research stream to progress.

From the perspective of practice, our results point to the importance of eschewing a strictly utilitarian perspective. According to Agarwal and Karahanna [1,p. 688], ‘‘As technology developments continue to focus on richer and more appealing interfaces, the importance of experiences that are intrinsically motivating, i.e., pleasurable and enjoyable in and of themselves, might dominate as predictors of usage intentions.’’ Our findings suggest this may indeed be the case. Within a work environment, an organization that encourages experimentation and exploration, i.e., enjoyment, may find that its employees are more willing to contribute to and use internal knowledge management systems, or wikis, thereby taking full advantage of the available technologies for both organizational and individual benefits.

Assessment of personality traits could be useful in recruiting and screening applicants for positions, coaching dissatisfied professionals, and evaluating the success of training and development programs. For example, organizational recruiters may realize a competitive advantage if they are able to shape the organization’s image to appeal to individuals who are more likely to fit the organization’s culture. Fit would most likely be the greatest when both the applicant and the organization emphasize similar personality traits, values, and beliefs.

From an external perspective, organizations are relying more heavily on UGC to aid their innovation process and to co-create value with their customers [81]. According to Schneckenberg [89,p. 509], ‘‘An astounding 75 percent of respondents indicate in a recent global McKinsey survey of executives that their companies have invested and will either maintain or even increase their investments in Web 2.0 tools.’’ A network effect occurs when the value a consumer obtains from a product or a service increases as more people use it [50]. Such network effects can also be applied in the UGC domain. More users contributing content, creates the potential for additional users to utilize the content, and more contributors willing to share additional content will lead to a greater network effect.

To achieve economic efficiency in the UGC domain, UGC sites should understand who will visit and enjoy (or not enjoy) their sites. For example, Sheraton’s Web 2.0 site, Belong Starwood, enables multiple forms of user participation. Guests are invited to share their vacation videos and stories online for others to read and use as an aid in the evaluation and selection of hotel options [92]. In this case, if the Sheraton brand understands the individual who visits, uses, and enjoys its website and that individual is converted to a guest at a Sheraton brand hotel, the information on the user may help the Sheraton brand (and ultimately the Starwood group of hotels) with their customer relationship management (CRM) programs and systems. By supplying a broad range of functionality in their UGC websites, organizations may provide a platform to develop ongoing dialog and more intimate (and profitable) relationships with users.

Whether targeting external or internal users, organizations may want to further explore the relationships between user types and UGC platform types. Traditionally, platforms have been viewed in terms of blogs, wikis, and the like. Reconceptualizing the idea of a platform around usage (e.g., communication) and the target user type may help organizations connect to their users more effectively and efficiently. Thus, organizations may leverage the findings fromthis study to refine their websites (or other web 2.0 technologies) to encourage UGC usage and develop or sustain a competitive advantage. In addition, Chen et al. [15,p. 264] stated that ‘‘. . .experiencing flow is beneficial both to human beings as a whole and to individuals as members of a society, because the consequences of flow propel the society to evolve while also improving individuals’ quality of life.’’ For example, the 2010 Haiti earthquake emergency UGC encouraged users to join fundraising and relief efforts, and the UGC about bovine spongiform encephalopathy (i.e., mad cow) gave rise to social repercussions in Korea. Therefore, we assert that this research is worthy of further investigation from academic, organizational, and societal perspectives.

## 7. Conclusion

The overarching goal of this paper was to enrich our understanding of UGC usage and of the motivations informing UGC usage. Employing flow theory as our foundation, we empirically tested the impact of personality traits (extraversion and neuroticism) on the level of the flow state experience and three UGC usage types (entertainment, communication, and information). The findings indicate that extraversion is positively related to the use of UGCs for entertainment and negatively related to the use of UGCs for communication and information; neuroticism, on the other hand, is positively related to the use of UGCs for entertainment and communication. In addition, only extraversion has an influence on flow, which is positive, and flow has a positive influence on UGC usage for entertainment and communication. This study extends flow theory by exploring antecedents and outcomes of the flow state. Several avenues for future research remain, and we hope this study will stimulate others to further extend this line of research.

## Appendix B. General information excerpt for the questionnaire

UGC, a new Internet trend, is the abbreviation for user generated content. UGC is also known as consumer generated media (CGM). It refers to various types of media content that is publicly available and produced by end-users. The term entered mainstream usage in 2005 arising from web publishing and new media content production circles. It reflects the expansion of media production through new technologies that are accessible and affordable to the general public. These include blogs, wikis, tagging/social bookmarking, multimedia sharing, and podcasting.
