Monica Fallon,<sup>1</sup> Jana-Rebecca Rehse,<sup>2</sup> Armin Heinzl<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Mannheim, Germany, fallon@uni-mannheim.de <sup>2</sup>University of Mannheim, Germany, rehse@uni-mannheim.de <sup>3</sup>University of Mannheim, Germany, heinzl@uni-mannheim.de

## Abstract

Process mining is a set of analytical techniques aimed at gaining insights into business processes in organizations. Recently, information systems scholars have recognized its potential fo analyzing human behavior through digital trace data. In this paper, we draw on the conceptual and technical analogies between business processes and human behavior to thoroughly investigate the application and transfer of process mining techniques to the analysis of human behavior. This analysis, called human behavior mining (HBM), is conceptualized as a four-part framework. To illustrate HBM’s research potential, we apply this framework in an mHealth scenario. We explore dynamic concepts proposed by social cognitive theory to analyze changes in physical activity behavior through digital trace data collected through a dedicated app. This exemplary application demonstrates that HBM can be used to empirically test previously unspecified and uncontested dynamic concepts in human behavior. It also highlights HBM’s suitability for health analytics, given the vast amount of health-related behavior data available through apps and wearables, and the direct connection between behavior and health-related outcomes. Our research provides a dynamic and temporal perspective on human behavior, showcasing the potential of HBM to enrich theoretical frameworks in IS research.

Keywords: Social Cognitive Theory, Behavior Change, Behavior Mining, IS Use, Mobile Health, Process Mining, Behavior Analytics

## 1 Introduction

Process mining is a set of analytical techniques that aim to gain insights into business processes in organizations (Davenport & Spanyi, 2019; van der Aalst, 2012, 2016, 2022). They rely on digital trace data in the form of event logs, as generated by the enterprise information systems in which the processes are executed. The traces contain information on how processes truly unfold in practice, offering opportunities for analysis and improvement (Franzoi et al., 2023; Grisold et al., 2020). Whereas traditional methods for process analysis, such as interviews or workshops, rely on subjective human perceptions and limited data samples, process mining constitutes a data-driven approach (Dumas et al., 2018). It enables organizations to uncover patterns of process inefficiency or incompliance that might have otherwise remained undetected (Pentland et al., 2021). It also supports process managers in conducting detailed analysis of individual process segments, such as decision points or individual work routines. Thus, process mining provides a comprehensive technique for business process analysis and advancement (Badakhshan et al., 2022).

Process mining has recently attracted the attention of information systems (IS) scholars (Grisold et al., 2020; Mendling et al., 2020; Pentland et al., 2020; vom Brocke et al., 2021) who have realized that these methods, originally devised for analyzing the efficiency of business processes, can also be applied to digital trace data generated in information systems to study human behavior (Abb et al., 2022; Esnaashari et al., 2022; Malinova et al., 2019; Pentland et al., 2021). This is based on a conceptual analogy between business processes and human behavior, which can be seen as a process of “a collection of interrelated activities that collectively lead to a certain outcome” (Weske, 2019). Whereas the activities in a business process denote single units of work, the “activities” of human behavior relate to observable actions, such as clicking a button in a user interface (UI) (Abb & Rehse, 2022) or tracking physical activity in a mobile health (mHealth) app. Similar to a business process, these behavioral activities are temporally or logically interrelated, e.g., by succession (Button B can only be clicked, once Checkbox C is enabled) or exclusive choice (an activity can be either walking or biking). The outcomes of these interrelated activities relate to the explicit or assumed objective of the behavior (Jiang & Cameron, 2020): Enterprise resource planning (ERP) system users click buttons and enter text to confirm sales orders for products offered; mHealth app users track physical activity to manage their individual fitness.

Besides this conceptual analogy between business processes and behavioral processes, there is also a technological analogy (Mendling et al., 2020): Process mining was originally enabled by the increasing digitalization of business processes, which facilitated the collection of event logs as digital evidence of real-life process executions (van der Aalst, 2012). Currently, the increasing personal digitalization of individuals is generating digital evidence on real-life human behavior (Pentland et al., 2020). The most obvious contributors are personal computers and handheld devices such as smartphones and tablet computers, which are used for many tasks. Since these devices require active interactions between users and the IS, they inevitably record usage behavior (Rehse et al., 2024). However, due to advances in sensor technology, such as that used in wearables, individuals no longer need to actively interact with their devices for data to be collected on their behavior. Instead, use occurs passively through real-time data collection, e.g., in the form of fitness trackers, which record physical activity behavior (Conroy et al., 2020). This implies that we now have access to vast amounts of real-time digital trace data that captures human behavior at a previously invisible level of detail.

To improve the understanding of human behavior, behavioral scientists have developed numerous theories and models that aim to explain and predict it. However, few theories of human behavior explicitly consider the role of time in describing and explaining the relationships between the phenomena involved (Scholz, 2019). Instead, behavioral theories often consider static phenomena and linear perspectives. Considering the role of time in explaining and predicting human behavior has the potential to provide novel insights into whether and when relationships between phenomena occur, including potential reciprocal relationships between variables, discontinuous changes, and cyclical patterns (Scholz, 2019). Accordingly, there is a need to shape and adapt current theories on human behavior to include a more dynamic and temporal perspective. In addition, current technologies offer opportunities to collect large amounts of real-time digital trace data on user behavior over long periods of time. We argue that this digital trace data can be used to refine and adapt current theories on human behavior with a more dynamic and temporal perspective with new analytical methods.

In this paper, we draw on analogies between business processes and behavioral processes to investigate how to transfer process mining techniques and apply them to a theory-guided analysis of human behavior. We call this approach human behavior mining (HBM). Based on the vast amounts of behavior-related digital trace data, we argue that HBM offers a unique opportunity to analyze human behavior, which can, in turn, facilitate the exploration, confirmation, and refinement of theoretical patterns. This research aims to advance behavioral analytics, in general, and health analytics, in particular, by answering the following research questions:

1. How can process mining be applied to digital trace data to explore and predict patterns in health-related human behavior?

2. How can insights from human behavior mining be leveraged to refine and enhance current theories of human behavior, incorporating a more dynamic and temporal perspective?

To answer our research questions, we first developed an HBM framework that is generally applicable to digital trace data to explore and predict patterns in human behavior. We then applied the HBM framework to demonstrate its research potential in an mHealth scenario. Specifically, we sought to explore and predict the dynamic relationships proposed by social cognitive theory (SCT) to analyze changes in physical activity behavior, using digital trace data from a dedicated app. To the best of our knowledge, there have been no empirical explorations of the dynamic feedback loops postulated by SCT. Thus, we leveraged HBM to deepen the understanding of a well-established theory. The exemplary application we created for this research demonstrates that HBM can be used to empirically test previously implicit and uncontested theoretical hypotheses in human behavior, particularly in health analytics, where the abundance of health-related behavioral data from apps and wearables allows for direct connections between individuals’ behavior and health-related outcomes. We employed process mining methods to explore and predict the interdependent and reciprocal relationships proposed in SCT. Specifically, we used eventuallyfollows graphs (EFGs) to explore the interdependent relationships, recurrent neural networks to predict the interdependent relationships, and regression tree boosting to investigate the reciprocal relationships. Our findings reveal that although many SCTproposed interdependencies occur during mHealth use, they are not entirely reciprocal, offering both methodological and theoretical contributions.

## 2 Related Literature

Successfully guiding humans to give up unhealthy behaviors and engage in healthier behaviors is a major public health challenge (Davidson & Scholz, 2020). Therefore, many behavioral researchers focus on encouraging changes in health-related behavior. In this paper, we investigate the application of process mining to health-related digital trace data as a novel approach for exploring and predicting individual human behavior. In the following section, we first review analytical approaches for studying human behavior. Then, we review prior literature that has applied process mining to health-related and behavioral data.

## 2.1 Studying Health-Related Human Behavior with Analytical Methods

Insights into individuals’ health-related behavior can be gained in multiple ways. In the IS literature, health-related behavior is often studied using empirical methods such as interviews (Rieder et al., 2021; Savoli et al., 2020) and surveys (Fu et al., 2023; James et al., 2021). These methods are valuable for capturing user attitudes, cognition processes, and intentions, but they primarily reflect subjective perspectives rather than objective measures of actual behavior. They neither provide a detailed account of executed behavioral activities over time nor do they capture behaviors that users may not actively perceive or be able to describe.

In view of these shortcomings, analytical approaches for studying human behavior are a promising alternative. These approaches attempt to generate new insights into behavior by analyzing digital trace data, i.e., the digital records of activity and events that involve information technologies (Berente et al., 2019; Howison et al., 2011). The increasing availability of health-related digital trace data provides new opportunities to study dynamic behavior over long periods of time in natural and quasi-natural experiments (Bardhan et al., 2020). The insights gained from analyzing digital trace data can contribute to IS theory development in at least two ways (Berente et al., 2019; Lindberg, 2020): On the one hand, they allow for testing theoretical hypotheses that previously went uncontested because digital trace data can capture behavior at a new level of detail over a longer period of time. On the other hand, they provide a means to “automate” the manual process of grounded theory development such that researchers can develop or shape theories based on their analytical findings (Berente et al., 2019). In the following, we review examples of how researchers have applied different analytical methods to achieve these objectives thus far.

When using digital trace data to test theoretica hypotheses, researchers typically collect or extract very detailed digital trace data to quantitatively capture the behavior in question. Examples from a health-related context include Bao et al. (2020), who examined how the use of a patient portal was associated with patients’ long-term health outcomes; Babar et al. (2023), who studied the effect of nudges on physical activity in a wellness program; and Yang et al. (2023), who investigated the effects of gamification on exercise behavior. These studies analyzed the impact of the IS on individuals’ healthrelated behavior using panel-level linear regressions (Yang et al., 2023), fixed effects regressions (Bao et al., 2020), or general linear models (Babar et al., 2023). Although these methods of analysis are powerful tools for testing hypotheses for statistical significance, they assume linearity, which oversimplifies human behavior and overlooks dynamic relationships. Therefore, although the studies relied on trace data capable of providing theoretical insights on dynamic human behavior, the chosen analysis methods did not fully exploit the data’s potential.

Considering these limitations, researchers have replaced or supplemented traditional analysis methods with more advanced analytical approaches. Such examples include Son et al. (2020), who used sensor readings from smart asthma inhalers to identify anomalous patient behavior with a generalized linear mixed effects model employing a grid-based quasi-Poisson distribution; Yu et al. (2024), who used hidden Markov models to determine the optimal timing for triggering protective devices to prevent fall injuries; Wang et al. (2020), who employed vector autoregressive models to investigate physicians’ online-offline behavior dynamics using data from both online and offline channels; and Liu et al. (2020), who used a long short-term memory (LSTM) neural network to categorize health-related videos. These papers all emphasize analytical rather than theoretical aspects; nonetheless, they manage to move beyond the assumption of linearity and apply analytical methods that can capture the dynamics of human behavior.

These existing analytical approaches, including bu not limited to statistical models and machine learning models, are powerful tools that can be leveraged to achieve many new insights on human behavior. They all share one shortcoming, however: They conceptualize behavior as a time series. This means that they separate their respective study period into predefined subsequential intervals of equal length, e.g., months, weeks, days, or hours. For each interval, the occurrences of individual activities, such as interactions with a specific feature of an information system, are measured over the duration and summarized at the end. The counts per interval then form a series of observations, i.e., a time series, which is then used as input for the analytical approach of choice.

This conceptualization of behavior as a time series— albeit powerful—has two major limitations: First, it requires all intervals to be of equal length, hence assuming a certain pattern in the behavior and not considering potential dynamics of behavior. Second, it does not provide useful information on dynamic behaviors within the boundaries of the fixed interval. Thus, obtaining more fine-grained insights into health-related behavior and its potential changes would require a method for analyzing behavior in time intervals of variable length. The ability to analyze the sequence and duration of activities, as well as the transition (or waiting) time between activities in variable time intervals, would enable a much richer understanding of the dynamic behaviors of the humans involved. To achieve this, we adapted and contextualized the technique of process mining for analyzing human behavior.

## 2.2 Process Mining for Health-Related and Behavioral Data

Process mining techniques were originally devised to analyze the performance of business processes, such as purchase-to-pay or plan-to-produce workflows (Davenport & Spanyi, 2019), but were quickly adopted by other application domains (Emamjome et al., 2019). A detailed description of process mining and its relevant foundations can be found in Appendix C. Following the idea of an individual process execution, digital trace data is separated into units of analysis of variable length, called cases (De Weerdt & Wynn, 2022). The separation of data into cases is determined by a socalled case notion, which can freely be chosen by the user and determines the scope and focus of the analysis. For example, in a purchase-to-pay process, the case notion can be an individual purchase order item. In healthcare processes, the typical case notion is the individual patient.

Process mining offers a plethora of methods to analyze the collection of cases (van der Aalst, 2022). Original process mining methods include process discovery, which visualizes all cases in a joint process model, and performance analysis, which identifies bottlenecks in the process execution. In addition, process mining can be combined with other analytical approaches. For example, it has been combined with time-series analysis in order to enrich a process event log with other data, e.g., sensor readings (Dunkl et al., 2015). Most frequently though, process mining is combined with machine learning. With unsupervised machine learning, for example, we can use cluster analysis to find groups of similar cases (Zandkarimi et al., 2020) or outlier detection to find anomalous process cases (Nolle et al., 2022). Supervised machine learning, typically in the form of neural networks, is mainly used for predictive process monitoring (Di Francescomarino & Ghidini, 2022), which can predict how a running case will unfold in the future. Typical prediction targets are the occurrence of an activity in the remainder of a trace (Galanti et al., 2020), the next event in a sequence (Evermann et al., 2017), the outcome of an instance (Kratsch et al., 2021), or the time until its completion (Verenich et al., 2019).

With respect to contributing to theory, the value of process mining techniques has been acknowledged and demonstrated (Grisold et al., 2020). However, this has so far been limited to original process mining techniques, which are inherently exploratory (Rehse et al., 2024). Confirmatory applications of process mining techniques to test theory-derived hypotheses, for instance, are missing from the literature. Nevertheless, process discovery or performance analysis might already provide new insights into the captured behavior, e.g., in terms of behavioral relations or waiting times between activities, which can be used for theory development, both deductively and inductively (Grisold et al., 2020). For example, Pentland et al. (2021) studied the behavior of patients and staff at a healthcare clinic to theorize about process dynamics. Notably, they also divided their data into sequences of equal length (in this case, days) but then studied the behavior within these sequences and how it changes over time. Besides this paper, theory-driven applications of process mining are rare, as most papers in this domain focus on technical contributions. Nevertheless, the insights gained through process mining can be used for theorizing. One example of this is the work of Beerepoot et al. (2021), which identifies ad hoc workarounds in healthcare processes. Examining these workarounds can help organizations reduce harmful noncompliance or identify areas for improvement, but these insights could also provide empirical evidence and theoretical knowledge on the workarounds themselves (Alter, 2014).

Both of these examples come from healthcare, which is an important application for process mining because it can support patients and healthcare workers by providing a data-centric view of patient trajectories and hospital administration (Pentland et al., 2021). In addition to healthcare, process mining has also been applied to study human behavior in other contexts. For example, it has been used to demonstrate gender differences in shopping behavior, based on sensor readings in a mall (Dogan et al., 2019). Applications to e-learning environments have identified different self-regulation patterns of students (Esnaashari et al., 2022) and the effects of instructional support on selfregulated learning (Sonnenberg & Bannert, 2019). Finally, process mining can be used to examine the practices and routines of office workers—for example, by investigating similarities and differences between them (Malinova et al., 2019), to group different types of user behavior in an ERP system (Abb et al., 2022), or to make sense of knowledge intensive analysis tasks (Zerbato et al., 2021).

Another relevant application of process mining with behavioral data is the analysis of software usage behavior through low-level user interaction logs, known as user behavior mining (UBM) (Rehse et al., 2024). UBM has been used in various application cases, e.g., to reveal typical usage paths in a software (Rubin et al., 2014), to group different types of user behavior in an ERP system (Abb et al., 2022) or to identify user tasks that can be automated (Leno et al., 2021). In their recent paper, Rehse et al. (2024) subsumed different applications of UBM into a UBM framework consisting of four parts: (1) data, i.e., the low-level logs used to capture usage behavior; (2) technology, i.e., process mining techniques used to analyze these data; and (3) objective, i.e., to analyze, assist, or automate that behavior. The fourth part is an optional theory that may be included in UBM to guide the analysis and gain new insights but is not mandatory for a UBM application, which can be purely technical or exploratory. We see similarities but also key differences between HBM and UBM, which we elaborate on in the next section.

## 3 Human Behavior Mining: A Framework

We consider human behavior mining (HBM) to be a theory-guided approach to using digital trace data for human behavior analysis by means of process mining, with the goal of gaining novel theoretical insights. As such, HBM is similar to UBM, but it differs in four key aspects: First, UBM is limited to the usage behavior of an information system, implying that it has a much smaller scope than HBM. Second, the consideration of a theory is optional in UBM because it can be purely technical or exploratory, whereas a theoretical lens is essential in HBM, considering the vast amount of knowledge that already exists about human behavior. This does not mean that HBM is only applicable if an according theory already exists, it just stresses that the main purpose of HBM is to inform theory development and facilitate rethinking existing theories of human behavior. Third, HBM focuses on analyzing human behavior for theoretical insights, with the objective of contributing to theory, omitting the alternative UBM objectives of automating or assisting behavior. Fourth, HBM does not focus on humans as users of digital apps; instead, it emphasizes the primary role of humans in everyday life, e.g., their role as patients, athletes, students, etc.

Based on these considerations, we conceptualize HBM as a four-part application framework in this section. This framework depicted in Figure 1 is based on the UBM framework proposed by Rehse et al. (2024). However, we have adapted the four components of that framework to fit the scope of HBM, as described above. The data in question is behavioral trace data, which is processed by means of process mining methods in order to achieve a dynamic analysis of human behavior. This empirical analysis is guided by theories on human behavior, to which the empirical results can further contribute.

The four framework components are discussed in detail in the following sections. We start with the objective, i.e., the dynamic analysis of human behavior, and then explain how the other components contribute to that objective.

## 3.1 Dynamic Analysis of Human Behavior

The subject of interest in HBM is analyzing the dynamics of individual human behavior. We define behavior in this context as a sequence of observable actions taken by individuals over time, often recorded through digital trace data from interactions with technology. We argue that HBM enables a more nuanced, dynamic analysis of human behavior, allowing researchers to observe behavioral patterns as they evolve over time. Traditional analytical methods applied to trace data, although powerful, typically assume linearity, which can oversimplify human behavior and overlook its complex, interdependent relationships. HBM, however, offers methods that go beyond fixed intervals and linear assumptions, providing a flexible framework to capture these nuanced dynamics.

Just as process mining has capitalized on the digitalization of business processes in (enterprise) information systems, HBM leverages the increasing prevalence of individual information systems (Baskerville, 2011), such as computers, smartphones, or wearables that capture digital traces of human behavior. More specifically, these devices can record at least two different types of behavior (Jiang & Cameron, 2020; Noorbergen et al., 2019):

Usage behavior denotes active interactions between the human and the IS, for example, clicking a button or opening an app.

Contextual behavior denotes human activities that occur in the vicinity of the IS and are passively tracked by it, for example, running or biking.

## 3.2 Theories on Human Behavior

Various theories and models from psychology are used to explain and predict human behavior. We argue that these psychological theories provide fundamental support for HBM as they can be used to guide the application of process mining methods and to facilitate the development of new insights for theory building and refinement. Theories on human behavior allow researchers to conceptualize and operationalize behavioral data and related variables; in turn, researchers can subsequently analyze the data to provide insights for the theories employed.

Among the many relevant theories, the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991), self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000), the transtheoretical model (Prochaska & Velicer, 1997), and social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1991) are frequently applied to explain changes in human behavior (Rhodes et al., 2021). These theories offer a strong foundation for explaining and predicting human behavior; however, a major limitation of current theories on human behavior is their emphasis on static snapshots of attitudes, cognitions, or beliefs at a single point in time (Scholz, 2019). For example, many of these theories implicitly assume that an individual’s state—whether an attitude, cognition, or actual behavior—remains stable over time, thereby promoting a linear perspective that overlooks dynamic interactions and temporal changes. In fact, there are relatively few theories that explicitly specify the role of time in the conceptualization of psychological phenomena, and too little theorizing has been conducted on the dynamic relationships between these phenomena over time (Scholz, 2019).

Among these theories, social cognitive theory (SCT) stands out for its integration of ongoing, dynamic feedback loops that change in response to an individual’s behavioral, cognitive, and environmental states (Bandura, 1991)—an aspect that other behavioral theories lack (Beauchamp et al., 2019). SCT introduces two key dynamic concepts that shape behavior: selfregulation and reciprocal determinism (Bandura, 1978, 1991). These concepts emphasize dynamic interdependencies, suggesting that an individual’s behavior is continuously influenced by personal and environmental factors while also influencing these same factors in a reciprocal, dynamic manner. However, although SCT provides a strong conceptual framework, it has limitations in its empirical application. The theory does not specify the constructs within its dynamic concepts with sufficient granularity to reveal structures between variables. Consequently, SCT’s theoretical propositions remain largely at a conceptual level, resulting in limited empirical evidence on the dynamic relationships it proposes. This has led to a reliance on a traditional, linear graphical model (Bandura, 2004) that fails to capture the theory’s dynamic and reciprocal interdependencies. This static representation contradicts Bandura’s narrative descriptions of SCT as a dynamic model (Riley et al., 2016).

We argue that this discrepancy may be attributed to previous constraints on data availability, particularly in face-to-face behavioral interventions where it was difficult to capture the real-time, longitudinal data needed to model these dynamic relationships. With advances in technology, researchers now have access to extensive longitudinal data on behavior and its predictors, which provides a unique opportunity to enrich traditional theories of human behavior with temporal and dynamic insights. We contend that through process mining methods and, more specifically, HBM, real-time and low-level trace data can provide theoretical insights at a granular level that can reveal a more dynamic perspective of human behavior. There are two main reasons for this: First, HBM can consider human behavior events at any level of granularity, zooming in or out as the theory or the researchers require. This allows researchers to gain insights into behavioral patterns at a previously unrealized level of detail. Second, HBM considers human behavior over the progression of time, without relying on fixed-length intervals for its analysis. This makes it easier to identify temporal patterns and developments in behavior over time. For instance, HBM can add value to theories like SCT by introducing a temporal component and providing a detailed, dynamic perspective on behavior that better aligns with SCT’s foundational principles.

## 3.3 Behavioral Trace Data

Process mining data takes the form of event logs. To adapt the process mining toolset to HBM, we must transfer this data structure to behavior-related digital trace data. In so doing, we can reinterpret the basic process mining entities in a behavioral context, as presented in Table 1. For each entity, we provide an example taken from the context of an mHealth app: A user uses the tracking feature of a fitness app to record that they were physically active that day.

In process analysis, the main reference point is the activity, an abstract notion of a single unit of work, such as “create purchase order.” To transfer this into an HBM context, we define behavior as a sequence of observable actions, framed as a process comprising a collection of interrelated activities that collectively lead to a specific outcome (Weske, 2019). An action constitutes a behavioral activity if it fulfils the following criteria:

It is discrete, i.e., it has an observable start and end. Continuities like “age” or state descriptions like “being a student” do not count as behavioral activities.

It is indivisible, i.e., it does not make sense to subdivide it for the intended scope. Indivisibility depends on the context of the analysis: If we study user behavior in an ERP system, we will record individual clicks; but if we study work distractions, it suffices to record the active apps on an employee’s device.

It is externally observable so that it can be captured in digital trace data. Unobservable internal actions, such as cognition, are not considered behavioral activities (Hedman et al., 2013).

It is executable by a single individual human. Group activities like “engaging in discussion” and automated activities like “query database” do not count as activities.

Behavioral activities describe an action outside a specific context. A concrete execution of a behavioral activity is captured by a behavioral event: a digital record that an individual executed an activity in a specific context at a given point in time. In process mining terminology, the individual is the resource; the point in time is denoted by a time stamp; and the execution context is further specified by other (optional) event attributes. Each behaviora event relates to exactly one behavioral activity. Note that events occur at a specific point in time, whereas activities can have a duration. For further analysis of events, we must know the relationship between the event and the activity, i.e., whether the event denotes the start or end point of the activity execution.

## 3.4 Process Mining Methods

To analyze the usage behavior or the contextual behavior of an individual human in the vicinity of an information system, we can apply process mining methods to the captured behavioral event log. These methods fulfill two basic analysis goals: They can explore the data to reveal previously unknown patterns, and they can leverage the data to predict what will happen in the future.

The most important technique for exploring behavioral event logs is process discovery, which represents the activities in the log and their relations in the form of a process model (van der Aalst, 2022). It can be used to reveal initial patterns to explore further or to check whether the data supports deduced statements (Grisold et al., 2020). To deepen the exploration, process discovery can be combined with, e.g., trace clustering (Abb et al., 2022), which can identify different behavioral patterns in different groups of individuals (Esnaashari et al., 2022).

After the initial discovery, various other process mining techniques can be applied to explore the behavioral event log in more detail. Conformance checking (Burattin et al., 2016; Carmona et al., 2018) compares a log to a predefined set of rules to find deviations between them. In a behavioral context, it is particularly interesting when the rules capture expected behavior, e.g., social norms or theoretical constraints. Process enhancement discovers problems in the behavioral performance, such as bottlenecks (Dadashnia et al., 2020). Anomaly detection utilizes machine learning to find atypical behavior, such as errors or fraud (Rehse et al., 2024). Concept drift detection (Maaradji et al., 2017) can identify sudden or gradual changes in the behavioral patterns—for example, if they changed at a certain point in time. Finally, causal analysis (Bozorgi et al., 2020) identifies causes for or discriminating factors between different behavior patterns.

The insights gained from exploration can then be used to make predictions about future behavior. For that purpose, we can leverage predictive process monitoring (Di Francescomarino & Ghidini, 2022) to forecast, e.g., whether the individual will engage in a certain kind of behavior, what their next actions will be, whether their behavior will lead to the intended outcome, or how long a certain behavior will take.

## 4.1 Dynamic Analysis of Human Behavior: mHealth Use and Physical Activity Behavior

Although physical activity has been linked to improved health outcomes (World Health Organization, 2020), global activity levels still fall short of the World Health Organization’s (WHO) recommended physical activity goals (Guthold et al., 2018). mHealth apps have the potential to influence physical activity behavior by enabling users to track their activities, set and monitor progress towards behavioral goals, gain peer recognition, and engage in social comparison (Fallon et al., 2019; Thomas Craig et al., 2021). However, the specific ways in which interactions with mHealth apps translate into real-world behavior remain unclear (Fallon et al., 2019).

Employing HBM within an mHealth context allows for a dynamic analysis of user behavior, capturing the ongoing, interdependent relationship between usage behavior (e.g., actively engaging with app features) and contextual behavior (e.g., performing physical activity). Here, usage behavior denotes deliberate interactions with mHealth features (e.g., viewing feedback on goal progress), while contextual behavior denotes physical activity behavior passively tracked by the IS (e.g., minutes spent running or biking). This dynamic approach provides insights into how mHealth use unfolds over time, highlighting the interdependencies between in-app behaviors and realworld activity.

## 4.2 Theories on Human Behavior: Social Cognitive Theory

As outlined in Section 3.2, SCT is well suited for guiding HBM due to its focus on the dynamic and reciprocal relationships among behavior, cognition, and the environment (Bandura, 1991). Self-regulation and reciprocal determinism are the key concepts specified in SCT that highlight these interdependencies (Bandura, 1978, 1991), which are discussed in detail in sections 4.2.1, 4.2.2, and 4.2.3. However, SCT has two key limitations: Its constructs lack specificity for revealing detailed relationships, and its traditional linear models fail to capture the theory’s proposed feedback loops (Bandura, 2004; Riley et al., 2016). These issues, likely due to past constraints in data availability, can now be addressed with process mining and digital trace data. This section applies SCT within HBM to operationalize self-regulation and reciprocal determinism in an mHealth context, leveraging trace data to capture real-time data and empirically test the dynamic relationships proposed by SCT.

## 4.2.1 Social Cognitive Theory: Leveraging mHealth Features to Access Constructs

Self-regulation, a core cognitive function in SCT, operates as a dynamic feedback loop in which individuals self-monitor and adjust their behaviors to meet goals (Bandura, 1991). Through self-monitoring and feedback, individuals assess their progress against desired outcomes, adjusting their goals or behavior as needed to reduce discrepancies (Carver & Scheier, 1982). Negative feedback can help keep individuals aligned with their goals, while goal adjustments allow for continuous progress or increased challenge (Bandura, 1991).

Reciprocal determinism in SCT refers to the dynamic interdependencies among cognition, behavior, and the environment, where each element influences and is influenced by the others (Bandura, 1978, 1991). This dynamic concept considers three key determinants: The cognitive determinant is reflected as self-regulation (described above), the behavioral determinant is reflected by the executed desired behavior (e.g., physical activity), and the environmental determinant is conceptualized as external sources of influence (social comparison and social support). Social comparison involves evaluating one’s own performance relative to others, and social support encompasses encouragement through praise (Bandura, 1991). These determinants interact bidirectionally over time, reinforcing behavioral patterns in a feedback loop.

SCT provides general guidance on the dynamic concepts (i.e., self-regulation and reciprocal determinism) involved in human behavior, so deducing mHealth features based on SCT allows for access to constructs within these concepts. SCT has been commonly applied in the physical activity domain to guide mHealth features (Daryabeygi-Khotbehsara et al., 2021; Fallon et al., 2019). For instance, mHealth apps can support the self-regulation of physical activity through features that facilitate self-monitoring, setting goals, and viewing feedback about those goals (Fallon et al., 2019). Physical activity can be tracked automatically via sensors or through self-reporting with real-time visualizations, allowing users to monitor progress and adjust goals based on feedback, e.g., by raising the bar in view of positive feedback or training more frequently to respond to negative feedback (Fallon et al., 2019). Furthermore, the environmental determinants in SCT include social comparison and support. Social comparison can be integrated into mHealth apps through leaderboards, enabling users to compare their achievements with peers. Social support can be facilitated through social feeds, where users interact through likes and comments, providing encouragement and enhancing motivation (Fallon et al., 2019). Finally, the behavioral determinant represents actual physical activity as tracked by the app.

The dynamic concepts outlined in SCT are illustrated in Figure 2, which highlights the interdependencies among cognitive, behavioral, and environmental determinants. By leveraging SCT in the design of mHealth features, it becomes possible to empirically explore and test the dynamic relationships that drive human behavior.

## 4.2.2 Self-Regulatory Feedback Loop

Self-regulation is central to promoting sustained behavior change (Bandura, 1991; Carver & Scheier, 1982) achieved through features that support selfmonitoring, feedback, and goal adjustment (Fallon et al., 2019). We propose a reciprocal self-regulatory feedback loop between self-monitoring behavior and viewing feedback on behavior (H1) and between viewing feedback on behavior and adjusting goals (H2), as shown in Figure 2 and Table 3. These feedback loops operate through two interdependent relationships. First, individuals self-monitor their physical activity by uploading workouts and then evaluate how these activities contribute to their goal progress (H1a) (Bandura, 1991). In turn, feedback on goal progress reinforces self-monitoring behavior, encouraging individuals to continue tracking and uploading their activity (H1b). Second, individuals use the viewed feedback to transcend current achievements and set new, realistic goals for themselves (H2a) (Carver & Scheier, 1982). Similarly, setting a new goal motivates individuals to view progress toward it, reinforcing the feedback loop (H2b) (Bandura, 1991).

## 4.2.3 Reciprocal Determinism

Reciprocal determinism relates to the interdependent relationships between cognitions, behavior, and the environment, where each element bidirectionally and dynamically influences the others (Bandura, 1978, 1991). Our hypotheses H3, H4, and H5 represent these reciprocal relationships, as shown in Figure 2 and Table 3.

H3 assesses the reciprocal relationship between external influences and self-regulation. We propose that social comparison and social support impact the cognitive functions of self-regulation by influencing self-appraisal, triggering affective self-reactions, and activating goal-setting and self-monitoring (Bandura, 1991). In mHealth, features for social comparison and social support can affect how individuals assess their performance and motivate goal pursuit (Fallon et al., 2020, 2021), suggesting that these features encourage interactions with self-monitoring, feedback, and goal adjustment (H3a).

Additionally, SCT proposes that self-regulation can shape individuals’ perceptions of external influences (Bandura, 1978). For example, progress towards goals alters how social information is perceived, with its effects shifting as individuals approach their physical activity goals (Fallon et al., 2021; Huang, 2018). This effect is driven by underlying cognitive processes in self-regulation (Fallon et al., 2021), where individuals seek social information to boost motivation during goal pursuit. In mHealth use, this relationship manifests as users interacting with self-regulation features (selfmonitoring, viewing feedback, adjusting goals) and subsequently interacting with external influence features (social comparison, social support) (H3b).

H4 assesses the reciprocal relationship between external sources of influence and actual behavior. Social comparison and social support can encourage individuals to adhere to behaviors through social recognition (Bandura, 1991). This is supported by mHealth research showing that users of social apps engage in more physical activity compared to those using apps with no social features (King et al., 2016). We propose that this relationship unfolds during mHealth use as individuals interact with socialcomparison or social-support features and subsequently partake in physical activity behavior (H4a).

Additionally, engaging in socially approved-of behaviors, such as physical activity, can enhance self-pride, selfesteem, and self-efficacy (Bandura, 1991; Beauchamp et al., 2019). In mHealth, individuals often seek social approval or compare their performance to others, which can be achieved through interactions with social comparison and support features (Fallon et al., 2020). Thus, we propose that users who partake in physical activity will subsequently interact with social-comparison and social-support features (H4b) to reinforce self-pride and boost self-esteem and self-efficacy.

H5 assesses the reciprocal relationship between selfregulation and actual behavior. According to SCT, selfregulation operates as a discrepancy-reducing feedback loop through cognitive processes such as selfmonitoring, standard-setting, evaluative judgment, and self-appraisal (Bandura, 1991). For example, in mHealth, individuals who receive reminders to engage with self-regulation features also partake in more physical activity (Liu & Willoughby, 2018), illustrating the direct impact of self-regulation on behavior (H5a).

Additionally, physical activity can feed back into selfregulation, as achieving targeted behavior affects goal progress, sustaining the self-regulatory feedback loop (Bandura, 1991). We expect that, after partaking in physical activity, individuals will view their goal progress and potentially adjust their goals to be more challenging or achievable (H5b). The proposed interdependencies and reciprocal relationships can be empirically evaluated using suitable behavioral trace data.

## 5.1 Data Collection

The app’s goal was to leverage SCT to support individuals in becoming more physically active. Users could record their physical activity and observe their progress towards daily and weekly goals. The goal was initially set to 150 minutes of physical activity per week, following WHO recommendations, but users were able to change daily and weekly goals to make them more difficult or more achievable. In addition to the self-monitored physical activities, the app automatically pulled physical activity behavior from native health apps (Apple Health and Google Fit). When users opened the app, they viewed a dashboard from which features could be accessed. The app contained three self-regulation features. Selfmonitoring allowed users to record physical activity either live with a stopwatch or manually afterwards. Feedback visualized users’ progress towards weekly and daily goals, along with a list of previous activities. Goal adjustment allowed users to update their weekly and daily goals of physical activity. The app also contained two external influence features. The social comparison feature was a leaderboard that ranked users based on weekly active minutes, percentage of goal progress, and number of goals achieved. The social support feature was a social feed where users could view, like, and comment on others’ physical activities and goal achievements, which were automatically posted to the social feed.

To capture trace data on feature use, we relied on internal app logging (Agostinelli et al., 2020), which recorded time-stamped records of each user interaction with each feature of the app. Our app had access to native fitness apps and thus also objectively traced physical activity (contextual behavior). For HBM, such internal app logging is the preferable logging option. It is easy to deploy and requires almost no additional resources. It yields high-quality trace data because the logging has direct access to internal app data, which reduces the likelihood of recording noise. Internal app logging does, however, require access to the source code, so it is not applicable to proprietary apps. Also, it is limited to one app, so it cannot trace behavior across different apps. In those cases, data must be collected on the system level by using an external, system-wide logging functionality that monitors user behavior displayed visibly on the device interface (Jimenez-Ramirez et al., 2019). This system-level logging occurs independently from the target app. It is resourceintensive and error prone, so it should be treated as a fall-back option for HBM.

The data collected in our field study contained 61 users over a period of four weeks. The time frame of four weeks was based on other research evaluating the effectiveness of mHealth for physical activity behavior over a similar time frame (Degroote et al., 2021; Klasnja et al., 2019). The average age was 21.69 years (SD = 1.76). 64% of users were female, the most frequent occupation was “student” (95%), and the highest academic degree was Alevels <sup>1</sup> (93%). We used a student sample to find participants for our study, which is appropriate if clearly articulated and justified (Compeau et al., 2012). A student sample matched our research goals because younger, educated adults are the largest group of mHealth users (Carroll et al., 2017; Paradis et al., 2022). Thus, our results are relevant for a large majority of mHealth users. Nonetheless, not all characteristics of students may transfer to other social groups; consequently, the results are limited in their generalizability.

Over the course of the four weeks, we recorded 1,142 physical activities, amounting to an average of 18.7 physical activities per user or 0.66 physical activities per user per day. The total duration of activities was 856 hours (average of 30 minutes per user per day). The average duration of a physical activity was 41.8 minutes, with a median of 30 minutes. The total time spent in the app was 31 hours, amounting to an average of 1 minute and 11 seconds (SD = 54 seconds) per user per day. The leaderboard was used for 485 minutes, the feedback feature for 407 minutes, and the social feed for 167 minutes. The average time spent per view was 11 seconds (SD = 27 seconds) for the feedback feature, 23 seconds (SD = 58 seconds) for the leaderboard, and 21 seconds (SD = 50 seconds) for the social feed.

## 5.2 Operationalization and Preprocessing

The collected raw data needed to be processed to serve as input for process mining methods. This processing included defining the activities, selecting a case notion, and preprocessing.

## 5.2.1 Defining the Activities

The concrete definition and contextualization of behavioral activities depend on the context of the HBM analysis. In process mining, the set of activities that make up a process is defined ex ante, and events are mapped to activities either manually or automatically. Transferring this approach to HBM means that we needed to define the set of behavioral activities that we expected to occur in the behavior log and specify how these activities should be mapped to the observed behavioral events. In our application scenario, we defined one behavioral activity per relevant SCT construct, shown in Table 5. User interactions that refer to these behavioral activities were included in the log, along with their completion time stamps. Physical activity was the only activity that could relate to two events (start and end).

Using this set of activities, our final log contained 3,157 events from six behavioral activities that occurred, on average, 526 times, with a median of 514, a maximum of 1,142 (physical activity), and a minimum of 29 (goal adjustment).

## 5.2.2 Selecting a Case Notion

The main unit of analysis in process mining is cases. As outlined in Section 2.2, the separation of an event log into cases is determined by the case notion. The choice of a case notion is typically straightforward in the context of a business process, where it relates to individual process executions. For HBM, the choice of a case notion is more complex (Rehse et al., 2024). For our behavioral event log, we considered the following potential case notions:

Individuals: Grouping all events related to a single user allowed us to track behavior over a longer period. However, since we were interested in specific behavioral patterns, this case notion was too coarse-grained for our analysis context.

Individuals and time spans: Grouping all events related to a single user in a 24-hour time window allowed for a fine-granular analysis and comparison of behavioral patterns. However, this case notion disconnected activities before and after the point of separation. In our context, if we separated cases at midnight, we would have overlooked that a user, for example, used the app before going to sleep and then performed physical activities early the next morning.

Individuals and specific activities: Grouping all events related to a single user in the vicinity of one specific type of activity allowed for a detailed analysis of that activity. This makes sense when the analysis goal is to study how this specific behavior can be encouraged or discouraged. This was the case in our application scenario, where we wanted to examine the impact of mHealth use on physical activity behavior. This is why we decided to construct our cases around the physical activities of our users.

Selecting and implementing the individuals and specific activities case notion required a somewhat intricate grouping mechanism to ensure full coverage of the event log. Our goal was to divide the four-week trial period into a sequence of mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive, non-fixed-length time intervals per user, such that each interval contained exactly one physical activity and potentially multiple app interactions. Therefore, we first identified all physical activities and labeled them with a unique ID. Then, we determined a surrounding “interval of influence” of variable length per physical activity, assuming that each usage event in this interval related to the contained physical activity. To ensure that each event belonged to exactly one case, we calculated the time span between the end point of one physical activity and the starting point of the subsequent one. The first half of this time span was assigned to the preceding physical activity; the second half was assigned to the succeeding one. This assured an even distribution of intervals around activities, even if their length varied. The first interval started at the beginning of the study period; the last interval ended at the end of the study period. Each app interaction within an interval was assigned the label of the contained physical activity as its case notion. Figure 3 illustrates this approach. The dark-colored sections denote the physical activity, while the light-colored sections denote the respective intervals of influence. This choice of the respective case notion illustrates the advantage of process mining over time-series analysis as a tool for analyzing human behavior: Relying on a freely configurable case notion allowed us to analyze user interactions, even if they occurred in time intervals of variable length.

## 5.2.3 Preprocessing

The goal of preprocessing is to make the log more suitable for its intended analysis (De Weerdt & Wynn, 2022). It might include actions such as integrating logs from different sources, reducing noise, or separating the logs into smaller sub-logs for more detailed analysis. In our case, we focused on noise reduction. Specifically, we filtered out all physical activities that lasted less than 10 minutes because those activities were typically automatically recorded walking or biking activities that we considered to be necessities of everyday life instead of intentionally planned physical activities. We also filtered out physical activities that occurred before or after the designated study period because we could not be sure that they occurred within the context of mHealth use.

Our final behavior log contained 1,142 traces. The average trace length (number of events per trace) was 2.7 (minimum 1; maximum 7). The average trace duration was 6.4 hours, with a median of 47.5 minutes (minimum 10 minutes; maximum 21 days and 3 hours). Note that the duration of a trace was not equal to the duration of an interval but computed as the time span between the first and last event in the trace.

## 8 Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced and applied a novel analytical method called human behavior mining by adapting process mining to the analysis of human behavior. Demonstrating its feasibility and potential, we investigated previously uncontested theoretical hypotheses in an mHealth use context. Subsequently, we discuss the findings in relation to the methodological and theoretical contributions of our research and its limitations and opportunities for future research.

## 8.1 Methodological Contributions

Our study offers several methodological contributions. We introduce process mining as a powerful approach for studying human behavior from a dynamic data-driven perspective, building on its analytical capabilities and drawing on the potential that has been recognized by many IS scholars (Abb et al., 2022; Esnaashari et al., 2022; Malinova et al., 2019; Pentland et al., 2021). Our four-part HBM framework provides a high-level methodological orientation for future research. By leveraging a large amount of automatically collected digital trace data on human behavior, we contribute to IS research by implementing data-driven approaches for the scientific discovery of dynamic phenomena (Frické, 2014; Maass et al., 2018) while maintaining a strong connection to theory (Grover et al., 2020). This approach ensures that the empirical findings are grounded in and contribute to the development of existing theories. To promote future applications of HBM, Appendix A provides answers to questions that scholars might have about adapting our framework to their specific research context. This helps to further strengthen our methodological contribution beyond the scope of this specific paper.

Besides contributing to IS, we also contribute to the further development of process mining methods in two ways. First, by studying the eventually-follows relations in a behavior log, we may discern the individual parts of a reciprocal relationship between different variables and analyze their prevalence and internal balance. This is noteworthy because IS researchers have so far focused on process mining as an analytical method to detect and theorize about changing behavior on an organizational level (Grisold et al., 2020; Pentland et al., 2021). By applying process mining techniques on event logs of user interactions, we extend the scope and demonstrate that process mining in the form of HBM can also be used to detect and theorize about human behavior at an individual level. This shift in focus not only broadens the application of process mining; it also opens new avenues for understanding the intricacies of behavior dynamics. It highlights the potential of HBM to provide valuable insights into dynamic behavioral patterns, feedback loops, and reciprocal relationships. As a second methodological contribution, our application of process prediction techniques, introduced in Section 6.2, provides a novel way to test theory-deduced hypotheses by means of process mining methods. This is noteworthy because, as mentioned above, process mining has thus far only been applied as an exploratory research method that did not provide statistical significance. This shift in paradigm widens the applicability of process mining as a research method for behavioral trace data, enhancing its value for researchers in IS and beyond.

Furthermore, the HBM framework is also a valuable methodological contribution for behavioral health researchers interested in data analysis approaches to inform and refine behavior change theories with a more dynamic perspective (Hekler et al., 2018; Wongvibulsin et al., 2020). Having the capability to analyze behavior at such a granular level can inform the development of better tailored and more effective mHealth solutions, ultimately leading to improved health outcomes. The use of HBM can facilitate the identification of patterns and trends that may not be immediately apparent through traditional methods.

## 8.2 Theoretical Contributions

The novel HBM research method provides a unique means to investigate human behavior theories in IS research. To demonstrate its capacity, we selected social cognitive theory. Our findings contribute to SCT in several key ways while also showcasing the broader potential of HBM to refine and develop other behavioral theories.

First, our study provides one of the first empirical validations of cause-effect relationships posited by SCT, specifically self-regulation and reciprocal determinism. Although SCT has traditionally addressed self-regulation and reciprocal determinism at a conceptual level (Bandura, 1978, 1991), our research is the first empirical investigation into these dynamic concepts. This builds on the existing knowledge in SCT by revealing the structures between specific variables involved in self-regulation and reciprocal determinism. We achieved this by exploring the constructs that facilitate the dynamic and reciprocal relationships proposed by SCT. To do so, we deduced relevant mHealth features based on SCT, revealed the structures between variables in SCT concepts, and analyzed digital trace data to capture dynamic relationships. Our research not only validates SCT constructs but also demonstrates the utility of digital trace data and HBM in enriching theoretical frameworks within IS research.

Second, we explicitly evaluate the dynamic relationships proposed by SCT, which, to the best of our knowledge, have not yet been investigated or statistically analyzed. Although SCT was originally based on assumptions of dynamic behavior, these assumptions are largely implicit in the original theorizing (e.g., Beauchamp et al., 2019). We leveraged the potential of digital trace data and HBM to evaluate these dynamic relationships empirically. Our findings provide empirical evidence that many of the proposed theoretical interdependencies do indeed occur during mHealth use, although their reciprocity remains questionable. Consequently, our research contributes to SCT by exploring and predicting the proposed interdependencies and reciprocal relationships, offering valuable insights for theoretical progress. By moving beyond static depictions of SCT, we capture the complexities of human behavior in more detail. The promising results from our application of HBM to SCT can pave the way for similar advancements in other theoretical frameworks.

Third, our empirical findings suggest several potential areas for refining SCT. Our data does not support the reciprocal relationships posited in self-regulation. Additionally, while the interdependencies in reciprocal determinism can be observed in our data, they are not symmetrically reciprocal because the triggering effect is unequal, i.e., it is stronger in one direction than the other. These findings indicate that the dynamic relationships proposed by SCT do not hold consistently during mHealth use. This discrepancy is likely due to the complexity of human behavior, which is influenced by numerous factors both within and beyond the interactions with the IS. SCT originally considered social, environmental, and cognitive factors in face-to-face contexts. Directly transferring these factors to an mHealth context, however, might introduce interactions that SCT does not account for. In our study, cognitive, environmental, and behavioral factors were considered in the narrow relation to mHealth features that users interacted with. A broader range of contextual factors occurring outside mHealth use, such as in-person social interactions, beliefs, and attitudes about behavior, and time constraints, likely also influenced the users’ behavior. Consequently, SCT may not fully capture all the relevant factors involved in human behavior in an mHealth context. Future research should consider additional factors that might influence the reciprocal relationships involved in human behavior when users interact with an IS and contextual factors occurring outside mHealth use. Methodological reasons for these findings are discussed in the limitations section below.

Beyond our contributions to SCT, our research demonstrates the broader theoretical potential of human behavior mining. HBM provides researchers with empirical tools to refine existing theories by analyzing dynamic feedback loops, interdependencies, and reciprocal relationships over time intervals of variable length. Additionally, HBM offers a pathway for developing new theoretical models and propositions in digital behavioral research. By capturing granular, real time data on individual interactions within digital environments, HBM can uncover behavioral patterns that traditional theories may overlook. This capability not only strengthens existing frameworks but also lays the groundwork for advancing our theoretical understanding of human behavior in an increasingly digital world. Researchers employing HBM in their work can leverage its data-driven insights to contribute to a wide range of behavioral theories, providing new perspectives on dynamic and reciprocal relationships while uncovering novel patterns that can inform the development of entirely new theoretical models.

## 8.3 Limitations and Future Research

Our work is subject to a few methodological and theoretical constraints, which ultimately provide avenues for future research. Methodologically, one limitation of HBM is that it can only analyze behavior that was digitally recorded, i.e., captured in the trace data of log files. This implies that our approach cannot consider unrecorded (contextual) behavior or human cognition and emotion. For these reasons, HBM may be supplemented with additional research methods. For example, SCT considers self-efficacy, which refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to successfully perform and achieve specific tasks or goals (Bandura, 1991). High self-efficacy can enhance motivation for behavioral change (Bandura, 1991). Because HBM can only analyze behavior that is digitally recorded, self-efficacy could not be captured in our data analysis. Future research could investigate the role of self-efficacy on the dynamic feedback loops proposed using a mixed methods approach for capturing subjective user cognition during mHealth use. Combining quantitative data from digital traces with data from self-reports, interviews, or surveys could provide a richer and more comprehensive understanding of the interplay between recorded behavior and underlying cognitive and emotional factors.

Another methodological limitation relates to the null findings for the interdependencies proposed in H1a, H1b, and H2b, as well as the null findings for the reciprocal relationships. These nonsignificant results can be explained by the relatively short time period of our study (H1a and H1b) and the suggested initial goal value (H2b). When tracking goal progress over the course of four weeks, users cannot be expected to change their goal multiple times, especially if it is already set to a realistic value, such as the WHO recommendation of 150 minutes. As proposed by SCT, individuals must eventually transcend the feedback loop to initiate more challenging courses for themselves, reflected through goal adjustment (Bandura, 1991). Again, a four-week period might not be sufficient for participants to experience significant changes in their behavior or to adjust their goals multiple times. We based our study time period on other research that has looked at mHealth, goals, and physical activity behavior (Degroote et al., 2021; Klasnja et al., 2019). However, to better capture these dynamics, future research should consider extending the study period, allowing participants more time to engage with their goals and potentially initiate new challenges. A longer study period would also provide a stronger basis for testing reciprocal relationships more robustly, as a longer time frame may be necessary to observe these dynamic processes. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of how dynamic and reciprocal relationships in SCT unfold over time in the context of mHealth use.

An additional methodological constraint relates to choosing users’ individual physical activities as our case notion. This allowed us to study relations between the individual app features without relying on fixed time intervals. However, differences in physical activity frequency between the individual users led to differences in case length, such that some of the observed feedback loops might not have been executed in temporal proximity, thereby weakening their assumed impact. This issue could be addressed in the future by using object-centric process mining techniques (van der Aalst, 2019) that do not require an explicit case notion.

From a theoretical point of view, our findings on symmetric reciprocity should only be considered as initial evidence towards a more nuanced understanding of reciprocal relationships in SCT during mHealth use. Testing dynamic relationships offers opportunities to shape and refine theories by capturing temporal changes and understanding feedback loops (Scholz, 2019). Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to observe how these reciprocal relationships evolve over time in order to provide deeper insights into the adaptive nature of SCT concepts within the mHealth context. Moreover, incorporating dynamic models in other theories on human behavior will allow for the identification of critical points and nonlinear relationships, enhancing our ability to predict and intervene in health behaviors more effectively. This approach not only strengthens the theoretical premises of theories on human behavior but also improves the practical application in developing more responsive and personalized mHealth interventions.

## 8.4 Conclusion

In this study, we introduce human behavior mining (HBM) as a novel analytical method for studying dynamics in human behavior through process mining. By applying HBM in the context of mHealth use, we demonstrate its feasibility and uncover critical insights into the dynamics of social cognitive theory (SCT). This work not only contributes methodologically by extending the application of process mining to individual behavior analysis but also offers theoretical advancements by providing empirical evidence on the interdependencies proposed by SCT in an mHealth context. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies, mixed methods approaches, and incorporating broader contextual factors to further explore and validate these findings. Ultimately, our HBM framework paves the way for more dynamic, data-driven, and theoretically grounded approaches for understanding and influencing human behavior in the digital age

## Appendix A: HBM Questions and Answers

To enable future adoptions of HBM, this section provides answers to the most important questions that scholars might have when applying HBM in a research project.

<table><tr><td>Q: What data do I need to apply HBM?</td></tr><tr><td>You need a collection of events. Each event needs to contain three (mandatory) data points: (1) an activity, which describes the recorded behavior, (2) the time at which the behavior was recorded, and (3) a case notion which groups the events together into units of analysis.</td></tr><tr><td>Q: What are potential data sources for applying HBM, and is it necessary to develop a custom app or rely on publicly available data?</td></tr><tr><td>The choice of data source for HBM depends on the research goals. Publicly available datasets are suitable for exploratory or large-scale studies, while custom apps are necessary for collecting specific, theory-driven data or ensuring data quality. A hybrid approach can combine the benefits of both, depending on the study&#x27;s requirements.</td></tr><tr><td>Q: Is it necessary to develop hypotheses prior to data collection, or can the analysis follow a quasi-experimental approach with post hoc theorizing?</td></tr><tr><td>The choice between ex ante and post hoc theorizing in HBM depends on the research goal. Ex ante theorizing with an experimental setup is ideal for testing specific hypotheses and ensuring scientific rigor, while post hoc, quasi-experimental approaches are better for exploring patterns in naturalistic or publicly available data. A hybrid approach, starting with broad hypotheses and refining them post-analysis, could also be used to combine the strengths of both methods.</td></tr><tr><td>Q: How do I define the activities?</td></tr><tr><td>The activities should be defined prior to conducting the HBM analysis. If you have done ex ante theorizing, it makes sense to define the activities directly from the theoretical constructs that you are interested in, devising a mechanism for mapping the raw events in your data. In the case of post hoc theorizing, the activities will be defined from the events that are already found in the data. In most cases, however, the events should be preprocessed, e.g., to exclude irrelevant ones or to aggregate similar events into more meaningful activities.</td></tr><tr><td>Q: How fine-granular should my activities be?</td></tr><tr><td>Again, this depends on your research goal. In general, the more fine-granular the activities are, the more difficult it becomes to identify patterns in their execution because there are so many possible execution variants, but the more interesting these patterns become. Therefore, we recommend defining the activities on multiple levels of granularity, organized in a tree-like structure: If the most fine-granular activity definition does not yield meaningful patterns, you can then abstract the activities (all or some) to the next-higher level and hope that you find more meaningful patterns there.</td></tr><tr><td>Q: What if my activities do not align with the theory constructs?</td></tr><tr><td>It is the responsibility of the researcher to ensure the alignment between activities and theory constructs. If you use HBM in the ex ante theorizing fashion, this alignment can be ensured by choosing an appropriate mapping and, ideally, collecting the data in a way that is determined by the theory. However, in a post hoc theorizing application, such a mapping cannot be ensured. It is acceptable to, e.g., exclude events that are not relevant to the theory constructs, merge multiple events into one activity, or map multiple activities to the same theory construct, given that this makes sense in the given research constructs. However, HBM will not help to squeeze insights out of data that is not in the same scope as the research focus in question.</td></tr><tr><td>Q: How do I choose an appropriate case notion?</td></tr><tr><td>Again, the choice of a case notion depends on the research goals and cannot be universally answered. A case notion groups multiple events, such that they are analyzed jointly. This also means that you will not be able to see (direct) relations between events in different cases. This should be taken into account when choosing a case notion for a specific HBM project.</td></tr><tr><td>Q: Can I have multiple case notions?</td></tr><tr><td>Yes. In many cases, it makes sense to analyze your data multiple times, using different case notions. For example, you can analyze the journey of a user over time (using “users” as your case notion) and the behavior of users in one day (using “days” as your case notion).</td></tr><tr><td>Q: Are time stamps really mandatory?</td></tr><tr><td>No. Technically, you only need an ordering mechanism that puts your events within one case in a sequential order. However, without time stamps, you cannot analyze, e.g., durations between events, which can be helpful for your analysis.</td></tr><tr><td>Q: What can I use additional attributes for?</td></tr><tr><td>If your events have additional attributes, you can use them for additional analyses of all sorts. For example, you can use them to separate your event log into multiple sub-logs for a more detailed analysis or you can look at correlations between attributes and event patterns.</td></tr></table>

## Appendix B: Foundations of Process Mining

## Business Processes and Process Models

To fabricate and deliver products or services, organizations execute business processes, i.e., series of activities carried out within a specific organizational and technical context to achieve a business goal (Weske, 2019). Business processes are captured as process models to increase their transparency and comprehensibility, enable their analysis, and facilitate their automation. In Figure B1, a simplified purchase-to-pay (P2P) process is depicted as a business process model and notation (BPMN) process diagram (Object Management Group, 2013). First, a purchase order is created. Once goods and the invoice are received, a three-way matching is conducted to check that the amounts on the invoice and goods receipt match the amounts on the purchase order. Only if this is the case can the invoice be cleared and the process completed. Otherwise, the issue in the matching needs to be clarified before the matching is repeated.

Figure B1. Example Purchase-to-Pay Process (P2P) as a BPMN Diagram

Process models can represent different perspectives on a set of activities (Dumas et al., 2018):

Control flow: The control flow encapsulates the order relation between activities, indicated by the solid arrows and the diamond-shaped gateways. For example, the goods and invoice receipt can occur concurrently, indicated by the parallel gateway. The three-way matching can only be conducted after the goods and invoice have been received. The progression of the process after the three-way-matching depends on its outcome, as indicated by the exclusive gateway and the annotations on its outgoing edges: If the amounts match, the invoice is cleared. Otherwise, the issue must be clarified before the matching is restarted.

Data: The data perspective captures the data requirements of the activities, e.g., in the form of documents. In our example, the three activities “create purchase order,” “receive invoice,” and “receive goods” each create a document, which the three-way matching needs to be executed. Output and input relations between documents and activities are indicated by dashed arrows.

Resources: The resource perspective denotes responsible actors per activity, illustrated as pools and lanes. Our example has two relevant actors (lanes): The finance specialist receives and clears the invoice; the procurement specialist conducts the other activities.

BPMN is a very expressive modeling language, but due to its complexity, it can be hard for nonexperts to understand (Recker & Dreiling, 2011). Therefore, processes are often modeled in simpler notations, such as the directly-follows graph (DFG) (van der Aalst, 2022). In Figure B2, the P2P process from Figure B1 is displayed as a DFG. Compared to the BPMN, this model contains only activities and control-flow relations. An arrow between two activities denotes a directly-follows relation. For example, after creating a purchase order, either the goods or the invoice can be received. It is important to note that DFGs are simpler but less expressive than BPMN diagrams. As an example, the DFG in Figure B2 does not distinguish between the parallel gateway and the repetition shown in Figure B1.

## Event Logs and Cases

Business processes are often supported by information systems, which record data on individual process executions in so-called event logs (van der Aalst, 2012). An event log denotes a collection of event sequences, called traces (van der Aalst, 2016). Each trace relates to a case, i.e., an individual execution of a process, which constitutes a single unit of analysis. Each event represents the execution of a certain activity, i.e., a well-defined step in a process. Therefore, an event e is denoted as a tuple (case, activity, time stamp), where case refers to the trace to which the event belongs, activity denotes the executed process activity, and time stamp denotes the time at which the event was recorded. Both events and cases can have additional attributes, such as the resource that executed the activity or the overall cost that a case has incurred.

For any event log, the case notion determines how the events are grouped into traces. In a healthcare process, one case could be a single patient (Pentland et al., 2021). For most processes, however, there is not one obvious case notion (van der Aalst, 2019). Instead, the choice depends on the analysis, and different choices lead to different views on the process. In a purchasing process, events can, e.g., be grouped by purchase orders or by purchase order items. Whereas the former is better suited for an analysis of dependencies between different purchase order items, the latter allows for a direct mapping of payment activities onto individual items.

Table B1 displays an example event log for the P2P process from Figure B1. Each row in the table corresponds to one event, with a case ID, an activity, a time stamp, a resource, and a corresponding purchase order (PO) number. The log contains 12 events in two cases. Because this P2P process is executed once per created PO, one case corresponds to one PO document, which is noted as a case attribute in the column “PO.” Besides the mandatory event attributes (case, activity, time stamp), the event log provides the resource as an optional event attribute.

Two POs are initiated in this log: Case 101 for PO 2040 and Case 102 for PO 5413. Molly is the responsible procurement specialist for both cases. As their role as finance specialist, Aaron handles Case 102, and Zoe handles Case 101. Although both POs are created on the same day, case 101 progresses much faster than case 102. This is caused by the earlier goods receipt, which occurs after two days for 101, but takes a week for 102. We also see that the three-way matching for 101 was apparently successful on the first try because Zoe cleared the invoice immediately afterwards. For 102, Molly must repeat the three-way matching and clarify an issue in between.

## Process Mining

Process mining subsumes all techniques that analyze process-related event logs with the objective of improving the underlying process. Process mining research has its roots in theoretical computer science and is driven by analytical capabilities (van der Aalst, 2016): Researchers develop sophisticated techniques for the data-driven identification, visualization, analysis, and eventual improvement of business processes (Pentland et al., 2020).

![](/api/attachments/ZJ4QPNRM/fulltext/images/63e0bd9f7c48aaf4f1ba3ba88bf61b221931095a61a9b1a0ca783ed828a6addd.jpg)  
Figure B3. Simplified Overview of Process Mining (adapted from van der Aalst 2022)

Figure B3 outlines the process mining analysis pipeline (van der Aalst, 2022). Process mining starts with one or multiple information systems, which support the execution of business processes and therefore contain processrelated event data. These data are extracted from the system and transformed into an event log to be analyzed by process mining techniques. After an initial exploration to better understand the data, the captured process is described by discovering a process model. Usually, this discovered as-is process model differs from the designed to-be process model. Along with the event log, it serves as the basis for a more intricate process analysis. This might include process conformance, i.e., the comparison of as-is and to-be process behavior to find commonalities and discrepancies; process performance, i.e., the identification of bottlenecks or other process execution problems; and process diagnostics, i.e., the identification of potential causes for compliance or performance problems. The obtained insights can be used to shape the future process by predicting and eventually improving it.
