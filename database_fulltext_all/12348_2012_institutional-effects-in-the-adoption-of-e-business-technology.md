# Institutional effects in the adoption of e-business-technology Evidence from the German automotive supplier industry

Markus Hertwig ⁎

Universität Duisburg-Essen, Fakultät für Gesellschaftswissenschaften/Faculty of Social Sciences, Institut Arbeit und Qualifikation (IAQ)/ Institute for Work, Skills and Training, Forsthausweg 2, LE 626, 47057 Duisburg, Germany

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 21 October 2011 Received in revised form 19 June 2012 Accepted 21 June 2012 Available online 21 July 2012

## a b s t r a c t

This article analyzes the adoption of B2B-e-business technology in German automotive supplier companies during the internet hype period, i.e. in the very early stage of technology diffusion. While from the outset e-business was assumed to be used by companies in order to increase ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and reduce costs, some previous IOS research and theoretical considerations suggest that both ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and environmental expectations can be important reasons for companies to implement e-business. On this backdrop, the paper analyzes the reasons for e-business technology adoption, focusing on the explanatory value of institutional factors like mimesis, coercion, and normative pressures. Theoretically based hypotheses are developed and tested using regression models with a quantitative data set of more than 1900 companies.

The article tries to make an innovative contribution to the <sup>fi</sup>eld of IOS research, <sup>fi</sup>rst, by drawing on theories of organizational structuring — new institutionalism, social psychology, and transaction cost economics; second, variation of adoption rationales in different types of companies and different business functions inside a company is taken into account. It appears that the adoption of e-business is explained by external pressures from other customers, bandwagon effects and herding behavior during the e-business hype, and expectations and interests of professionals inside a company. In the ‘core’ areas of a <sup>fi</sup>rm, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency rationales play an important role, too. The data reveals that the reasons of e-business usage differ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly by department and company type.

## 1. Introduction

Since the 1990s, internet-based technologies have become wide-spread tools for <sup>fi</sup>rms to support a variety of business processes (for example see Liu & Vijayaraman, 2007). Around the year 2000, with the rise of the new economy, many industries faced an ‘e-business hype’. Firms expected that modern internet tools would become a precondition for enhancing ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and maintaining competitiveness. While the media. management scholars and consultant agencies have pointed to the overall positive effects of B2B-e-business<sup>1</sup> and focused on advising promising ways to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from e-business technology (see Papazoglou & Ribbers, 2006; Fox, 2006, for instance), little attention has been paid to the risks and obstacles <sup>fi</sup>rms face when redesigning their structures and processes with e-business technology. If e-business technology per se is a prerequisite for coping with the challenges of the global economy, why are many companies reluctant to use electronic business? Why do some stick to more simple technologies only, like email, while others invest in highly advanced e-business systems?

The purpose of this paper is to determine the rationales for the adoption of e-business technology by automotive supplier plants in an early period of innovation diffusion, i.e. between 2001 and 2002, under circumstances of a ‘hype’ situation. To this end, I seek to apply recent theories of organizational structuring to the <sup>fi</sup>eld of technology diffusion. While there has been valuable research on IOS as well as on e-business diffusion (see Robey et al., 2008), in this study two broader concepts of organization research are utilized in order to explain the adoption of e-business among suppliers: the new institutionalism of the sociology of organizations (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Meyer & Rowan, 1977); and transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1985; Coleman, 1994).

I refer to those two broader theoretical paradigms for three reasons. Firstly, in a ‘hype’ situation, rational explanations for IOS adoption might faint as social expectations, fads and herding behavior gain importance (Hirschheim et al., 2012). New institutionalism and social psychology provide some theoretical rationales to analyze those processes, some of which have been successfully applied to the <sup>fi</sup>eld of IOS in previous research (see Fichman, 2004; Teo et al., 2003: 41); however, as the sector under investigation is assumed to be highly competitive, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency reasons cannot be excluded from the theoretical concept. Second, linking IOS adoption research more closely to broader paradigms in sociology and economics may support the connection to current debates in social sciences. The theories chosen here appear to provide highly sophisticated rationales, enabling to ground the explanation in elaborated theoretical frameworks of (contradicting) assumptions about organizational behavior.<sup>2</sup> Thirdly, as the new institutionalism emphasizes power relations and legitimacy instead of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, it is capable of avoiding the ‘pro-innovation-bias’ because, confronted with institutional pressures, companies “may adopt innovations that are not right for them and, thus, experience outcomes that fail to meet expectations” (Robey et al., 2008: 509). Transaction cost economics, on the other hand, provides a good counterpart which has already been widely employed in organization science and IOS studies.

In the remainder, both theories will be presented brie<sup>fl</sup>y and discussed with respect to the possible explanations for e-business adoption. I will point out that the theoretical models reveal different rationales for the diffusion of e-business.<sup>3</sup> E-business technology diffusion might be triggered by <sup>fi</sup>rms strategically planning to increase ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (market pressures); it can also stem from ‘political processes’ in the automotive industry, which is characterized by the asymmetric allocation of power in customer–supplier-relationships. Finally, as companies are embedded in their institutional environments, normative expectations or cultural demands might account for e-business technology usage among suppliers. Based on our theoretical re<sup>fl</sup>ections, hypotheses will be developed and tested using multivariate models based on data from 1902 companies within the German automotive supply industry.

## 2. Basic characteristics of e-business-technology and the German automotive supply industry

Concerning e-business technology adoption, the automotive industry appears to be a good research <sup>fi</sup>eld for different reasons: Firstly, since in the beginning of the internet age automotive companies were ‘pace-makers’ (Mühge et al., 2004). The industry has always been a forerunner regarding the development and use of innovative technology (Freyssenet et al., 1998), and also in the internet-based business, automotive OEMs (original equipment manufacturers) and their suppliers took the lead. Second, the global car industry is highly competitive. Market pressures force OEMs and suppliers to constantly detect and exploit <sup>fi</sup>elds for enhancing ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and reducing costs, while at the same time increasing quality and innovativeness (Boyer et al., 1998; Womack et al., 1990). One might expect that the competition deriving from the globalized market is the ‘whip’ for ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency-enhancing innovation throughout the industry. Third, the German supply industry is very heterogeneous which is partly a result of restructuring waves that occurred since the 1990s. In their aim to focus on their core business, OEMs chose to outsource a number of (highly complex) business functions (modules or systems), which fostered a new ‘division of labor’ among automotive assemblers and their suppliers and the evolution of a new supplier type — the so-called ‘<sup>fi</sup>rst tier’ supplier. As a result, the industry is characterized by long supply chains, which comprise different tiers (a supplier hierarchy). With the OEMs at the top, at least three different supplier types can be distinguished: the system suppliers (<sup>fi</sup>rst tiers), which develop and (pre-) assemble complex systems and modules (for example the front end of a car) through modular production.<sup>4</sup> Their products are often delivered directly to the OEM's assembly line (Lung & Volpato, 2002). The second (component manufacturers) and third tiers (parts) are mostly small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), which nevertheless make up the largest part of the industry.

It is noteworthy that developing and assembling cars now required much more cooperation (at least between OEMs and systems suppliers), which triggered the usage of IOS (Mühge et al., 2004). With the spread of internet technology, some OEMs as well as big suppliers created their own e-business sourcing platforms, like SupplyOn or Covisint, which certainly affected the adoption dynamics in the sector (I return to this issue in Section 4).

Today, internet technologies can be found in nearly all areas of social life. Since the purpose of this paper is to discover the rationales and motives for the adoption of internet technologies in automotive supplier companies, I focus on internet applications which are used to support business transactions. Thus, our analytical focus is limited to business-to-business (B2B) systems (e-business) while transactions between companies (businesses) and consumers (B2C) or administration (B2A), for instance, are not considered.

E-business is a generic term which comprises many different technologies (for instance, Liu & Vijayaraman, 2007). In order to access the <sup>fi</sup>eld, in this study I use a number of categories or distinctions: In some previous research. internet technology has been distinguished according to its complexity. Email and static websites are regarded as low complex systems while more complex systems comprise websites with certain functions or the exchange of data via the internet. In this study, e-business technology refers only to complex internet systems. Another distinction can be made between the business functions (or departments) of purchasing, sales, production/logistics, and research and development (R&D). During the e-business hype public attention was attracted by e-business applications for purchasing and sales in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place. In those areas, e-business technology comprises a set of applications: with reverse auctions (E-Auctions), a focal company increases competition among its suppliers which compete for tenders. In a similar way, electronic requests for quotes or bids (E-RFQ or E-RFB) require suppliers to submit quotes not with a printed letter but by <sup>fi</sup>lling out an online form. Another technology is the E-Catalog, which enables companies to more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently process orders of MRO parts, for instance.

Especially OEMs and <sup>fi</sup>rst tier suppliers have been using information and communication technology (ICT) in the production and logistics department for a long time. Particularly larger companies have set up electronic data-interchange (EDI) or electronic data interchange for administration, commerce and transport (EDIFACT) for logistic and production operations (Iacovou et al., 1995). In this study, I consider e-business technology which comprises the internet-version of those tools: web-EDI, electronic computer aided manufacturing (E-CAM), E-Logistics, or internet based inventory management, which allow for exchange of data about inventory or production planning, for instance. In R&D departments, virtual reality tools and E-CAD ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the same functions as previous technologies, but may enable different companies to jointly participate in the design process by having access to the same data via internet databases.

Basically, all those e-business systems facilitate new options for organizational re-structuring and increasing ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. However, the logic of e-business systems used for market transactions and those used for production or R&D is different: while in the purchasing and sales departments e-business aims at enhancing the “broker effect” (transparency, market functions) in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place, in production/logistics and R&D e-business is designed to integrate and optimize communication processes (Malone et al., 1987)

At the end of 2002 (when the data for this analysis was collected) about one third of all German supplier companies used e-business in their sales (31%) or purchasing (35%) departments. Compared to those <sup>fi</sup>gures, e-business-systems were rarely used in production/logistics (23%) and R&D (12%). The usage rates vary signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly between the different supplier types, with system suppliers using e-business technology more often than component and parts suppliers (Mühge et al., 2004).<sup>5</sup> Only 7.4% of the suppliers were “of<sup>fl</sup>ine”, which means that they did not use any internet based technology. Most of the suppliers used complex systems (63.5%), allowing for exchange of data or providing speci<sup>fi</sup>c functionality. Only 29.1% used internet systems with low complexity like emails or static websites.

## 3. Theories of organizational structuring and IT adoption

The following section contains a brief review of current theories of technology diffusion and organizational structuring that are capable of explaining why e-business technology is used in automotive supplier companies. A lot of previous research on innovation diffusion has provided valuable insights in the reasons for technology diffusion, like EDI or also internet technology (see Robey et al., 2008 for an overview). Many of those studies reported that a variety of factors are relevant, emphasizing the role of the external environment, organizational readiness, perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, transaction characteristics (Chwelos et al., 2001; Iacovou et al., 1995) and also cultural and institutional aspects like mimetic in<sup>fl</sup>uences (Teo et al., 2003; Zhu et al., 2003). A number of those factors can already be found in the classical diffusion of innovation theory (Rogers, 1995). However, on the one hand, in the e-business hype period environmental expectations assumedly play an important role, drawing attention to institutional factors; on the other hand, the high competition in the automotive sector points to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency rationales which are explicitly conceptualized by transaction cost economics. As the latter account has already been used in a number of studies, our research focuses the role of institutional accounts

## 3.1. Transaction cost economics

Rational choice theory assumes that companies pursue the goal of pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximization (Milgrom & Roberts, 1992: 40). As a part of the new institutional economics, transaction cost theory is based on rational choice assumptions, however, providing some additional assumptions exceeding the simple maximization rule. The goal of transaction cost economics is to explain the selection of alternative institutional arrangements for conducting transactions (Williamson, 1985). Transaction cost theory assumes that <sup>fi</sup>rms select an appropriate form for organizing transactions based on a calculation of transaction and production costs which are mainly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by uncertainty and opportunism, asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city, and transaction frequency. Some scholars developed hypotheses about the effects of ICT based on transaction cost ideas. Malone et al. propose that ICT has an information, a communication, and a brokerage effect, which in<sup>fl</sup>uence the cost structure and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of ICT usage (Malone et al., 1987; for the debate about the effects of ICT on organization's transactions see also Holland & Lockett, 1994; Mühge et al., 2004). In short, ICT can be used strategically to reduce opportunism, moral hazards, and uncertainty (information effect); to increase inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm interaction and cooperation (communication effect); or to reduce ex ante transaction costs by enhancing transparency concerning prices, alternative suppliers, or other market information (broker effect).

## 3.2. New institutionalism

In recent years the new institutionalism has become “the leading perspective among organizational sociologists” (Boxenbaum & Jonsson, 2008; Mizruchi & Fein, 1999: 678). Scholars from that stream of thought focus on legitimacy as an important condition for organizational survival, which can be gained through compliance to institutionalized expectations. Once an organizational form or practice is institutionalized it works as a compelling model: “[O]rganizations are driven to incorporate the practices and procedures de<sup>fi</sup>ned by prevailing rationalized concepts of organizational work and institutionalized in society. Organizations that do so increase their legitimacy and their survival prospects, independent of the immediate ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of that acquired practices and procedures” (Meyer & Rowan, 1977: 340). In opposition to the rational ‘ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency-perspective’, institutionalism argues that, under certain conditions, conformity is more important than performance, i.e. ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and cost control.

Environmental in<sup>fl</sup>uences on organizations have been captured theoretically by a number of concepts of which the organizational field has gained much attention. Following DiMaggio and Powell, an organizational <sup>fi</sup>eld consists of “organizations that, in the aggregate, constitute a recognized area of institutional life: key suppliers, resource and product consumers, regulatory agencies, and other organizations that produce similar services or products” (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983: 148). In essence, the concept is deeply based on cultural– cognitive ideas because <sup>fi</sup>eld organizations are assumed to interact more frequently with one another than with outsiders and to be part of a common meaning system which comprises typical world views, social norms and rules (Scott, 1994: 207-8.).

In their seminal paper, DiMaggio and Powell argued that structural isomorphism is the product of three institutional processes that occur inside organizational <sup>fi</sup>elds. Those mechanisms can be regarded as channels by which organizations are effected by institutionalized cultural and environmental expectations: coercive, mimetic, and normative isomorphism.<sup>6</sup> Coercion stems from dependency and power. Organizations may be forced to conform to the expectations of customers, suppliers, the state, or other in<sup>fl</sup>uential actors in the <sup>fi</sup>eld (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983: 150). The institutional process of mimesis assumes that organizations imitate other organizations which are regarded to be more successful or legitimate. The concept is consistent with what has been termed herd behavior or bandwagon effect in social psychology (cf. Abrahamson & Rosenkopf, 1997; Banerjee, 1992; Rook, 2006). This concept is based on the idea that, in any kind of social network, diffusion of technology (or ideas, products etc.) is affected by pressures which evolve when a large number of organizations has already adopted an innovation (Granovetter, 1985). Organizational leaders do ‘what everybody else does’, and hence, organizational structures appear to be shaped by the behavior of other organizations which are either regarded a reference group or belong to the same organizational <sup>fi</sup>eld. Organizations may also follow certain trends or fashions in order to appear modern or progressive, and thereby gain legitimacy (Abrahamson, 1996).<sup>7</sup> It has been argued that one important source of those herding or mimetic processes is ambiguity or uncertainty:

“Modeling, as we use the term, is a response to uncertainty. (…) When organizational technologies are poorly understood (…), when goals are ambiguous, or when the environment creates symbolic uncertainty, organizations may model themselves on other organizations” (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983: 151).

In a similar way, social psychologists argued that organizations seek alignment with a reference group and with organizations that share the same values and opinions. However, it has been noted that innovations may rather diffuse through weak ties than through direct links between organizations (Granovetter, 1973). Hence, organizational leaders would not imitate organizations which appear most similar, but mainly those which are similar in certain aspects (Turner, 1991). In large part, those assumptions are consistent with the institutionalist concepts attached to organizational <sup>fi</sup>eld approach. Interestingly, both emphasize that, in stark contrast to economic models of organizational structuring, innovations are being adopted even if they do not appear rational or ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency enhancing (Meyer & Rowan, 1977).

Normative institutional pressures derive from professionalization and the efforts of the members of an occupation to determine legitimate standards of their work and the hurdles for entering an occupation (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983: 152). Two sources of normative pressures can be distinguished. When members of a profession receive similar training, they become socialized into similar worldviews. Further, professional and trade associations provide occasions for interaction (like regular workshops or symposia) which foster the diffusion of ideas among professionals (Mizruchi & Fein, 1999: 657). Members of occupations, thus, tend to develop typical shared views of appropriate organizational methods or behavior that in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence organizational structure.

## 4. Hypotheses

The purpose of this section is to derive hypotheses from the theories discussed above about the rationales of automotive supplier companies to implement e-business technologies. The short overview of e-business technology and the automotive industry allows us to propose two preliminary assumptions that will guide our further considerations: First, in the various business functions of a company distinct e-business systems are in use. As the logic of those e-business systems varies it can be expected that the reasons for diffusion strongly differ by department. Second, due to the heterogeneity of the automotive supply industry, motives for e-business adoption may vary by segment, i.e. within the supplier hierarchy.

## 4.1. New institutionalism

Concerning previous IOS research, Teo and colleagues found all three institutional mechanisms conceptualized by DiMaggio and Powell to be important predictors of intention to adopt <sup>fi</sup>nancial electronic data interchange (Teo et al., 2003). Basically, new institutionalism offers a large reservoir for assumptions and sources for hypotheses. However, the three mechanisms have been applied diversely in previous research (Mizruchi & Fein, 1999), which is partly due to the fact that theoretical concepts are sometimes vague.<sup>8</sup> In the subsequent sections, I discuss each of the three main institutional mechanisms as possible sources for the adoption of e-business.

(a) With respect to the structure and inter-dependencies in the automobile supply industry it can be assumed that companies use e-business technology because of customer coercion. Since OEMs and system suppliers control important resources in the industry they can probably exercise power to make their suppliers conform to their wishes. Further, it seems plausible to assume that the lower the tier status – and, thus, the more standardized and replaceable a product – the more likely coercive isomorphism will occur.

Hypothesis A1A. Suppliers adopt e-business technology to conform to customer requirements. Parts manufacturers (third tiers) are more likely to conform to coercion than second or <sup>fi</sup>rst tier suppliers.

Coercion is probably more relevant in companies which depend on other companies which can enforce the implementation of e-business (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978), or in companies which face coercive pressure to conform as they are more deeply embedded in the social system of the automotive sector through alliances or company networks. A broad indicator for those aspects is the share of turnover a company earns through business with other automotive companies (Granovetter, 1985).

Hypothesis A1B. Suppliers which highly depend on the automotive industry are more likely to implement e-business technology.

(b) Mimetic isomorphism is considered the central and most widely utilized concept of institutional theory (Mizruchi & Fein, 1999). However, the application of mimesis in empirical studies is diverse, and many attempts to empirically discover imitation have been discussed controversially. Therefore it seem reasonable to go back to some central premises of both new institutionalism and social psychology of herd behavior which emphasize the role of uncertainty. DiMaggio and Powell (1983: 149) refer to a ‘poor understanding of technologies’ or ‘ambiguous goals’ when they claim that uncertainty is a trigger for imitation. During the e-business hype, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the new technology were widely discussed in the media — but it was rather a common belief that e-business technology would offer great ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains which was not proven. Further, it appeared as if large numbers of suppliers were already using e-business — which, according to our data, was only partly true: Indeed, only 7.4% of the suppliers declared to have no internet at all (see Section 2), but at the same time, even in purchasing e-business ‘density’ was still only 35%. Case study research revealed that sometimes organizational leaders had strong doubts concerning the consequences of e-business technology and were in large part unable to estimate the risks and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Hertwig, 2008); especially SMEs revealed a poor understanding of how e-business technology might affect their business processes. But as it appeared to many that the majority of suppliers were already one step ahead, social pressure emerged which made organizational leaders jump on the bandwagon. Consequently, we assume that in this ambiguous situation suppliers were likely to conform to prevailing practice in the organizational <sup>fi</sup>eld and chose to imitate other suppliers which already used e-business.

Hypothesis A2A. A high level of uncertainty will increase the likelihood of e-business technology adoption.

When e-business technology is – as assumed – a wide-spread institutionalized model, large suppliers which are monitored by the media and exposed to public observation are more likely to use e-business technology because they are more dependent on public encouragement and legitimacy (Mezias, 1995). For example, it would be more harmful for huge multinationals like General Motors or Ford to obtain an image as ‘technologically old-fashioned’ or ‘anti-modernist’ than it would be for a small parts supplier, which may have more latitude implementing the latest technological innovations. Hence, large companies or headquarters of trusts may implement e-business technology more often in order to ensure public support, to be perceived as modern, and to gain legitimacy.<sup>9</sup>

Hypothesis A2B. Large suppliers or supplier company headquarters are more likely to use e-business technology.

(c) Normative pressures to use e-business technology potentially arise from four different sources. First, manager-controlled <sup>fi</sup>rms behave differently from owner-controlled ones (Salomon & Smith, 1979; Williamson, 1967). As opposed to owners, managers do not own the <sup>fi</sup>rm, possess less power, and therefore have to prove their capabilities. The individual career of a manager far more often depends on short-term success. Using the most advanced techniques, organizational fashions and following bandwagons helps management to demonstrate that it is aware of the best methods to increase the <sup>fi</sup>rm's performance (Abrahamson, 1996). In this respect, e-business technology is a means to maintain their own legitimacy even in case the <sup>fi</sup>rm should fail.

Hypothesis A3A. Manager-controlled suppliers are more likely to use e-business technology than owner-controlled ones.

A supplementary reason for the expected effect of management control is that managers may possess different cognitive structures and mental schemata than owners do because they have been socialized in a certain way and are equipped with certain worldviews, which guide their cognition, thinking, and actions (Lounsbury & Kaghan, 2001). The same holds for some professional groups or actors inside and outside a company, such as the ICT staff and consultants (see, for instance, Barley, 1996). Mizruchi and Fein notice that “[n]ormative isomorphism can be measured by either social network ties or common background ties, such as having attended the same or similar universities or originating in similar functional arenas of a <sup>fi</sup>rm” (Mizruchi & Fein, 1999: 666). In addition to the typical worldviews they have internalized, ICT personnel as well as consultants probably have a strong self-interest in enlarging the ICT infrastructure of a <sup>fi</sup>rm. They normally gain additional power resources through increasing “zones of uncertainty” which are relevant for the functioning of the whole organization and which only they can control (Crozier & Friedberg, 1979). Consulting agencies can attract new business when they advise <sup>fi</sup>rms to implement voluminous e-business technology instead of, for example, starting with a low-complex website solution. Thus, when those groups of actors are involved in a company's considerations about implementation of e-business it is likely that the technology is <sup>fi</sup>nally implemented.

Hypothesis A3B. Involvement of the ICT department in e-business decision processes increases the probability that a supplier will implement e-business technology.

Hypothesis A3C. Involvement of external consultants in e-business decision processes increases the probability that a supplier will implement e-business technology.

In the German automotive industry, until the year 2002 (when the data for this survey was collected), there existed at least two relevant e-business marketplaces (Bernhart & Feige, 2001). SupplyOn is a platform owned and run by large system suppliers. Covisint was founded in 2000 by Ford, GM, and (then-) DaimlerChrysler and was shut down in 2004. In some respects, both companies worked as consultancy agencies which try to attract suppliers to their marketplaces and convince them to use their e-business applications. Therefore, adoption of e-business technology is more likely in companies which received advice by consultants of at least one of those platforms.

Hypothesis A3D. Involvement of the platform suppliers Covisint or SupplyOn increases the probability that a supplier will implement e-business technology.

Note that involvement of both companies does not presume that e-business technology is already being adopted by a company. Consultancy often takes place during a period when a company considers adoption (and the form of adoption). Further, a possible tautology is avoided because we either separately consider different business functions or the intensity of e-business usage.

The impact of the institutional factors discussed here relies on the assumption that e-business is already a prevailing mode of conduct for certain business processes. However, data suggests that this is only partly true for e-business technology, which is being used in the purchasing and sales departments (see Section 2). In production, and even more in R&D, only a small share of suppliers had already adopted e-business technology. Each department of a company might be confronted with different (and sometimes contradictory) requirements concerning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and institutional expectations (see Lawrence & Lorsch, 1969). As production and R&D are considered the core of a company, it can be assumed that those business functions are less likely in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by institutional effects than purchasing and sales.

## 4.2. Transaction cost economics

Theoretical concepts of transaction cost economics have been employed in many studies on technology adoption (Robey et al., 2008). Basically, it is assumed that companies decide to implement e-business based on their strategic considerations, in this case to obtain the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts ascribed to the new technology. Hypotheses can be derived with reference to some basic concepts of transaction cost theory, i.e. the role of production and transaction costs, uncertainty, opportunism, and asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city.

Both new institutionalism and transaction cost theory embrace a concept of uncertainty — however, in different manners. According to transaction cost theory, uncertainty can arise regarding the conditions, circumstances, and future development of a transaction; it can also allude to technological uncertainty (David & Han, 2004). But as uncertainty is supposed to increase transaction costs, suppliers are likely to avoid implementing e-business technology when facing doubts concerning its risks and effects. With this assumption, an alternative hypothesis to A2A can be phrased:

Hypothesis B1. Under uncertainty, suppliers are likely not to adopt e-business technology.

Many scholars have argued that, as companies grow, transaction costs are likely to rise because coordination efforts and opportunism may increase (Chandler, 1962; Fligstein, 1985). When increased complexity leads to a loss of control, ICT such as e-business can probably <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap and provide a safeguard against opportunism since coordination, communication, and information processing are being optimized due to the effects of ICT (Malone et al., 1987). Therefore, one important reason why <sup>fi</sup>rms use e-business technology is to optimize, restructure or integrate business processes in order to reduce opportunism and transaction costs, and at the same time enhance performance. It can be expected that companies which anticipate those possible effects of e-business in their process of deciding whether to adopt or not are more likely to implement e-business.

Hypothesis B2. Suppliers aiming to increase performance, integrate processes, and increase standard ization are more likely to use e-business technology.

The concept of asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city has been utilized in empirical research in different ways. In their study of <sup>fl</sup>exibility in IT alliances, Young-Ybarra and Wiersema focus on the transferability of assets, which are dedicated to speci<sup>fi</sup>c transactions, because assets are supposed to in<sup>fl</sup>uence organizational decisions whether to modify or exit an alliance (Young-Ybarra & Wiersema, 1999: 441). Concerning the effect of assets on the probability of e-business adoption it can be assumed that speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets come with a high complexity of business processes which generate a number of problems and risks that have to be addressed by the companies. For instance, opportunism may rise when the option to exit a business relation is hindered because of the sunk costs created by speci<sup>fi</sup>c investments. As larger companies and the big system suppliers have invested in speci<sup>fi</sup>c assets more often, and reveal more complex inter- and intra-organizational processes, the relative effect of e-business on transaction costs is higher than in small <sup>fi</sup>rms. In other words: Since e-business-technology can help companies to manage the risks that come with asset speci<sup>fi</sup>city (for instance, monitoring supplier behavior, reducing opportunism, increasing transparency), they are more likely to implement e-business-technology.

Hypothesis B3. Suppliers with complex business processes are more likely to adopt e-business technology.

## 5. Data and measurement

The empirical investigation relies on a data set of 1902 German automotive supplier companies. Companies were randomly selected from a supplier database owned by the survey institute TNS Emnid, which contains 5770 suppliers. Thus, the response rate is 32.96%. The survey was carried out between October and November 2002 using computer-assisted telephone interviewing (CATI). The interviews were conducted with IT-executives or CEOs, depending on who was concerned with e-business technology in the company.

## 5.1. Dependent variable measurement

Two kinds of regression analysis are carried out. (1) The analysis of the factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the probability of e-business adoption will be conducted separately for each of the four business functions (departments) discussed above. I use four dummies indicating whether a company is using e-business technology for purchasing, sales, production/logistics, or R&D activities (dummy value=1) or not (0). The appropriate method of analysis is the logistic regression. Note that, following our de<sup>fi</sup>nition of e-business technology, only complex internet systems are regarded as ‘e-business technology’ and, consequently, coded as 1, whereas suppliers which only use email, simple internet sur<sup>fi</sup>ng or static websites are coded 0. Hence, in each regression model, the reference group consists of companies which do not use the internet at all; companies that use only internet based systems with low complexity; and companies that use e-business technology in other business functions than the one under investigation.

(2) In addition, an ordinal logistic regression on the importance of E-RFQ systems in sales departments is calculated. E-RFQ is usually initiated by purchasing departments of larger suppliers which seek to streamline their sourcing activities. The company which installs an E-RFQ usually asks its suppliers to use the system when participating in tenders — instead of providing information via fax or by post. Hence, in sales departments of suppliers, E-RFQ is assumedly used because customers demand it. The dependent is a rank variable (0 = not important, 4 = highly important).

## 5.2. Independent variable measurement

Table 1 shows a list of hypotheses and corresponding variables. It has to be mentioned that the indicators differ considerably from those used for instance in Teo et al.'s study on <sup>fi</sup>nancial EDI, but match some other research in new institutionalism quite well (for instance Beck & Walgenbach, 2006; Mezias, 1995). Literature reviews have shown that empirical indicators for institutional concepts like mimesis (see Mizruchi & Fein, 1999) as well as well as concepts of transaction cost economics (Shelanski & Klein, 1995) differ according to subjects. The operationalization which is chosen here is supposed to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the speci<sup>fi</sup>cities of the sector and the <sup>fi</sup>eld under investigation.

Coercion (A1) in our model is indicated by the involvement of customers or suppliers in a company's decision to implement e-business technology and by the dependency on the industry. The customer involvement variable (A1A) is a dummy with 1 indicating participation of customers or suppliers. Dependency is measured by a company's share of turnover with other automotive companies (A1B). As the share of turnover may also indicate the degree of embeddedness into the <sup>fi</sup>eld or the reference group, a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation was developed. I distinguish four categories (each is a dummy): 1–19% turnover, 20–49%, 50–70 and 71–100%. The <sup>fi</sup>rst serves as a reference

It has to be noted that involvement of other companies is only one possible way in which a customer or supplier can exert pressure; a great share of turnover with the industry still does not imply that a company is dependent on a small number of customers. For this reasons, an additional approach was chosen to analyze coercion. Interviewees should declare which goals the company pursued with the implementation of e-business technology in a respective department, and they were asked to rate the importance of possible goals (four item Likert scale). The list comprised ‘customer coercion’ and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency related goals like ‘optimizing work<sup>fl</sup>ows or transactions with other companies’, ‘minimizing personnel costs’ or ‘reducing purchasing costs’. Companies were then categorized, and each was assigned to one of three groups so that the <sup>fi</sup>rms could be differentiated according to the goals they pursue. Hence, we can distinguish between (1) <sup>fi</sup>rms that pursue ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency-related goals only, (2) <sup>fi</sup>rms that do not pursue any ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency goal but implement e-business technology only as a reaction to customer demands (coercion), (3) and <sup>fi</sup>rms with a mix of both goals. The variable is analyzed with descriptive statistics for each business function; it cannot be included into the regression because here only companies were asked which had already adopted e-business technology.

Referring to mimesis, Hypotheses A2A and B1, which predict contrary effects of uncertainty, can be measured using the same dummy. In order to determine the level of uncertainty, the interviewees were asked to estimate whether – according to their perception – e-business technology is accompanied by financial, jurisdictional, or organizational risks for the company on a four item Likert-scale (1 = high risk, 4 = no risk). Consistent with previous studies, uncertainty does not refer to a general characteristic of a company or management (like risk aversion), but to the subject under investigation (Mizruchi & Fein, 1999).

A2B refers to company size. Since we assume that mimetic effects do not simply occur with increasing size but apply only to very large companies, we use a dummy indicating whether a company possesses more than 500 employees. Another dummy indicates whether a company is the headquarters of a trust. Normative pressures (A3A to A3D) are measured by involvement of the respective actors, professions, or groups. As with the participation of customers we again use variables which indicate whether certain professionals or groups – the ICT department, consultants, SupplyOn or Covisint – participate in the implementation of e-business or not. Management control (as opposed to ownership control) is also measured using a dummy.

As mentioned above, operationalization of the hypotheses derived from transaction cost theory has been diverse in previous studies (David & Han, 2004). We rely on a few indicators which are only partly based on perceptual measures. To test B1, the corresponding uncertainty dummy is used. B2 refers to the question whether a company expects that adoption of e-business technology will improve business processes in one way or another. The companies were asked to rate three statements about the anticipated effects of e-business technology on a four item Likert scale: (1) ‘e-business technology will improve the cooperation between departments’, (2) ‘e-business technology will help to restructure processes und work<sup>fl</sup>ows’, and (3) ‘e-business technology enables integration with the existing technological infrastructure’. Factor analysis showed that the items strongly load onto the same factor, and therefore one new variable was generated and included in the regressions.

B3 is measured by two variables: (1) The supplier-type dummy indicates whether a company belongs to the upper segment of the supply industry (<sup>fi</sup>rst tiers) and, thus, produces highly complex products. (2) Complexity can also be indicated by company size; however, unlike Hypothesis A2B, here we refer to the metric variable which re<sup>fl</sup>ects the number of employees. Another dummy variable, ‘part of trust’, was supposed to indicate whether a company is an af<sup>fi</sup>liate of a trust. But as this dummy strongly correlates with two other variables – management control and company headquarters – it was removed from the models (see Draper & Smith, 1998).

As a control, a variable was included which re<sup>fl</sup>ects the number of ICT systems a company has been using prior to e-business technology. This variable is supposed to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the degree of organizational readiness and IT sophistication which – as has been emphasized by DOI research – are important predictors for adoption. However, two contradictory assumptions concerning the effect of IT sophistication can be phrased: On the one hand, companies which run a large number of ICT systems may be more likely to implement e-business technology because they are more familiar with ICT (i.e. experienced and ‘sophisticated’). On the other hand, those companies might be less likely to adopt e-business technology because they already possess ICT infrastructure. Mainly in production/logistics and R&D, e-business does not provide business processes and automation which is totally new, but often substitutes for prior technologies, like EDI. According to some studies, companies which already owned EDI systems (and had made large investments) were reluctant to use e-business technology. Among the motivations was the fear that internet transactions were not “safe”, and that companies did not want to lose investments in prior technologies.

The results of the logistic regressions appear in Tables 3a–3d. The overall signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of each of the full regression models is very high (p=0.000); and only for one transaction cost model it is lower than 5%. Hence, despite sometimes relatively low <sup>fi</sup>gures for the pseudo-R<sup>2</sup> – those lie between 0.101 and 0.403 for the full model and between 0.015 and 0.320 for the single models – the models <sup>fi</sup>t well enough to explain the variation of the dependent variable. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect is only found in R&D, which supports the transaction cost theory assumption. In sum, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency motivations for adoption are obviously more important in the core areas of a company like R&D and production.

Mimetic effects would be indicated by three variables which appear to exert some in<sup>fl</sup>uence on e-business adoption. Only in the ordinal logistic regression model on E-RFQs, uncertainty leads to a higher likelihood of adoption. As already mentioned, in R&D the result of the uncertainty variable even supports the transaction cost theory assumption. Thus, Hypothesis A2A can only partly be accepted. Large companies with more than 500 employees are more likely to adopt e-business technology only in the purchasing department. This <sup>fi</sup>ts our assumptions and is plausible in the context of the e-business hype when purchasing systems were regarded as an important means to reduce costs. In production/logistics, there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence, but it is negative. Further, headquarters of trusts, which were assumed to be exposed to public ‘monitoring’, do not adopt e-business technology more often than other <sup>fi</sup>rms. Hence, Hypothesis A2B is supported for purchasing but rejected for the other business functions.

Normative pressures are indicated by <sup>fi</sup>ve variables. Hypothesis A3A is only partly supported. Companies that are run by managers more often tend to adopt e-business in the R&D department. This is a surprising outcome, which is not easily interpreted since we expected managers to opt for e-business technology because of its high external visibility (Abrahamson, 1996), while in R&D, outsiders or the public would hardly recognize that e-business technology is used. ICT involvement plays an important role in three business functions. For e-business usage in sales (ordinal model), production/logistics and R&D, the involvement of ICT-personnel increases the likelihood of adoption. When we presume that ICT personnel seek to increase uncertainty zones and power resources, it appears plausible that they focus on the core areas of the company (Crozier & Friedberg, 1979). The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on E-RFQ adoption demonstrates that particular world views of IT professionals might be in<sup>fl</sup>uential. Hypothesis A3B is supported in this respect.

Hypothesis A3C is rejected because in none of the models the variable ‘consulting agency involved’ increases the likelihood of adoption. However, the variable displaying involvement of consultants from the e-business platform SupplyOn demonstrates that consultancies are indeed able to foster e-business technology adoption. When SupplyOn consultants participate in the implementation process, <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to adopt e-business technology in purchasing, sales, and production/logistics. Covisint does not reveal a comparable impact — in purchasing, the effect is even negative. One reason might be that Covisint systems were used by OEMs and system suppliers in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place, but to a lesser degree by the smaller second and third tiers, which make up the biggest part of the supply industry. Also, the overall importance of Covisint might have been lower in the German supply industry than in the US-American. In sum, hypothesis A3D is only partly supported.

The control variable organizational readiness/IT sophistication which was derived from DOI literature has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact in sales, production/logistics and R&D. Consistent with previous research on technology diffusion, e-business is obviously more often adopted in companies which already have experience with ICT.

## 7. Discussion and conclusions

The aim of this paper was to examine the reasons why automotive supplier companies implement e-business technology in an early period of technology diffusion and in a ‘hype’ situation. Data analysis reveals that the reasons for e-business adoption differ by supplier type and by department (see Table 6). In each of the four business functions we examined, at least one type of institutional process plays an important role. E-business implementation as a result of coercion – conforming to demands of business partners, i.e. customers or suppliers – is an important rationale in all business functions: When companies implement an e-business-system, they often force business partners to use the system in order to ensure that integration and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency effects are achieved. There is some evidence that coercion is a strong motivation at the bottom of the supply chain: small parts suppliers (third tiers) are more likely to be forced to use e-business technology (by component and system suppliers as well as OEMs) than system suppliers, which are mostly larger companies that produce speci<sup>fi</sup>c modules and, thus, tend to be more powerful.

In sales and R&D, companies with a medium or low degree of embeddedness in the automotive sector (turnover shares below 70%) are more likely to implement e-business technology than are companies which obtain a high amount (more than 70%) of turnover in the sector. It can only be assumed that e-business technology in sales and R&D was often utilized as an ‘enabler’ for those suppliers, which up to that point had been lacking technological integration (Barley, 1986; Malone et al., 1987). As the automotive industry is a pace maker in ICT, companies which are highly embedded in the industry certainly have been using some form of ICT in 2002, so that implementing e-business technology – as a substitute – appeared not an urgent task.

Ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency-related motivations are important reasons for companies to implement e-business technology in the core business areas of a <sup>fi</sup>rm, i.e. in the production and R&D departments. Obviously, those motives are less relevant in purchasing and sales. However, it is noteworthy that R&D and production/logistics nevertheless appear to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by institutional processes. Obviously, companies cannot shield their core functions from institutional in<sup>fl</sup>uences, even if they may jeopardize ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Two reasons might feasibly explain this result:

(1) Since the automotive sector is a highly competitive industry, coercion is an ambivalent process that may stem from the pressure to enhance ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency: When OEMs and <sup>fi</sup>rst tier suppliers are confronted with global market forces they may pass this pressure to their suppliers. Thus, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency pressures at the ‘top’ of the industry ‘trickle down the supply chain’ and may lead to coercion at the bottom.<sup>11</sup> The analysis of the goals companies pursue with e-business technology adoption shows that many suppliers are forced to use e-business, but at the same time they pursue ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency related goals. This indicates that companies still try to obtain bene<sup>fi</sup>ts when facing ‘awkward’ conditions they cannot circumvent.

(2) Normative pressures were found to be relevant in all business functions. It has to be taken into account that a company can hardly protect against this type of in<sup>fl</sup>uence because it is closely linked to the organizations' members, i.e. the worldviews deeply embedded in the cognitive structures of employees and management (Berger & Luckmann, 1966; Zucker, 1977). Thus, when IT professionals or consultants regard e-business technology as important (despite the possible risks and the lack of ability to measure its effectiveness), they may strongly advice management to adopt. Since what is perceived as rational and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by entities in the environment, organizational structuring becomes a question of de<sup>fi</sup>nition and cognition, and is thus subject to fashions, beliefs, and perceptions of best practice in the organizational <sup>fi</sup>eld. But also interests and the power of in<sup>fl</sup>uential members of an organization appear to be important in this context (Crozier & Friedberg, 1979). Consultants, for instance, will rationally ponder how to ‘sell’ e-business solutions and corresponding services to <sup>fi</sup>rms. Their behavior is not solely guided by taken-for-granted routines or scripts, but also by interests. Those issues have only recently been recognized in the institutional theory (Lawrence, 2008).

According to our calculation mimesis plays a role in the purchasing and sales department only. To some extent, this result is plausible because during the e-business hype it was mainly those two departments which were widely assumed to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the new technology. Herding behavior – doing what everybody else does – appeared to be an adequate way of acting. For larger corporations it seemed inevitable to implement e-business, since they would have been in jeopardy to be labeled as old-fashioned and outdated by business partners and the public.

However, the fact that the explanatory value of mimetic effects in the quantitative data is rather low may raise different (theoretical) considerations. Firstly, mimetic and normative processes appear to be closely linked. Mimetic effects usually point to the (meso) level of the organization as an aggregate, while normative pressures point at groups of individuals (professions). The internal relationship of institutional mechanisms has drawn only little attention yet, although it clearly affects the empirical practicability (Mizruchi & Fein, 1999). As both mimesis and normative pressures are (at least to some extent) rooted in the existence of common worldviews and internalized cognitive schemata, they can only partly be distinguished with broad quantitative measures.

A second consideration points to the inverse relation between mimesis and coercion. Some scholars have argued that institutions are anchored in the belief systems of individuals (Zucker, 1977). Thus, when the diffusion of e-business technology stems from coercion in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place, it can be assumed that e-business technology is not yet as strongly an institutionalized model. Vice versa, if e-business technology were highly institutionalized one could expect that companies do not need to enforce conformity of business partners because suppliers would voluntarily adopt e-business technology — simply because it is taken for granted as the legitimate and appropriate way of conducting business in the industry. However, there certainly are different degrees of institutionalization which come with different forms of pressures. Regarding our analysis, the focus is on an early stage of innovation diffusion, and thus, coercion might be a trigger for initiating institutionalization,<sup>12</sup> making e-business more widely accepted in subsequent years (Scott, 2001: 114; Palmer et al., 1993).<sup>13</sup> Herding behavior in the adoption of e-business technology, obviously, is driven by different forces: organizational leaders of larger corporations jump on the bandwagon as they seek to appear modern and innovative; but smaller companies follow because of direct pressures and anxiety to lose customers.

The results of our empirical investigation show that the theories applied in this study contribute to the explanation of e-business technology adoption. Future research will bene<sup>fi</sup>t from avoiding a monistic theoretical approach for explaining organizational dynamics (Abell, 1995). However, additional theoretical accounts still may provide further insight as the overall explanatory value of most models used here is rather low. It seems bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to include concepts of DOI research which have been successfully employed in previous research. Further, a more detailed picture of the organizational characteristics of the suppliers and their social contexts appears necessary. The automotive industry reveals certain features that may shape diffusion processes considerably: On the one hand, many <sup>fi</sup>rms in the sector are members of company networks. Very often, those are long-term alliances which reveal different levels of interdependency, cooperation and trust (see, for instance, Gulati & Singh, 1998), which will in<sup>fl</sup>uence the anticipated need for ICT (opportunism) and the way companies ‘bargain’ over its implementation. On the other hand, OEMs and system suppliers seek to foster competition among their suppliers in order to reduce costs. The result is an ambiguous industry structure marked by coincidence of cooperation and competition (Mühge et al., 2004).

In a highly structured organizational <sup>fi</sup>eld, decisions to adopt are also molded by social relations and bargaining processes between alliance partners (Granovetter, 1985). Hence, intercompany linkages and attitudes of individuals become important. For ICT usage in SMEs, for instance, it has been shown that CEO innovativeness, attitudes towards ICT adoption and IT knowledge are of great relevance (Thong, 1999). As more than 90% of the automotive supplier companies are small and medium sized enterprises (with less than 250 employees), we certainly <sup>fi</sup>nd speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways of adopting new organizational features. Institutional pressures are being treated in a company speci<sup>fi</sup>c way — interpreted with reference to existing organizational cultures, individual experiences and attitudes (Sahlin-Andersson, 1996) that may be explored with case study rather than survey methods. Future research may explore how to combine those different concepts.
