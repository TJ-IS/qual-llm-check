# Producing solidarity in social media activism: The case of My Stealthy Freedom

## A B S T R A C T

Solidarity, i.e., an individual's feelings of devotion and commitment to others in a collective, is one of the defining features of social movements. It is regarded as a key motivator of collective action in that it collectivizes what would otherwise remain individual experiences and emotions. However, as social movements are increasingly relying on Internet technology, particularly social media, for instrumental and expressive communication, some question whether solidarity plays any role in e-movements at all. Others propose that new forms of solidarity are developing in these mediated environments. Since most prior research fails to account for the role of physical bodies in online social movements, the objective of this paper is to explore the roles and forms of solidarity in contemporary social movements that hybridize online and physically-embodied action. To this end, we develop a multi-faceted and practice-based definition of solidarity through which we read two configurations of a social movement called My Stealthy Freedom (MySF), which opposes the compulsory veiling laws (i.e., hijab) in Iran. In both enactments of MySF, the activists' physical bodies played a significant role in the social movement's online presence but the forms of solidarity they produced difered. We therefore theorize the role of materiality – particularly social media and activists' physical bodies –in social media activism.

## 1. Introduction

Social movements are “networks of informal interactions between a plurality of individuals, groups and/or organizations, engaged in political or cultural conflicts, on the basis of shared collective identities” (Diani, 1992, p. 1). As this definition suggests, collective identity, that is, a shared sense of “we-ness” and “collective agency” (Snow, 2001), is regarded as not only a distinguishing feature of social movements, but also a condition for collective action. Particularly, contemporary social movements, such as the women's movement (Taylor & Whittier, 1992), the Tea Party (Rohlinger, Klein, Stamm, & Rogers, 2015; Williamson, Skocpol, & Coggin, 2011) and Occupy Wall Street (Kavada, 2015), which are also referred to as “new social movements” or “identity movements” (Fenton, 2007), depend on members' perceived sameness for bringing coherence to an otherwise fragmented aggregation of activists (d'Emilio, 2012).

Closely associated with collective identity – and frequently conflated with it – is the notion of solidarity. Solidarity often denotes a movement's ‘spirit’ (Hunt & Benford, 2004) or “esprit de corps” (Blumer, 1995, p. 67), which represents “the organizing of feelings on behalf of the movement.” This implies that solidarity is accomplished when emotions (e.g., rage, awe, joy) that are experienced at the individual level are made collective.

While esprit de corps highlights the emotional aspects of solidarity, i.e., reciprocal feelings of afection and compassion among group members, Dean (1996) notes that solidarity also represents a set of norms that oblige an individual to care about certain others especially members of the in-group. Solidarity and collective identity can thus be regarded as distinct but mutually constitutive: feelings of devotion to others in the group arise out of a sense of collective identity, and the sense of unity and sameness that is indicative of collective identity, develops out of individuals' empathy for each other.

As social movements rely increasingly on Internet technology, and particularly social media, for instrumental and expressive communication, questions about the feasibility and presence of key phenomena of social movements – particularly collective action (Bennett & Segerberg, 2012), collective identity (Bennett, 2012; McDonald, 2015), and solidarity (McDonald, 2002) – have garnered considerable attention (Gerbaudo & Treré, 2015). Composed of geographically-distributed individuals who are seldom – if ever – copresent, social media activism makes it more dificult for members to share common experiences or to participate in “an intersubjectively shared world” (Habermas, 1992, p. 120). The symbolic construction of a united “we,” the development of feelings of a shared fate and the evolution of norms of care and commitment to each other, are thus challenged in these environments.

Given that online settings are characterized by more individualized and fluid identifications than traditional social movements, which tended to involve the active engagement of protestors' physical bodies, Bennett and Segerberg (2012) advocate replacing the notion of collective action with connective action. In the latter, protest depends on the technologically-coordinated actions of relatively isolated individuals rather than the collective being an actor. Similarly, fluidarity, which McDonald (2002) argues should substitute solidarity in e-movements, captures individuals coming together momentarily to enact a public experience of self and other. In contrast, solidarity is a more long-lasting commitment among members of a protest movement

However, contemporary social movements increasingly are hybridizations of online and ofline practices of resistance (Lim, 2013; Postill & Pink, 2012). For example, the Arab Spring relied on social media to coordinate the collective action of protesters, who nevertheless took to the streets (Lim, 2013). In order to understand and preserve these emergent organizational forms as social movements, Bakardjieva (2015) maintains that the concepts of solidarity, collective identity and collective action, which are the identifying features of social movements, should not be abandoned or replaced. Instead, the new forms these phenomena take in technology-mediated activism needs to be explored. For example, recent studies highlight that social media have changed the forms of collective action by facilitating “more decentralized, dispersed, temporary and individualized forms of political action that subvert the notion of the collective as singular, unified, homogeneous, coherent, and mass” (Kavada, 2016, p. 8).

To explore these contemporary social movements as hybrid organizational forms, we adopt the concept of sociomaterial configuration (Barad, 2007; Mazmanian, Cohn, & Dourish, 2014), which connotes the ways in which sociality and materiality (both human and digital) figure together, i.e., configure, to constitute an agential assemblage (Suchman, 2012). This theoretical stance embraces the notion that “agency is not an attribute, but the ongoing reconfigurings of the world” (Barad, 2003, p. 818). Thus, the agency associated with social movements in general, and collective identity in particular, is the result of an ongoing and open-ended process through which collective is accomplished.

The objective of this paper is to shine a light on solidarity – its characteristics and roles – in social movements that are enacted in and through social media (Gerbaudo & Treré, 2015). Our focus on solidarity is, in part, motivated by the fact that emotions have largely been ignored in the social movement literature, even though “emotions are present in every phase and every aspect of protest” (Jasper, 2011, p. 14). Furthermore, emotions are embodied (Vachhani & Pullen, 2019) and the role of bodies is also frequently omitted in research on online space (Campbell, 2014; Dourish, 2004; Hansen, 2012; Schick & Malmborg, 2010) and especially in the IS field (Jones, 2014; Schultze, 2014). We examine how diferent configurations of technology-enabled protest produce diferent forms of solidarity. Specifically, we seek to answer the following research question: “How are new forms of solidarity produced by diferent hybrid configurations of social media activism?”

To answer our research question, we draw on an empirical study of My Stealthy Freedom (MySF), a social movement protesting the compulsory veiling of women in Iran. In particular, we compare two configurations of activism within this social movement – namely the MySF Facebook (FB) fan page and the White Wednesdays (WWeds) campaign. The configuration that constituted the MySF FB page privileged virtuality, by posting anonymous photographs of women who had taken their headscarves of in a public place. The activists' anonymity largely limited solidarity (as well as collective identity) to the realm of the imagination (Acquisti & Gross, 2006; Gruzd, Wellman, & Takhteyev, 2011), afective attunement (Papacharissi & de Fatima Oliveira, 2012), and normatively motivated feelings of obligation towards others (Nepstad, 2004). We label this imagined solidarity.

In contrast, the WWeds configuration of collective action emphasized weekly, physically embodied encounters among activists who signaled their membership in the movement by wearing a white headscarf on a Wednesday. Videos of their experiences during the day were also posted on their personal social media accounts. The richness of insight into the actual protest situation – either first hand or by following the identified activist online – created the conditions for a solidarity motivated by negative emotions, e.g., rage for mistreatment of an individual (Vachhani & Pullen. 2019) and empathy for identified others (Pedwell. 2012). We develop the notion of situated solidarity to capture this.

It is important to note that the two enactments of the MySF movement were highly intertwined: the Facebook page preceded WWeds, and events related to WWeds were posted on the MySF Facebook page. Through comparison and contrast, our goal is to gain insights into how the two configurations of cyberactivism generated distinct forms of solidarity.

Our paper proceeds as follows: We outline the prior literature on social movements, focusing on the definition of solidarity and its relationship to collective identity. We then review social media activism, highlighting the distinction between identity and digita movements and the mechanisms through which they operate. After introducing the two configurations of collective action we studied and explaining how the research was conducted, we read the two enactments of MySF through each other, as well as through our conceptual framework. By means of abductive theorizing, we then derive two forms of solidarity, their defining features and the practices that produce them. We also articulate of the paper's contributions by developing a configurational framework of solidarit in hybrid social movements, as well as its limitations and implications for research and practice.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Social movements: solidarity and collective identity

Social movement theorizing has moved from resource mobilization theory (McCarthy & Zald, 1977) to more cultural explanations of “new social movements” (Tilly, 1978, 1985). These new social movements (e.g., feminist, environmental, and civil rights movements), which are also referred to as identity movements (Fenton, 2007), difer from their class-based predecessors by uniting people who may have highly diverse experiences, but who nevertheless identify with a common cause (Hunt & Benford, 2004; Polletta & Jasper, 2001). While concepts such as class consciousness (Marx & Engels, 1970), collective efervescence (Durkheim, 1965), and party (Weber, 1978) were originally seen as uniting individual actors into a social movement, solidarity and collective identity have become the coalescing forces in these new social movements.

In the social movement literature, solidarity and collective identity are generally seen as highly intertwined (Gamson. 1991: Hunt & Benford, 2004) and even as synonymous (Saunders, 2008, p. 234). Indeed, the terms are often used interchangeably (e.g., Taylor & Whittier, 1992). However, we maintain that the concepts are distinct, despite being mutually constitutive.

Collective identity (see Appendix A) is generally defined as “a shared sense of ‘oneness’ or ‘we-ness’ anchored in either real or imagined shared attributes and experiences among those who comprise the collectivity and in relation or contrast to one or more actual or imagined sets of others” (Snow, 2001, p. 2). Melucci (1995), theorizes collective identity as a process by which the movement as a unified actor is formed. Viewing collective identity as an action system, this perspective views it as “a result rather than a starting point” (Melucci, 1995, p. 43).

In contrast, solidarity, is defined as “emotionally and normatively motivated readiness for mutual support, as in the slogan ‘one for al and all for one’” (Laitinen & Pessi, 2014, p. 1, emphasis added). It connotes “action-readiness” (Thalos, 2012, p. 64) and a “duty of assistance” (Trifunović, 2012, p. 155). Based on our literature review (see Table 1 for summary), we identify a number of characteristics that are core to the meaning of solidarity: it is relational (i.e., it resides between group members); it assumes the existence of a collective; it is an individually-embodied feeling that is nevertheless shared among group members; and it motivates action. We can thus discern three elements that are necessary for solidarity to be manifest: (i) the motivation to take action on behalf of the group, as wel as an individually-held (ii) normatively- and (iii) emotionally-motivated readiness to support others in the group.

To explore the relationship between solidarity and collective identity, we first need to consider the scope of a collective. Schwartz (2007) distinguishes between particular solidarity, i.e., a motivation to help others that arises from a shared identity or membership in a group, and universal solidarity, i.e., a motivation to help borne out of a more basic obligation that people qua human beings have towards each other (Manning, 2008; Schultze & Brooks, Forthcoming). Since collective identity is “first and foremost a statement about categorical membership” (Holland, Fox, & Daro, 2008, p. 97), we argue that collective identity is a pre-requisite of particular solidarity, or what Bayertz (1999) referred to as the factual level of solidarity. However, at the universal level, human solidarity (Scholz, 2015) can exist and be exercised without identification with a collective.¹

The mere existence of a collective does not guarantee solidarity. Instead, solidarity is performative, that is, a collective feeling or emotional energy (Collins, 2001) that is produced when individuals perform protest rituals (Juris, 2008; Routledge, 2012), e.g., embodied actions such as dressing in certain colors, holding banners or chanting. As “protest performances generate powerful emotions, which prepare activist bodies for action” (Juris, 2008, p. 65), understanding the repetitive and symbolically meaningfu practices of a social movement is key to gaining insight into how diferent types of solidarity are generated through distinct configurations of collective action (Brown & Yaffe, 2014)

## 2.2. Practice perspective on solidarity

In order to gain insights into the role of technology in the production of solidarity, we conceptualize contemporary social movements as enacted through a hybridization of physically-embodied and virtually-performed action. We employ the notion of sociomaterial configurations (Mazmanian et al., 2014: Orlikowski & Scott. 2008: Scott & Orlikowski. 2014). that is. “a toolkit for constitutive and generative, reiterative and (potentially) transformative material-semiotic conjoining” (Suchman, 2012, p. 57) for our theorizing. It leads us to focus on how humans and technologies are “figured together—or configured—” (Suchman, 2012, p. 49) in the performance of collective action.

To investigate a given configuration, we need “to recover the practices through which it comes into being and sustains its efects” (Suchman, 2012, p. 49). Focusing on the practices performed to enact cyberactivism, as well as the implications of these practices on the production of solidarity, we adopt Blumer's (1995) three processes of solidarity production: (i) ceremonial behaviors, (ii) informal fellowship, and (iii) in-group out-group relations.

Ceremonial behavior constitutes the visible, front-stage actions of a social movement's members (Flesher Fominaya, 2010). It includes the display of symbols (e.g., slogans, emblems, gestures) representative of the collective (Blumer, 1995). However, engaging in ceremonial behavior does not only symbolize an individual's identification with and representation of the group; it also produces the social movement by generating an afective response in the individuals performing them. Interpreting these embodied sensations as emotions aligned with those of others in the movement, i.e., afective attunement (Collins, 2004), produces the collective. In other words, these visible and ritualized forms of ceremonial behavior are performative of the individual as activist and the collective as a body.

Informal fellowship refers to back-stage, latent actions (Flesher Fominaya, 2010) through which members of a collective get to know each other as individuals, rather than as representatives of the movement (Blumer, 1995). By socializing and having fun with each other outside of their formal, ceremonial performance of resistance, participants are likely to develop mutual sympathies and a sense of intimacy. Feelings of isolation and alienation that peripheral members of the movement might experience, are likely to give way to a sense of acceptance and support when informal fellowship is exercised (Blumer, 1995).

To establish a social movement, its boundaries have to be defined (Milan, 2015). This frequently takes the form of establishing ingroup vs out-group relations (Dean, 1996). Identifying a common enemy, e.g., an unscrupulous and vicious other, lends the in-group a sense of unifying righteousness that also comes with a responsibility to defend and preserve its superior values (Blumer, 1995). Creating an us-vs-them dynamic produces a sense of common purpose, ideology and solidarity among the members of the in-group.

What we know about how these practices are enacted in technology-mediated setting is the focus of our ensuing literature review. In particular, we explore the role of social media in enacting social movements.

## 2.3. Social media activism

Vegh (2013) defines online activism as “a politically motivated movement relying on the Internet” (p. 71). He distinguishes between two types of activist strategies in online social movements: Internet-enhanced, which includes activities that use the Internet for facilitating traditional activism strategies (e.g., organizing and coordinating street rallies), and Internet-based, which denotes activities that are possible only through the Internet (e.g., virtual sit-ins or hacking). This distinction is also reflected in Priante. Ehrenhard, van den Broek, and Need's (2017) categorization of CMC (Computer Mediated Communication)-supported and CMC-based collective action respectively. However, Earl, Kimport, Prieto, Rush, and Reynoso (2010) offer a more fine-grained typology of Internet activism: brochure-ware (e.g., updating websites of social movement organizations), online facilitation of ofline activism (e.g., logistical support for actual marches), online participation (e.g., email campaigns), and online organizing (e.g., online petitions where the entire campaign or movement is online).

Importantly, what these typologies highlight is that social media in activism – also labelled cyberactivism (Illia, 2003; McCaughey & Ayers, 2013) and e-movements (Earl & Kimport, 2011; Schussman & Earl, 2004) – is instantiated through a variety of configurations of activists' physical embodiment and virtual presence. For example, the Internet's use as a resource for coordination and communication among Arab Spring activists (Bruns, Highfield, & Burgess, 2013; Van Niekerk, Pillay, & Maharaj, 2011) or as a medium for spreading and amplifying news globally (Lotan et al., 2011; Mueller & van Huellen, 2012), illustrate the hybridization of on- and of-line activism.

With the increasing use of technology-mediation in activism, the viability of generating solidarity in social movements is cast in doubt (e.g., Saunders, 2008). For example, Diani (2000) highlights that the hiding of personal identities, lack of commitment to others, occasional participation, and dyadic or triadic interactions, which are key features of online sociality, are indicative of a weak sense of mutual obligation and further contribute to diminish it. This is because the mechanisms for building trust, commitment and long-term interpersonal relationships are disrupted in CMC:

“[Online social movements] are rather limited in fostering this feeling of collectiveness as they cannot aford experiences of a common ‘now’. Instead, activists experience their participation each in their own ‘now’ and located in diferent physical places. While this allows activists to participate in the movement at a time of their own choosing, it may also create feelings of loneliness and alienation.”

(Kavada, 2010, p. 51)

In the virtual space of isolated participation, the intense mutual bindings assumed necessary for developing collective norms and feelings of devotion to others are unlikely to emerge (Calhoun, 1998; Diani, 2000). Some researchers therefore conclude that online movements are efective as long as they are Internet-enabled and underpinned by actual social relationships among embodied individuals backed by local communities (Haythornthwaite, 2002).

Furthermore, the convenience associated with online activism, e.g., distributing and signing petitions without anyone going doorto-door. has generated such derogatory labeling of Internet-based collective action as “clicktivism" (Karpf. 2010: White. 2010). “slacktivism” (Christensen, 2011), and “engaged passivity” (Papacharissi & de Fatima Oliveira, 2012, p. 279). These designations highlight a lack of commitment to the cause and limited efort and risk-taking on the part of members. Unlike activists that physically take part in protest marches or sit-ins, slacktivists do not put their bodies at risk. The disembodied, virtual nature of Internet-based social movements thus diminishes them to an ‘almost' or ‘as-if' versions of the 'real thing' (Shields. 2002)

Some have argued that minimal-efort, online activism harms the public sphere in the long run (Shulman, 2009) and that it creates merely weak ties that will be unable to motivate and sustain street protests (Gladwell, 2010). As members accord little time and commitment to a given movement, it is unlikely that a sense of community (Kavada, 2010) and solidarity (Karpf, 2014) wil emerge. Indeed, Fenton (2008) argues that “[s]olidarity is about engaging beyond the click of a mouse” (p. 52).

## 2.3.1. Logic of action

As outlined above, identity movements that act as a collective, have two key characteristics: collective identity (a sense of commonality and unity) and solidarity (individually-held feelings of devotion that group members have towards each other). In contrast, the notion of connective action (Bennett & Segerberg, 2012) was developed to explain technology-mediated movements such as Arab Spring, los indignados in Spain, and Occupy Wall Street, where collective identity is not a requirement for membership; rather participation is formed around personalized paths. For example, instead of drawing on shared experience (e.g., being a racia minority) and thus a collective identity as a ‘person of color’ for participation in a movement, individuals join for a diverse set of personal reasons, e.g., commitment to a cause, search for political advantage.

Furthermore, expressions of interest in and allegiance to a given set of issues sufice for participation in the E-movement (Papacharissi, 2015). These personalized paths produce action networks that are constituted through sub-communities that each take somewhat independent action (Fenton. 2016).

McDonald (2002) argues that collective identity, one of the social movement orthodoxies, should be abandoned, as it is likely to hold back research on contemporary, computer-mediated forms of activism. Seeking to capture the fluidity of networked society

This highlights that social-media movements defined by connective logic, construct an individual's identity as an activist and raise the consciousness of individual members through the sharing of personal – rather than shared – experiences (Radley & Bell, 2007).

“the interactions and interests that tie dispersed social media users together to form protest movements, generating instant moments of togetherness, inevitably dissolve when social platforms algorithmically connect users to the next wave of trending topics … social media are focused on connecting users only momentarily.”

(Poell & van Dijck, 2015, p. 533)

With the durability of afective ties towards other group members being in doubt, McDonald (2002) goes so far as to argue that the notion of solidarity be replaced with fluidarity. Compared to the solidity and fixity implied in solidarity, fluidarity embraces movement, incompleteness, partial knowledge and vulnerability. Coined by Nelson (1999). it captures and honors the distinctiveness of individuals' experiences. As “an attitude and practice that embraces the complexity of engaging the other in pluralized and everchanging struggles” (Maruggi, 2012, p. 307), fluidarity thus militates against homogenizing the diferences among group members.

## 2.3.2. Source of group cohesion

In identity movements, group cohesion is based on embodied experiences that they have in common (Barnes, 1999; Blumer, 1995). For example, the shared experience of ‘being a woman’ is claimed as the source of unity and identity within the feminist movement, despite the diferent political persuasions that this movement represents (Mohanty, 1995). Theories of collective action thus tend to adopt a representational understanding of identity that draws on an essentialist ontology and attributes to individuals some fixed properties that are assumed to reside somewhere in their physical body (Van Doorn, 2010). In other words, by exhibiting the physical features that mark an individual as having the female sex, this individual is expected to share actual experiences common to all women.

However, experiences that members of a collective have in common can be shared in actuality (i.e., same space-time) or in the imagination (i.e., distributed over space and time). While the former relies on the co-presence of physically embodied individuals, the latter draws on individuals' ability to experience events vicariously and to feel empathy. Imagined collectives (e.g., nations) are characterized by individuals who “will never know most of their fellow-members, meet them, or even hear of them, yet in the minds of each lives the image of their communion” (Anderson, 1983, p. 49). To the extent that the production and re-production of an imagined communion focuses on what is common among members – thereby glossing over diferences – it universalizes and privileges collective identity (Calhoun, 1993).

Another mechanism for building imagined collectives is afective attunement (Papacharissi, 2015). Afective attunement represents not only the desire and ability to understand and respect another's inner world, but also the matching of the others' emotional state with affective expressions to achieve a level of inter-subjectivity where emotions are perceived as shared (Stern, 1985). While afective attunement does not only operate in online interactions (Boadella, 2005), e-movements are nevertheless deemed highly susceptible to strong emotional associations (Dean, 2010; McCosker, 2013). Furthermore, the content (e.g., the blending of opinions, facts and emotions) and structure (e.g., networked and always on) of social media contribute to e-movements' afectivity (Papacharissi & de Fatima Oliveira, 2012).

Attuning to another's emotional state through empathy implies a more performative conceptualization of identity in e-movements (e.g., Milan, 2015). A performative ontology posits that identity is the efect rather than the source of people's identity performances (Hickey-Moody & Wood, 2008). Adopting such a perspective, implies that individuals' doings and saving enact who they become (Butler, 1993). Thus, men become feminist activists by attuning to women's inner worlds (i.e., performative lens), rather than b experiencing the world as a transgendered woman (i.e., representational lens).

Having outlined the key diferences between how prior research theorizes the ways in which Internet-enabled and Internet-based social movements accomplish joint action, we now leverage these insights in our empirical exploration into how diferent forms of solidarity are produced by two distinct configurations of technology-enabled activism.

## 3. Method

To answer our research question, we rely on a comparative analysis of two configurations of activism within a social movement called My Stealthy Freedom (MySF), which protests the compulsory hijab in Iran. Adopting an interpretivist approach, we abductively analyzed data from two sources: 24 interviews with MySF members and social media content, particularly Facebook and Twitter. In the following sections, we explain our empirical setting, as well as the data collection and analysis approaches we employed.

## 3.1. Two social media campaigns: My Stealthy Freedom and White Wednesdays

Launched on May 3, 2014, the MySF Facebook (FB) page was founded by Masih Alinejad, an Iranian journalist, who fled Iran after the 2009 presidential election and who works as a journalist in the West, specializing in human rights in Iran. To this day, Masih owns and maintains the MySF Facebook page. The MySF site has become a place for men and women to voice their opposition to the Iranian law that requires that women veil themselves (i.e., wear a headscarf and a manteaux) in public. Failure to ‘respect hijab,’ is punishable by a jail term of up to two months or a fine.

Hijab is a widely accepted Islamic rule that requires adult Muslim women to cover their body except their face, hands up to wrists, and feet in situations where adult males other than their immediate family members are present. Wearing headscarves, chador (black covering from head to toe), and manteaux have been widely practiced by some Iranian women throughout history. However, after the Islamic revolution, in March 1979, which made wearing the hijab legally compulsory, many oficial and unoficial forces emerged to ensure compliance with this veiling law. These range from the morality police (whose job is to arrest women without proper hijab) to cultural norms.

The legalization of the compulsory hijab turned veiling from a personal choice and a woman's expression of her religious beliefs, to a political and power issue. Since “where there is power, there is resistance” (Foucault, 1978, p. 95), Iranian women have opposed the veiling regulations from the beginning. Ever since the Islamic revolution, Iranian women have protested for their civil and constitutional rights and have stood in opposition to the regime's unequal treatment of and systematic discrimination against them (Mohammadi, 2007). Furthermore, their everyday resistance over the years has significantly diluted the norms of veiling as it practiced in Iran today from the ideal promoted by government propaganda.

ICT and the Internet have been adopted widely by these feminist movements (Abbasgholizadeh, 2014; Lerner, 2010; Shirazi, 2012). For example, the “One Million Signatures” campaign, launched in 2006 by Iranian feminist activists, with the objective of collecting one million signatures for a petition in support of changing discriminatory laws, is among the most effective efforts of this kind (Ameripour, Nicholson, & Newman, 2010; Sameh, 2010).

The genesis of MySF was a photo of Masih that she published on her own FB page. It was of her in Iran, driving a car with her headscarf dropped to her shoulders. In the photo's caption she invited other women in Iran to claim this freedom – however stealthily – for themselves. Surprised by the Iranian women's great response to her invitation, Masih then founded the MySF page on FB. With Masih as the site's owner and the curator of most of its content, MySF became the venue for mostly Iranian women to engage in acts of protest by anonymously publishing hijabless photos of themselves. In Mid-October 2017, the MySF site comprised of around 2050 photos, each of which received tens to hundreds of comments, “likes,” and re-shares.

In May 2017, about three years after MySF's launch, Masih announced a new campaign called White Wednesdays (WWeds). Women were invited to wear white scarves and clothes and to carry white symbols in public on Wednesdays to protest the veiling law. In addition, they were encouraged to capture their experience in the form of short videos of themselves talking about their opposition to the compulsory hijab. She explained her motivation for starting a new campaign within the MySF movement as follows:

“You know, MySF was working for three years and I had made a big network… And they were asking me all the time for announcing a meeting so that they can meet each other. So, I thought now is the time for them to identify each other in public, to meet each other and find each other through a single sign, a unique symbol (here, white color). So, I announced the WWeds [campaign]. And most of the people who had participated in MySF came forward. Because they were waiting for the next step, they wanted to do something new.”

During the five months (May to October 2017) of WWeds' operation, Masih published around 310 videos and 120 photos pertaining to the campaign. Furthermore, the campaign's “#WhiteWeds” hashtag meant that activists could post their content under this signifier on their own accounts and on other social media platforms, while maintaining the link to the collective.

Given our interest in the role of sociomaterial practices in the production of solidarity in social movements, the MySF movement ofers us the opportunity to leverage the diferences between how activism is performed on the MySF Facebook page and through its WWeds campaign by members of the same movement

## 3.2. Data collection

We collected data by:

(i) interviewing Masih, 22 MySF participants of the MySF and WWeds campaigns (18 women and 4 men), and one MySF administrator,

(ii) capturing the content of the MySF page (photos/videos, captions, and comments), and

(iii) capturing the social media content on Twitter, Instagram and Facebook that was identified as belonging to the WWeds campaign.

This was accomplished by searching on one of the following hashtags: #WhiteWednesdays both in Persian and English, and #Wednesdays\_Without\_Compulsion in Persian.

Given the political sensitivity of the protest, as well as the anonymity of the photos on the MySF page, we relied on Masih to identify and provide us with contact information for potential interviewees. Based on her relationship with each interviewee, she secured their commitment to participate in our inquiry. We therefore encountered no ‘no-shows.’ Snowball sampling only generated three candidates, highlighting Masih's centrality as a trusted broker in the MySF network

Given the security concerns that some MySF activists might have talking to a perfect stranger over the phone about a punishable ofence they had committed, the first author – an Iranian native who conducted all the interviews – recorded a short WhatsApp video message in which she introduced herself. She sent this clip as part of her request to interview a given candidate. In order to establish some degree of trust and rapport with the interviewee, she talked about her experience with hijab and feelings towards the law in her video. She also explained her interest in MySF and the motivation behind her study.

To generate rich and experience-near data, an interview strategy that focused on a particular incident was chosen. By discussing the production and publication of a particular photograph or video through which the interviewee had enacted his/her protest, we were able to collect not just descriptions of events and general opinions, but also insight into how the interviewees made meaning of and felt about their performance (Schultze & Avital, 2011). The phone interviews, which lasted between 30 and 95 min (average 45 min), were conducted by the first author in Persian between June 2016 and October 2017. All interviews but one were recorded and transcribed. All the material in an interview that related to themes around identity, collectivity, practices, embodiment and afect, was translated into English, not only to make interview quotes accessible to the second author, but also to prepare them for future use in publications. On average, at least half of the material that constituted an interview was translated.

The first author also conducted one synchronous interview with Masih in June 2016, but continued to stay in touch with her throughout the project to request contact information for additional interviewees – Masih provided us with two to five names every few weeks – and to ask follow-up and clarification questions. These requests for additional information were submitted to Masih via the WhatsApp voice feature; she would then answer through the same application. This asynchronous form of interviewing generated voice files that totaled 35 min. We also interviewed a MySF administrator who helps Masih translate the photo captions to English and to manage the page in general. Table 3 shows the information of our interviewees. Apart from Masih, all the names used in this paper are pseudonyms.

## 4. Empirical analysis

In order to gain insight into the role that different configurations of cyberactivism play in the constitution of solidarity in social movements (Hunt & Benford, 2004; May, 2007), we compare the enactment of the MySF movement via its FB page and WWeds respectively. Following a process logic, we rely on Blumer's (1995) work on the production of solidarity in social movements to analyze the practices of each technology-enabled activism configuration. We then discern the forms of solidarity they produce. Our findings are summarized in Table 4.

## 4.1. Enacting My Stealthy Freedom via Facebook

In this section we discuss how MySF is enacted through the practices of ceremonial behavior, informal fellowship, and identification via Facebook.

## 4.1.1. Ceremonial behavior

The practices of activism in the MySF FB campaign manifested themselves primarily in the content posted on its fan page. We identified three practices under the umbrella of ceremonial behavior: the practice of protest, sharing and amplification.

4.1.1.1. Practice of protest. On the FB page, practice of protest that performed the opposition to the compulsory hijab, entailed taking of the headscarf – however momentarily – in a public space and documenting this illegal act by taking a photo. The experience of being without a headscarf in public tended to produce powerful emotions including fear, stress, excitement, joy and pride. Sahar, a woman in her early thirties described her emotional response to enacting this form of protest:

“I was walking in the Shariati St. I took of my scarf. I was extremely scared; but I dropped the scarf to my shoulders and started taking selfies.”

Some women attributed their ability to engage in this risky and illegal act and to deal with the strong emotions it generated, to the obligation they felt towards the movement in general. Nilan, in her early twenties, ascribed her calmness to the sense of duty she felt towards Iranian women in general:

“I am paying my debt to the people whose voice has been silenced for years… At the time, although [what I was doing] was frightening, I don't know why I was calm; I was thinking this is my responsibility…”

It is interesting to note that this normatively-motivated solidarity is reflective of universal rather than specific solidarity in that Nilan enacted her protest in support of imaginary women that had been silenced in the past rather than members of the MySF movement. The lack of individuated solidarity is understandable in light of the fact, that the acts of resistance documented on the MySF fan page were anonymous. Unable to identify group members, the opportunities for feelings of devotion to develop for MySF members were limited

After taking of their scarves in a public space the women posed for a photo. Even though each photograph submitted to the MySF FB page was unique, the images nevertheless contained a number of elements that rendered them representative of the movement. These included (i) holding the headscarf in the wind like a flag of freedom (Fig. 1) or dropping it like a discarded tissue (Fig. 2): (ii) being located in a public space (from deserted locations to populous religious sites); and (iii) enacting gestures of freedom (e.g., jumping, throwing arms into the air, letting hair flow in the wind, smiling broadly).

These elements afected not only the woman in the photograph, but also those who viewed it. The riskier the act of being hijabless (e.g., in spaces with Islamic meaning such as shrines or national monuments) and the more likely the women engaged in the practice of protest was to getting caught (e.g., presence of supporters of the hijab), the braver the act was perceived to be. Armita, a woman in her late 30s, explained:

“I like the photos that are taken in the real context of the society… the ones that are not taken in a deserted place and are not that ‘stealthy’… Because I had the same experience of taking these kinds of photos and I know that it isn't easy at all.”

Evident in this interview quote, is the notion of afective attunement. Even though Armita did not know any of the women in the images, she stepped into their shoes and imagined how the moment of protest must have felt for a given individual. Armita's own experience with this act of resistance facilitated her vicarious re-living the event depicted in the image. Indeed, despite being enacted in diferent places, and at diferent times, the removal of the headscarf in a public space was an experience that MySF activists shared, albeit in a highly individuated manner. By feeling their way through the other's act of protest and experiencing it in their imagination, viewers of photographs assessed both the action and the actor in terms of the degree of bravery exhibited.

Such an empathic viewing of the MySF images motivated some to mimic acts of resistance that they admired. In this way, they communicated their devotion to the activist whose actions had inspired them. Amir, a man who sent a photo wearing a hijab, was moved to do so in just this manner:

“I was following MySF page. Two or three other photos of men were shared at the time before I sent my photo… I liked it and thought I can also express myself and my solidarity through a similar photo…”

Thus, re-enacting protest actions was an expression of solidarity, as it supported other activists. Importantly, re-enacting the other's protest action went beyond afective attunement, which relies largely on the power of the viewer's imagination to step into the metaphorical shoes of another. Instead, actually enacting a particular practice of protest meant that the activist and those that copied her actions got as close to a shared experience as possible in the absence of co-location.

4.1.1.2. Practice of sharing. Practice of sharing renders public and collective the situated and individual acts of protest described above. In MySF, the practice of sharing includes publishing the hijabless photo on MySF's fan page. However, the practice of sharing in MySF followed a process that subverted some of the Facebook afordances, especially the attribution of content to individua contributors.

After taking the photo, the protestor sent it to Masih using Facebook's private message feature or her Telegram or WhatsApp account. Although sending a photo for Masih to post anonymously provided some sense of safety, contributors always faced the risk of being identified (e.g., by a neighbor or colleagues that recognized them). Thus, the decision to publish a hijabless photo, which provided evidence of breaking the law, weighed heavily on most MySF members. Moreover, given the objective of MySF campaign, sending a photo to Masih for publication on the FB fan page politicized the act of resistance. The publication of a hijabless photograph thus produced strong emotions, as Shiva, a woman in her late fifties, described:

“I hid it (sending a photo to MySF) from my family. Because my husband is very conservative… Then I was expecting every second that someone would knock the door and arrest me.”

While taking of one's scarf in public to take a photo for MySF required considerable courage, this practice was mostly momentary and visible to those within perceptual distance. While recording the illegal act with a camera already gave it some permanence, this was further extended through the practice of sharing. Making the ‘stealthy’ act of being hijabless outside the home public on the MySF fan page, rendered it not only globally visible but also potentially punishable. To make activists aware of the risks, a fairly elaborate protocol for gaining activists' unequivocal consent to publish their photo was developed for the MySF FB page. Even though the photographs were posted anonymously, Masih personally and repeatedly asked contributors to reflect on the potential consequence of their activism.

The photo's publication on the MySF FB page claimed this protest performance for the movement. By adding it to the repository of prior acts of protest, the single, situated act contributed to the sense of collective and unity. Indeed, the movement was produced by aggregating in one place (i.e., MySF page) evidence of these geographically-distributed and individual acts of resistance. The community's existence was reassuring to the activists. As Shiva, an interviewee in her late 50s, highlighted that the sense of belonging she got when she first entered the MySF page evoked:

“When I entered the MySF page, I really enjoyed the courage of my fellow country women. It gave me hope. I told myself ‘God! I was thinking I am the only victim [of this law]. Look how many people are like me’… You know, there [on MySF], you feel like you are part of a movement; that you are not alone.”

Elika, a woman in her early forties, expressed her association with the collective as a source of power for social change in allegorical terms:

“When an ant had poured just a bit of water on Ibrahim's fire, it put the fire out. It's the same for us too. If we become more and more, we are more influential… As they say ‘a sea is constituted of small drops’. I want to say we can do something if we do it together; we can create a wave to change something. This is why I sent a photo to MySF”.

This quote highlights Elika's solidarity to the movement as a whole, rather than its members. Implied in it is also Elika's sense of duty towards those who have already put themselves at risk by participating in the MySF FB presence as the motivation for her to publish a hijabless photo.

However, building and maintaining the integrity of MySF as a purely virtual organization came with considerable responsibility. In particular, Masih had to satisfy herself that the women who sent her photographs were indeed who they claimed to be. The reliability of the MySF FB page as a representation of Iranians' resistance to the compulsory hijab rested in large part on Masih's ability to spot suspicious contributions (e.g., stings by the government) that were intended to discredit the site. Completing background checks on new contributors was thus important albeit time-consuming work.

4.1.1.3. Practice of amplification. Practice of amplification in social media extended the reach of the content, making others outside the boundaries of the in-group aware of the group's actions. These practices included liking, commenting, and sharing MySF content (i.e., metavoicing (Majchrzak, Faraj, Kane, & Azad, 2013)). When one likes, comments, or shares a content on social media, algorithms calculate a page rank, which determines a post's visibility to others. Nasim, an interviewee in her late 30s, explained that ‘liking’ MySF photos reflected a normatively-motivated form of solidarity:

“In order to support the campaign, I “like” the photos. And as you may know, when I “like” a photo, my friends can also see it in their news feed.”

Each photo on MySF had tens to hundreds of “likes”, comments and replies to comments, each of which was identified. Members of MySF relied on the practice of amplification to enact their devotion to the others in MySF, especially those that had published a photo. Nooshin, an interviewee in her early 30s who had published a hijabless photograph, explained the afective response others comments evoked:

“There were many encouraging comments … they inspire you and give you energy. They were writing ‘Good luck! We support you…’ This made me really happy”.

Empathic comments and praise like “Bravo!”, “You are a brave and strong Persian lioness! Respect!”, “Keep fighting!”, and “Stay strong!” not only expressed solidarity, but also served to drown out the negative comments that were inevitably posted by supporters of the compulsory hijab. Importantly, the practice of amplification produces statistics that make the size and engagement of MySF as a social movement readily visible. These countable acts of resistance demonstrate that, despite its virtuality, MySF is a collective body in that all acts of protest enacted by its members, are accumulated in one place.

## 4.1.2. Practice of informal fellowship

Informal fellowship refers to practice of back-stage interpersonal exchanges that allows the members of a movement to get to know each other as individuals. Due to the anonymity of members and the individualistic nature of the practice of protest, enacting informal fellowship was virtually impossible on the MySF fan page. To the extent that any informal fellowship occurred, it was limited to the dyadic exchange between Masih and an individual member who sought to publish a photo or video on the FB page. For example, Masih occasionally wrote private messages to women who had posted content on the site to give them a sense of the difference their contributions were making, Sahar, a woman in her early 30s, described receiving a note from Masih thanking her for the video she had posted on MySF:

“I can never forget that a few months after my first video Masih sent me a message telling me that ‘Sahar! I wanted to thank you! Because after your video, women became braver.’ Because [until then] lots of women were sending photos and videos with sunglasses or something to not be identifiable. This message made me so happy that I decided to send another video.”

Some MySF members also reached out to Masih to share their concerns about the MySF movement and current events in Iran that had implications for women's rights. In turn, Masih reached out to members in the MySF collective who she regarded as potentia leaders in order to engage them in back-stage discussions about strategy and new campaigns. The women she confided in helped her shoulder the burden of managing and maintaining the movement and mobilizing MySF members.

The lack of informal fellowship among MySF members limited access to other activists' emotions and ideas. This made it dificult to develop feelings of caring and devotion for individuals based on anything but what the viewer experienced by looking at a staged photograph of an act of resistance.

## 4.1.3. Practice of identification: in- and out-group relations

Practice of identification defines a movement's membership boundaries. While the primary boundary is between the in- and the out-group, stratification of either of these is also possible. In general, individuals were part of the MySF movement if they contributed content that opposed compulsory veiling in Iran. In contrast, the out-group was composed of people who actively insulted, denigrated and harassed MySF activists, as well as those who were aware of the site but did not contribute any content at all. This invisible group of lurkers was dificult to identify however, and thus did not have the generative efect on in-group identification as the movement's detractors did.

The out-group's ability to communicate its disdain for the MySF movement on the MySF page itself, galvanized the movement's members. Abusive comments reminded MySF members that they had a common enemy and an ongoing battle on their hands. It galvanized them around their sense of rage about compulsory veiling. It also motivated them to post supportive comments on the MySF page, thereby boosting the collective's engagement statistics. However, as the following quote from Nooshin, a 30-year old female interviewee indicates, people who posted photographs were considered braver than those that merely posted comments:

“In the comments, you can see how many people are like you. They were wishing me success and were sympathizing with me… There were even comments telling me ‘we're like you but we don't have the courage to send a photo.’”

Thus, actions such as liking and commenting, which identified the contributor, but which were not illegal, create a sub-group within the in-group. Furthermore, the more identifiable the activist in a given photograph was, the braver she was deemed to be:

“I wasn't that brave I guess, I chose the photos in which my face wasn't shown but the photo conveys the message!”

(Nasim)

In sum, practices of protest and sharing enact a braver member of the in-group than practice of amplification does. And those who render themselves identifiable in their photographs, are regarded as braver than those who try to hide their identity.

## 4.2. Enacting White Wednesdays (WWeds)

As the second configuration of the hybrid online-ofline enactment of the MySF social movement, WWeds interfaced considerably with the MySF FB page; specifically, WWeds activists sent photos and videos to Masih for anonymous publication. To avoid duplication, we will limit our analysis below to the practices that were unique to the WWeds configuration of activism in the three practices of solidarity: ceremonial behavior, informal fellowship, and identification.

## 4.2.1. Ceremonial behavior

4.2.1.1. Practice of protest. Practice of protest in WWeds entailed women wearing white on a Wednesday and recording a video of themselves in public. While signaling their opposition to the veiling laws, women enacting this form of protest were not actually breaking the law. The white scarf symbolized their desire to emerge out of the dark' political times Iran women were in, as well as the women's opposition to compulsory veiling, of which the black chador represented the most extremely conservative form. As one of our interviewees pointed out, “We are protesting hijab using hijab itself.”

While the alliteration of WWeds made the campaign's name memorable, the middle of the week was chosen because it was neither a weekend in Iran (i.e., Thursday and Friday) nor in Western countries (i.e., Saturday and Sunday). Since Tuesday had already been appropriated by another social movement (i.e., one that sought to reduce car use), the last day of the Iranian work week (i.e., Wednesdays) was chosen. Masih explained her reasoning as follows:

“It was important for me to be able to have media coverage in … [the] international environment. And in the domestic environment, in work places – universities and all these places that they now send videos and photos from – there I wanted to create a ‘real’ discussion around the issue.”

While the practice of protest on which the MySF movement was built was enacted ‘stealthily’ and for a relatively short duration, WWeds' acts of resistance extended over the entire day. This increased the opportunities for fellow activists to identify each other and engage in informal fellowship. With the white headscarf – and white clothing for men – acting as a uniform that identified its wearer as a potential member of the MySF movement, the collective manifested itself in the color of ‘the streets:’

“Men and women, together, shoulder by shoulder, we paint the streets of our city white. #WhiteWeds.”

(from Twitter, #WhiteWednesdays)

Wearing white at the same time and in the same space aforded collectivization, as protesters became aware of one another and capable of synchronizing their behavior. There were some eforts underway to organize gatherings such that the collective as a body could be performed:

“Slowly, and step by step, united, through a collective work we take white Weds in certain hours to city squares, so that you can see the power of our civil movement.”

(from Twitter, #WhiteWednesdays)

The imagery of ‘streets painted white’ is emblematic of the campaign's objective to make visible just how many ordinary Iranian citizens, both men and women, were opposed to compulsory veiling. Since the Iranian government and supporters of hijab maintained that the vast majority of Iranians favored the veiling laws, a key objective of WWeds was to challenge these claims openly.

Apart from making the collective visible, white clothing worn on Wednesday served as a mechanism for identifying members of the MySF movement and for individuals to identify with it, even outside of Iran. For example, Masih took to wearing white and putting a white flower in her hair during media appearances. Sara, whose protest actions had gotten her arrested a few times and who had since fled Iran, shared a photo of herself, dressed in white, taken in Turkey. She wrote the following caption:

“Before [fleeing Iran] I would still wear white and post a WWeds photo on Weds: but that was different. I knew I was gonna go back [to Iran] … Now I don't know anymore; but I know that I am still a protester. So, I still wear white every Wed… I am a member of WWeds movement and a protester to the compulsory hijab…”

By means of WWeds' explicit symbol of collective identity, a form of solidarity that was based on situated and inter-persona relationships with members of the community was produced. For instance, since non-activists might also wear white headscarves or clothes on Wednesdays, individuals signaled their membership in the MySF collective by means of subtle gestures (e.g., smiling, nodding) directed at co-present others:

“I had a white scarf as always. A girl with a white scarf was passing in the street, she showed me V with her fingers.”

(from Twitter, #WhiteWednesdays)

The regularity and rhythm with which WWeds was enacted meant that activists had the opportunity to build personal relationships with other activists. This implied that the motivation to help others rested increasingly on personal knowledge of group members:

“The advantage of white Weds is that it is a reminder every week, … and by seeing other brave leaders, the fear of others will decrease as well.”

(from Twitter, #WhiteWednesdays)

Indeed, the physically-embodied, repetitive and local nature of WWeds activism ritualized the practice of protest. Enacting the ritual of wearing white on a weekly basis, solidified not only an activists' identification with the MySF movement, but it also gave her an appreciation for the persistence and continued dedication that was needed to prevail in their desire to make hijab voluntary. Narges, a woman in her early 20s explained:

“I think if you raise your voice only once, nobody would hear you. The protest that you start should be continuous. You should continue it until you get what you want, until they give back your right. For example, if I send a photo only once and tell myself OK, it's done now, nothing will change. I need to continue… I will continue participating and sending photos and videos until the law is changed and hijab becomes optional in Iran.”

The personally-identifiable and rhythmic nature of WWeds' protest practice meant that this campaign was riskier than MySF despite their legality. Increasingly, WWeds activists found themselves being summoned and arrested. Niusha, who had posted a WWeds video under her own name and who was subsequently summoned by the authorities, recorded a video of herself in front of the court with her white scarf on her shoulders saying:

“They've summoned me because of the WWeds campaign… I just know that no matter what, with all the summoning and detaining, they can't stop us and cause us to give up our legitimate rights. No to the compulsory hijab!”

It is interesting to note, that Niusha's use of collective pronouns framed her act of protest as representative of the collective (i.e., “can't stop us and cause us to give up our legitimate rights”). As such, her act of defiance in front of the court can be seen as an expression of solidarity in that she is likely to endure punishment on behalf of the other MySF members.

The choice of video as the medium for representing WWeds practice of protest increased the afective aspects of an individual's experience of activism. Video provides insight into the situated performer, i.e., the specific place, time and emotional state in which she enacts her protest. Since the videos were shot from a close-up angle as the activist held the camera phone herself, viewers had first-hand access to the performer's facial expressions, her movements and her intonations. With this intimate portrayal of being a WWeds activist, others were better able to infer the activist's emotions and inner struggles. This meant that less imagination on the part of the viewer was needed to empathize and step into the shoes of the protester.

The content of the WWeds' videos also tended to be more emergent and impromptu than the content of the photos on the MySF page. For example, one activist sent a video of herself with her white scarf on her shoulders and started to tell her story of being arrested by the morality police and sleeping in jail for one night two years ago. She got emotional reliving her experience and completed her account on the verge of tears. Her video caption read “Sorry my dear Masih! I started to cry in the middle of the video. Every time I remember the night they arrested me and how they humiliated me, I feel a lump in my throat.” Her video elicited an outpouring of solidarity reflective of viewers' affective attunement: “I cried all the way... Sorry." “My dear, I am sad and angry to see how you were treated” and “I am in solidarity with your sufering, dear sister.”

4.2.1.2. Practice of sharing. In addition to sending WWeds videos to Masih to be published anonymously on the MySF FB page, activists also started sharing them on their personal social media pages using the #WhiteWednesdays hashtag. Use of #WWeds to identify social media posts on individuals' personal Facebook, Twitter and Instagram accounts made it easy to identify and get in touch with individual activists. This created the conditions for increased back-stage, informal fellowship, as both on- and off-line interactions among WWeds activists becomes increasingly viable.

One of the afordances of tagging social media contributions with a unique hashtag in this context was that individuals' and the collective's acts of protest could be aggregated and quantified across multiple social media platforms. In this way, the body of the collective was globally manifest, independent of local time and space. Indeed, like wearing white in ofline space, hashtagging content in online space became a symbol that signaled group membership and thereby identified the collective.

However, with the distribution of WWeds content across multiple social media platforms and sites, the values and beliefs of the movement became more pluralistic. For instance, the #WWeds hashtags were used with various other hashtags to align other social and political issues with the WWeds protests. Examples of hashtags that were combined with the WWeds handle include #IranRegimeChange, #Nationwide\_Demonstrations, #9\_million\_dollar (referring to the \$9 million stolen from a public fund), and #Shortage\_of\_Water. This concatenation of causes in WWeds began diluting MySF's almost-singular focus on the veiling law and it saw the emergence of new sub-groups and new leaders. some of whom criticized Masih:

“I have a question, why @MasihAlinejad has lowered our ultimate demands to stealthy freedom and White Wednesdays. Now, our demands have moved further and reached #IslamicRegimeMustGo.”

(from Twitter)

The practice of sharing WWeds videos on individuals' social media pages across multiple platforms created the conditions for the expansion of MySF's values, beliefs and leadership. The movement's larger footprint increased MySF's reach, normalized participation in it and created new leaders, such as Sara who started participating in WWeds with more than 6000 followers on Instagram. By mid 2018, Sara's following had increased to 31,000. As more members of Iranian society were confronted with posts protesting the compulsory hijab that acquaintances, friends and family members made weekly, the hope was that the fear of expressing opposition to the veiling law would subside.

4.2.1.3. Practice of amplification. The metavoicing (Majchrzak et al., 2013) practice aforded by social media, e.g., “liking,” commenting, retweeting and sharing, amplified individuals' digitally-shared acts of protest. However, when WWeds content was posted on individuals' personal social media accounts, the support and responses from the network of users tended to be more personal than on MySF's Facebook page. For example, a disabled woman who had initially sent her WWeds a video to the MySF FB page, specifying that it be posted in a way that identified her, amassed a 20,000-strong Instagram following. She described the reactions she got from people as follows:

“I still get direct and private message and comments saying ‘bravo’ or telling me that I'm very inspiring… (After these reactions and my WWeds experience) I believed in myself and my capabilities even more, and now I am even more excited to try new things and new ideas that can be interesting for people and for my followers.”

Another way of amplifying another's activism was to copy her actions and post them with a reference to her. For example, one woman sent a hijabless photo taken in a famous historical place, where previously one of the WWeds activists had been arrested. The caption read:

“Here is Kashan, the place where [WWeds activist] was arrested; I was hijabless here the whole time…”

The arrested WWeds protestor, whose activism had sparked this performance of resistance, in turn re-shared this photo, thereby amplifying her initial actions further. She formulated the following caption:

“I am happy that my trace is in Kashan… that there are men and women going to that place remembering me… I love all of them and thank them for their support.”

As the caption highlights, copying an activists' practices of resistance was an act of solidarity.

## 4.2.2. Practice of informal fellowship

Practice of informal fellowship was enabled in WWeds as people met on the streets and could be identified on social media. Wearing the uniform of white scarves and clothes on Wednesdays meant that supporters of MySF not only performed their protest, but in so doing also developed an infrastructure for identification, both of the MySF movement and individual's afiliation with it. For example, the caption accompanying a photograph that was sent to Masih to post as part of the WWeds campaign on the MySF page read:

“First, it was only my daughter standing there with a white headscarf, then other women joined her gradually and asking her if she was there for ‘White Wednesdays.’ This is how we find each other in public to say NO to the compulsory hijab.”

Furthermore, using the WWeds hashtag, members were able to find each other and interact online. Narges, a 21-year-old woman, who posted photos of herself wearing white almost every Wednesday, explained how this aforded the identification of like-minded people:

“You know, using WWeds hashtag has resulted in finding new friends on Instagram… finding people, who are like myself against the compulsory hijab or are using the same hashtag to protest it.”

These opportunities for informal communication had important consequences for solidarity: it becomes more personal and more afective. This implied that individuals' dispositions to take care of and support other group members grew stronger. For example, when one member of the MySF movement (Aida) was arrested for a few days, another woman, who did not know Aida personally, published a video encouraging her and letting Aida know she was neither alone nor forgotten. Masih describes this instance of support as follows:

“When [Aida] was arrested, she was in a very bad condition, she was beaten in prison… I remember that a girl published a video supporting her and then when [Aida] was released she saw the video and she was very impressed… Then the girl went to [Aida]'s house to meet her; and now that girl herself is one of the important faces of the campaign… This was a very good instance of support because that girl actually tried to be [Aida]'s voice.”

Eforts to stand in – either on- or of-line – for others who could not be present, might represent the ultimate form of solidarity. For instance, endeavoring to speak for the other while she was detained, can be seen as quite literally standing in the other's shoes.

## 4.2.3. Practice of identification: in- and out-group relation

Being identified as a member of the MySF movement through the WWeds campaign was thoroughly bound up with its protest practice, i.e., wearing white on a Wednesday, making a video, publishing the video (especially on activist's personal website), and hashtagging it. Despite the legality of wearing a white head-scarf, their identifiability put the WWeds activists at considerable risk of arrest. It also opened them up to harassment online when they posted their videos on their personal social media accounts. For this reason, they were regarded as more courageous than those who send their WWeds videos to Masih for anonymous publication on the MySF page. Elika, an interviewee who had submitted a video to be posted on MySF explained:

“I really admire them [who post WWeds content on their own pages]. I can't believe how brave they are… It takes so much courage to do that. I didn't dare to publish my videos on my own page.”

Just as in the MySF campaign, derogatory comments posted in response to WWeds content enacted the out-group. Since harassing comments could be directed personally at identified members of the in-group, the threats posed by the out-group were highly personal and disconcerting. For example, Sara, who published her WWeds photos on her Instagram page every week, explained:

“Two of my photos got famous and then people flooded my Instagram page insulting or threatening me. … They find people who have published photos for White Wednesdays and start their harassment.”

Additionally, the openness of the social media platforms gave the out-group the capability of tarnishing individual members reputations by posting fake content. Sara, the interviewee who had been harassed on Instagram continued:

“I know that for a few women, [members of the out-group] even made fake pages and put photoshopped pornographic photos of them—using these women's photos.”

The digital and distributed nature of the WWed online content made distinguishing between the actions of the in- and out-group challenging. This would potentially undermine the credibility of the MySF movement, whose legitimacy had thus far been established by tightly managing access to the sharing of content on the MySF page.

## 5. Discussion

The objective of our study is to investigate how new forms of solidarity are produced by diferent hybrid configurations of social media activism. This objective is motivated by the current debate in the social movement literature about the relevance of solidarity as the basis for collective action in the era of Internet-enabled and -based activism (Bennett & Segerberg, 2012; Gerbaudo & Treré, 2015: McDonald. 2002). In answer to our research question, we distill two forms of solidarity from our data analysis and consider the role that the diferent sociomaterial configurations of online and ofline practices of resistance play in producing them.

## 5.1. Two forms of solidarity

Our findings suggest that solidarity is still a prominent emotion and consequential phenomenon in online social movements. However, due to the specific configuration of practices enacting a given movement, solidarity takes on different forms. We identified imagined solidarity (observable mainly in MySF's FB presence) and situated solidarity (observable mainly in WWeds) as new forms that arise out of Internet-enabled activism. A summary of these two types of solidarity is presented in Table 5. Below, we elaborate on each.

## 5.1.1. Imagined solidarity

Enacted through MySF's FB configuration of protest, imagined solidarity is grounded in an idealized and undiferentiated sense of the MySF collective. This collective body is performed through the production and consumption of courageous hijabless photos on the MySF page. Although MySF members identify with the movement as a whole, since perception of the other members is only based on staged photos that provide little insight into the individual activist, their identification with the movement is through the act of protest, rather than the activist that performs it.

However, despite the of-line practices of protest being performed by individual women on their own, without the presence of other activists, individual MySF members have a strong sense of “we-ness” and group cohesion online. This is because each member is experiencing the activism of the other vicariously. drawing on her own experiences and assumptions to step into the shoes of the performer in the photograph. A given MySF member thus develops her perceptions of the other's and collective's feelings, values and beliefs by projecting her own onto the protest performances (e.g., hijabless photo). As a result, the experiences and perceptions of the individual member and the imagined other(s) are perfectly aligned. In the absence of counterfactuals to challenge their imagined reality (e.g., interactions with or testimonials of the actual women in the images), an individual member's perceptions of the identit and unity of the community, are likely to be reinforced.

Furthermore, with informal interactions among MySF members being impossible, the idealized image individuals construct of the community are likely to go unchallenged. In fact, demeaning and hurtful posts made by the out-group on the FB page are experienced by any MySF member that reads it, even though it might have been made in response to a specific photograph. Thus, the shared experiences that are key to building MySF into a collective are individually enacted by engaging with posted photographs, i.e., afectively attuning to them, as well as reading, liking and commenting on content.

Indeed, afective attunement is the primary mechanism for generating feelings of imagined solidarity. However, since the emotions generated through attunement are felt vicariously, thus giving them a virtual quality (i.e., ‘as if’ or ‘almost’) the solidarity that is produced is more normative than afective. In other words, the decision to help another activist is based on a normative commitment that is the result of socialized beliefs or the moral imperative to fight injustices (Nepstad, 2004)

It is interesting to note that even though the MySF FB protest configuration was primarily computer-mediated, the centralized and controlled nature of the fan page contributed to a relatively strong sense of collective identity that is generally associated with identity movements (Fenton, 2007). Furthermore, imagined solidarity has some similarities with universal solidarity (Scholz, 2015; Schwartz, 2007); with the members of the collective being anonymous, the participants tend to identify with the movement's overal cause (i.e., opposing the hijab). Some frame it as their duty to engage in the movement for the sake of Iranian women living in the past (e.g., grandmothers) and in the future (e.g., daughters). In other words, imagined solidarity is primarily motivated by norms.

## 5.1.2. Situated solidarity

Situated solidarity, which was enacted in the WWeds configuration of the MySF movement. distinguished itself from the MySF FB campaign primarily in terms of the identifiability of the individual activists. Not only could fellow protesters be identified by the white headscarves and clothes (for men) they wore on a Wednesday, but their reliance on personal social media pages (which were locatable through hashtags) made them accessible even to members who were geographically remote. These affordances created the conditions for the development of sub-groups within MySF that distinguished themselves either on geographical or ideologica grounds. Thus, instead of protesting only the compulsory hijab, there was a range of issues opposed by various WWeds factions manifesting themselves in a concatenation of hashtags.

In contrast to imagined solidarity, situated solidarity is grounded in a broader base of shared experience of seemingly small and ritualistic – rather than dramatic and immediately punishable – practices of resistance. Specifically, wearing white on a Wednesday was not illegal, however, it required considerable commitment to perform this protest once a week as part of an activist's everyday life. What this felt like was thus not the stuf of awe and imagination that might be felt when encountering a hijabless photo taken in front of a police station, but repeated first-hand encounters of making a highly contested and political statement in the face of family, friends, colleagues and neighbors, some of whom might support the compulsory hijab.

In other words, devotion to other activists and the readiness to support them came out of an embodied – rather than imagined – sense of what it takes to fight for one's convictions. The fact that WWeds activists, especially those that posted videos on their personal social media accounts, were likely to be personally harassed, physically threatened and summarily arrested heightened individuals' sense of commitment to ofer aid based on their own afective appreciation for what it meant to be a WWeds activist, as well as the inter-personal bonds that the activists had forged.

In sum, situated solidarity is feelings of devotion towards identified others with whom the supporter has a relationship of some kind, e.g., membership in geographically- or ideologically-based factions of the larger social movement. Based on relatively undramatic but regularly-enacted practice of resistance, activists share a physically-embodied sense of what it is like to protest. As a result, relative to imagined solidarity, situated solidarity is more afectively-motivated.

## 5.2. Theorizing solidarity in hybrid configurations of Internet-enabled activism

Given our configurational approach to theorizing the practices of resistance in MySF, we can now relate imagined and situated forms of solidarity with the hybridization of connective and collective action across the online and ofline space. Table 6 summarizes the configurations of contemporary activism.

This framework highlights that under conditions where collective action is enacted in the ofline space (e.g., protesters have shared experiences of protest either because they are co-located and/or acts of protest that involve the physical body are enacted regularly and with high frequency), a more personally-motivated and afect-driven form of solidarity will emerge (i.e., situated solidarity). This has many similarities with afective solidarity (Vachhani & Pullen, 2019) that is prevalent in online feminist movements (Hemmings, 2012; Vachhani & Pullen, 2019).

In contrast, when the collective action is enacted online, such that individuals have to rely on a vicariously-experienced understanding of acts of resistance (e.g., afectively attuning with others to create a sense of unity), an imagined form of solidarity is likely to emerge. Relative to situated solidarity, this is more normatively than affectively motivated (Nepstad. 2004) and is thus more similar to universal than specific solidarity (Schwartz 2007) A key challenge of imagined solidarity is a false universalism ot protestors' experiences (Phipps, 2016).

With regard to the remaining two quadrants, we draw on the prior literature to hypothesize the types of solidarity that will be produced in each. Where connective action characterizes both the on- and off-line aspects of the movement's configuration of practices, fluidarity best describes the form that solidarity takes on. Fluidarity is the temporary and fleeting feeling of devotion that results from individuals coming together momentarily to enact a public experience of self and other (McDonald. 2002). Importantly. it acknowledges and preserves the distinctiveness of individuals' experience (Nelson, 1999) and embraces the dynamism, complexit and plurality of contemporary social movements (Maruggi, 2012)

In contrast, in social movements that enact collective action in both online and ofline spaces, solidarity as a long-term commitment among movement members that have a perceived sameness (d'Emilio, 2012) is likely to emerge. In identity movements, group cohesion is based on embodied experiences that members share (Barnes, 1999; Blumer, 1995), either by virtue of joint action or a representational understanding of identity that attributes to individuals some fixed properties common to all in a social category (Van Doorn, 2010). The solidarity that emerges in this context is of the type that is frequently conflated with collective identity (Abizadeh, 2005; Hunt & Benford, 2004; Saunders, 2008).

## 5.3. Contributions

Our study makes a number of contributions to the literature on social movements, particularly contemporary movements that rely on hybridized configurations of protest practices in online and offline spaces. First, it offers theoretical clarity by developing a multidimensional definition of solidarity consisting not only of three elements (i.e., group identity, normative motivation and afective motivation), but also three practices (i.e., ceremonial action, informal fellowship and in- and out-group relations). Additionally, we untangle the meaning of collective identity and solidarity, two constructs that are frequently conflated in social movement research

(Abizadeh, 2005; Hunt & Benford, 2004; Saunders, 2008).

Second, our focus on solidarity addresses key gaps in the social movement literature, which has paid relatively little attention to the afective nature of activism, as well as embodiment (Jasper, 2011; Van Ness & Summers-Efler, 2018). Since the body is the seat of emotions, our theorizing of how the social and the material are con-figured to accomplish solidarity, extends research on hybrid, Internet-enabled activism in particular

Third, we answer our research question by identifving and profiling two new forms of solidarity, namely imagined and situated solidarity. Their role in sustaining MySF, which started out as having only an online existence, suggests that there is much value in preserving the concept in e-movement research (Bakardjieva, 2015). Instead of abandoning such phenomena as solidarity (McDonald, 2002, 2015) and collective identity, our results suggest that the continuity between identity movements and social media movements should be acknowledged in order to recognize the new forms of solidarity and protest practices that are emerging

Four, we also contribute to the concept of afective solidarity (Hemmings, 2012; Vachhani & Pullen, 2019), i.e., a sense of devotion to others that is developed by the afective means that shifts ‘individual experience to collective feminist capacity (Hemmings, 2012, p. 150). This form of solidarity has been found to be a prominent source of collectivity in feminist movements (Ahmed, 2004; Hemmings, 2012; Vachhani & Pullen, 2019). We expand this research by highlighting that the site where (on- vs. ofline) and mechanism (imagined vs embodied) through which shared emotions are produced matters in the variations of solidarity that can be accomplished.

Five, we ofer a configurational framework that summarizes the practices under which diferent forms of solidarity will emerge. Through this framework, we argue that the feeling of solidarity is performative and thus, solidarity can be performed diferently through various practices. This view contributes to the theory of performative resistance (Harding, Ford, & Lee, 2017) and how the acts of resistance and a collective with solidarity can be co-constituted through embodied and sociomaterial practices. Thus, we go beyond viewing solidarity as an antecedent or driver for act of protest (Deaux, Reid, Martin, & Bikmen, 2006; Subašić, Reynolds, & Turner, 2008; Van Zomeren Postmes, & Spears, 2008; Wiley & Bikmen, 2012) and argue that resistance and acts of protest perform and are performed by solidarity

Lastly, our study also contributes to the IS research on members' attachment (Ren et al., 2012) and commitment (Bateman, Gray, & Butler, 2011) in online communities. Instead of taking diferent types of commitment as given and as independent variables (Bateman et al., 2011) to explain participation in the online community, we explore how diferent forms of commitment are performed in practice. Furthermore, since solidarity allows us to explore the emotional aspects of social movements (Jasper, 2011), we note that its conceptua infrastructure ofers IS research on identity and bond-based attachment (Ren et al., 2012) a third, afect-based dimensions

## 6. Theoretical and practical implications

Our contributions, particularly the two types of solidarity and the configurational framework, have significant implications for social movement research, which has taken a rather dichotomous view of solidarity in on- and ofline social movements, as well as in collective and connective action (Bennett & Segerberg. 2012: McDonald. 2002). Specifically, scholars have argued that online social movements lack commitment (Diani, 2000) and that they lead to clicktivism (Kavada, 2010). Others have maintained that the afective attunement and feeling of togetherness in online activism do not lead to a sense of community (Poell & van Diick. 2015) and that these communities are temporary and fall apart quickly (Juris. 2012). Our research highlights that a more nuanced understanding of how e-movements are enacted as sociomaterial configurations (Barad, 2003; Mazmanian et al., 2014) is warranted to move this field of inquiry forward (Bakardjieva, 2015). Such an approach preserves the continuity between historical and contemporary enactments of collective action, and thereby acknowledging the historicity of cyberactivism. as well as the importance of activists' bodies in e-movements

While some have argued that the solidarity construct be abandoned in research on contemporary collective action (McDonald, 2002, 2015), our configurational approach to studying social movements advocates a focus on the doings and sayings that activists engage in during the enactment of a social movement. Such a practice lens (Scott & Orlikowski, 2014) afords an understanding of cyberactivism that takes the con-figuration of the social and material into account in the act of protest. One implication of this performative perspective, as also suggested by Harding et al. (2017), is that future research on social movements examines how collective identity, solidarity and individual identity unfold in a moment-by-moment manner.

Our development of imagined and situated solidarity, as well as our configurational framework should ideally serve as theoretica starting points for future research that develops more diferentiated types of solidarity enacted through more fine-grained and complex hybrid configurations of protest practices. Additionally, the relative strengths and weaknesses of the four types of solidarity advanced by our configurational framework should ideally become a key focus of attention, especially since this will help practitioners determine which configuration of on- and ofline practices to pursue in order to achieve the desirable “esprit de corps” (Blumer, 1995) for their social movement.

With regard to practice, we maintain that the two types of solidarity as well as the configurational framework developed in this paper, might be used to determine which type of solidarity is desirable for a given social movement and how it might best be achieved. Specifically, our configurational framework ofers relatively straightforward design considerations, namely how the socia movement's online presence and its ofline actions should be arranged to achieve either a sense of collective identity grounded in collective action or a sense of diversity and individuality based in connective action. Furthermore, the four archetypes of solidarity outlined in the framework could serve as benchmarks to determine the most appropriate for the given Internet-enabled movement

## 7. Limitations

Our findings and contributions need to be understood in light of the limitations of our study. First, the two campaigns (MySF's FB page and WWeds) were both part of the same social movement and they were not entirely independent. Not only did WWeds build on the foundations laid by MySF, but WWeds activists also occasionally posted videos anonymously on the MySF FB page. While the intertwining of the two enactments of MySF makes comparison and contrast somewhat dificult, we endeavored to resolve this by representing the FB page and the WWeds campaign as distinct sociomaterial configurations. This approach highlights the distinctiveness of each campaign's practices, which is captured in our configurational framework, where they occupy orthogonal positions. By diferentiating the two enactments of MySF at the practice level, we argue that their interdependence is a strength as a number of dimensions (e.g., cause, demographic of participants) remain constant, thus strengthening the results of our comparison and contrast.

A second limitation relates to our sampling strategy. Since the contributors to MySF were anonymous and very little information about them was available, it was not possible to apply any form of sampling. We relied on Masih's referrals to secure interviews with diferent contributors. Since Masih knew the participants, she did endeavor to accommodate our request for a diverse sample. However, all of our interviewees had contributed photos or videos to MySF, thus omitting from our sample people who participated in the movement in other ways, e.g., engaging only in metavoicing or merely lurking. Since contributions from both the in- and outgroup can be associated with an individual, future research should be able to secure data that will provide insights into the role that these groups play in the production of solidarity.

## 8. Conclusion

In light of recent debates in the social movement literature about the relevance of collective identity and solidarity in cyberactivism, our research objective was to gain insight into the new forms of solidarity that are produced through the enactment of onand ofline practices of resistance. Developing a multi-dimensional practice-based definition of solidarity, we identify two forms of solidarity produced through diferent configurations of cyberactivism: imagined and situated solidarity. We profile each and identify the sociomaterial configurations that produce them. We thus theorize the development of feelings of devotion towards others in Internet-enabled cyberactivism, paying particular attention to the con-figuration of the technical and social dimensions of the practices enacting it. We also identify promising research directions for extending this, our initial theorizing of how solidarity and what type of solidarity is accomplished when cyberactivism is enacted through a hybridization of online and ofline practices of protest.
