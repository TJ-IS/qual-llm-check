# Platform Structures, Homing Preferences, and Homophilous Propensities in Online Social Networks

HYEOKKOO ERIC KWON, WONSEOK OH, AND TAEKYUNG KIM

HYEOKKOO ERIC KWON (hkkwon7@business.kaist.ac.kr) is a Ph.D. candidate in information systems at Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST). His research interests include economics of information systems, mobile addiction, social media platform competition, and patent trolling. He has published his work in Information Systems Research. He has also presented his work at the International Conference on Information Systems, Workshop on Information Systems and Economics, and Conference on Information Systems and Technology.

WONSEOK OH (wonseok.oh@kaist.ac.kr; corresponding author) is the KCB Chair Professor at the College of Business, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology. He received his Ph.D. in information systems from the Stern School of Business at New York University. His research interests include economics of information technology, mobile consumption and addiction, social networks, and e-book pricing. His work has been published in Information Systems Research, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, Journal of the AIS, Journal of Management Information Systems, Management Science, MIS Quarterly, and Production and Operations Management.

TAEKYUNG KIM (kimtk@suwon.ac.kr) is an assistant professor of business administration at the University of Suwon, Korea. He received his Ph.D. in management information systems from Seoul National University. His publications appear in Information Systems Research, International Journal of Information Management, and Asian Pacific Journal of Information Systems.

ABSTRACT: This study investigates how the attributes of online social platforms (e.g., open vs. closed, symmetric vs. asymmetric social networks) and user preferences regarding platform diversity (e.g., single-homing vs. multi-homing) moderate the influence of homophily on user behaviors across varied social segments. On the basis of panel data, we delve into the interplay among homophily, structural diversities across various online social networking services (SNSs), and users’ homing preferences in such social platforms. The data feature of the SNS consumption behaviors of 10,172 individual users are obtained over a period of 134 days. An agent-based simulation model is developed to further validate and generalize the empirical findings. The simulation and empirical results consistently indicate that propensity toward homophily differs significantly across platform types and users channel adoption behaviors. Online homophily is more pronounced in closed, private social networks than in open, public social networks. Users of asymmetric and symmetric SNSs exhibit weak and strong homophily, respectively. Moreover, whereas users who adopt a single SNS channel tend toward homophily, those who subscribe to multiple SNSs confirm the idea that “opposites attract.” These findings suggest that users of online SNS channels manifest complex human interactions typified by the combination of homophily, heterophily, and asymmetric social preference, although homophily is the most prominent disposition. Our findings suggest that homophily should be considered a dynamically changing human characteristic rather than a static attribute, and stakeholders should exploit the complex nature of users’ homophilous behaviors to address social problems in online SNSs and to enhance the effectiveness of social advertising.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: age homophily, agent-based simulation, gender homophily, homophilous relationships, multi-homing, negative binomial regression, panel data, platforms, SNS, social networking services, social segregation.

Homophily—the inexorable tendency to associate disproportionately with similar others—is one of the most pervasive and fundamental features of our social lives [52, 59]. This “love of the same” phenomenon is widely observed across diverse sociodemographic dimensions (e.g., age, gender, ethnicity, and race) [60]. Homophilous relationships may develop as a result of structural proximity or opportunities for interaction (i.e., induced homophily), or may be a consequence of individual preference (i.e., choice homophily) [59]. Whether inadvertently induced or deliberately chosen, homophily shapes and often regulates individuals’ social undertakings, exerting a profound impact on the information that they receive, the attitudes and norms that they form, and the interpersonal associations that they experience [60]. Although homophily is a key catalyst of positive interpersonal contact, it is often criticized as a root cause of social, political, and economic stratification, including racial segregation and political polarization [5, 56], social division [17], cultural boundaries [74], and economic inequality [78].

Researchers have recently identified an interesting pattern of homophily-driven segregation in major online social networking service (SNS) platforms. Adolescent Facebook users, for example, were discovered to shun alternative, private SNS platforms primarily because their parents began using popular networks and “invading” their online social territories [45]. This participation drastically altered the age structure of Facebook [13]. As approximately 25 percent of teenagers migrated to alternative sites (e.g., Instagram and Path) over the past three years, the proportion of Facebook users age 50 and older increased by more than 80 percent during the same period. Many emerging SNSs offer users a more private space, affording only close friends access to a given circle’s day-to-day activities, including photo sharing and relationship updates. Many studies have presented intriguing insights into how online homophily affects intergroup dynamics on major SNS platforms, but such research is wanting in that it has inadequately clarified the extent of these tendencies, or the frequency with which users interact across dissimilar social groups. In addition, extensive literature (see [40] for a detailed review) regarding homophily in professional, task-oriented settings (e.g., work establishments) exists, but research on the extent of homophily in online social environments is rare. The present study attempts to fill this void.

More important, this research examines how the attributes of online social platforms (e.g., open vs. closed, symmetric vs. asymmetric social networks) and user preferences with respect to platform diversity (e.g., single-homing vs. multi-homing) moderate the influence of homophily on user behaviors across varied social segments. Although physical structures in organizational work settings (e.g., job function, work unit, and affiliation) can alter individuals’ propensities toward homophily [46], little is understood about how the structure and characteristics of online SNSs influence users’ homophilous behaviors. Open platforms (e.g., Facebook) allow “strangers” to form new friendships and interact online, whereas closed platforms (e.g., Path, KAKAO Story<sup>1</sup>) permit only acquaintances who share mobile numbers to engage in personal dialogue. A more nuanced investigation of the association between platform accessibility and the extent of homophily in online social networks is required.

We also delve into whether users of symmetric social networks (e.g., Facebook), in which all social relationships must be mutual and reciprocal, exhibit a stronger propensity toward homophily than do users of asymmetric social networks (e.g., Twitter), wherein one-way or two-way interpersonal relationships can form. We likewise compare the homophilous propensities of multi-homing users, who adopt multiple SNS platforms and their single-homing counterparts, who patronize a single platform. Furthermore, we explore the multifaceted factors of the interaction dynamics created by age and gender homophily on several major online SNS platforms with varying degrees of openness and symmetry. Our study is unique in that we use panel data on the actual SNS usage behaviors of 10,172 individual users over time rather than perceptionbased survey instruments to determine the degree to which platform structures and homing preferences influence individual predisposition toward age and gender homophily. Using real individual consumption data enhances the methodological precision necessary to probe into the concept of online homophily. Extant studies on online homophily define and characterize individual social relationships and homophilous propensities solely on the basis of SNS links and connections instead of the intensity of interactions. This approach can be inaccurate and unreliable given the inequalities among the social relationships formed through SNS forums (e.g., those that are inactive and exist only in a friend list vs. those that are cultivated through “likes” every day). For instance, many Facebook account holders have several thousand Facebook friends, but most of them cannot be considered friends in the conventional sense as social interaction may rarely take place among them. Excluding the nominal relationships manifested in SNS friends lists, we instead use actual mobile SNS app consumption at the aggregate level to refine the operational notion of homophily.

In addition to conducting a usage-oriented empirical examination, we develop a simulation model based on the work of Thomas Schelling [68] to further generalize the empirical findings. Triangulation or the use of multiple methods [9] by means of simulation experiments, in which model parameters are tuned according to empirical data, not only reinforces the credibility of findings in a complementary fashion but also captures a holistic portrayal of a phenomenon under investigation [38, p. 603]. The agent-based simulation mechanism designed for this study permits the flexibility of predicting outcomes in various scenarios [15], thus advancing a holistic view of homophily-related human behaviors in the SNS context. The perspectives offered in this work also extend beyond empirical observations. The combination of real consumption-based empirical scrutiny and simulation-based comprehensive assessment provides an enlightened glimpse into the interplay among homophily, structural diversities across various online SNSs, and users’ homing preferences in such social platforms.

This study provides valuable guidance for stakeholders, such as platform providers, advertisers, and policymakers. By recognizing social interaction patterns among different gender or age groups, advertisers can more precisely identify the dynamics of social contagion (i.e., word of mouth), which can critically promote the design of effective targeting campaigns. Homophily can serve as an important resource for targeted advertising in online social networks because of its potential encouragement of behavioral isomorphism and collective rationality. However, our results suggest that for certain demographic social clusters, heterophily, rather than homophily, more accurately characterizes users’ social relationships and preferences. The extent of homophily fluctuates substantially across diverse age and gender segments, and it is even possible for one of two socially connected people (e.g., a teenager) to exhibit homophilous propensities while the other (e.g., his/her parent) displays heterophilous tendencies. The results reveal weaker homophilous propensities among older age groups than younger age cohorts, suggesting that individual propensity toward homophily changes dynamically as people mature. We also found that individuals exhibit different tendencies toward homophily, depending on the type and number of platforms used. Homophilic tendencies may differ not only across individuals but also across time and place within an individual domain. The key findings (e.g., varying extent of homophilous propensities, dynamic evolution of homophily over time, and platform-dependent homophilous tendencies) suggest that homophily cannot be characterized as an individual’s static attribute but should be viewed as a dynamically evolving human characteristic. These empirical results are expected to help marketers develop effective targeting schemes that would outperform those constructed on the basis of simple demographic characteristics.

Our findings may also sensitize platform providers to the shift occurring in the online social landscape because of the rapid changes in online social structures given that the results uncover the manner by which users distribute and manage their SNS consumption across distinct social categories. The observed regularities may considerably illuminate the degree of “nurturing” that platform owners should devote to homophily and the extent to which they should stimulate diversity. Moreover, the patterns of online homophily may pose different ramifications for various platforms. Online homophily may minimally affect the sustainability of certain providers but may create a slew of challenges for others, depending on platform structure and accessibility. Finally, policymakers should be alerted that homophily formed and nurtured through SNSs can be a primary source of the “filter bubble,” which refers to an environment where users develop an affinity only for individuals of backgrounds and predilections similar to their own and avoid intermingling with those who espouse opposing views. Our findings present policy implications related to the adverse effects of SNS-driven online homophily on social segregation and political polarization.

## Theoretical Background And Hypotheses

Numerous theoretical frameworks and empirical manifestations have explored a variety of dimensions of homophily, including race, ethnicity, age, gender, and religion. In this study, we focus primarily on two attributes of homophily—age and gender—since trends regarding these factors are strong and pervasive across all global communities. Although race and ethnicity may have the strongest impact on social structures and political segregation in certain regions of the world, their effects are limited in many countries around the globe as well as in online social network platforms [77].

## Homophily in Online Social Network Platforms

According to Fischer [21], age-based homophilous ties persist longer, with more frequent personal exchanges, than do relationships formed by people of dissimilar ages. Individuals in similar age cohorts are likely to encounter similar life course experiences that influence their values, norms, and interests because they have grown up within the same sociohistorical context [48]. Given that the sharing of views, norms, and interests is an important function that online SNSs offer, users of similar ages would naturally exhibit homophilous propensities in such platforms. In fact, age-based homophily is naturally cultivated early in life because of structural age segregation in educational institutions and care facilities such as nurseries and day-care centers [81]. These institutions cluster children by age throughout their childhood; similarly, sports and music programs are organized according to school grade or age. This pattern is further reinforced as children are exposed to television programs and movies that target particular age groups. Various social influences promote the adoption of the behaviors of others, and assimilations occur frequently between individuals in similar age cohorts [24]. These structural and institutional forces are part of a culture that encourages age homophily [81]. Homophily becomes the norm through which individuals of a similar age can establish trust and solidarity more easily than those who are of dissimilar age [49].

Verbrugge [82] reveals that only about 20 percent of individuals name someone of the opposite sex as their closest friend. In addition, Marsden [56] finds a strong preference for social ties and friendships between same-sex individuals. Ibarra [36] shows that men tend to form more gender-driven homophilous networks than women, particularly in work establishments where they represent a strong majority. According to Aldrich et al. [1], both men and women tend to rely on men as routes through which they can accomplish tasks and obtain key information beyond their immediate networks. An online SNS platform such as Facebook is a social place where people of different generations interact and converse with their close friends, create their life stories, and share moments and memories. In addition, people use SNS sites to reconnect with old acquaintances (e.g., campus buddies and high school sweethearts), find jobs and mates, play social games, and recommend favorite products or services. In contrast to physical, offline social networks, online social networks enable users to form new ties beyond geographical barriers and outside their social boundaries (e.g., in the school and workplace). In a recent study, Singla and Richardson [73] find that friends who communicate through instant messaging services share similar demographic characteristics (e.g., age and gender). Similarly, based on a survey of 35 Facebook users—primarily students and working staff at a suburban college in the United States—Gilbert and Karahalios [25] predict the effects of various factors on the strength of ties formed through online social networks. Their findings suggest that while thread depth negatively affects tie strength, emotional closeness has a positive effect on relational strength. A research team at Facebook describes the principle of “birds of a feather surf together,” which indicates the homophilous tendencies of online SNS users who interact frequently to share similarities and consume similar information [3]. According to Fiore and Donath [20], despite the lack of physical, face-to-face interaction, users of online dating systems prefer partnering with people like themselves in terms of race, lifestyle, religion, and education level, among other characteristics. Gu et al. [30] find that homophily is prevalent in virtual investment communities, despite the benefits from heterophily. Park et al. [63] adopted the homophily paradigms to identify users’ communication patterns and develop trust validation mechanisms. Yan et al. [84] discover that homophily plays a pivotal role in constructing and maintaining a patient’s online health-care social network. These findings provide evidence that homophily is an apparently universal phenomenon, as evident in both online and offline social worlds.

Regarding the homophily phenomenon in online social environments, research has shown that SNS users welcome activities of members who share similarities in terms of age and gender in their SNS platforms because of the positive network externalities of doing so [42]. The value of social exchanges increases as more potential peers with similar attributes are active in SNS platforms. As similar others enter the SNS platform, additional social learning opportunities arise, which induces people to use their SNS platforms more frequently [17]. Social learning effects are reinforced with the addition of peers who enhance the utility of social and relational resources within the cohort. Conversely, users may feel less comfortable when the number of members with different characteristics in their SNSs is increased. Many users reluctantly accept friend requests from people in dissimilar groups (e.g., parents, uncles, teachers, and bosses) because refusing the invitation can be viewed as impolite and antisocial [41, 58]. If discomfort levels are high enough, users may become less active when members from dissimilar groups use their SNS more actively. Therefore, we posit the following:

Hypothesis 1 (Online Homophily Effects): SNS users exhibit homophilous behavior in their SNS platforms.

## Platform Accessibility and Online Homophily

SNS platforms differ in many aspects, including their degree of openness. Some SNS platforms (e.g., Facebook and Instagram) are relatively more open and public in that even “strangers” (e.g., a friend’s friend’s friend) can easily enter others’ social circles [76]. SNS users of open platforms can connect to anyone using the same services. However, this openness often comes at a cost. Users of open platforms often find it difficult to refuse or ignore a friend request for social and personal reasons. In order to expand its network influence, the Facebook company uses a matching algorithm to send friend requests, and encouragement of users to accept these requests is built right into its structure [72]. As a consequence, users are often left with no option to refuse; they have no choice but to get connected with “strangers.” In contrast, other SNS platforms (e.g., Path) are far more personal and protective, allowing users to state their preferences and block strangers from crossing social boundaries [76]. For example, users of these “closed” SNS platforms can become connected only when they exchange phone numbers or other identifying information.

Open platforms foster induced homophily as a result of structural and systematical constraints (e.g., mandatory friend requests) [59]. On the other hand, closed platforms, such as Path, foster choice homophily, or growth of networks that are established and maintained by individuals’ own choice (e.g., via exchanges of phone numbers). Networks in closed platforms are more homogeneous than those in open platforms, as users choose their own friends with whom they have regular contact [26, 41]. Consequently, homophily will be more pronounced on closed platforms compared to open platforms. Relationships that are developed and cultivated on closed platforms are characterized by strong ties, similarities, and homogeneities, including age and gender. In contrast, relatively less homogeneity is evident in relationships on open platforms, since many of the ties and bonds are created by the open structure, and users have little choice about them (e.g., weak ties with strangers and algorithm-induced friend requests). Therefore, we posit:

Hypothesis 2 (Platform Accessibility Effects): Online homophily is more pronounced in closed, private social networks than in open, public social networks.

## Relationship Symmetry and Online Homophily

Although both Facebook and Twitter are commonly referred to as online social networks, they differ fundamentally in the communication and socialization methods users use to form networks and social relationships. Twitter operates based on an “asymmetric” model in which a user can follow many people who do not follow him or her and be followed by many users that he or she does not follow. The directed nature of asymmetric social platforms does not require user reciprocation, so a link created by one user to another may not exist in reverse. In contrast, Facebook uses a “symmetric” model as follows: whenever a user adds others as friends, these friends must do so in return in order to establish a link. Thus, all connections and social relationships must be mutual in Facebook-type symmetric social networks, but can be one- or two-way on Twitter-like asymmetric social networks [65].

Kwak et al. [50] searched the entire Twitter site to investigate how its 41.7 million users are connected. They found that, unlike symmetric social networks such as Facebook and MySpace, Twitter’s asymmetric network exhibits a nonpower-law distribution with shorter effective network diameter and lower reciprocity. From a sociometric network perspective, these structural properties tend to produce networks rich in “weak ties.” According to Granovetter [27], the strength of a tie is determined by the levels of interaction, emotional intensity, intimacy (mutual confiding), and reciprocity. Twitter restricts interaction to a 140-character limit and requires neither mutual confiding nor reciprocity as a precondition for social interaction. Consequently, the architecture of its asymmetric network has many structural holes and weak ties [50]. On one hand, this followed–follower asymmetry can result in vertical social hierarchy and positional inequality in terms of access to network resources [47]. On the other hand, however, the asymmetric social structure enables users to broaden their scope of communication and socialization, embracing structural alienation and social diversity [47]. In contrast, strong ties are more pronounced in symmetric social networks where relationships are usually formed and sustained (at least temporarily) through mutual confiding and reciprocity [27]. The symmetric model relies heavily on relational balance and lateral cohesion to sustain social systems. Empowered by many close-knit ties, the symmetric social structure naturally preserves homogeneity.

Relationship symmetry may be significantly associated with users’ propensity toward homophily. Granovetter [27, p. 1376] states that “weak ties are more likely to link members of different small groups than are strong ones, which tend to be concentrated within particular groups.” Full of structural holes and weak ties, the asymmetric network (e.g., Twitter), therefore, has the power to connect different social segments that may have heterogeneous characteristics. As a result, the social structure formed in the asymmetric model promotes diversity instead of uniformity. In contrast, the symmetric model is designed to support local cohesion. Users bonded by strong homophilous ties are more likely to socialize through symmetric than asymmetric networks. Therefore, we posit:

Hypothesis 3 (Relationship Symmetry Effects): Online homophily is more pronounced in symmetric social networks than in asymmetric social networks.

## Multi-Homing and Online Homophily

Today, consumers increasingly own or subscribe to multiple services with comparable features and functionalities. For example, many consumers own multiple credit cards to optimize the rewards and incentives offered by card providers, or they use one as a complement to the other. Similarly, numerous Internet users download and activate multiple browsers on their computers or mobile platforms to cope with compatibility issues. Web surfers seek knowledge by adopting more than one search engine. Many readers regularly subscribe to multiple newspapers or paid online news services. A user’s propensity to subscribe to multiple services is often dubbed “multi-homing” [55, 67]. The concept originates from Internet technology, wherein connection of computing devices to multiple networks or platforms to enhance reliability is common. Users are more inclined to exhibit multi-homing behaviors when incompatibility prevents them from maximizing the benefits of each network or platform individually [18, 61]. Multihoming is particularly predominant in two-sided markets in which platform competition is driven by the size of the affiliated user base and cross-side network effects [6, 64, 67].

Many factors encourage users to adopt multi-homing, including incompatibility and differences in scope and modalities across diverse online SNS platforms. Many Facebook users utilize multiple online SNSs to maximize social utility [14]. Facebook consumers may subscribe to Path as a more private mode of communication to converse about personal matters. They may simultaneously use Twitter to follow the news, announcements, and particular events occurring in their social circles, while subscribing to LinkedIn and MySpace to look for and maintain professional contacts and share their interests in art and music, respectively. These multi-homing users leverage multiple SNSs to expand their social circles and enhance the network benefits that accrue from having a variety of networks with different interpersonal structures [61]. Conversely, users of a single SNS platform may value making deeper social connections and minimizing the “homing costs” associated with managing several social venues. Such single-homing users may prioritize the intimacy and familiarity of social interactions rather than their number or network size.

From a sociobehavioral perspective, the number of SNS platforms used simultaneously may have important implications for the propensity toward homophily. Multi-homing users are naturally exposed to diverse social networks that vary in terms of member composition, social dynamics, and intended benefits. Because multi-homing users desire to expand the breadth and horizons of their social relationships, they may be more proactive and open-minded in getting to know people with different attributes than users who maintain a single SNS. Multi-homing can result in increased utility for users who exploit the benefits of an expanded network in which member heterogeneity and diversity naturally increase. Therefore, we posit:

Hypothesis 4 (Homing-Preference Effects): Online homophily among SNS users is more pronounced in single-homing environments than in multi-homing environments.

## Data

We collected panel data on individual users’ daily activity on three major mobile SNS apps frequently used in Korea: Facebook, Twitter, and KAKAO Story.<sup>2</sup> Although these mobile apps have been consistently ranked among the top social network platforms, they vary in degree of openness and asymmetry. Twitter (TWT) is a representative asymmetric SNS (AS) platform because it is not necessary for a TWT user to follow people who follow him or her. Conversely, Facebook (FB) and KAKAO Story (KS) are classified as symmetric SNS (SS) platforms since all friendships are mutual and reciprocal in both. Moreover, FB is classified as an open SNS (OS) platform because its users can connect and socialize with all FB users in the open. In contrast, KS is considered a relatively closed and private SNS (CS) site because the process of becoming KS friends is far more complicated and restrictive. For example, KS users can become friends only when they know each other’s identity or share phone numbers.

The data used in this study are provided by Nielsen Korean Click—a global marketing research company that collects information on mobile and PC usage through self-installed tracking apps embedded in smartphones. The tracking programs count how frequently the users on the participating panels activate the mobile SNS apps on a daily basis. To represent the population more precisely, 10,172 panels were initially chosen by stratified random sampling using a proportionate allocation strategy based on diverse demographic information (e.g., age, gender, and geographical region). The data include usage frequency for 134 days from June 20, 2013 to October 31, 2013. Of the 10,172 participating panels, 6,305, 3,178, and 689 users utilized the KS, FB, and TWT apps, respectively, at least once during the sampling period. The panels were divided into two gender groups and four age categories: (1) under 18, (2) 19–29, (3) 30–39, and (4) over 40.

Table 1 provides the descriptive statistics. The second column indicates the proportion of users in the four age categories and two gender groups in our sample. Users in the 19–29 age group made up the largest proportion of the sample on FB, but the over-40 cohort makes up the largest proportion on both TWT and KS. However, female and male users are evenly distributed on all SNS sites. The third column shows the average number of activations per day for TWT, FB, and KS platforms. On all SNS platforms, the younger groups of users tend to use SNS applications more frequently compared to older groups of users. Moreover, users of KS are more loyal to their SNS platform compared to users of TWT and FB.

## Model Development and Estimation

## Model Development

To investigate the extent of homophily in users of online SNS sites and its association with platform characteristics and multi-homing, we constructed a model with the following dependent and independent variables. In keeping with Trusov et al. [79], the dependent variable, $Y _ { i , t } ,$ , represents the frequency of each individual i’s SNS app usage at time t, which denotes the number of log-ins.<sup>3</sup> The independent variables include the number of active users who are in the same group based on a specific demographic factor X (e.g., age or gender) with individual i at time t – 1 on the same SNS site $( N S X _ { i , t - 1 } )$ and the number of active users who are in a different group based on the specific demographic factor X with individual i at time t – 1 on the same SNS site $( N D X _ { i , t - 1 } )$ . Lagged values of these two covariates are used to identify their effects on the dependent variable. It should be noted that $N S X _ { i , t - 1 }$ and $N D X _ { i , t - 1 }$ are computed by counting the active users in the sample population.

We examine whether homophily exists among users of SNS platforms by comparing the estimated parameters of $N S X _ { i , t - 1 }$ and $N D X _ { i , t - 1 }$ . More specifically, the presence of homophily can be validated if (1) the estimated parameters of $N S X _ { i , t - 1 }$ and $N D X _ { i , t - 1 }$ are both positive and (2) the parameter of $N S X _ { i , t - 1 }$ is greater than that of $N D X _ { i , t - 1 }$ . Strong homophily exists when individual users respond positively to $N S X _ { i , t - 1 }$ but negatively to $N D X _ { i , t - 1 }$ . Separate models are constructed to identify the extent of age and gender homophily.<sup>4</sup> In the first model, we use age as a demographic factor X and then include two variables, $N S A g e _ { i , t - 1 }$ and $N D A g e _ { i , t - 1 }$ 2 which denote the number of active users in the same or different age group with individual i at time $t - 1$ , respectively. Similarly, the second model incorporates $N S G e n d e r _ { i , t - 1 }$ and $N D G e n d e r _ { i , t - 1 }$

In addition to these analyses, we divide the samples into demographic clusters and examine how the two main covariates are distributed across these diverse demographic groups. This analysis is designed to validate whether individuals become less age homophilous as they mature [71], and whether men are more homophilous than women [35]. The first model is established by incorporating the interactions of $N S A g e _ { i , t - 1 }$ and $N D A g e _ { i , t - 1 }$ for three different age groups: (1) under 18, (2) 19–29, and (3) 30–39. The second model incorporates the interactions of $N S G e n d e r _ { i , t - 1 }$ and $N D G e n d e r _ { i , t - 1 }$ for the female group. It should be noted that the over-40 group and the male group are employed as bases in each model. Furthermore, previous scholars (e.g., [39]) suggest that SNS platforms are used relatively less frequently on weekends and holidays, as users prefer to engage in outdoor activities. To control for the potential effect of weekends and holidays, we include a dummy variable, $H _ { t }$ , which is equal to 1 when day t is either a weekend day or a holiday.

Instead of Poisson regression, negative binomial regression was adopted as the main analytical strategy to test the homophily principle in the context of online SNS since Poisson distribution strongly assumes “equi-dispersion,” which means that both the mean value and variance of $Y _ { i , t }$ are equal to the mean parameter of Poisson distribution $\lambda _ { i , t }$ . This assumption is often identified as an inherent weakness of the Poisson regression [28]. It is not uncommon to find empirical cases where the variance of $Y _ { i , t }$ exceeds the mean of $Y _ { i , t } .$ , which reflects overdispersion in the data [33, 75]. The failure of the Poisson regression to consider this possibility increases the magnitude of the effect on reported standard errors and t-statistics [8].

Hausman et al. [33] propose a standard mechanism to deal with this overdispersion problem. Their method relaxes the mean parameter to follow a gamma distribution with parameter $( \gamma _ { i , t } , \delta _ { i } )$ and also specifies $\gamma _ { i , t } = e ^ { X _ { i , t } \beta }$ , where $X _ { i , t }$ is a vector of covariates for individual i at time t and $\beta$ is a vector of corresponding regression parameters to be estimated (Equation [1]). Then, $Y _ { i , t }$ follows the negative binomial distribution with parameter $( \gamma _ { i , t } , \delta _ { i } )$ , and the variance-to-mean ratio is greater than 1; accordingly, $V \big ( Y _ { i , t } \big ) / E \big ( Y _ { i , t } \big ) = ( 1 + \delta _ { i } ) / \delta _ { i } > 1$ . To test for the possibility of overdispersion in our data, we adopt Cameron and Trivedi’s [7] simple regression-based procedure. Specifically, the empirical model regresses $z _ { i } = \bigg \{ \Big ( y _ { i } - { \widehat { \lambda _ { i } } } \Big ) ^ { 2 } - y _ { i } \bigg \} / \Big ( { \widehat { \lambda _ { i } } } \sqrt { 2 } \Big )$ , where $\widehat { \lambda _ { i } }$ is a fitted value from the Poisson regression, on either a constant term or $\widehat { \lambda } _ { i }$ without the constant term. Following this procedure, the null hypothesis (e.g., the presence of equi-dispersion) is rejected, as both t-statistics are significantly different from zero at the 99 percent level of confidence $( t = 1 3 1 . 6 5$ and 119.76 for the first model, $t = 1 3 9 . 8 1$ and 118.56 for the second model). Therefore, it is more robust to model $Y _ { i , t }$ based on a negative binomial regression (Equations [1], [2a], and [2b]) than on the Poisson regression:

$$
\operatorname * {P r} \left(Y _ {i, t} = y _ {i, t} \mid \gamma_ {i, t}, \delta_ {i}\right) = \frac {\Gamma \left(\gamma_ {i , t} + y _ {i , t}\right)}{\Gamma \left(\gamma_ {i , t}\right) \Gamma \left(y _ {i , t} + 1\right)} \left(\frac {\delta_ {i}}{1 + \delta_ {i}}\right) ^ {\gamma_ {i, t}} (1 + \delta_ {i}) ^ {- y _ {i, t}}\tag{1}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln \left(\gamma_ {i, t}\right) = \left[ \begin{array}{c} 1 \\ N S A g e _ {i, t - 1} \\ N D A g e _ {i, t - 1} \end{array} \right] ^ {\prime} * \left[ \begin{array}{c} \beta_ {0} \\ \beta_ {1} \\ \beta_ {2} \end{array} \right] + N S A g e _ {i, t - 1} * \left[ \begin{array}{c} A g e 1 _ {i} \\ A g e 2 _ {i} \\ A g e 3 _ {i} \end{array} \right] ^ {\prime} * \left[ \begin{array}{c} \beta_ {1 1} \\ \beta_ {2 1} \\ \beta_ {3 1} \end{array} \right] + N D A g e _ {i, t - 1} \\ * \left[ \begin{array}{c} A g e 1 _ {i} \\ A g e 2 _ {i} \\ A g e 3 _ {i} \end{array} \right] ^ {\prime} * \left[ \begin{array}{c} \beta_ {1 2} \\ \beta_ {2 2} \\ \beta_ {3 2} \end{array} \right] + \beta_ {4} H _ {t} \end{array}\tag{2a}
$$

## Estimation

The individual-level panel data allow us to model behavioral heterogeneities across individuals [28]. Both the fixed-effect and the random-effect models control for all time-invariant effects specific to each individual. However, the fixed-effect model can only identify the unobserved individual effects to be correlated with the included regressors. In contrast, the random-effect model produces inconsistent estimators when a correlation exists between the regressors and the random effect. The Hausman [32] specification test reveals that we cannot reject the null hypothesis. This indicates that the estimates based on the two modeling approaches should not differ systematically (Prob $> \chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 0 0 0$ for both models). Consequently, we adopt the fixed-effect model, which is less restrictive. The fixed-effect model enables us to account for unobserved individual specific heterogeneity, which could result in a bias against the number of log-ins. The fixed-effect negative binomial model proposed by Hausman et al. [33] relates the joint probability of the counts for each individual to the sum of the counts for the individual $\sum _ { t } y _ { i , t }$ . Parameters are estimated to maximize the following log-likelihood function, where $w _ { i }$ indicates individual weights (Equation [4]). This modeling specification addresses both issues: the overdispersion in data distribution and the potential serial correlation that may stem from the specific heterogeneity of an individual.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln L = \sum_ {i} w _ {i} \Big [ \ln \Gamma \Big (\sum_ {t} \gamma_ {i, t} \Big) + \ln \Gamma \Big (\sum_ {t} y _ {i, t} + 1 \Big) - \ln \Gamma \Big (\sum_ {t} \gamma_ {i, t} + \sum_ {t} y _ {i, t} \Big) \\ \qquad + \sum_ {t} \Big \{\ln \Gamma \Big (\gamma_ {i, t} + y _ {i, t} \Big) - \ln \Gamma \Big (\gamma_ {i, t} \Big) - \ln \Gamma \Big (y _ {i, t} + 1 \Big) \Big \} \Big ]. \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

## Multi-Homing and Online Homophily

Figure 2 provides holistic insight into the number of SNS platforms a user activates on a given day (i.e., NofUsingSNS ), revealing that this parameter has a significant impact on users’ age-homophilic tendencies (i.e., the darker the color of the diagonal line compared to the off-diagonal line, the stronger the age homophily). Online age homophily is pervasive across users in all four age groups who are loyal to a single SNS $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } N o f U s i n g S N S _ { i } = 1 )$ . The diagonal line shows that the propensity of age homophily diminishes as the number of SNS platforms a user activates increases from 1 to 3 (from left to right in Figure 2). In addition, as the multi-homing propensity increases, users in all four age groups exhibit weak age-homophilic behaviors.

Table 3 presents the results of the statistical analyses showing that except for users in their 20s, the age-homophilic tendencies of teenagers and users in their 30s and 40s who use more SNS a day are weakened. For example, teenagers who use only one SNS $( \mathrm { i . e . , } N o f U s i n g S N S _ { i } = 1 )$ , increase their usage when new members are from the same age group, but reduce their usage when new members of different age groups enter their SNS platforms. However, teenagers who use more than two SNSs on an average day (i.e., $N o f U s i n g S N S _ { i } > 2 )$ do not exhibit strong homophilous behaviors. Users in their 30s and 40s exhibit age homophily when they adopt a single SNS platform, but their homophilic tendencies are weakened as they activate more SNS platforms. In addition, because of the nested structure of Equations (2a) and (3a), which incorporates the interactions of NofUsingSNS , we perform the likelihood ratio test to confirm that Equation (3a) fits better than Equation (2a) $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Thus, H4 is supported with respect to age homophily.

We conduct additional analyses to unravel the causality between homing preferences and homophilic behaviors. Such causality should be clarified to address potential endogeneity issues by examining users who switch from single- to multihoming status or vice versa. In so doing, we identified both the within- and betweenuser effects of homing types on the extent of homophily, both of which are required to validate the causality between the preference for homing and the extent of homophily. We confirm that a switch from single to multi-homing status or vice versa causes significant changes in the propensity toward age- and gender-based homophily (for details, see Online Appendix B). We also ascertained that the effects of the explanatory variables are economically significant despite their small coefficients (see Online Appendix C for details) and that the results remain robust to the aggregate-level analyses (for more information, see Online Appendix D).

## Agent-Based Modeling and Simulations

Our empirical findings indicate that users exhibit different patterns of homophilous behaviors depending on platform characteristics (i.e., openness and asymmetry) and multi-homing propensities. Note, however, that the empirical analyses were performed at the aggregate level (i.e., individuals’ aggregated daily activities), with limited attention paid to the specific interaction patterns of users in their ego networks. Such individual activities are highly private, thereby preventing the company from collecting the confidential information. Therefore, to incorporate individuals’ ego network behaviors in the analyses and generalize our empirical findings, we adopt a complex adaptive system perspective and develop computersimulated experiments on the basis of Schelling’s [68] work.

## Schelling’s Simulation Model

Homophily pertains to the tendency of people to identify themselves with others who parallel their own traits. Relating this to the specific context of race, Schelling demonstrated that even a minimal preference among people to be around neighbors similar ethnicity causes segregation in a region. The social scientist probed into the dynamics of spatiosocial structures by positioning pennies and dimes on a chess board in different patterns. The chess board is a metaphor for a city, and a penny or a dime represents a family or an ethic group. The penny and the dime signify contrasting groups of men and women, smokers and nonsmokers, ethic affiliation, and so on. This exploration gave rise to the Schelling segregation model (SSM) or the Schelling tipping model (STM). The original SSM’s simplification of the world as disjointed land sectors indicates that only one object exists within a square, which stands for a house or district. The distance of influence is also limited to the adjacent boundaries of one square. Theoretically, we can assume that eight neighbors at most exist around a single district. Schelling moved pennies and dimes individually if the represented groups were in a socially dissatisfactory environment or what he referred to as an “unhappy” situation. Happiness or unhappiness is a result of interaction with adjacent neighbors. The operating rule is simple. If an agent (i.e., a penny or a dime) finds many agents who are similar to him or her, the agent stays within the current spatiosocial environment. If a given space is inhabited by numerous differing agents, an unhappy agent will randomly search for a place that more favorably suits him or her. The process continues until every agent is happy with his or her location.

The SSM represents one of the first constructive models of a complex system comprising dynamically interacting agents. Even on the basis of a simple desire to independently improve a situation, agents who break away from a given group can be reunited in a separate one. Social separation in this theoretical model is the result of pursuing self-interest. Schelling discovered that the chess board was rapidly filled with patterns of strong segregation.

Shelling used physical objects to test his idea, but using a computer program can also effectively illustrate how the SSM works (e.g., [83]). Simulation involves the use of computer software to model the manner by which real-world processes, systems, or events operate [15, 43]. Davis et al. [15] explain that using a simulation program advances not only the understanding of social events but also the constructive derivation of theoretical arguments. We adopted the computational logic of the SSM to define the basic motivation behind homophily. Using NetLogo [83], we simulated social interactions in social networking platforms. NetLogo provides a powerful set of functions that simultaneously implement cellular automata (CA) and network simulation (NS) features, thus enabling us to use Shelling’s simple but robust approach and emulate network growth that is otherwise unobservable because of privacy compliance issues. As presented in Table 4, we carefully followed the seven-step roadmap proposed by Davis et al. [15] in carrying out our simulationbased validation.

## Simulation Calibration

As articulated in Step 7, we used the parameter values derived from the empirical analyses to calibrate the simulated model. This is a very crucial aspect of our simulation designs. We also compared the results of simulation experiments with those obtained from the empirical analyses. The construction of the simulation resembles Shelling’s [68] process in that we created an initial distribution of white and black agents sets randomly, which may represent young and old or male and female users. We then programmed the agents to search for a better neighborhood. In adherence to Shelling [68], we set the neighborhood as a “Moore neighborhood,” which is a square-shaped locality. The range, $r ,$ for a given cell $( x _ { 0 } , \ y _ { 0 } )$ is defined by $N _ { ( x _ { 0 } , y _ { 0 } ) } ^ { M } = \{ ( x , y ) | | x - x _ { 0 } | \leq r , | y - y _ { 0 } | \leq r \}$ . Although Shelling [68] defined a neighborhood as a Moore neighborhood of range 1, we conceived it as a Moore neighborhood of range 2 because online social network are characterized by a greater range of neighborhoods than are offline social networks. That is, in the current work, a neighborhood pertains to 24 surrounding cells instead of 8 surrounding cells. This conservative assumption increases the generalizability of our findings.

In the simulator, white and black agent sets are designed to have opposite preferences; in this respect, homophily is illustrated by the proportion of similarly oriented agents in neighborhoods. In keeping with Shelling [68], whether each agent is satisfied with his or her neighbors or not (i.e., happy or unhappy) was determined by the number of similar agents in the neighborhood in relation to the number of dissimilar agents in that circle. First, we defined a closed SNS as a site that allows users to state their preferences and block strangers from crossing social boundaries. We defined an open SNS as a site in which strangers can easily enter others’ social circles, and users find refusing or ignoring a friend request difficult for social and personal reasons. To apply these definitions, we extended the simulation algorithms for agent movement under closed SNS conditions. An agent can move to a different cell only if the average happiness of the agents in the cell’s neighborhoods increases as the agent moves to that cell. In a closed SNS, therefore, users cannot easily penetrate others’ social circles and join only the neighborhoods that “welcome” them.

Second, we defined an asymmetric SNS as a site in which friendships do not have to be reciprocal. Every friendship in our simulation model is reciprocal because friendships are represented by neighborhoods, and all agents have an equal range of neighborhoods (i.e., r = 2). For an asymmetric SNS, therefore, we established different ranges of neighborhoods for each agent and refined neighborhoods from being reciprocal links (e.g., friends on Facebook) to unreciprocated links (e.g., followers on Twitter). This fine-tuning means that agent A, who is in agent B’s neighborhood excludes agent B from B’s own neighborhood. For computational convenience, we set three different ranges of neighborhoods for each agent in an asymmetric SNS. To determine the appropriate ranges and proportions of agents with each range, we collected information on the number of followers of 73,750 Twitter users, who were active between June 1 and December 1, 2011 (6 months). We then allowed the multi-homing agents to skip movement at each time. Singlehoming users carefully identify their neighbors and consequent happiness in every interaction because they ascribe more value to deep social connections than do multi-homing users. A possibility, however, is that multi-homing users may be motivated to pay less attention to the composition of their SNS neighbors by the fact that leveraging multiple SNSs to expand social circles and enhance network benefits produces significant homing costs. As a result, multi-homing users strongly tend to forgo the process of identifying their neighbors and move to another site on the basis of consequent happiness. We programmed multi-homing agents as exhibiting a 30 percent chance of skipping movement at each time. In addition, we conducted sensitivity checks for several simulation parameters: the density of agents, proportion of two agent sets, range of neighbor $r ,$ and probability that multi-homing agents will forgo movement. Because the empirical analyses reveal the existence of heterophilous groups (e.g., the over-40 group in the results of the full sample estimation), we conducted the same simulation experiments with the assumption that one agent set (e.g., white) is a homophilous group and another (e.g., black) is a heterophilious group. Lastly, we identified whether the main results remain consistent even after we allow users to leave a platform due to their serious unhappiness. Practically, we removed every agent who is unhappy enough to leave a platform at each time. Note that an agent is assumed to be unhappy enough to abandon platform membership if the proportion of similar agents to all agents in his/her neighborhood is smaller than a leaving threshold. We also conducted sensitivity analyses for the leaving threshold. The results are robust under all the alternative specifications.

The results derived from the simulation-based analyses are generally consistent with those obtained from the empirical investigations. One variation is that one of the hypotheses that is partially validated by the empirical assessment finds full support from the simulation experiment. This difference is a matter of balance; that is, simulation-based validations complement empirical evaluations that are encumbered by data deficiencies. More specifically, the simulation experiment deals with all eight possible combinations derived from the 2 (platform accessibility) × 2 (relationship symmetry) × 2 (homing preference) conditions, but our empirical data do not include all such situations. Given that the simulation model is tuned and calibrated on the basis of the empirical data, it expands the empirical setting and produces reliable outcomes.

## Implications

## Implications for Research

Since the seminal work by Manski [54], researchers (e.g., [2, 53, 57, 69, 70]) have shown increased interest in distinguishing the effects of social influence from those of homophily in dynamic networks. An influential study by Christakis and Fowler [11], which demonstrates that obese individuals tend to connect with each other through social networks, was criticized by many who argued that homophily, not social influence, may have caused the observed trend toward obesity among friends. Our findings may have important implications for such studies that attempt to isolate homophily from the effects of social contagion. Researchers should be wary of the fact that the extent of homophily may fluctuate substantially across diverse age and gender segments. For certain demographic social clusters, heterophily, rather than homophily, may better characterize users’ social relationships and preferences. Therefore, it is even possible for one of two socially connected people (e.g., a teenager) to exhibit a predilection for the homophilous while the other (e.g., his/her parent) displays a tendency for the heterophilic. Since individual propensity toward homophily and heterophily changes as people mature, a more nuanced inquiry into the dynamic and evolving nature of homophily is warranted.

In addition to homophily, the notion of social (peer) influence and its effects within and across social networks should also be clarified and refined. Scholarly works that seek to separate social contagion from homophily conventionally assume symmetrical and reciprocal relationships between “friends” who form ties with one another. Our findings suggest an alternative view that certain social relationships could be more effectively described as asymmetrical or one-way. Consequently, caution must be used to interpret the direction and magnitude of results for social influence when isolating its effects from those of homophily. The results of the present study suggest various directions for research on heterophilous ties and asymmetric social structures across users with diverse sociodemographic attributes.

In this study, we also examine the association between platform characteristics and individual propensity toward homophily. In addition, the extent to which a user’s multi-homing environment influences his or her tendency toward homophily is investigated. The findings indicate that the characteristic of online social settings can influence users’ norms, values, and behaviors in terms of social interactions and interpersonal relationships. Network benefits accruing from multi-homing and strong ties afforded by platform characteristics both play an important role in determining perceptions and attitudes toward online homophily. Taking these results into account, researchers should not view individual propensity toward online homophily as a stationary state, but rather as a moving, dynamic target that can change in response to environmental factors.

In line with the general benefits offered by agent-based simulation, our model also provided us desirable opportunities to examine the growth of homophily. Agentbased simulation models are viable and effective instruments through which to understand complex systems that arise from bottom-up emergent and collective behaviors of multiple agents [10, 34, 62, 66]. Although we endeavored to collect substantial data on users, privacy protection laws prohibited us from fully capturing data instances from personal mobile devices. On the basis of theories and parameters tuned by observations, we generated time frames that reflect the behavioral parameters of agents. The data analyses offer strong support for our hypotheses. In our view, empirical analysis supplemented by agent-based simulation is a powerful way of developing theories.

## Implications for Policymakers

Understanding the patterns and magnitude of online homophily across diverse sociodemographic groups and their association with platform characteristics and multi-homing is important for policymakers. Online SNSs are progressively becoming a core channel through which people receive daily news, share their values and interests, and form and express their opinions and beliefs (e.g., political views). A potential risk emerging from this mode of social communication is the broadening of ideological differences and biased assimilation owing to exclusive interaction with like-minded others, selective consumption of information content that validates individual beliefs, and denunciation of individuals with opposing values and viewpoints—a phenomenon known as a filter bubble [29]. A recent study by Flaxman et al. [22] reveals that individuals exhibit higher ideological segregation when reading news over social media than over a news site’s home page. SNS-reinforced online homophily can create a form of echo chamber, in which users listen only to those who mirror their views on key controversial issues (e.g., politics, religion, same-sex marriage, and world affairs). The findings of the current work may augment policymakers’ understanding of the mechanisms through which they can positively shape public opinion and minimize ideological polarization.

## Implications for Platform Providers and Advertisers

Users’ homophilous tendencies, particularly among adolescent users, shed light on the current outflow of members in this age group experienced by Facebook and other major open SNS providers. The results should alert these SNS companies to the potential for precipitous decline caused by massive user migration to alternative, more closed SNSs where members can have the pleasure of interacting with similar others without the disadvantages of open SNS. It seems that one format does not fit all when it comes to patterns, preferences, and social intercourse in online SNS spaces. To curtail this exodus, providers of open SNS platforms should develop closed socialization mechanisms to operate in parallel with their generally open online environment so that younger users can enjoy the best of both worlds. Facebook’s recent acquisition of WhatsApp, a mobile messaging service company with only 50 employees, for \$19 billion may be an effective, cost-saving portfolio strategy for this purpose. Using this service allows users in different age groups to communicate and socialize through their choice of mechanisms.

Our findings may also offer a plausible explanation as to why social advertising has yet to live up to its much-vaunted potential as an indispensable marketing channel. Social advertising is a new marketing paradigm that uses information about consumers’ social networks to target ads and contextualize their content and presentation [4]. According to a recent report by Forrester [19], brand pages managed by Facebook reached only 6 percent of fans in 2014. This figure drops even further to 2 percent when only pages with over 500,000 fans are considered. The Forrester study also reveals that the organic distribution of posts on Facebook’s brand pages has drastically diminished in recent years. BusinessWeek reports that “tweets, likes, and shares don’t make us [Americans] buy stuff [12].” These negative sentiments suggest that the way social advertising currently works may have its own systematical and functional limitations. This is an area for future study.

In certain situations (such as when its targeting mechanism is not designed wisely and appropriately), social advertising could damage rather than enhance consumers preferences for a given brand, resulting in eroded sales. Most SNS platform providers and advertisers do not fully understand the complicated nature of the social structures and interaction patterns inherent in online social networks; they focus primarily on behavioral isomorphism, social capital, and the positive homophilous ties that develop among peers. These SNS stakeholders have ignored the business impact of “social liability” on brand preference and sales. According to Labianca and Brass [51], social capital (i.e., positive network relationships) and social liability (i.e., negative relationships) coexist in social networks. Dewan and Ramaprasad [16] also demonstrated that social media can result in both positive and negative consequences on business performance. While social capital facilitates information cascades and knowledge transfer [80], social liability may impede the exchange of information and weaken viral contagion. Furthermore, a brand preference posted by someone with whom a user forms negative relationships may adversely affect his or her purchasing intent toward that specific brand. Our findings clearly demonstrated the presence of social liability in online social networks. Many users tend to reduce their SNS usage in response to the entry of other users with whom social liability exists.

The coexistence of social capital and social liability in online social settings may prompt platform providers to fine-tune their current social advertising strategies. SNS functions, such as likes, tweets, and shares, are designed to capitalize on social capital, but no such infrastructures are currently in place for minimizing social liability. Obviously, it would be a daunting task for platform providers to develop a system through which social capital can be precisely isolated from social liability in online social networks. One plausible way to address this issue while also revitalizing social advertising is to use our approach in this study, which is to identify social capital and social liability based on age- and gender-driven homophily-heterophily analytics. Platform providers and online advertisers must learn from the lesson of the failure of the Levi’s brand. Although Levi’s was considered an American classic and dominated the casual clothing market for extended periods of time between the 1980s and 1990s, the brand suddenly collapsed in the late 1990s due (among other factors) to faulty positioning and branding [23]. When teenagers stopped buying the brand, perceiving Levi’s as “the jeans my Dad wears,” the product began its inevitable decline. This could easily occur in the context of social advertising. If teenagers repeatedly view social advertisements for clothing of the wrong generation, youth might react negatively to such campaigns, increasing the exodus from one platform to another.

Our study also has strategic implications for advertisers. Advertisers should carefully choose advertising channels according to their targeting strategy. Our findings suggest that if advertisers desire to target mass audiences, an open and asymmetric SNS is far more effective than its closed and symmetric alternatives. This is because information (e.g., ad messages) spreads throughout asymmetric, open networks much faster than it does through symmetric, closed networks due to the heterogeneity and asymmetry of the former. Conversely, advertisers who wish to target a specific group of users should focus their attention on users of closed and symmetric SNS. Specifically, if strong homophily is evident in a particular target group, concentrating on that group through advertisements on closed SNS may be the most efficient and effective strategy. However, if a target group exhibits strong heterophily, targeting other groups through advertisements on closed SNS may be the most effective strategy, and it may also have unexpected positive effects on nontargeted groups. Moreover, closed SNSs that are losing advertising business to open SNSs because of their comparatively short advertising reach should capitalize on the fact that they are superior to open SNSs in terms of advertising intensity and efficiency.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations that suggest directions for future studies. First, although the websites chosen for this study (i.e., Facebook, Twitter) are representative online social network services, we make no attempt to generalize our findings to other such services. In contrast to these well-established and popular social forums, new emerging social network sites may vary in terms of member composition and interaction dynamics. Future research may validate the patterns, social structures, and characteristics of interpersonal relationships identified here in other smaller, growing online social networks. In addition, we focus exclusively on two aspects of homophilous propensity (age and gender). Other sociodemographic attributes, such as ethnicity, race, and religion, could come into play in the complex social dynamics and interpersonal relationships of SNS users. The Nielsen’s data obtained for use in this study did not include these additional personal attributes. Including additional characteristics that may be related to homophily might make the findings more comprehensive and further our understanding of the social dynamics among users of online social networks. We acknowledge this as one of the limitations of this study and call for future studies to extend the homophily spectrum.

It should also be noted that our findings reflect only the behaviors of SNS users in one particular region. Users of various cultures may behave differently given that attitudes and social norms toward age and gender vary substantially across diverse settings. Although some previous studies (e.g. [37]) found no significant variations across cultures with respect to the motivation for SNS use, cultural differences may account for heterogeneities in other dimensions of SNS usage. Kim et al. [44] found that the SNS usage patterns exhibited by Korean students are dissimilar to those shown by their U.S. counterparts. Whereas American students tend to use SNSs for a larger number of casual relationships with minimal effort devoted to each connection, Korean students focus on reinforcing existing relationships with a small circle of close friends. We therefore acknowledge that cultural differences can play an important role in shaping and governing the effects of online homophily. Finally, human interactions and social connections can occur through a variety of communication forms, including e-mail, phone conversations, and face-to-face meetings. However, the findings reported in this study represent social exchanges that took place only through online social networks; therefore, they assuredly are not comprehensive. Although online SNSs are increasing in prevalence and are becoming more and more embedded in our everyday social lives, many individuals still prefer interacting through conventional face-to-face interaction or by telephone, resisting the use of online venues. Future studies should include additional sources of social interaction to enhance our understanding of individual propensity toward homophily.

## Conclusion

This study explored the extent to which platform structures and homing preferences influence homophilous propensities in online SNS platforms by using panel-based SNS usage data and agent-based simulation experiments. The findings suggest that SNS characteristics—that is, whether open or closed—significantly moderate the propensities of individual users toward homophily across diverse cohorts. The symmetrical structure of SNS platforms equally affects users’ social preferences and interaction dynamics. Furthermore, whether users adopt a single SNS or multiple SNS platforms can predict their predispositions for homophily.

One of the key regularities observed implies that social segregation according to age and gender differences is pervasive and takes many forms, at least for users of the major SNSs examined in this work. This stratification can hinder mutual understanding among different groups, thereby increasing prejudice across different generations. The potential upshot of such discrimination is damage to social integrity and disrespect for the values and norms of various groups. Policymakers and SNS providers should respond to this stratification and prevent emergent hostility by designing new services that reduce the generation gap and encourage users from all life stages and gender affiliations to come together for the purpose of improving understanding and tolerance of differences. Online homophily formed through SNS platforms should not create a form of echo chamber, wherein users only hear what they want to hear and attack people with different ideologies and perspectives on the numerous controversial issues that surround our values and norms. Online social networks should become collective entities, in which varied patterns of social coupling and interaction preferences are inextricably woven into the socialization process. After all, social networks, whether online or offline, are enriched by membership with “birds of the same or different feathers.”
