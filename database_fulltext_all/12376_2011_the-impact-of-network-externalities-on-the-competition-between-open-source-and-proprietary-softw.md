# The Impact of Network Externalities on the Competition Between Open Source and Proprietary Software

HSING KENNETH CHENG, YIPENG LIU, AND QIAN (CANDY) TANG

Abstrac t: In this paper, we build analytical models to examine the impact of network externalities on the competition between open source software (OSS) and proprietary software. We investigate the competing OSS and proprietary software products with comparable functionalities in four different scenarios depending on whether they are compatible with each other and whether the underlying market is fully covered (i.e., all consumers adopt one of the two products). Furthermore, we study which party has the most incentive to make its product compatible with its counterpart. When the market is fully covered, the installed base and the profit of proprietary software increase at the expense of a decreasing user base for OSS in the presence of network externalities. This competitive imbalance becomes more pronounced when OSS and proprietary software are incompatible and the market is partially covered. Finally, we find that in the presence of network externalities, being compatible with its rival is not desirable for the proprietary software, but highly beneficial to the OSS community.

Key words and ph rases: competition, network externalities, open source software, software compatibility.

Open sourc e software (OSS) is one of the most important recent developments in the software industry. OSS refers to those programs “whose licenses give users the freedom to run the program for any purpose, to study and modify the program, and to redistribute copies of either the original or the modified program without having to pay royalties to previous developers” [26].

Increasing numbers of OSS products have become available in the market, ranging from common office suites such as OpenOffice.org<sup>TM</sup> (formerly known as StarOffice) by Sun Microsystems to database systems such as MySQL and thousands of specialized scientific applications. OSS has gained escalating popularity among corporations because it is cheaper to adopt, less vulnerable to viruses or other rogue programs, more reliable and scalable, and most important, enables users to adapt the software to their needs with access to the source code.

Although the rise of OSS to prominence signifies the start of fundamental changes to the software industry, extant literature on various intriguing issues of OSS is sparse. Pioneering research on OSS to date focuses mainly on why developers participate in OSS projects [7, 9, 13, 14, 15, 20, 21], the incentives of firms to adopt open source initiatives [11, 18, 22, 25], and the success factors of OSS projects [2, 4, 6, 10, 23].

As OSS proves to be a viable technology solution for businesses, another stream of OSS research turns its attention to the competition dynamics between OSS and proprietary software. This stream of research includes analysis, both empirical and theoretical, of the public and free nature of OSS products and their effect on the marketplace for software products. For example, Casadesus-Masanell and Ghemawat [3] modeled the competition between Windows and Linux as a dynamic “mixed duopoly,” where a not-for-profit competitor (Linux) interacts with a for-profit competitor (Windows). The authors showed that as long as Windows’ pricing decision was not myopic, the result of competition would be either the coexistence of the two products or Linux being driven out of market. Jaisingh et al. [15] characterized the response of a firm developing proprietary software, where proprietary software is a revenue earner, in the presence of an OSS in its market. In particular, they looked at the firm’s choice of resource investments to improve quality and the firm’s pricing decisions. They found that the firm produces lower-quality proprietary software when it faces competition from an OSS than when it does not, and the quality of the proprietary software decreases as the quality of the OSS increases. Sen [24] analyzed a software market consisting of three competing software products—a freely available OSS, the commercial version of this OSS (OSS-SS), and the competing commercial proprietary software. He found that when the market is characterized by a low network effect, the proprietary software vendor is better off facing the competition from the OSS-SS. When the network effect is strong, however, the proprietary software vendor is threatened by the presence of the OSS-SS and can survive only if it offers a higher-quality product than the competing OSS-SS.

Our paper studies the competition dynamics between OSS and proprietary software, but differs from the past literature in three aspects. First, our work focuses on the impact of network externalities on the competition rather than the result of competition between open source and proprietary software. We model the heterogeneous consumers’ preferences for products to depend on two major factors—the software quality and the network size—and we allow the OSS and the proprietary software to have different intensities of network externalities. Second, some users perceive proprietary software as having higher quality for its ease of use and efficient customer service, whereas more technically inclined consumers may prefer better memory management, stability, and the relative security of OSS, and thus consider the quality of OSS to be higher than that of the proprietary software counterpart. We incorporate this feature into our model by differentiating the potential users into two groups based on their predisposed preferences for OSS or proprietary software. Finally, we investigate the strategic choices and incentives for compatibility between OSS and proprietary software vendors—a topic majorly overlooked in previous studies on OSS.

Software products, including OSS, have several distinct characteristics when compared with traditional physical goods. Most notably, software, excluding those applications used for competitive purposes, exhibits positive network externalities. A positive network effect, or network externalities, refers to the increase of consumer utility when more consumers become the users of the same product. The positive network externalities effect of software may lead to a software product (not necessarily the higher-quality one) enjoying a commanding market share. To survive and win in the battle of market shares, OSS and proprietary software vendors may strategically choose to have their products be compatible or incompatible with their rival software. For example, Windows, a proprietary software product, is incompatible with Linux, an OSS product. In the case of Web browsers, however, Internet Explorer is completely compatible with its open source rival Mozilla’s Firefox in the sense that files created for Explorer users can be used by Firefox users and vice versa. Different compatibility strategies will lead to different network sizes, and thus result in different network generated values and profits for both OSS and proprietary software vendors.

The network externalities effect associated with different compatibility strategies between OSS and proprietary software presents a series of interesting research questions: Does the positive network effect always have a positive effect on both OSS and proprietary software? If not, which party will likely benefit and which party will be hurt? How would the choice of compatibility affect the network externalities effect?

In this paper, we address these research questions by separating our analysis into four different cases (see Table 1) depending on whether the rival software products are compatible with each other and whether the underlying market is fully covered (i.e., all consumers purchase either OSS or proprietary software).

The main findings of this paper are as follows. When the market is fully covered and the competing software is compatible, comparing the maximized profit and optimal market share of proprietary software versus those without the network externalities effect reveals that network externalities lead to the increase of market share and profit for proprietary software vendors. When the competing software is under the same influence of the network externalities effect, γ, we find that the OSS vendor is better off by making its product compatible with its opponent rather than the other way around; that is, the network effect benefits the proprietary software provider more, whereas the compatibility effect, when the network externalities effect is present, benefits the OSS vendor more. When the learning cost incurred in getting familiar with the software product is considered, some consumers may derive negative utilities and, hence, choose not to adopt either OSS or proprietary software, leading to a partially covered market for our analysis. In later sections, we examine the effect of network externalities in a partially covered market taking learning cost into account. Analytical and computational results show that network externalities have a positive effect on OSS if the OSS is made compatible with proprietary software and if the network externalities intensity is strong, but have a negative effect if it is made incompatible. The OSS software vendor is found once again to have the most incentive to make its product compatible with proprietary software.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows. First, we present the underlying model that characterizes the marketplace where OSS and proprietary software compete for customers. Then, the case of no network externalities effect is analyzed as the benchmark followed by the competition between OSS and proprietary software when the market is fully covered. Several key propositions are derived to examine the impact of network externalities on the dynamics of competition. Furthermore, we extend the model to investigate the competition between proprietary software and OSS that exhibits different learning costs and network externalities effects in a partially covered market. Finally, we provide the directions of future work and concluding remarks.

## The Model

Assume th at th e total number of potential c ustomers who will adopt either OSS or proprietary software is $N ,$ which is normalized to 1 without loss of generality. Let θ represent a customer’s preference for software quality and be uniformly distributed in [0, 1]. In the absence of network externalities, a customer derives $u = { \boldsymbol { \theta } } \cdot s - p$ utility of adopting a software product of quality s charging price $p .$

Let $\gamma$ represent the intensity of the network externalities effect. When $Q$ is the installed base of the software (i.e., the total number of users adopting the software), the network externalities effect increases each consumer’s preference (θ) by $\gamma Q$ in accordance with the increase in willingness to pay when an additional user joins the network. When purchasing a software product of quality s in a network of size $Q$ at price $p ,$ the consumer θ obtains a net utility $u = ( { \Theta } + { \gamma } { Q } ) s - p$ under the influence of network externalities. Farrell and Saloner [8] describe q · s as the product’s “networkindependent” or stand-alone value, which depends on both consumer’s preference and product quality. The “network-generated” value is γ Qs and exhibits complementary effects between network size and quality. Therefore, a higher-quality product has both higher stand-alone and network values than a lower-quality product in the same network. In the work of Katz and Shapiro [16] and most other research on network externalities, products are homogeneous and consumers differ in their total willingness to pay. The network-generated benefit therefore depends only on the network size.

Let $s _ { p }$ denote the quality of proprietary software. Generally, software quality can be measured in several dimensions, such as performance, ease of use, customer support, reliability, and security. For the sake of generality, our study is not restricted to any particular dimension. Rather, we define software quality as representing the overall characteristics of software other than the price. To capture the fact that some consumers prefer proprietary software while others favor OSS, we differentiate potential users into two groups based on their preferences for either OSS or proprietary software. Let α be the proportion of consumers who have a predisposed preference for OSS. For example, consumers using Unix machines will most likely adopt OSS rather than Microsoft software products. Accordingly, let $\beta$ be the proportion of consumers who prefer proprietary software, where $\beta = 1 - \alpha$ . As a result, consumers who have a predisposed preference for OSS perceive higher quality about OSS than proprietary software. Likewise, those who have a predisposed preference for proprietary software consider it to have higher quality than OSS. Let $s _ { \infty }$ and $s _ { \beta o }$ describe the perceived OSS quality for those who prefer OSS (the α group) and proprietary software (the $\beta$ group), respectively. Then, one has $s _ { \alpha o } > s _ { p } > s _ { \beta o }$ , where $s _ { p }$ denotes the proprietary software quality. Finally, we let $Q _ { o }$ and $Q _ { p }$ denote the installed user base of OSS and proprietary software, respectively. Table 2 summarizes the notation used in this paper.

To study the effect of the introduction of OSS on the market share of proprietary software, we consider a two-period model where proprietary software has an established dominance in the first period and then we analyze the market share change of proprietary software when OSS is introduced in the second period. In the first period, proprietary software is the only product available to the potential consumers.

## Network Externalities

Network externalities exist when the consumer’s utility of a product is increasing in the number of users in the same product network. In general, there are two types of network externalities—direct and indirect. A direct network externalities effect arises when there is “a direct physical effect of the number of purchasers on the value of the product” [16, p. 424]. Katz and Shapiro [17] define networks having a direct physical effect if the value of joining this network depends on the number of other consumers who join by adopting the same or compatible product. Not surprisingly, the canonical examples are communication networks such as telephone, e-mail, and facsimile standards.

An indirect network externalities effect, on the other hand, often refers to the increasing utility derived from users of the complementary goods. A well-known example in the personal computer industry is perhaps the competition between the Macintosh operating system and the Windows operating system for personal computers before Windows became the de facto standard. While Macintosh was touted as having a better operating system than the competing Windows product, a potential buyer of a personal computer tended to choose the “Wintel” platform, that is, the Windows operating system with an Intel CPU (central processing unit) because far more users had already adopted the Wintel platform than the Macintosh platform. The more users the Wintel platform attracts, the more software developers write applications for that platform, which in turn increases the value of the Wintel platform and makes it a more attractive option to potential consumers. This positive network externalities effect can be easily understood as positive spiral, a term coined by the founder of Microsoft, Bill Gates, in his best seller The Road Ahead [12].

In our study, we choose to focus on the direct network externalities effect when examining the competition between OSS and proprietary software, because other than operating systems, we hardly observe this hardware/software complementary phenomenon in the existing software market. In addition, most software users make their decisions to join a software network based on their private benefits and do not take into account that others in the network are also better off by their decision to join, a typical attribute for networks characterized by direct network effects [5].

## The Impact of Network Externalities in a Fully Covered Market

We first present how consumers’ utility functions of the open source and proprietary software change according to different network externalities effect intensities and compatibility strategies. Next, we summarize the impact of network externalities by comparing the equilibrium demands of OSS and proprietary software with the benchmark results reported earlier. By comparing the equilibrium demands among cases before and after the competing software products are made compatible with each other, we are able to find which party (the OSS or proprietary software vendor) has the most incentive to make its product compatible.

## Case 1: OSS and Proprietary Software Are Compatible with Each Other

When rival products are compatible with each other, the network size for either software product, where the network value is generated, is equivalent to the sum of the two networks, $Q = Q _ { o } + Q _ { p }$ . Thus, the effective network size amounts to one when the market is fully covered.

Recall that potential consumers are differentiated into α and β groups depending on their predisposed preference for either OSS or proprietary software. The net utilities for a β-group consumer adopting OSS and proprietary software are given by

$$
u _ {o} = (\theta + \gamma Q) s _ {\beta o}
$$

and

$$
u _ {p} = (\theta + \gamma Q) s _ {p} - p,
$$

where $s _ { p } > s _ { \beta \mathfrak { o } }$ and $Q = 1$

The marginal consumer $\theta _ { \beta , o p }$ in the β group who is indifferent between OSS and proprietary software is characterized by

$$
\left(\theta_ {\beta , o p} + \gamma Q\right) s _ {\beta o} = \left(\theta_ {\beta , o p} + \gamma Q\right) s _ {p} - p.\tag{1}
$$

The installed bases for OSS and proprietary software among β-group consumers are therefore described by

$$
Q _ {\beta o} = Q _ {\beta , o p} (1 - \alpha) \text {   and   } Q _ {\beta p} = (1 - \theta_ {\beta , o p}) (1 - \alpha).\tag{2}
$$

From Equations (1) and (2), the marginal consumer $\theta _ { \beta , o p }$ can be derived as

$$
\theta_ {\beta , o p} = \frac {p}{s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o}} - \gamma .\tag{3}
$$

Accordingly, the number of open source users and proprietary software buyers among the β-group consumers are

$$
Q _ {\beta o} = \left(\frac {p}{s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o}} - \gamma\right) (1 - \alpha)
$$

and

$$
Q _ {\beta p} = \left(1 - \frac {p}{s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o}} + \gamma\right) (1 - \alpha),
$$

respectively.

Likewise, the net utilities for an α-group consumer adopting OSS and proprietary software are given by

$$
u _ {o} = (\theta + \gamma Q) s _ {\alpha o}
$$

and

$$
u _ {p} = (\theta + \gamma Q) s _ {p} - p,
$$

where $s _ { \infty } > s _ { p }$ and $Q = 1$

Because $u _ { o } > u _ { p }$ due to $s _ { \alpha o } > s _ { p } ,$ consumers in this α group have no incentive to purchase proprietary software. Thus, the entire α group will choose to adopt OSS. Adding the OSS and proprietary software users in both the α and $\beta$ groups together, we obtain the total installed bases for both software products as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} Q _ {o} = \alpha + Q _ {\beta o} = \alpha + \left(\frac {p}{s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o}} - \gamma\right) (1 - \alpha) \\ Q _ {p} = Q _ {\beta p} = \left(1 - \frac {p}{s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o}} + \gamma\right) (1 - \alpha). \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

The comparative statics of $Q _ { o }$ and $Q _ { p }$ with respect to p reveals that

$$
\frac {\partial Q _ {o}}{\partial p} = \frac {1 - \alpha}{s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o}} > 0 \text {and} \frac {\partial Q _ {p}}{\partial p} = \frac {- 1 + \alpha}{s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o}} <   0.\tag{5}
$$

If the price of proprietary software increases, then the demand for the proprietary software decreases while the demand for the OSS increases. In order to guarantee nonnegative demand for OSS, the following condition must hold:

$$
\gamma \leq \frac {p}{s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o}} + \frac {\alpha}{1 - \alpha}.\tag{6}
$$

The condition in inequality (6) implies that the intensity of the network externalities effect on the consumer’s net utility is bounded above by

$$
\frac {p}{s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o}} + \frac {\alpha}{1 - \alpha}.
$$

Similarly, in order to ensure nonnegative demand for proprietary software, the feasible range of the proprietary software price must be within

$$
\bigg (\gamma - \frac {\alpha}{1 - \alpha} \bigg) \Big (s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o} \Big) \leq p \leq \big (1 + \gamma \big) \Big (s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o} \Big).\tag{7}
$$

The proprietary software vendor maximizes its revenue by setting the price optimally. The decision problem of the proprietary software vendor is therefore formulated as

$$
\begin{array}{c} \max _ {p} p Q _ {p} = p \cdot \left(1 - \frac {p}{s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o}} + \gamma\right) (1 - \alpha) \\ \text { s.t. } \left(\gamma - \frac {\alpha}{1 - \alpha}\right) \big (s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o} \big) \leq p \leq (1 + \gamma) \big (s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o} \big). \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

Kuhn–Tucker conditions lead to

$$
p ^ {*} = \frac {s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o}}{2} (1 + \gamma).\tag{9}
$$

We then plug the optimal price into Equation (4) to calculate the resulting demands (i.e., installed bases) of the proprietary software and OSS as follows:

$$
Q _ {o} ^ {*} = \frac {1}{2} (1 + \alpha - (1 - \alpha) \gamma) \text {   and   } Q _ {p} ^ {*} = \frac {1}{2} (1 - \alpha) (1 + \gamma).\tag{10}
$$

When we compare the market share of proprietary software before and after the introduction of OSS, we find that the proprietary software vendor incurs a market share loss of

$$
\frac {\alpha + (1 - \alpha) \gamma^ {2}}{2 (1 - \gamma)}
$$

to its OSS competitor. The profit of the proprietary software vendor is accordingly

$$
\pi_ {p} ^ {*} = \frac {(s _ {p} - s _ {\beta o}) (1 - \alpha) (1 + \gamma) ^ {2}}{4}.\tag{11}
$$

By comparing the above results to the benchmark demands and profit, we have the following proposition:

Proposition 1: Compared with the benchmark case in a fully covered market where the OSS and the proprietary software are compatible with each other, the installed base of the proprietary software increases and the installed base of the OSS decreases. Both the optimal price and the optimal profit of the proprietary software increase in the presence of network externalities.

Proofs for all the propositions are relegated to the Appendix.

## What Is the Best Strategy—Compatible or Incompatible?

For either the OSS or the proprietary software vendor, making its product compatible with its competitor allows the users of that product to benefit from the network of its opponent, which therefore adds to the value of the product and increases consumer willingness to pay and their net utilities for adopting the software. However, in a fully covered market, the increase of one party’s installed base leads to the decrease of that of the other party, and the best response for the competitor in such a situation is to make its product compatible as well. Therefore, in equilibrium, we will see that software products are either incompatible with each other or fully compatible with each other. One-way compatibility (one product is compatible with the other, but not the other way around) is therefore not sustainable and is ignored in our analysis. Furthermore, we are more interested in which party has the most incentive to make its product compatible. The answer to this question can be found by a head-to-head comparison of the payoffs, such as market shares and profits, for both the OSS and the proprietary software vendors when the software is made compatible with their rival product. Whichever party receives the highest boost in its payoffs is deemed to have the most incentive to make the change.

Comparing Cases 1 and 2 in Table 3, we find that the OSS vendor has the most incentive to make its software compatible with its proprietary counterpart, a key finding that is summarized in Proposition 3:

Proposition 3: The OSS community has the most incentive to make its product compatible with its proprietary counterpart, as manifested by the expanded market share after the change. The proprietary software vendor, on the other hand, is not willing to make its product compatible with the OSS, as doing so will decrease its market share and profit.

## Competition Between OSS and Proprietary Software: Partially Covered Market

Th e mark et studied in th e previous sec tion wh ere OSS and proprietary software compete with each other is assumed to be fully covered. In some situations, however, this condition may not hold, especially when users’ learning cost is taken into consideration. Users may derive negative utilities as software becomes increasingly complicated and requires much effort to learn, therefore resulting in the situation where the users are better off not adopting either the OSS or the proprietary software. This leads to a partially covered market for both software products.

It has been well established that proprietary software often comes with better documentation and customer support than its OSS rival, with Microsoft’s MSDN and Oracle’s Documentation as some notable examples. Furthermore, proprietary software usually has been in the market for a longer period of time than its OSS counterpart, a reality modeled in our study by having only proprietary software in the first period. Thus, proprietary software users have had some degree of familiarity and would incur a lower learning cost when adopting it or moving on to a newer version of the same product. Further, other factors such as the lack of formal technical support and less friendly user interfaces cause OSS users to incur a higher learning cost than proprietary software users. In addition to relaxing the full market coverage assumption, we further extend our model to incorporate these characteristics of OSS and proprietary software by letting open source users incur a higher learning cost $( { \mathrm { i } } . { \mathrm { e } } . , c _ { o } > c _ { p } )$

Because OSS does not provide formal technical support and relies on the backing of a volunteer community, the network externalities effect will play a critical role for OSS to effectively compete with its commercial counterpart. Compared with proprietary software, OSS has several distinct advantages due to the positive network externalities effect. For example, the widespread peer review process involved in the open source development makes OSS more error free and resource efficient than proprietary software. In addition, the free rights to redistribute modifications and improvements to the code and to reuse other open source code propagate the accrued advantages to all members of the OSS communities. These advantages offered by OSS to each individual customer obviously come from the contribution of every other user in the network. Hence, compared with proprietary software users, OSS users conceivably derive more utilities from one more added consumer who becomes the user of the same OSS network. In the extended model, we therefore allow OSS to exhibit a stronger network externalities effect than the proprietary software product (i.e., $\gamma _ { _ { o } } > \gamma _ { _ { p } } )$ , whereas almost all past literature [1, 19, 24] assumes that these two types of software have the same network effect. Thus, it is of most interest to find out how the impact of network externalities would vary and how software vendors would be affected regarding their compatibility strategies under the new scenario.

## What Is the Best Strategy—Compatible or Incompatible?

Here we perform computational analysis using the same set of baseline parameters as in the previous section to investigate whether OSS or proprietary software has the most incentive to be compatible with the other.

Comparing the optimal demands of OSS and proprietary software before and after making them compatible, Figures 7 and 8 illustrate the head-to-head comparison results of Case 3 versus Case 4 in Table 4.

Numerical analyses show that making the OSS compatible with the proprietary software is always desirable for the OSS vendors. Doing so not only increases the demand for OSS but also helps OSS become impervious to changes of network effect intensity. For example, as shown in Figure 7a, when the OSS and the proprietary software are incompatible (solid line), the OSS loses more of its demand to the proprietary software as network externalities increase. However, once the OSS becomes compatible with the proprietary software, increasing network externalities intensity benefits the OSS, as evidenced by the upward demand curve (dashed line) in Figure 7a. Figures 7b and 8, however, indicate that the proprietary software vendor is better off not making its products compatible. Thus, the proprietary software vendor should try to make its product difficult for OSS to be compatible with because it is in the proprietary software vendor’s best interest to maintain a market in which software products are incompatible. One of the possible strategies to accomplish incompatibility is to create new file formats that can only be accessed by a proprietary software product. Microsoft Office 2007 provides a good example of this strategy. Once Microsoft realized that its OSS opponent, OpenOffice.org<sup>TM</sup>, was fully compatible with its Office 2003 product, it created a new series of office file formats (e.g., “.docx”) in Office 2007, and these formats were found to be difficult to open or edit in OpenOffice.org<sup>TM</sup> for a long time after the release of Office 2007. Reverse engineering of Office 2007 formats has not been successful thus far, resulting in incompatible products between OpenOffice. org<sup>TM</sup> and Office 2007. Not surprisingly, this intentional incompatibility has helped Microsoft maintain its dominant position over other productivity software.

In summary, in our extended model where the market is partially covered due to the learning cost, we once again find that the OSS community has the most incentive to make their products compatible, but not vice versa. Network externalities have a positive effect on proprietary software under both scenarios of compatibility, where significantly higher network externalities intensity for OSS may allow OSS to command a decent market share from its proprietary competitor in Case 3. OSS is better off in the presence of network externalities if it is made compatible with proprietary software.

A natural extension to our research is to examine the effect of introducing a commercially supported version of OSS on the competition dynamics. A commonly observed strategy utilized by an OSS company is to sell a commercially supported version of its OSS. A canonical example would be MySQL, where the software firm offers the MySQL Community Server for free but charges a \$599 per server per year license fee for the commercially supported and better-performing Enterprise Edition version. Compared with its proprietary opponent Oracle’s RDBMS, the licensing fee of the MySQL Enterprise Edition is much lower. However, it is believed by many that Oracle still holds a decent lead in product quality and delivers better performance than the commercially supported MySQL Enterprise Edition. In such a scenario, the OSS vendor attempts to maximize its profit derived from its commercially supported version rather than react passively to the pricing decision of the proprietary software vendor. As the closed-form solutions in this study without the presence of a commercially supported version of OSS are already too complex, numerical methods seem to be the only viable approach to glean any managerial implications. The analysis of this research assumes that the qualities of the OSS and the proprietary software are exogenous parameters. Another extension to our research is to allow the OSS and proprietary software developers to compete in the market by optimally deciding on the quality of their respective products.

## Discussion and Conclusion

We investigate th e impac t of network external ities effec t on the competition between open source and proprietary software. We analyze four different scenarios depending on whether OSS and proprietary software are compatible and whether the market is fully covered (i.e., all consumers will adopt either OSS or proprietary software). When the market is fully covered and when the OSS and the proprietary software are compatible, the network externalities effect benefits the proprietary software in terms of an increased installed base and optimal profit compared with the benchmark case of no network externalities, whereas OSS suffers with a decreased installed base. When the OSS and the proprietary software are incompatible in a fully covered market, we derive a threshold involving the quality of the proprietary software and the perceived quality of OSS among those consumers who prefer proprietary software such that if the proportion of potential consumers who have predisposed preferences for OSS is greater than this threshold value, then OSS benefits from the presence of network externalities; otherwise, proprietary software benefits.

In a partially covered market where OSS users incur a higher learning cost than users of proprietary software, and OSS exhibits a stronger network externalities effect than proprietary software, analytical and computational results show that the network externalities effect benefits the proprietary software. This effect is more pronounced when OSS and proprietary software are incompatible with each other. OSS is better off in the presence of network externalities if it is made compatible, but worse off in the presence of network externalities otherwise.

Finally, we find that making the software compatible with its competing rival is not desirable for the proprietary software vendor, but is highly beneficial for the OSS community. Consequently, an effective strategy for the proprietary software vendor to maintain its dominant position in the market is to make it difficult for the OSS counterpart to be compatible with its product.
