# Profiting from Knowledge Management: The Impact of Time and Experience

Dong-Gil Ko

College of Business, University of Cincinnati, Cincinnati, Ohio 45221, donggil.ko@uc.edu

Alan R. Dennis

Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, Indiana 47405, ardennis@indiana.edu

lthough many organizations are implementing knowledge management systems (KMS), there is little empir ical evidence about whether KMS use can improve individual performance, and how time and experience influence the value derived from KMS use. Using hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) statistical analysis, we examined the impact of using a codification-based KMS on the sales performance of 2,154 sales representatives in a pharmaceutical firm over a 24-month period. We found that KMS had significant positive impacts on indi vidual performance and that these performance benefits grew over time. Moreover, experience moderated the relationship between KMS use and individual performance. Knowledge workers with more experience were able to more quickly absorb and apply the knowledge from the KMS than were those with less experience, who took longer to benefit from KMS use. However, over time experience played a diminishing role in leverag ing performance gains from KMS use, and knowledge workers with less experience eventually derived similar performance benefits as those of their more experienced counterparts.

Key words: knowledge management systems use; performance improvement; time; job experience; longitudinal study; hierarchical linear modeling (HLM)

## Introduction

With the growing strategic importance of knowledge management, more firms are implementing knowledge management systems (KMS), “a class of information systems applied to managing organizational knowledge” (Alavi and Leidner 2001, p. 114). These organizations implement KMS to facilitate personalization-based (i.e., people-to-people) or codification-based (i.e., people-to-documents) approaches for supporting their performance improvement programs (PIP) such as enhanced productivity, improved customer service, or reduced cost (Gallivan et al. 2003, Repenning 2002, Hansen et al. 1999). The goal with either approach is to provide pertinent knowledge to employees so that they conduct their tasks more effectively and efficiently (Markus 2001).

The general premise is that a KMS that is actively used by employees can help improve individual learning and minimize knowing-doing gaps and thereby accrue performance benefits (Alavi and Leidner 2001, Pfeffer and Sutton 2000). Popular press accounts suggest that KMS use can have beneficial impacts on firm performance (e.g., Derr 1999), although very few studies have been conducted on the impact of KMS use on performance or on the use-performance link with other types of systems (Ahearne et al. 2004, DeLone and McLean 2003, Rai et al. 2002, Pentland 1989, Lucas 1975).

Although there have been interpretative studies of KMS use in organizations (such as Orlikowski 2000), we are only aware of two studies that quantitatively measured the impact on KMS use on individual or group performance, both of which found that KMS use decreased performance. Haas and Hansen (2005) found that the use of a codification-based KMS in a large consulting firm reduced performance; the more the employees used the KMS, the less likely they were to win bids. Haas and Hansen (2005) used a retrospective, cross-sectional survey in which participants were asked to recall their use of the KMS in preparation of bids and thus it may be criticized on these grounds, although Haas and Hansen took extensive precautions to prevent this. Nevertheless, a cross-sectional study of KMS use-performance link may not be an optimal approach given that it takes time to realize results because of an assimilation process involved in turning knowledge into action (Repenning 2002, Pfeffer and Sutton 2000).

One approach to overcome the findings obtained through a cross-sectional survey is to perform a longitudinal study. Using systems dynamics theoretical lens, Gallivan and his colleagues (2003) examined a “help desk” KMS over a 17-month period that focused on “deliberate” PIP; i.e., an initiative with objectives and incentives specifically designed to improve productivity, service quality, and other metrics. However, once again, use of the KMS decreased performance. Gallivan et al. suggest that there may be a delay in realizing performance gains; the use-performance link for KMS focused on PIP may produce opposite short-term effects, referred to as worse-before-better dynamic (Repenning and Sterman 2001).

It is possible that KMS use does indeed decrease performance. The knowledge in the KMS may have been difficult to find, extract, and use especially for inexperienced workers (Haas and Hansen 2005). Alternatively, it could be that there is a delayed effect in realizing performance gains (Gallivan et al. 2003, Repenning and Sterman 2001). Findings that system use has a negative effect on performance do not suggest that use of all such systems will always have a negative effect on performance. It is possible that the findings from these two studies may be tied to peculiarities of these studies or to the study settings—it could be a lack of formal training and incentive programs (Gallivan et al. 2003), or it could be based on volitional usage contexts (Rai et al. 2002). Of course, it could be any combination of these. We believe that gaining an understanding of the situations in which system use leads to negative performance and positive performance remains an important research and practical issue. Without such an understanding, managers could abandon projects that could prove valuable to the firm.

This study examines the role of job experience and time as important factors influencing the KMS useto-performance link. Haas and Hansen (2005) speculate using a KMS impairs performance because it takes users away from more productive activities, and it may be more costly to the more experienced individuals because of opportunity costs in performing other value-added tasks. On the other hand, Gallivan and his colleagues (2003) suggest that KMS use may translate into performance improvements, but it takes time before such gain is realized; i.e., it takes time for workers to learn to effectively use the knowledge.

Therefore, in this paper, we adopt a temporal lens to empirically examine whether there is a relationship between KMS use, experience, and individual performance by studying actual KMS use, not retrospective recollections, over a two year period in a quasivolitional KMS usage context. Therefore, the research question is: “When and for whom does KMS use improve or impair individual performance?” In an attempt to answer this question, we worked with a large multinational pharmaceutical firm (herein called “Farmaco”) to examine the effects of the use of their field sales KMS by sales representatives with a variety of experience.

This paper’s key contribution is that it is one of the first to investigate temporal aspects of the relationship between information technology (IT) use and individual performance in the KMS context.<sup>1</sup> Opening up this temporal dimension offers insight about the timing of the benefits from KMS use and how that varies across individuals. In the next section, we review prior literature to develop hypotheses. Next, we describe our field study methods and introduce the research site, knowledge workers, and the KMS. We then discuss our analysis and results, and finally draw implications for research and practice.

## Prior Research

## Knowledge Management and Knowledge Management Systems

There are many different approaches to managing knowledge and building KMS to support the knowledge management (KM) process and performance improvement programs (Repenning 2002, Earl 2001). One of the most fundamental dichotomies is the KM strategy adopted by the firm, whether codification or personalization (Grover and Davenport 2001, Hansen et al. 1999). With a codification strategy, the firm’s knowledge is carefully organized into reusable knowledge documents that are stored in a formal KMS and knowledge is shared through the reuse of these documents. With a personalization strategy, knowledge is shared through person-toperson contacts; whereas a KMS might help knowledge seekers find experts, the KMS itself plays a much smaller role in the personalization strategy than it does in the codification strategy.

Neither strategy is inherently better or worse than the other; it depends on the fit of the strategy to the needs of the organization (Hansen et al. 1999). A codification strategy, for example, is best suited to organizations that reuse the same knowledge repeatedly. The role of IT and KMS in facilitating the transfer of knowledge is central to the success of a codification KM strategy (Alavi and Leidner 2001, Miles et al. 1998), whereas the role of IT and KMS may be less central to the success of a personalization strategy (Brown and Duguid 2000, 2001; Hansen et al. 1999; McDermott 1999). Following Haas and Hansen (2005) and Gallivan et al. (2003), we chose to focus on codification-based KMS.

A codification strategy is essentially a people-todocuments approach, in which the firm expects to derive performance benefits through reusing knowledge documents (Teece 1998). Managing these codified knowledge documents means careful planning of knowledge captures such as the codifying and storing knowledge in databases where it can be made available for others to access (Markus 2001, Hansen et al. 1999). It also requires careful screening of the knowledge to ensure that it is valid within the intended target context (Davenport and Glaser 2002). Because the goal is to reuse the knowledge as much as possible (Hansen et al. 1999), the organization is more likely to invest in acquiring and validating knowledge with high applicability to many users. The 80-20 rule may apply (Craft and Leake 2002), in that the knowledge selected for the KMS may be the most common 20% likely to be of value to 80% of the users; because collecting and packaging knowledge is very costly, the organization will selectively package a small proportion of their knowledge (i.e., 20% of knowledge) to benefit the majority of their users (i.e., 80% of users).

## KMS Use and Performance

Organizations invest in KMS for the same reason they invest in other information systems: they believe that the value derived by the individuals using the system will exceed its cost (Santhanam and Hartono 2003). Use is the conduit through which information systems can affect performance (Trice and Treacy 1986, Attewell and Rule 1984). Use is the key link between investment and performance (Devaraj and Kohli 2003, Doll and Torkzadeh 1998): “without system use, there can be no consequences or benefits.” (DeLone and McLean 2003, p. 16). However, system use does not always produce positive value (Seddon 1997). Use is necessary but not sufficient for value (DeLone and McLean 2003, Doll and Torkzadeh 1998, Seddon 1997). Value arises when the system fits the needs of the users and use of the system enables individuals to perform their work in ways that are more efficient, more effective, and more satisfying.

Measuring the improvement in individual and organizational performance because of information system use is challenging. DeLone and McLean (2003) summarize six field studies that attempt to make this link between system use and improved performance. All rely on users’ perceptions of system use, and unfortunately, self-reported usage is often not a reliable surrogate for actual usage (Szajna 1996, Straub et al. 1995). We are aware of two studies that have examined the link between actual use (measured by the system itself) and performance: Devaraj and Kohli (2003) found that use of a decision support system in a hospital significantly reduced mortality rates and increased net patient revenues, and Ahearne and his colleagues (2004) found that use of a CRM (involving precall and postcall planning, reporting and territory analysis activities) by sales representatives improved initial sales performance but there were diminishing returns of technology usage. This body of research suggests that information system use can in some cases provide significant benefits, especially when tasks and technology are aligned (Devaraj and Kohli 2003, Goodhue and Thompson 1995).

Such prior studies have focused primarily on the use of systems where users work directly with the system to perform the task (e.g., decision support systems). Use of a KMS is one step removed from the task itself—the system is not used to work on the task, but rather to acquire knowledge needed to perform the task. As such, benefits from use of a KMS may be more difficult to observe because they do not occur while the system is in use or from the use of an entire KMS; rather, benefits accrue after the use of some of the KMS features (Griffith and Northcraft 1994) as the knowledge from the KMS begins to be used. Although many prior studies have examined the relationship between information system use and immediate benefits, there is a dearth of empirical evidence investigating the delayed impact on performance from the use of KMS and their features.

Thus, the question of interest is when and for whom there are benefits to individual knowledge workers from using an information system explicitly designed to provide knowledge to help them perform their work: a KMS. With a codification-based KMS, the knowledge in the KMS has been explicitly designed and validated to be correct and useful within the target users’ work context, and such context-specific knowledge should improve performance (Davenport and Glaser 2002)—after all, this is why the knowledge was added to the KMS.

However, prior research shows that using a KMS impairs performance (Haas and Hansen 2005, Gallivan et al. 2003). Haas and Hansen speculate that using a KMS hurts performance because it takes users away from more productive activities—that is, KMS use decreases performance because of the opportunity costs it imposes on performing other value-added tasks (Pinsonneault and Rivard 1998, Markus 1994). In contrast, Gallivan and colleagues suggest that performance improvement is likely to improve after a passage of time; i.e., there is a time lag between KMS use and performance improvement. In other words, measuring initial performance could be misleading given that it takes time for users to absorb, understand, and apply new knowledge in their environment to achieve the desired outcomes. Consequently, time is an important consideration affecting the relationship between the KMS use-to-performance link.

One key to this argument may lie in learning curves (Lapre and van Wassenhove 2001, Pisano et al. 2001). When individuals acquire new knowledge, it takes some time for the benefits of the new knowledge to appear (Repenning and Sterman 2002). As knowledge is transferred, the recipient of the knowledge must change his or her work processes to reflect the new knowledge. Initial performance often drops, as workers must learn to apply the new knowledge; performance will gradually improve as “practice makes perfect” (Pisano et al. 2001, p. 753). Performance gradually improves over time as the newly acquired knowledge is refined by practice (Farrell and McDaniel 2001) and applied continuously under different circumstances (Schilling et al. 2003). Thus measuring performance the first time new knowledge is used may lead one to conclude that KMS use impairs performance, when in fact the value from KMS use may only emerge after the user has employed the knowledge several times and moved down the learning curve (Heine et al. 2003, Repenning 2002, Pfeffer and Sutton 2000). Thus, unlike other systems where users work directly with the system to perform the task, the performance benefits from KMS and knowledge use may only appear after some time has elapsed after the acquisition of knowledge. Therefore we have the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1 (H1). Benefits from the use of a codification-based KMS increase over time.

## Experience and the Value of KMS Use

There is also evidence that individual differences; i.e., prior knowledge and skills of the potential knowledge user, play an important role in affecting the relationship between KMS use and performance (Haas and Hansen 2005, Markus 2001, Pfeffer and Sutton 2000). A given knowledge document in the KMS may or may not lead to performance improvement for everyone. Some individuals will find little value in the knowledge contained in the KMS because they either already know the knowledge or find it irrelevant (McDermott 1999). But others may find it relevant to their situation, leading to greater potential to improve performance. Thus, one major factor that may affect performance benefits (or impairments) from KMS use is the amount of experience one has, because experience is a reasonable proxy for job-related knowledge (Haas and Hansen 2005, McDaniel et al. 1988, Schmidt et al. 1986).

Prior research has identified three major dimensions to “experience”: organizational tenure, the length of time with the organization; job tenure, the length of time in the current job in the current organization; and job experience, the length of time within the profession (McEnrue 1988). Some studies have found job experience to be the dominant experience-based predictor of job performance although the effects have not always been consistent (e.g., McDaniel et al. 1988, McEnrue 1988, Schmidt et al. 1986). In the context of sales (the context of this study), job tenure has been linked to sales performance (Brown and Peterson 1993), and substantial research attention has been directed at job tenure rather than the other dimensions (see Naumann et al. 2000 for a review). In industrial selling environments, job tenure has shown to influence customer selling orientation (O’Hara et al. 1991), which impacts performance (Saxe and Weitz 1982). Therefore, the term “experience” in this study is defined as an individual’s length of time in the current job in the current organization—that is, job tenure.

As Markus (2001) points out, knowledge seekers may differ in experience, needs, and expectations for the KMS. To improve performance, knowledge must be relevant to the task at hand and must not be previously known (Vinokur et al. 1975). Some KMS users will be knowledge workers with little experience searching for answers to immediate problems and seeking to improve their job performance. These users will have the greatest knowledge needs because they lack the general knowledge of their more experienced counterparts (Markus 2001). They are likely to be the largest users of the KMS and the most likely to benefit from the 80-20 rule of providing routine knowledge. In contrast, knowledge workers with greater experience are more likely to seek knowledge for challenging or unusual situations that they have not previously encountered (Markus 2001) or focus on rare events and undervalue common knowledge (Johnson 1988). Those with little experience are more likely to find relevant knowledge not previously known to them in a codification-based KMS (Haas and Hansen 2005).

Finding relevant, new, knowledge is necessary but not sufficient to improve performance. The knowledge must first be contextualized to the user’s own environment before it can be absorbed and used (Haas and Hansen 2005, Faniel and Majchrzak 2003, Pfeffer and Sutton 2000). The ability to contextualize and absorb new knowledge is closely tied to the potential user’s prior knowledge (Ko et al. 2005). Following Vandenbosch and Higgins (1996), we adopt the terminology and definition of Brewer (1987, p. 193) to refer to these stocks of prior knowledge as mental models that are “   all forms of mental representation, general or specific, from any domain, causal, intentional, or spatial.”

A long stream of rich and varied research has examined the mental models and cognitive processes of more and less experienced workers (e.g., Morris et al. 1999, Sternberg 1997, Bereiter and Scardamalia 1993, Adelson and Soloway 1988, Pennington 1987, Chi et al. 1982). There are substantial differences in the mental models and cognitive processes because of experience (Bell and Kozlowski 2002, Chi et al. 1988) that lead to differences in the abilities to process new knowledge and to perform knowledge-related tasks (Bell and Kozlowski 2002, Bonner et al. 2002, Van Overschelde and Healy 2001).

There are two key differences because of experience that may affect the ability to effectively use new knowledge. First, those with more experience are more likely to recognize and accurately value new knowledge presented to them because they have a better sense of what knowledge is relevant (Nelson et al. 2000, Shanteau 1988), particularly in less structured environments (Brucks 1985). They are more selective in the information they acquire and use (Nelson et al. 2000, Shanteau 1992a, Johnson 1988). Those with more experience are also likely to more quickly recognize patterns between new knowledge and their existing mental models (Shanteau 1992b) because their more developed mental models afford them more ways to find links between new concepts in a knowledge document and concepts in their existing mental models (Van Overschelde and Healy 2001).

Second, more experienced knowledge workers are better able to understand and adapt the new knowledge to their context (Arnold et al. 2004, Bell and Kozlowski 2002, Kanfer and Ackerman 1989). Their more developed mental models better enable them to engage in deep processing, which involves elaboration, critical thinking, and the integration of new information with their existing mental models (Bell and Kozlowski 2002). Those with more experience are likely to more quickly adapt new knowledge to their individual contexts because of the greater organizational, contextual, domain-relevant, and jobrelated tacit and explicit knowledge in their mental models (Markus 2001, Van Overschelde and Healy 2001, Choudhury and Sampler 1997, Cohen and Levinthal 1990).

In summary, more experienced knowledge workers are better equipped to immediately act on the new knowledge they receive (Bell and Kozlowski 2002, Bonner et al. 2002, Jacoby et al. 2001) because of their ability to more quickly recognize its relevance and to contextualize and absorb it, and then apply it in their contexts (Markus 2001, Pfeffer and Sutton 2000, Chi et al. 1988).

In contrast, it takes less experienced individuals longer to contextualize, absorb, and apply the knowledge, so the benefits of the new knowledge take longer to appear (Repenning and Sterman 2001). Those with less experience have less developed mental models that constrain the amount of job-relevant knowledge that is available to them (Kolodner 1983). To someone with little experience, such limited knowledge, even if available in their mental model, is not nearly as well organized or useable relative to that in the mental models of more experienced workers (McKeithen et al. 1981). Thus, it is harder and takes longer for them to assess the relevance of new knowledge (Nelson et al. 2000, Shanteau 1988, McKeithen et al. 1981).

Furthermore, those with less experience have more difficulty contextualizing knowledge to their situation (Arnold et al. 2004, Bell and Kozlowski 2002, Kanfer and Ackerman 1989). Less experienced workers often begin by following procedures as outlined without adapting them to their own context (Repenning and Sterman 2002). Initially, they work by rote, not by integrating the new knowledge with their existing mental models. Therefore, initial performance may drop as those with little experience must learn to apply the new knowledge (Pisano et al. 2001). Therefore we have the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2A (H2A). Experience will moderate the relationship between the use of a codification-based KMS and the speed at which benefits from use appear such that more experienced knowledge workers will derive benefits faster than less experienced workers.

As less experienced workers continue to use the knowledge, they establish links and connections among concepts; i.e., their mental models develop. Over time, they begin to adapt and contextualize the knowledge as they use it in their context. Performance gradually improves as the acquired knowledge is refined by practice in the worker’s context (Schilling et al. 2003, Farrell and McDaniel 2001, Pisano et al. 2001, Ackerman 1988). After some time, this part of the less experienced workers’ mental models may very well converge with those of their more experienced counterparts. Therefore, although more experienced workers may benefit more initially given their abilities to process new knowledge and successfully apply them immediately (Bell and Kozlowski 2002, Bonner et al. 2002), this initial benefit may be shortlived once less experienced workers have had some time to use the knowledge and refine it (Repenning 2002, Pfeffer and Sutton 2000). Therefore we have the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2B (H2B). The differential performance benefits between more and less experienced knowledge workers from the use of a codification-based KMS will converge over time.

## Method

We conducted a field study at a large multinational pharmaceutical firm, which we call Farmaco. Our focus is on the field sales KMS used by Farmaco to support its U.S. field sales representatives. We interviewed KM managers and sales managers, and “shadowed” two KM associates for a day, but our primary focus is quantitative. The next section provides brief background information on Farmaco. This is followed by a description of the knowledge workers and the field sales KMS. We then discuss the data we used and the analysis technique.

## The Firm

A leader in the pharmaceutical industry, Farmaco develops and markets pharmaceutical products throughout the world. They are organized by both functional business units and geographic business units. Research and manufacturing are organized in separate business units, but sales is organized by geographic region. The largest individual sales business unit is the U.S. affiliate, which is responsible for all sales within the United States. The U.S. affiliate has marketing and other support functions, but its largest component is the dozen or so principal sales divisions,<sup>2</sup> each focusing on a different set of disease states for which the firm offers drugs, or types of physicians (e.g., primary care physicians, specialist physicians, HMO administrators).

Because Farmaco focuses on prescription drugs, almost all of its revenues come from a very small set of products. The success in managing this select set of brands, each of which has only a limited life before generics arrive, is extremely important to the firm’s success. Most of Farmaco’s sales revenue comes from the sales of drugs designed to treat chronic illnesses (e.g., diabetes, asthma, mental illness) which have more stable demand patterns than drugs designed to treat temporary conditions (e.g., infections). Farmaco markets their products primarily through the use of their knowledge workers—sales representatives.

## The Knowledge Workers

The pharmaceutical industry is a knowledge intensive industry. Each sales representative markets Farmaco’s product by interacting with physicians and informing them about existing and newly released products. The role of the sales representatives is constrained by government regulation so there are many rules that representatives must follow when providing information. Each sales representative receives six months of training in the sales rules and procedures, the field sales KMS, Farmaco’s products, and other companyspecific policies. This process ensures a good fit between the field sales KMS and the tasks performed by sales representatives (Goodhue and Thompson 1995). In short, all new hires are provided with the necessary background and training (e.g., companyspecific vocabulary, FDA regulations) to effectively use the field sales KMS and conduct sales activities in his or her sales territory.

Each sales representative is assigned to one division, and is responsible for the assigned doctors within his or her sales territory for a given set of drugs. Sales representatives do not compete for the same physicians, so there is no conflict of interest in sharing knowledge. Instead, they work to increase the knowledge of disease states and Farmaco’s products during their interactions with physicians. A sales representative’s role is not to sell, per se; they cannot take orders (e.g., door-to-door salesperson model). Rather, sales representatives educate physicians about Farmaco’s products which often require multiple presentations (e.g., large system sales model). A physician’s prescription of a drug that is purchased by a patient reflects a “sale,” which often lags the sales activities.

A sales representative’s performance is tied to the number of prescriptions which are filled, and his or her ability to meet or exceed preestablished quota for sales is tied closely to his or her compensation. Sales quotas are set annually based on the expected sales from the representative’s sales territory independent of the performance of the individual representative.<sup>3</sup> Because career progression is related to an individual’s sales performance, there is a confluence of interest between the firm and its sales representatives.

## The Knowledge Management System

Farmaco’s field sales KMS is a Lotus Notes-based system that allows the highly mobile sales representatives to work online or offline through a replicating process. The ability to work off-line is critical because most work occurs in the field (e.g., in cars or doctors offices).<sup>4</sup> Knowledge is posted for sales representatives to search (a “pull” system), but several times per month Farmaco will “push” critical knowledge to sales representatives—hence, we view Farmaco’s field sales KMS as a quasivoluntary system (Rai et al. 2002). Although it is a part of sales representatives desktop and is helpful for many tasks performed, Farmaco does not mandate the use of the KMS.<sup>5</sup>

The KMS contains three types of knowledge. The first is knowledge about the drugs that representatives sell, provided by the brand team (or the medical organization through brand team). This includes basic knowledge (e.g., chemical processes by which drugs work, side effects) and competitive sales information (e.g., analyses of competitors’ drugs, selling messages against those competitors, process templates for handling common questions). The second type of knowledge is general sales knowledge developed by the sales operations management (e.g., selling techniques, rules for good promotional practices, legal guidelines for conducting continuing medical education). The third type of knowledge is the best practices, lessons learned, and “stories” submitted by sales representatives. All knowledge items submitted from the field are first evaluated by a peer review panel of expert sales representatives to ensure the submitted item is both relevant and new, and then is reviewed by the legal department, sales management and the brand team before being added to the KMS.

The general sales knowledge is common to all divisions, but the knowledge about the drugs is customized for the sale representatives in each division so that it fits their needs. Sales representatives only see the knowledge they need about the drugs they sell. Knowledge had to be customized to the individual needs of each division because each division serves different types of doctors; the knowledge needed to interact with a specialist is different than that for a primary care physician. Each brand team and the sales operations group has one knowledge associate assigned (either full time or part time) to help it manage its knowledge. Each division receives 1–2 new knowledge postings per day (excluding the unusual flurry around the launch of a new drug). A total of 40,000 to 50,000 knowledge documents are retrieved by sales representatives each month.

## Data

We used three independent sources of data: KMS usage data from Farmaco’s knowledge management group, experience data from Famaco’s personnel management system, and sales performance data managed by a third-party outside contractor. Our analyses are constrained by the data provided to us by Farmaco. After conducting an analysis involving quarterly data (reported in Ko and Dennis 2004), we were given access to 24 months of data (January 2002 through December 2003), plus the prior year’s performance. The KMS had been in use for approximately three years before this time period, although the current version of the KMS software had only been in use for one year. During the spring of 2002, Farmaco consolidated some sales divisions and deployed new information systems to others. To avoid any potential impacts, we eliminated these divisions from our study, resulting in a total of six divisions.

We eliminated any sales representative with less than six months of experience at the start of our study, because sales representatives spend most of their first six months in a training program. We eliminated all data from sales representatives who transferred between territories after the date of their transfer; their pretransfer data are included. The resulting data set includes data for 2,154 sales representatives with 31,418 monthly observations.

Performance. Consistent with prior studies on sales representatives (e.g., Engle and Barnes 2000, Van de Walle et al. 1999), our dependent variable was the monthly sales performance, defined as an individual’s percent of sales quota achieved—that is, how an individual’s actual monthly sales compared to the sales quota for their sales territory set for that month. Percent of quota is a strong measure of performance because it controls for externalities including market potential, economic conditions, and territory size (Churchill et al. 1985). A sales representative who exactly met his or her quota would receive a 100, and a representative under quota by 1% would receive a 99. Because different divisions sell different products, the mean percent of sales quota achieved for all the sales representatives in a division were different across the divisions studied. For this reason, we include the sales representative’s division as a control factor in our model.

KMS Use. The first set of independent variables is KMS use. Computer-recorded measures of system use are generally preferred to subjective measures such as self-report measures (Straub et al. 1995, LeBlanc and Kozar 1990), so we used the number of knowledge documents displayed on an individual sales representative’s screen in the current month (current use) and in the prior three months (prior use). These data exclude all navigation information and simply count the number of knowledge documents in the Notes repository that each person opened (a knowledge document is defined as a document containing knowledge that is stored in the KMS; thus, e-mail messages were not considered as knowledge documents). Use was nonnormally distributed, so we used a square root transformation because the data are counts (Snijders and Bosker 1999).

The use data suffer from the common problems associated with computer recorded metrics (e.g., undercounting of use for documents printed and referred many times on paper rather than in the system, and overcounting of documents that are displayed on the screen but not read or used). Such issues are generally believed to be outweighed by the greater accuracy that computer recording brings over perceptions (Straub et al. 1995, LeBlanc and Kozar 1990) because any imperfections in the measure are likely to be less than those in a perceptual measure and because such imperfections would tend to increase error variance and render insignificant any relationship between use and performance. In short, even though counting the number of knowledge documents displayed is an imperfect measure of knowledge use, it is a conservative and reliable measure.

Experience. Experience is defined as the number of months an individual had been employed by Farmaco as a field sales representative. Experience changes over the data set, as sales representatives gain one more month of experience for each month in the data. We used one constant value of experience in the model—the initial experience for the first time period (see Raudenbush and Bryk 2002, Snijders and Bosker 1999). Because experience was nonnormally distributed, we used a log transformation on the data prior to analysis.

Control Variables. We include division and prior year’s sales performance because Farmaco’s Knowledge Management group identified these as important factors affecting performance.

Reliability. Our data are directly measured variables, not survey items, so the usual tests for reliability do not apply. The performance data, usage data, and experience data are collected centrally by three separate departments. Whereas errors in recording can occur, there is little chance of systematic bias across the three departments that would influence our results. Random recording errors would, of course, reduce the likelihood of finding significant effects, and thus make our hypothesis tests more conservative.

## Analysis

Similar to Ang et al. (2002), we used hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) (Raudenbush and Bryk 2002) for our analysis because traditional regression techniques were not appropriate for our matched set of performance and use nested within individuals. HLM is designed to analyze this type of multilevel research design.<sup>6</sup> (Raundenbush and Bryk 2002, Snijders and Bosker 1999, Hoffman 1997). We have a two level model:<sup>7</sup> the lowest level (level 1) is the month with the matched set of performance, current use and three months of prior use; the second level (level 2)

is the individual sales representative and his or her characteristics (experience, prior performance, and division).

As shown in Figure 1, our level 1 model includes the within time period variables (current use and the three prior months of use), plus 20 fixed effect indicator variables representing the 21 individual months in our period of study<sup>8</sup> (see Snijders and Bosker 1999 for time period modeling in HLM). Our level 2 models contain the individual-specific factors that we hypothesize affect the magnitude of impact that use has on performance for a specific sales representative. The model for the intercept attempts to estimate a specific sales representative’s mean performance independent of use and includes (1) an intercept term; (2) his or her experience; (3) his or her prior year sales performance (as a control variable, because prior performance is often a good predictor of current performance); (4) a set of five indicators for his or her division (as a control); and (5) an error term.<sup>9</sup> In summary, the performance for sales representative j in month i (performance ) is the sum of seven components:

• the intercept, which is the overall estimated mean performance of that individual $( \beta _ { 0 j } ) ;$ plus

• the value that individual derives from current use $( \beta _ { 1 j }$ times the amount used in that month); plus

• the value that individual derives from use one month ago $( \beta _ { 2 j }$ times the amount used in the prior month); plus

• the value that individual derives from use two months ago $( \beta _ { 3 j }$ times the amount used two months prior); plus

• the value that individual derives from use three months ago $( \beta _ { 4 j }$ times the amount used three months prior); plus

• an adjustment for the current month that reflects global changes faced by the company $( \beta _ { 5 }$ through $\beta _ { 2 4 } ) ;$ plus

• an error term.

## Hypothesis Tests

Hypothesis 1 argued that the benefits from the use of a KMS would increase over time. To test this, we examined the coefficients on use for the different time periods in the intercept model (Step 4 in Table 2). The coefficient on use one month ago (0.662, row h) is significantly greater than the coefficient on use in the current month (0.129, row e) $( t ( 2 , 1 5 2 ) = 4 . 7 7 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ Likewise, the coefficient on use two months ago (1.039, row k) is significantly greater than one month ago $( t ( 2 , 1 5 2 ) = \bar { 3 . 6 8 } , p < \bar { 0 . 0 0 1 } )$ , and significantly greater than current use $( t ( 2 , 1 5 2 ) = 7 . 9 9 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The coefficient on use three months ago (1.218, row n) is not significantly greater than two months ago $( t ( 2 , 1 5 2 ) =$ 158, $p = 0 . 0 5 7 )$ , but it is greater than the coefficient on use one month ago $( t ( 2 , 1 5 2 ) = 4 . 9 9 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and current use $( t ( 2 , 1 5 2 ) = 8 . 9 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . We conclude that H1 is supported, although the benefits from KMS use level off and fail to increase after three months.

Hypothesis 2A argued that more experienced individuals would gain immediate performance benefits from KMS use and that it would take longer for less experienced individuals to gain benefits. Hypothesis 2B argued that over time the performance differences between the more experienced and less experienced workers would disappear. To test these, we need to examine the significance of experience as a moderator of the relationship between use and performance (see Step 5 in Table 2), There are four such tests: for the current month (item f ), one month ago (i), two months ago (l), and three months ago (o). As Table 2 shows, the models for the current month, one month ago, and two months ago all have significant positive coefficients on experience $( f , i , l )$ but the model of three months ago does not (o). Therefore, we conclude that more experienced sales representatives gained greater performance benefits than did less experienced sales representatives for three of the four months (current month (f ), one month ago (i), two months ago (l)), but that over time the differences disappeared. Hypotheses 2A and 2B are supported.

## Discussion

The results of this study show that use of a codification-based KMS had significant positive impacts on individual performance and that these performance benefits grew over time. The more a sales representative used the KMS, the more likely they were to meet or exceed their sales quota. Those with greater experience derived immediate value from KMS use in the same month they used the KMS, but all users derived significant value in the second, third, and fourth months after use. More experienced sales representatives improved their performance by a greater extent each time they used the KMS than did less experienced sales representatives, although there were no differences in performance because of experience three months after use.

Keeping in mind the importance of context as suggested by Rai et al. (2002), the most relevant prior studies that examined the KMS use-performance link are the works of Haas and Hansen (2005) and Gallivan et al. (2003) which found that KMS use decreased performance (Haas and Hansen 2005, Gallivan et al. 2003), and that more experienced teams experienced greater declines in performance from each use than did less experienced teams (Haas and Hansen 2005). Our results contrast sharply with these prior studies. It would be possible to attribute this to the differences in the KMS, organizations, industries, PIP initiatives, and selling processes examined between the two studies, but we believe there is a more robust theoretical mechanism at play here.

Both our results and those of prior research suggest that there are users who derive little value from KMS use soon after new knowledge is acquired. In our study, the impact of KMS use increased over time: the mean marginal impact of knowledge use on sales went from 0.129 $( \gamma _ { 1 0 } )$ in the current month to 0.662 $( \gamma _ { 2 0 } )$ one month later, to 1.039 $( \gamma _ { 3 0 } )$ two months later, to 1.218 $( \gamma _ { 4 0 } )$ three months later (see Table 2, Step 4). One explanation for the increasing impact on performance observed in this study may be because of the learning that an individual undergoes. Once an individual acquires new knowledge, initial performance may drop before a recipient of knowledge develops ways to effectively apply this knowledge because change—even a change for better—can decrease performance until the user has successfully integrated it into his or her new work processes— a worse-before-better dynamic (Farrell and McDaniel 2001, Pisano et al. 2001, Repenning and Sterman 2001, Ackerman 1988).

More experienced knowledge workers can more quickly recognize new knowledge, contextualize it, and adapt it to their work practices and thus they more quickly gain the benefits from using it. They may gain immediate (modest) benefits from knowledge, although these benefits increase quickly over time. Less experienced workers are slower to benefit from new knowledge as it takes them longer to effectively integrate it into their work processes. Those with less experience tend to exert slow, effortful, and error-prone performance because of their “controlled” “by rote” processing (Farrell and McDaniel 2001, Ackerman 1988). Less experienced individuals’ lack of knowledge may impede their ability to quickly understand and integrate the knowledge from the KMS, such that they are more likely to initially integrate the knowledge incorrectly (Arnold et al. 2004). Thus less experienced workers show little initial performance gain, and may even see an initial performance decrease (Haas and Hansen 2005, Gallivan et al. 2003). It may take weeks or months before less experienced knowledge workers see positive benefits from new knowledge, although eventually they derive similar benefits from it as do their more experienced counterparts.

Therefore, we conclude that time and experience matter in understanding the benefits knowledge workers derive from using knowledge in a KMS. We attribute earlier research findings that concluded that KMS use impairs performance to time and experience effects. Users need to refine through practice the new knowledge they receive from a KMS before that knowledge can improve their performance. Prior studies examined only the initial impact of KMS use and thus missed the benefits that accrue over time as knowledge workers refine this new knowledge into their work contexts. More experienced knowledge workers are better able to more quickly integrate new knowledge and therefore they realize the benefits from new knowledge sooner; less experienced workers require longer time before they can reap the benefits of new knowledge.

One might speculate that those with more experience were more parsimonious in their consumption of knowledge, choosing only those documents that had direct applicability to their current needs because they better recognize the value of the knowledge. Table 1 shows a pattern of small but significant negative correlations between experience and use; more experienced knowledge workers tended to use fewer knowledge documents but the effect was small (with a correlation of about 0.100). On the other hand, the KMS in this study may have been designed to fit the tasks and the individuals, but there was likely some variation in the fit of specific knowledge documents for specific users in the sample.

The frequent provision of new knowledge documents (20–40 per month) coupled with their relatively short life span (typically six months) suggests that a significant proportion of the knowledge contained in this KMS is relatively new, short-term knowledge (e.g., flashes on how to respond to breaking news or competitor’s actions, new marketing campaigns), rather than the generic, long-term, general-purpose fundamental knowledge (e.g., drug performance, side effects). We believe that the specific and short-term knowledge is likely to be of value to both more and less experienced individuals. Hence, one interpretation of the performance differences is that less experienced sales representatives do not have much prior knowledge that enable them to effectively understand and apply the new organizational, contextual, and task-specific knowledge in the KMS. In other words, the people most likely to successfully understand and apply the new knowledge from the KMS are those who are already skilled at their job—i.e., mental models of those with more experience allow them to be better at absorbing and customizing new knowledge for meeting their needs. For example, as is common in sales (Leigh 1987), this KMS contained procedural sales knowledge (e.g., selling scripts). Those with less experience frequently follow such step-by-step procedures verbatim although those with more experience are likely to use “adaptive selling” and tailor the script to the needs of the situation (Sonnentag and Lange 2002, Blessing and Anderson 1996, Spiro and Weitz 1990).

The ability to recognize the value of “new knowledge,” absorb it, and effectively apply it in a short period of time is critical to deriving value from new knowledge (Cohen and Leventhal 1990). A breakdown in any one of these three processes will lessen the impact of KMS use. It is difficult to speculate which of these three activities may have caused the most difficulty for the sales representatives with less experience in this study. New knowledge documents were added to the KMS every business day during our 24-month period of study, so there may simply be an “information overload” problem such that sales representatives with less experience were less likely to locate needed knowledge or experienced difficulty recognizing the relevance of knowledge. It may also have been that the knowledge was not packaged sufficiently well to allow less capable users to effectively understand and integrate it. Once the knowledge was found, sales representatives with less experience lacking tacit and explicit knowledge may have had more difficulty in assimilating the knowledge into their mental models. Alternatively, it may have been that once the knowledge had been integrated into their mental models, the less experienced sales representatives were less likely than the more experienced sales representatives to effectively apply it when working with physicians. Or, it may be some combination of the above.

Our use of HLM attempts to explain both withinperson and between-person variation in the dependent variable, sales performance. Although prior research on sales performance found variance explained ranging from 3% to 9% (Churchill et al. 1985), our final models explain both within-person and between-person variation of 26% and 46%, respectively, both of which Cohen (1988) would term “large” effects. These effects stem from the combined, integrated, effects of current and prior use, experience, prior performance, and an adjustment for month. It is difficult to disaggregate the integrated effects of these separate factors, but the intermediate steps in the modeling process provide some clues. For example, the introduction of the monthly control variables in step 2 improves within-person $R ^ { 2 }$ by 4.3% and between-person $R ^ { 2 }$ by 1.9% (“small” effects according to Cohen 1988). More interestingly, the first introduction of KMS use improves within-person $R ^ { 2 }$ by a “medium” amount of 10.4% and between-person $R ^ { 2 }$ by a small amount of 6.5%. Thus we can conclude that KMS use explains at least a medium amount of the within-person variation from month to month, and a small amount of the between-person variation.

## Limitations

Our final model explains 26% of the within individual differences and 46% of the between individual differences, and there remains significant unexplained variance in the level 1 model, suggesting that other, unmeasured, factors play a major role in influencing an individual’s sales performance. Some of these other factors are likely outside of the individual sales representative, such as the introduction of new drugs, competitors’ actions, and changes in the number of patients seeking treatment. Nonetheless, the variance explained is significantly greater compared to other findings in the literature which range from 3% to 9% (Churchill et al. 1985).

We studied only one firm in one industry which reduces generalizability. The industry, prescription pharmaceuticals, does not permit sales representatives to sell directly to the end consumer. Instead, sales representatives inform doctors who prescribe medicines to patients, who ultimately choose to purchase or not. This purchase decision chain is long, and may be one factor influencing the delayed impact of knowledge on performance. However, the fact that sales representatives with more experience derived immediate value from KMS use, suggests that not all of the delayed impact is because of the long purchase chain. Likewise, an initial negative impact from use for the least experienced sale representatives (e.g., Figure 2) suggests that the long purchase chain is not the primary factor influencing this pattern of performance. We speculate that our findings may be generalized to organizations with a similar KMS with a large sales force that share common knowledge and need fast access to accurate and timely knowledge.

## Implications for Research

We believe that this study offers several implications for future research. First, in sharp contrast to the most relevant prior KMS use-performance studies conducted by Haas and Hansen (2005) and Gallivan et al. (2003), our study demonstrates a significant positive link between the use of the knowledge in a codification-based KMS and knowledge worker performance: use improved performance. The first key distinction and contribution from this study is a consideration for time; i.e., we examined initial as well as delayed impact of KMS use on individual performance. Past studies (which studied only the immediate impacts of KMS use) concluded that KMS use did not improve performance. We theorized and found empirical evidence that performance benefits emerge over time because of the need for knowledge workers to absorb and integrate the new knowledge. Prior studies that concluded KMS use had no impact were not wrong, just too narrowly focused on the immediate impact of KMS use; they missed the need for workers to absorb and integrate the knowledge, which leads to improved benefits after weeks or months have elapsed. Our study is the first to illuminate this delayed impact of KMS use on performance. Clearly, more research is needed to test and verify this link between KMS use and performance in other organizations and knowledge contexts.

This finding also adds to the relatively small set of prior studies that have examined information systems success and the link between the use of information systems and individual performance (e.g., Goodhue and Thompson 1995, DeLone and McLean 1992, Fedorowicz et al. 1992). Prior studies have focused primarily on the use of decision support systems. This study extends the results to a new class of information system: a knowledge management system. With a decision support system, users work directly with the system to perform the task, and thus time may not be an important consideration. Use of a KMS is one step removed from the task itself—the system is not used to work on the task, but rather to acquire knowledge needed to perform the task. As such, benefits from use may be more difficult to observe because they do not occur while the system is in use. Nonetheless, our study suggests that there are significant benefits from KMS use, and over time.

Second, a fundamental premise of the learning curves argument is that the beneficial impacts of experiential learning on performance decreases as individuals become more experienced (Lapre and van Wassenhove 2001, Pisano et al. 2001). Paradoxically, our data suggests otherwise. Over time, all individuals derived value from KMS use, but those with more experience moved down the learning curve more quickly than those with less experience. Nonetheless, the beneficial impacts enjoyed by the more experienced individuals converged over time with those of their less experienced counterparts; the benefits to experience were temporary, indicating that experience enabled faster, but not overall better, knowledge absorption and use.

Much prior knowledge management research has focused on providing knowledge to less knowledgeable and less experienced individuals (e.g., Ko et al. 2005, Argote 1999). Our results suggest that this focus is not ideal for a codification-based KMS; both more and less experienced workers benefit from KMS use. Thus it may not be best to promote knowledge sharing and transfer activities downward to the less experienced; rather, there are immediate benefits to knowledge sharing and transfer to those that are more experienced. More research is needed on the knowledge needs of highly experienced workers (cf. Markus 2001).

KMS use affects performance when users can selectively find the new knowledge they need, recognize its relevance, absorb it into their mental models, and effectively translate and apply it to change their behavior. Thus two plausible explanations for why those with more experience derived value sooner from KMS use may lie in (1) the ability of more experienced workers to more quickly recognize the relevance of new knowledge and, (2) the ability of more experienced workers to more quickly assimilate, and effectively apply the knowledge. In other words, the two theoretical mechanisms by which experience increases the value of new knowledge may be either the faster knowledge recognition skills, or the faster knowledge absorption skills, or both.

Research is needed to further examine the paradox found in this study—i.e., why do the beneficial impacts of knowledge on performance increase as individuals become more experienced (instead of decrease with experience as prior research suggests;

see Haas and Hansen 2005)? At what level of experience (over time), if any, do the beneficial impacts of learning on performance begin to decrease, and is this specific to certain contexts? Another related issue for future research lies in better understanding the knowledge absorption construct in the KMS use to performance chain. How and why does the user’s experience affect knowledge absorption and performance, and can KMS be better designed to facilitate knowledge absorption by both the experienced and less experienced? Part may lie in the better design of KMS systems themselves (e.g., navigation, search, knowledge presentation); other parts may lie in the knowledge and its packaging (e.g., taxonomies, explanations, examples) and in the training for the KMS. Part may reside in examining one’s experience. More research is needed on what aspects of KMS and knowledge design have the greatest impact on knowledge absorption.

Third, we need to better understand how knowledge improves performance and what types of knowledge seem to have the greatest impact. This KMS, for example, provided three distinct types of knowledge from three very different sources: product knowledge including competitive analyses from the brand team responsible for marketing; sales process knowledge from the sales management organization; and best practices and tips from the sales representatives themselves. Although these types of knowledge are specific to sales KMS like the one in this study, we believe that there may be more fundamental underlying categories of knowledge that may improve performance in different ways in different situations (e.g., declarative versus procedural; see Alavi and Leidner 2001). Understanding how and why different types of knowledge affect performance may enable us to better design KMS and better focus scarce organizational resources on creating and capturing the most high-value knowledge.

Fourth, whereas we recognize that “no single determinant can explain a very large proportion of the variance in objective sales performance” (Churchill et al. 1985, p. 116), our models explain 26% of the within-individual variation and 46% of the betweenindividual variation. Nonetheless, there are other factors that affect sales representative performance. In our study, performance was measured by the number of prescription drugs actually purchased divided by the quota (i.e., the predicted number expected to be purchased). Many factors not included in our model may affect this measure including: poor tasktechnology fit; lack of proper KMS features; lack of usable knowledge; high opportunity costs; number of patients seeking treatment in a given month; influence that competitors have on a physician prescribing practices; introduction of new drugs; changes in the quota formulas; probability that a patient will actually purchase the drug prescribed by the physician (which may be affected by the wholesale price of the drug, retail pharmacy prices, and insurance rules); and so on. Accounting for these factors and other contingencies that are likely specific to the industry in our study, and more important, the general nature of such factors applicable to other organizations, will greatly improve the precision and accuracy of understanding how individuals derive different value from KMS use.

Fifth, if the use-performance link found in this study does exist elsewhere, especially in similar contexts, then individual performance can be improved by using KMS. In our study, KMS use varied between zero and 239 documents read per month. Understanding user perceptions of the KMS and why users choose to use or not use the KMS would help lead to better KMS and better training programs that could increase KMS use. Whereas the technology acceptance model (Venkatesh 2000, Davis 1989) suggests a number of important factors (e.g., perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use), understanding the antecedents to these factors in the context of KMS would provide additional insight on knowledge use and ultimately performance.

Finally, a better understanding of the knowledge needs of both greater and less experienced knowledge workers could advance the design and use of KMS and knowledge documents. We found that those with more experience who more quickly capitalized on the use of knowledge in this KMS benefited than were those with less experience. This is consistent with prior research which suggests that those with varying experience have different needs (Markus 2001, Nidumolu et al. 2001). Perhaps there is a “fit” between knowledge contents and level of experience—i.e., more concrete and basic concepts for those with less experience and more abstract and advanced concepts for those with more experience (Hinds et al. 2001)—or perhaps the builders of this specific KMS were good at crafting the system and its knowledge to serve those with more experience better. In either case, we need more research to better understand the knowledge needs of both experienced and less experienced who perform similar tasks and are supported by a common KMS.

## Implications for Practice

We believe that this study has five implications for practice. First, an important characteristic unique to KMS is the notion of deriving value by reusing knowledge documents. Contrary to prior research that found that KMS use decreased performance, we found that KMS use improved performance. However, benefits from use appeared only after the users had time to absorb and integrate the new knowledge and had gained skill in applying the knowledge in their context, which may take weeks or months. Very experienced users are the most likely to quickly improve their performance from the new knowledge; less experienced workers will take more time to improve performance. We examined only individual performance, but we speculate that improved individual performance should lead to improved organizational performance (especially in the context of sales).

Second, knowledge workers should understand that it is the contextualization of new knowledge into the workers’ environment and the gradual refinement of its use by practice that leads to the greatest benefits. KMS do not provide “instant gratification”; instead, only by diligent integration of new knowledge into ongoing work practices is performance likely to increase gradually over time. Firms that strive to use codification-based KMS to provide “just in time knowledge” (Davenport and Glaser 2002), such as consulting firms providing new knowledge to meet the immediate needs of proposal writers, are likely to see only modest benefits or even performance reductions the first time the knowledge is used (e.g., Haas and Hansen 2005). Just in time knowledge management is likely to have immediate value only to more experienced workers who are able to more quickly benefit from new knowledge.

Third, though codification-based KMS supports everyone in an organization, they are often intended to support new hires and less experienced workers (Gray and Meister 2006, Hansen et al. 1999); Farmaco’s KMS was originally implemented for this reason. However, our results show that experienced workers gain just as much as less experienced workers—and more quickly. Therefore, in designing KMS initiatives, firms should not overlook the needs of more experienced workers. After all, there is likely to be a faster return on investment from them.

Fourth, for organizations embarking on a KMS implementation initiative or those who already implemented a KMS, careful planning for an appropriate incentive structure may be in order. Unlike other systems where task is performed while using the system, KMS is one step removed. Therefore, results are not likely to be immediate; it takes time before performance benefits are realized. The delayed impact found in this study suggests that managers should evaluate performance benefits after users have had some time to absorb, integrate, and apply acquired knowledge found in the KMS. Therefore, managers need to carefully devise and implement a proper incentive structure and a corresponding set of performance metrics.

Finally, the effect of use over time is about a 10 percentage point increase in R-squared at level one (a medium effect) and a 6.5 percentage point increase at level two (a small effect). The inclusion of experience as a moderator increased the variance explained by 0.1% (a small effect); nonetheless, the model in step 5 is a significantly better fit (deviance, AIC, and BIC) than the model in step 4. The final model (step 5) explains 26% and 46%, respectively. Though this compares very favorably with past research on sales performance that has typically found R-squared values of 3%–9% for the entire model (Churchill et al. 1985), a qualitative interpretation of the practical significance of the small change in effect size must always be made in a given context. The 0.1% change in effect size observed in this study, although statistically significant, should be interpreted with caution when determining practical significance.

In this study, the impact of KMS use in the firm we studied was huge. Farmaco was hoping for a 0.1% increase in sales because of KMS use. Our results show the total impact of use led to an increase in sales after three months of use of about 15% (see Figure 2). They accrued the benefits realizing that “   our first KM initiative was a failure because it was an IT solution” but they learned it ought to be “   75% behavioral change, 20% process, and 5% technology, but that technology better work!” (KM Director, Farmaco). Accordingly, in designing and building KMS, needs, wants, and concerns with field sales representatives ranging from least experience to most experienced were obtained. They concluded that special care should be taken in packaging knowledge so it was accessible to those with less experience as well as the more experienced. Designing systems to make it easier to recognize, absorb, and apply the knowledge in the KMS, and helping users, particularly those with less experience, to perform these functions are key steps in deriving value from the KMS investment. An approach focusing on the needs of both the experienced and less experienced is likely to lead to more widespread individual benefits from use—particularly if a majority of the target users are not those with more experience— which may also lead to greater benefits to the organization as a whole.

## Conclusion

In this study, use of a codification-based KMS improved individual performance, but only after a passage of time. Knowledge workers with more experience derived immediate benefits from KMS use; those with less experience took longer to benefit from KMS use. Those with more experience, with more developed mental models, were able to more quickly adapt and integrate new knowledge, and thus reaped the benefits of the new knowledge much sooner than those with less experience, who took longer to learn how to successfully use it. However, experience played a less significant role over time in leveraging performance gains from KMS use, and knowledge workers with less experience eventually derived similar performance benefits as those of their more experienced counterparts. We hope that these results will hearten proponents and builders of KMS in both academia and industry, and give pause to skeptics that claim that technology-based KMS cannot improve performance (e.g., McDermott 1999). We also hope that these results will trigger new research into the link between KMS use, experience, time, and individual performance.
