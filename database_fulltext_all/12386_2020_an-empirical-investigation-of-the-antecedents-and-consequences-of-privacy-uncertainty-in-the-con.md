# An Empirical Investigation of the Antecedents and Consequences of Privacy Uncertainty in the Context of Mobile Apps

Sameh Al-Natour,<sup>a</sup> Hasan Cavusoglu,<sup>b</sup> Izak Benbasat,<sup>b</sup> Usman Aleem<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. When using digital goods that extensively collect user information, privacy uncertainty, which is consumers’ difficulty in assessing the privacy of the data they entrust to others, is a major concern. We extend the existing literature on uncertainty in online marketplaces by incorporating privacy uncertainty, and we distinguish among three subdimensions of privacy uncertainty—namely collection, use, and protection. We subsequently theorize and empirically test the antecedents and consequences of this new construct in the context of mobile apps. Consistent with economic theory, we argue that because consumers possess less information than the app seller as a result of the presence of hidden characteristics and hidden action, privacy uncertainty is evoked. Using the factorial survey method, we test our theoretical model in the context of buying a mobile app. The results show that privacy uncertainty significantly influences potential users’ intention to use an app above and beyond their uncertainty about the seller and the product. Privacy uncertainty also affects the perceived risk associated with using an app and the price consumers are willing to pay for it. In addition, we find that privacy uncertainty is driven by the uncertainty about privacy practices in regard to the collection, use, and protection of data collected at the time of downloading the app—and more important, the data collected while the app is being used. The results of another factorial survey study suggest that privacy uncertainty is distinct from seller and product uncertainties and has unique drivers. The results of a survey of current users of existing mobile apps indicate that the effects of privacy uncertainty extend to the postadoption stage, where it remains a strong influencer of continued use intentions and perceived risk.

## 1. Introduction

Ever since the internet emerged as an alternative sales channel for products and services, scholars have studied the potential sources of friction in online marketplaces. The lack of face-to-face interaction between buyers and sellers, combined with the sheer number of vendors offering goods online, has generated new types of uncertainties for buyers. Hence, a rich body of literature has focused on understanding and reducing these uncertainties faced by consumers and on helping them assess the quality of the vendors and of the goods they are selling (Ghose 2009, Dimoka et al. 2012).

Consequent to the introduction of new data-driven business models, consumers’ personal information and usage patterns are being continuously captured and used by sellers as consumers search and shop for various products online and use the services available. News portals, social networking sites, online games, mobile applications, and Internet of Things (IoT) solutions are examples of technologies that collect consumer information well beyond the initial transaction. These technologies can also potentially collect information during use that is more specific, sensitive, and identifiable (Sutanto et al. 2013, Almuhimedi et al. 2015), such as location-based information or health and financial data (Xu et al. 2011). We term these technologies and other similar products and services information-collection-laden goods.

The continuous collection of consumer information introduces a new source of friction that concerns the privacy of the collected information. Though most consumers are not fully aware of the extent of the privacy threats to their personal information, they are nevertheless concerned about the privacy of that information (Gu et al. 2017). The providers of information-collection-laden goods can collect more information than what is explicitly disclosed, use information for different purposes than expected, and choose not to safeguard the information collected. Therefore, in this paper, we ask whether consumers consider privacy uncertainty (i.e., difficulty in assessing the privacy of the information they entrust to sellers) when deciding whether to use such goods.

To investigate this question, we focus on mobile apps mainly because of their pervasive use, which has prompted the popular adage, “There is an app for that.” In 2018, there were more than 7 million apps available in app stores<sup>1</sup> that were downloaded approximately 200 billion times, generating over \$365 billion in revenues.<sup>2</sup> Understandably, with this explosion in popularity comes serious incentives for another group of opportunists: entities and individuals seeking to take advantage of the vulnerabilities of mobile apps. This has manifested in the form of privacy breaches, such as those committed by Cambridge Analytica, a political data firm that harvested the information of up to 87 million Facebook users (Bowles 2018) or the case of MyFitnessPal, a popular workout app, where the details of 150 million users were stolen by hackers (Sommerlad 2018).

Apps are almost always used in a networked environment, which leads to a constant transfer of information from consumers to service providers. Although e-commerce platforms selling traditional goods require consumers to part with some personal and financial information, such information is limited to the details specifically requested by the vendors at the time of the transaction. However, vendors can also obtain sensitive information on a regular basis through their mobile apps to an extent that is often unknown to the consumer, essentially turning these apps into spies (Warzel 2019). Although consumers may not wish to disclose such information, the proper functioning of an app may require it. This raises important questions regarding the sufficiency of the “notice and consent” approach promoted by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), which mainly addresses preuse information collection, as an effective way to reduce privacy uncertainty in mobile app contexts. Therefore, we posit that when consumers purchase mobile apps (in return for money or something else), they are faced with uncertainty regarding the privacy of their information, whether that information is collected before or after purchase.

Consumers value their personal information (Xu et al. 2009, 2011; Keith et al. 2012). For instance, in return for their personal information, consumers were shown to request compensation in amounts far greater than the market value of that information (Hann et al. 2007) and exhibit a strong preference for online retailers that better protect consumer data (Tsai et al. 2011).

Furthermore, there has been recent evidence that in instances of a privacy breach, consumers are willing to retaliate and terminate their relationship with the offending firm, such as in the case of the 1.8 million Australians who are reported to have deleted their Facebook accounts in the wake of the Cambridge Analytica incident (Moore 2018) and the 1 of 10 British people who either did the same or stated intentions to do so (Tan 2018).

Although research shows a clear potential for offering safeguards to privacy-inclined users, we find contradictory evidence in the mobile app market, where consumers typically buy cheap and insecure apps (Warzel 2019). Accordingly, a stream of research has examined the behavioral aspects of privacy decision making in mobile contexts (e.g., Acquisti et al. 2017, Adjerid et al. 2018). In essence, it is suggested that making privacy-related decisions is a process in which cognitive heuristics and biases occur (Acquist et al. 2013). By contrast, we contend that mobile app markets’ lack of accommodation for privacy-inclined consumers is caused by the underlying privacy uncertainty in these markets. This uncertainty hinders a consumer’s ability to distinguish a more privacyrespectful app from another, consequently reducing the incentive for sellers to provide privacy-enhanced apps

In summary, whereas privacy uncertainty has been discussed as a potentially important factor that influences online transactions and behaviors, it is yet to be defined comprehensively, and we are yet to understand its impacts fully, especially in relation to other types of uncertainties that exist in online markets. In light of this, our research attempts to fully explicate the role of privacy uncertainty in the context of purchasing and using mobile apps by examining four research questions. We believe that the answers to these questions make novel and interesting contributions to the information systems (IS) literature.

Research Question 1. Is privacy uncertainty a salient and distinct belief in the minds of mobile app consumers?

Research Question 2. What role does privacy uncertainty play relative to other types of uncertainties in influencing consumers’ decision to use an app?

Research Question 3. What are the dimensions of privacy uncertainty, and what are the relative contributions of the uncertainty regarding data collected before and after usage to overall privacy uncertainty?

Research Question 4. Does privacy uncertainty remain as a relevant determinant of use in postadoption contexts?

To investigate these research questions, we conducted several studies and performed a number of analyses Table 1 summarizes these studies and describes how they contribute to answering the four research questions. Specifically, in relation to Research Question 1, the results of a qualitative analysis of potential users’ responses to why they may decide to use or not use an app reveal that privacy uncertainty is a salient consideration in the minds of consumers that can act as an inhibitor of adoption. Also in relation to Research Question 1, the results of a card-sorting exercise and exploratory factor analyses indicate that privacy uncertainty is a distinct construct from other types of uncertainties that are present in mobile app contexts. This conclusion is further confirmed by the results of a factorial study showing that perceptions of privacy uncertainty are only affected by privacy-related signals.

In relation to Research Question 2, the results of the main study reveal that privacy uncertainty affects consumers’ intentions to use an app and the perceived risk associated with its use, as well as the price they are willing to pay for it. Other results from the same study help to answer Research Question 3, where we find that postpurchase privacy uncertainty, especially in regard to the use and protection of data collected during use, plays a more dominant role in heightening consumers privacy uncertainty than does prepurchase information uncertainty.<sup>3</sup> In relation to Research Question 4, the results of a survey of existing app users indicate that the effects of privacy uncertainty extend beyond adoption to influencing consumers’ decisions to continue using an app and the risk associated with that use.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. First, we offer an overview of the nature and drivers of uncertainty in economic exchanges, and we subsequently discuss the types of uncertainties that cause friction in online markets. Drawing on agency theory, we then focus on the antecedents of privacy uncertainty and discuss how hidden characteristics and hidden action contribute to information asymmetry that subsequently gives rise to uncertainty regarding the seller’s privacy practices in relation to the collection, use, and protection of users’ personal information. Anchored in signaling theory, we also describe how the provision of pertinent information can reduce some of the information asymmetry in mobile app markets and consequently alleviate some of the existing privacy uncertainty. We test our theoretical model using a factorial survey of potential app users, as well as an additional survey of existing users, and then present and discuss the results and highlight the study’s contributions.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Uncertainty in Economic Transactions

Scholars in different disciplines have investigated decision making under uncertainty (von Neumann and Morgenstern 1944). Knight (1921) views uncertainty as the limited knowledge regarding future events. In economic transactions, uncertainty is context specific (Acquisti et al. 2015) and concerns information pertinent to the decision at hand that could affect the payoff of those involved (Laffont and Martimort 2002). For instance, in an employment decision, the employer tries to ascertain the eligibility of a candidate. Therefore, uncertainty regarding the qualifications of the candidate plays a role in that decision (Spence 1973). Similarly, in a used-car market, a buyer tries to ascertain the quality of a car. Hence, uncertainty regarding the condition of the car influences the purchase decision (Akerlof 1970).

In exchange relationships, uncertainty results from the lack of pertinent information about any factor that affects the payoffs of those involved in the exchange. Accordingly, in economic theory, uncertainty is said to arise because of asymmetric information—a situation where one party in the transaction has more information than the other in regard to a relevant object of uncertainty (Mishra et al. 1998). This could include uncertainty regarding the good or service being exchanged, the exchange partner, or any other determinant relevant to the exchange. When analyzing the uncertainty concerning an exchange partner, scholars often employ principal–agent theory (Ross 1973, Grossman and Hart 1983), which examines asymmetric information in an economic relationship in which one self-interested entity (i.e., principal) delegates a particular task to another self-interested entity (i.e., agent). In such an arrangement, the agent often has an informational advantage as a result of two types of information asymmetries—namely hidden characteristics and hidden action (Linder and Foss 2015).

The first type, hidden characteristics (often called hidden information), describes a situation in which the principal has less information than the agent regarding the characteristics of the agent (e.g., the candidate’s qualifications). Because hidden characteristics can be present before the agent is hired by the principal, it could lead to what is termed adverse selection, a situation in which the principal enters into an exchange relationship with a less than desired agent (Akerlof 1970), such as hiring an unqualified employee. Hidden action, by contrast, describes a situation in which the principal cannot ascertain the actions taken by the agent or the agent’s intentions. It could refer to whether the agent took, or will take, the right action or whether the action is performed at the level that the principal deems acceptable (Holmstrom¨

1979). Hence, hidden action is a type of information asymmetry that emerges after the exchange relationship is established, and it could lead to what is termed moral hazard, a situation in which the agent alters his or her behavior after the transaction to the detriment of the principal. For example, a driver might begin to drive in a riskier manner after purchasing car insurance, a moral hazard exhibited by the agent (driver) whose incentives might have changed after the contract (Arrow 1971).

Although in its original form principal–agent theory concerns the delegation of tasks by a principal to an agent, it can be applied to contexts involving the exchange of goods and services in buyer–seller relationships (Eisenhardt 1989). In such arrangements, the buyer who is interested in purchasing a good is the principal, and the seller is the agent who knows more about the quality of the good and can make decisions or take actions that affect the buyer (Mishra et al. 1998). If the information asymmetry cannot be resolved, a consumer becomes uncertain about the actual quality of the product as a result of its hidden characteristics (Arrow 1963). Under these conditions, an opportunistic seller may make false claims about his or her goods, causing the buyer to make a suboptimal buying decision—a situation akin to adverse selection (Akerlof 1970). Moreover, when a seller has more information about his or her intentions than does the buyer (hidden action), the buyer becomes uncertain as to whether the seller will take actions to the buyer’s detriment, such as reducing the product’s quality by not servicing it—a situation akin to moral hazard.

## 2.2. Uncertainty in Online Transactions

The effects of information asymmetry are more pronounced in e-commerce (Ghose 2009). Because sellers and buyers do not meet face-to-face, consumers must determine the quality of sellers based on whatever information available to them (Pavlou et al. 2007) Similarly, the proliferation of goods that consumers cannot readily evaluate introduces product uncertainty (Dimoka et al. 2012).

As described earlier, uncertainty could relate to any object relevant to an exchange decision and is caused by the lack of pertinent information about that object’s characteristics and actions. When applied to the context of sellers, hidden characteristics refers to the characteristics of the seller, whereas hidden action refers to their behavior. Consistent with this, Dimoka et al. (2012, p. 399) conceptualize seller uncertainty in the context of e-commerce as a consumer’s “difficulty in assessing the seller’s true characteristics and predicting whether the seller will act opportunistically.” Hence, they not only consider whether the seller is presenting accurate information about himself or herself (hidden characteristics) but also consider the seller’s actions and intentions (hidden action). When applied to the context of products, uncertainty relates to the characteristics of the product and its performance. Dimoka et al. (2012, p. 401) define product uncertainty as the consumer’s “difficulty in assessing the product’s characteristics and predicting how the product will perform in the future.” Hence, they also view product uncertainty as a multidimensional construct that is evoked by perceptions of hidden characteristics and hidden action.

In the mobile app context, seller uncertainty is amplified by the fragmented nature of app markets, where most app sellers (who number in the tens of millions) represent small development houses that lack the institutional trust attributed to major online sellers. Similarly, the vast number of available apps that are often meagerly described, and which can be adequately evaluated only after use, intensifies the consumer’s inability to a priori evaluate the quality of an app. Moreover, unlike situations involving the exchange of physical goods sold online, transactions involving mobile apps are enduring, where interactions between the buyer and the seller are ongoing and do not terminate once the exchange of the good is completed. Hence, uncertainty about the seller, and especially as it relates to the buyer’s future actions, could persist well after the exchange is concluded. Similarly, given their digital nature, mobile apps are continuously updated and maintained, thus resulting in an ongoing product uncertainty.

## 2.3. Privacy Uncertainty in Online and Mobile Contexts

The rise of digital goods, especially informationcollection-laden ones, calls for a deeper understanding of aspects of privacy in e-commerce markets. Privacy can also be an issue for consumers purchasing physical goods from an online seller because the consumer is required to disclose some personal information during the transaction. However, sellers of informationcollection-laden goods can further obtain additional data while the good is in use, such as the customer’s content, location, and usage patterns. This information collected during use has the potential to be more identifiable, more sensitive in nature, and possibly embarrassing to the consumer should it be made public (Chellappa and Sin 2005).

The notion that uncertainty regarding privacy is an important consideration when using mobile devices has found support in the literature. Studies looking at privacy in mobile contexts have applied what is termed a privacy calculus (e.g., Keith et al. 2012), where consumers are proposed to perform a comparison between the loss of privacy and the potential gains from the use of mobile services (Sutanto et al. 2013).

Essentially, the logic of the privacy calculus is similar to that of rational choice theories (Keith et al. 2012, 2013) in that it assumes that consumers base their intentions on rational assessments of benefits and costs. Hence, this stream of research largely overlooks situational and contextual factors associated with privacy (John et al. 2010, Li et al. 2010) and the behavioral aspects of privacy decision making (Acquisti and Grossklags 2007, Acquisti et al. 2017).

Another stream of research has looked at the personalization versus privacy predicament faced by users of information-collection-laden goods when asked to trade access to their information for personalized service (Chellappa and Sin 2005, Sutanto et al. 2013). Some studies have attempted to understand how the design of the technology affects the perceived benefits and costs of personalization. For instance, Xu et al. (2011) showed that the perceived benefits of a personalized service increase and the perceived privacy concerns decrease when the process is overt (i.e., when the process is transparent). Other research has shown that information delivery mechanisms, such as pull (where users explicitly request services) and push (where services are automatically triggered), can influence the extent to which perceptions of privacy risks are evoked, which affects the intentions to participate in the personalized services (Xu et al. 2009). Collectively, this research confirms that the transparency of the process plays an important role in influencing consumers’ perceptions of privacy risks associated with personalized services, highlighting the need for providers of digital services (including mobile apps) to explicitly communicate their privacy practices, especially in relation to information collected after purchase.

Extant research has also recognized the insufficiency of “notice and consent” mechanisms (e.g., Tsai et al. 2011), especially in the context of mobile devices (Balebako et al. 2015). Studies have shown that privacy policies are inefficient tools in managing consumers’ future disclosures (Adjerid et al. 2013), whether as a result of ineffective communication (Jensen and Potts 2004), presentational issues (Adjerid et al. 2014), unawareness (Almuhimedi et al. 2015), or a host of other potential factors (e.g., timing; Adjerid et al. 2013). Similarly, device-based permission managers are ineffective at warning users of potential privacy issues (Felt et al. 2012). Whether it is due to a lack of information (information asymmetry) and the resulting uncertainty (Acquisti and Grossklags 2012) or to the effects of contextual factors (John et al. 2010), users struggle with evaluating privacy ramifications that may arise while using mobile services. Hence, researchers have looked at alternative methods for communicating relevant privacy information, such as privacy nudges (Acquisti et al. 2017), which can empower users to more effectively control their privacy (Almuhimedi et al. 2015).

In summary, extant research highlights the difficulties users face in ascertaining the privacy of the information they entrust to information-collectionladen goods. Consistent with our earlier discussion of the nature of uncertainty in economic exchanges, privacy hence becomes an object of uncertainty that affects exchange decisions associated with informationcollection-laden goods. Similar to the case of sellers and products, privacy uncertainty is evoked by the lack of complete information regarding the nature of the information being collected (hidden characteristics) and what is done with it (hidden action).

A closely related concept is privacy risk, or an individual’s calculation of the likelihood of negative outcomes from sharing information (Featherman and Pavlou 2003). Whereas privacy risk is associated with specific consequences, privacy uncertainty relates to consumers’ inability to evaluate privacy risk as a result of imperfect information, and hence the two concepts should not be conflated (see Acquisti and Grossklags (2007) for a discussion about the distinction between uncertainty and risk). As Dimoka et al. (2012, p. 399) suggest, “Uncertainty differs from risk. While both uncertainty and risk deal with partial information, uncertainty deals with subjective probabilities, whereas risk is estimated with a priori calculable probabilities. We focus on uncertainty (as opposed to risk) because transactions in online markets do not come with objective calculable probabilities.” In other words, when considering privacy uncertainty, we are not concerned with consumers’ assessment of risk but rather with whether they can make an accurate assessment of the privacy of their information (Acquisti and Grossklags 2007).

## 2.4. Alleviating Uncertainty via Signaling

Agency problems, jointly caused by conflicting interests and information asymmetry, can be tackled by signals as well as designing contracts that efficiently allocate risk between the agent and the principal and incentivize the agent (Darrough and Stoughton 1986). Given that in most online purchasing contexts, a prospective buyer (i.e., principal) cannot design an individualized contract with the seller, signaling by the seller (i.e., agent) is the only plausible solution to alleviate uncertainty in commercial exchanges.

Signaling theory aims at reducing information asymmetry between the principal and the agent (Spence 2002). When the principal lacks information about the quality of the agent (i.e., when the hidden characteristic problem exists), the agent could reveal more information about his or her characteristics to the principal (i.e., signaling). For instance, in an employment context, acquiring educational credentials is a signal that the prospective candidate (i.e., agent) sends to the employer (i.e., principal) in order to credibly convey his or her ability levels (Spence 1973). In e-commerce settings, this is achieved by means that convey information about the seller’s quality, such as seller rating mechanisms (Dellarocas 2003).

When the agent’s actions are not observable by the principal (i.e., when the hidden action problem exists), the principal could design a contract that incentivizes the agent to behave more congruently with the principal’s interests (Winter 2000). However, in the absence of a contract, the agent can use informational signals, termed intent signals, to convey his or her intentions regarding future actions (Connelly et al. 2011), such as a job candidate’s declaration that he or she intends to conform to the company’s culture.

Signals can also allow sellers to convey information about the quality of their products. Because sometimes it is difficult to obtain information about product attributes before purchase (Mitra et al. 1999), providing additional information regarding those attributes reduces a consumer’s uncertainty (Crocker 1986). In online markets, this could be accomplished by offering detailed product descriptions. Signals can also be used for products whose quality attributes cannot be ascertained before purchase (Kirmani and Rao 2000). In online markets, this could be accomplished by offering diagnostic product descriptions (Dimoka et al. 2012). For attributes that are hard to verify even after purchase, information about other indicators of those attributes can be provided (Crocker 1986). For instance, a manufacturer can offer an extended warranty on its product, signaling the continued quality of its product and also its commitment to service that product (Boulding and Kirmani 1993). In e-commerce, this could be accomplished by offering third-party assurances (Dimoka et al. 2012).

In the context of mobile apps, signals can be used to reduce the information asymmetry driving the various types of uncertainties. For instance, sellers can use signals to convey information about themselves and intent signals to indicate their future action. Similarly, they can provide information about their apps (e.g., screenshots), as well signals concerning the performance of those apps and assurances concerning future updates. Mobile app sellers can also signal how they manage consumer privacy by disclosing the type of information that is collected (reduce hidden characteristics) and by providing information related to their information practices (reduce hidden action). Such signals can be explicit or address relevant indicators. For example, providing information such as “it would take $3 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 1 }$ years to crack the 256-bit AES [Advanced Encryption Standard] encryption used to store your information” can serve as an indicator of the quality of the information security procedures in place and help reduce the consumer’s uncertainty.

## 3. Research Model and Hypotheses 3.1. Overview of the Proposed Model

The proposed research model is depicted in Figure 1. From our earlier discussion of agency and signaling theories, we developed a “signal → information asymmetry → uncertainty → decision” framework to guide our research model. The app-purchasing context satisfies two main conditions of agency theory: (1) conflicting objectives between the principal and the agent and (2) differences in the information they possess (Laffont and Martimort 2002). A prospective buyer is the principal who is interested in an app, and the app developer (or seller) is the agent who creates the app and maintains it.

Consumers making the decision to use an app do not know all the benefits and costs associated with using the app. This leads to the evocation of Knightian uncertainty (Knight 1921), where buyers do not know all the eventualities associated with their decision, which may concern any number of relevant factors pertinent to that decision. This uncertainty can exist during both the pre- and postpurchase phases. For instance, consumers may have uncertainty about the quality of the app before purchase, but they may also continue to have uncertainty about its performance after purchase. Hence, we characterize uncertainty in app transactions as a combination of pre- and postpurchase uncertainties.

In our model, we use the intention to use an app to represent the purchase decision. Drawing on the literature showing that uncertainty in economic transactions has a negative influence on the outcomes of those transactions, we posit that uncertainties reduce the purchase intention of prospective app buyers. In the app context, we argue that privacy uncertainty is salient along with the other uncertainties that were identified in the e-commerce literature (seller and prod uct uncertainties). Because our focus is to explicate pri vacy uncertainty, we characterize privacy uncertainty to be formed by uncertainties pertaining to three privacyrelevant information practices—namely collection, use, and protection. Because uncertainty is driven by asymmetric information (Acquisti et al. 2015), we argue that information asymmetries regarding privacyrelated information practices—namely hidden characteristic, hidden use action, and hidden protection action— drive privacy uncertainties. Drawing on signaling theory, we argue that absence of information about privacy-related information practices causes these asymmetries.

## 3.2. Seller and Product Uncertainty

A consumer contemplating buying an app confronts both product and seller uncertainties because the seller who develops the app knows more about himself or herself and his or her app than does the buyer. This difference in knowledge leads to information asymmetry, which, in turn, creates uncertainty for the buyer (Acquisti and Grossklags 2012). Dimoka et al. (2012) theorized seller uncertainty as relating to the seller’s true characteristics and whether the seller will act opportunistically. Seller uncertainty in the context of mobile apps relates to how the app seller presents himself or herself and whether the app seller will deliver what he or she has promised. Therefore, prepurchase seller uncertainty is defined as the consumer’s difficulty, before the app is downloaded, in assessing the seller’s true characteristics and predicting whether the seller will act opportunistically. However, mobile app buyers will likely resolve the uncertainty regarding the seller’s characteristics after downloading the app. Yet the buyer will remain concerned about whether the seller will act opportunistically as the buyer continues to use the app. Hence, postpurchase seller uncertainty is defined as the consumer’s difficulty, after the app has been downloaded, in assessing whether the seller will follow on his or her future promises and perform his or her future duties.

Before the purchase, a buyer needs to discern an app’s features to assess its quality and predict its performance. Consequently, we define prepurchase product uncertainty as the consumer’s difficulty, before the app is downloaded, in assessing an app’s characteristics and predicting how the app will perform. However, the buyer will resolve the uncertainty regarding the characteristics of the app after he or she downloads it but will remain concerned about whether the app will perform as expected in the future. Therefore, postpurchase product uncertainty is defined as the consumer’s difficulty, after the app has been downloaded, in assessing how the app will continue to perform in the future.

In economic transactions, the more difficult it is to obtain information, the more uncertainty the buyer will face. When there is uncertainty, the consumer’s perceived value, which is the consumer’s overall assessment of the utility of a product based on his or her perceptions (Kirmani and Rao 2000), will be low. Hence, when a consumer perceives less value, he or she will have a lower intention to conduct the transaction or even defer the purchase (Davidson 1991) because individuals choose an option with less uncertainty over the one with more uncertainty (Ellsberg 1961). For example, the uncertainty regarding the perceived value of a given product affects a consumer’s belief about the overall utility he or she will see from the purchase (Bhatnagar et al. 2000). Similarly, when studying the role of various types of perceived risk in e-commerce transactions, previous research has found that adoption is adversely affected by performance-based risk perceptions (Featherman and Pavlou 2003). Drawing on such findings, as well as others (e.g., Pavlou et al. 2007, Dimoka et al. 2012), we propose the following.

Hypothesis 1. Seller uncertainty reduces the intention to use a mobile app.

Hypothesis 2. Product uncertainty reduces the intention to use a mobile app.

## 3.3. Privacy Uncertainty

The use of mobile apps necessitates the ongoing interaction between the consumer and the seller and the continuous transfer of information from the consumer to the seller. Because most apps are free or sold for a modest price, consumers often “pay” for these apps by sharing their personal information and usage patterns, which can be used to develop consumer profiles and offer personalized advertisements (Xu et al. 2011). Thus, the collection of information regarding consumers’ behaviors becomes a part of an ongoing transaction that only terminates when the app is removed from the mobile device.

Consequently, when consumers consider buying apps, they are interested in determining how the seller will use their personal information over time. Apps can require consumers to disclose personal information, such as phone number and gender, or can collect information directly from the device, such as contact lists and location (Felt et al. 2012). Often such information is automatically shared with the seller, sometimes without the consumer’s knowledge (Almuhimedi et al. 2015). In short, consumers confront a predicament regarding the privacy of their information when they download an app and consequently experience privacy uncertainty in the absence of complete information (Acquisti and Grossklags 2012).

Conforming to Knightian uncertainty (Knight 1921) privacy uncertainty is driven by the inability of consumers to predict the future state of their privacy, and it can be defined as the consumer’s difficulty in assessing how the privacy of his or her information is maintained when it is disclosed to the app or the app seller. In essence, transactions involving information-collection-laden goods do not only involve the exchange of the good itself but also of the information needed to complete the transaction (prepurchase information) and that necessary for the use of the good (postpurchase information). Information asymmetry regarding the nature of the information collected (hidden characteristics) and the relevant actions in its regard (hidden action) can evoke uncertainty regarding the privacy of that information, and this leads to situations of adverse selection (downloading/using an app that exploits persona information) and moral hazard (where actions that undermine the privacy of the information are taken).

We argue that privacy uncertainty is distinct from seller uncertainty and product uncertainty. Earlier research has not distinguished privacy uncertainty from other types of uncertainties as a result of the physical nature of the products considered in those studies, which do not entail the continuous sharing of information with the seller. However, because privacy is a concern for app buyers, privacy uncertainty should be treated independently. Furthermore, privacy uncertainty should not be conflated with either product uncertainty or seller uncertainty because it may not necessarily move in the same direction as the other two. For instance, whereas most people can trust that Facebook has not misrepresented itself (hence, low seller uncertainty), recent evidence suggests that many Facebook users are uncertain about their privacy.

It is important to note that our conceptualization of uncertainty (be it privacy, seller, or product) focuses on the nature of that uncertainty (or the object of uncertainty) rather than its source. This is an important distinction because in the case of privacy uncertainty, the source of uncertainty can be the seller but also can extend well beyond the seller to a set of many potential sources (e.g., third-party vendors or the app store). As a result, in our conceptualization of privacy uncertainty, we focus on the content of this uncertainty (e.g., “information collected” when measuring privacy regarding collection or “how information is used” when measuring uncertainty regarding use).

Privacy uncertainty affects the degree to which consumers can predict the state of the privacy of their information in the future. This future state could include various levels of privacy breaches, such as information being sold to a third party (leading to spamming or unwanted behavioral advertisements) or the server being hacked by malicious individuals (leading to identity theft). Because consumers tend to overestimate losses (Kahneman and Tversky 1979), a high level of uncertainty will cause them to predict a high level of privacy failure. In other words, when uncertainty is high, buying an app is perceived to lead to a future state that is potentially harmful to the consumer (Pavlou et al. 2007). In such a scenario, an uncertain consumer will refrain from using the mobile app. Hence, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 3. Privacy uncertainty reduces the intention to use a mobile app.

## 3.4. Dimensions of Privacy Uncertainty

Privacy is often defined as the ability of an individual to manage information about himself or herself (Belanger et al. 2002). Control of information disclosure and control over unwanted intrusions are key mechanisms by which privacy is managed by individuals (Goodwin 1991). As individuals interact with organizations using their personal information, individuals become concerned about the organizations information practices. Although there exist various categorization schemes, information practices are proposed to fall into three broad categories: collection, use, and protection (Malhotra et al. 2004). These dimensions are consistently covered in the privacy literature (e.g., Smith et al. 1996) and promoted by the FTC as the essential elements that sellers must consider if they wish to offer enhanced privacy to their consumers. In fact, the California Consumer Protection Act of 2018 specifically regulates privacy implications surrounding the collection, use, and protection of personal information.<sup>4</sup>

In this study, we focus on these three dimensions of privacy uncertainty because they address not only what information is being collected (characteristics) but also how organizations treat that information (action). Hence, in mobile app contexts, privacy uncertainty emerges as consumers have difficulty in understanding the privacy implications of these three information practices (refer to Appendix A in the online supplement for an analysis of these areas of concern). This is consistent with Almuhimedi et al. (2015, p. 788), who suggest that “in privacy contexts, asymmetric or incomplete information refers to a disparity between users’ and service providers’ knowledge of collection, use, sharing practices, potential consequences, and available protections concerning users’ personal information.”

We propose that for mobile apps, privacy uncertainty occurs because the buyer may not know what personal information is collected, and the buyer may be unaware of how the data will be used or if it wil be protected. For instance, the app seller may decide to use the data in a way that is not disclosed or by passing the data on to information aggregators. In addition, there is a chance of the app seller’s servers being hacked by a malicious entity, resulting in the buyer’s personal information being stolen. If a consumer is uncertain about these practices (i.e., collection, use, and protection), then the consumer cannot assess how his or her privacy is maintained. Therefore, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 4. Uncertainty regarding (a) collection, (b) use, and (c) protection positively contributes to privacy uncertainty.

Similar to seller uncertainty and product uncertainty, dimensions of privacy uncertainty are perceived differently depending on whether they concern pre- or postpurchase information. Therefore, we argue that each dimension of privacy uncertainty is constituted by its corresponding before and after subdimensions, as defined in Table 2.

We also hypothesize on the relative effects of preand postpurchase perceptions of privacy uncertainty. Given that consumers are accustomed to provide information, including personally identifiable information, during e-commerce transactions (Acquisti and Grossklags 2012), consumers’ uncertainty regarding the sharing of prepurchase information is likely to be lower. Hence, even if the app seller does not specify the information that is collected at the time of the transaction, a user can infer the nature of such information based on prior experience. By contrast, and as discussed earlier, one distinguishing characteristic of mobile apps is that they continue to collect, often covertly, information after the transaction is concluded. Hence, uncertainty regarding the collection of such information is likely to be more pronounced and exert a stronger influence on the intentions to use the app.

Similarly, in contrast to the information collected during the transaction, information collected during app use can potentially be more specific, sensitive, and identifiable (Sutanto et al. 2013). Given the more sensitive and specific nature of that information, consumers would be more concerned about whether such information will be used for other purposes or be shared with others. Hence, their uncertainty regarding the use of postpurchase information should more significantly affect their intentions. Likewise, the more specific and sensitive nature of the information that is collected during use makes concerns regarding its safety more salient. Therefore, any uncertainty regarding how that information is protected will exert a stronger effect on the intentions compared with information collected during the transaction. Finally, the continuous rate at which this information is transmitted to the app seller could further evoke uncertainty regarding its security during transmission, further contributing to the prominence of postpurchase uncertainty regarding protection. Therefore, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 5. Uncertainty regarding the collection, use, and protection of postpurchase information contributes more to privacy uncertainty than uncertainty regarding prepurchase information.<sup>5</sup>

3.5. Asymmetries Regarding Information Practices Uncertainty arises in the presence of information asymmetry regarding the hidden characteristics and the hidden actions of an object of uncertainty that is relevant to the principal’s decision making (Knight 1921, Akerlof 1970, Mishra et al. 1998). If the information asymmetry cannot be resolved, consumers will become uncertain (Arrow 1963). For mobile apps, a consumer is uncertain about the information practices as they relate to the collection, use, and protection of personal information. Therefore, consistent with the literature, we conceptualize hidden characteristics, hidden use action, and hidden protection action as the different types of information asymmetries that evoke uncertainty regarding the app seller’s information practices. Whereas hidden characteristics concern the characteristics of the information being collected, hidden use and protection actions concern the seller’s actions and intentions regarding the use and protection of the consumer’s information (constructs are defined in Table A1 in the online supplement). These information asymmetries cause consumers to wonder about the privacy of their information because they are unsure whether the seller is not being transparent because of negligence or malicious intent. Hence, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 6. Perceived (a) hidden characteristics, (b) hidden use action, and (c) hidden protection action influence privacy uncertainty regarding the (a) collection, (b) use, and (c) protection, respectively.

Because information asymmetry causes fear of seller opportunism, the consumer may feel that given his or her inability to determine the seller’s actions, the seller may collect more information than what is initially stated. Therefore, the presence of information asymmetry regarding how the seller will use consumers information will induce privacy uncertainty regarding collection. Similarly, because information asymmetry regarding protection creates a perception that the seller may not be diligent enough in protecting consumer information, it also evokes questions concerning the app seller’s diligence in determining the right level of use for consumer information. That is, the consumer may feel that given his or her inability to determine the seller’s actions, the seller may use more information than what the consumer is initially told. Therefore, we also contend that having no information about how the seller will protect consumers’ information will induce privacy uncertainty regarding use.

Hypothesis 7. (a) Hidden use action influences privacy uncertainty regarding collection. (b) Hidden protection action influences privacy uncertainty regarding use.

## 3.6. Signaling Information Practices

An agent, in a principal–agent setting, can alleviate information asymmetry by providing signals (Spence 1973). In relation to mobile app privacy, these signals should be targeted to reduce the information asymmetry that exists regarding information practices, i.e., what information the app collects, how the information collected is used, and how it is protected. The effectiveness of such signals is largely influenced by the extent to which the signals directly address the unobservable quality (termed signal strength or fit)— that is, the specific information-collection practice (Connelly et al. 2011). In the absence of such signals, perceptions of information asymmetry are heightened. Hence, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 8. A consumer’s perception of (a) hidden characteristics, (b) hidden use action, and (c) hidden protection action will be heightened in the absence of information about (a) what information is collected, (b) how it is used, and (c) how it is protected, respectively.

A lack of information about data collection can also trigger information asymmetry regarding a more abstract information practice, such as use. Because information asymmetry causes fear of opportunism, the consumer may feel that given his or her inability to determine what information is being collected, the seller has the incentive and the ability to misuse the collected information that is unknown to the user. In essence, not knowing what information is being collected makes it difficult to ascertain how any such information would be used, even if the app seller communicates some details about possible uses. By contrast, providing information regarding the specific data collected creates a context in which the consumer can interpret any supplemental information about use. If details about information use are not communicated, then the information about collection can allow the consumer to infer some aspects of use.

Similarly, a lack of information about data use could trigger asymmetry about the more abstract information practice of protection. Mobile app users expect that apps will use some of the personal information collected. However, when the app is not forthcoming about such use, users will question the app seller’s intent. Once the app seller’s use intent is questioned, it follows that the seller’s intent to protect the information collected will also be questioned Furthermore, providing information about use can endow the consumers with an understanding of how their data will be treated by the app seller. For example, by indicating that he or she will not share the data with others, an app seller also signals that he or she will protect the data from unauthorized access that can be a result of a privacy breach. Hence, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 9. (a) Hidden use action will be heightened in the absence of information about what information is collected. (b) Hidden protection action will be heightened in the absence of information about how the collected information is used.

## 3.7. Control Variables

The research model includes a number of control variables. Two of the control variables are derived from the model developed by Pavlou et al. (2007) to study the effects of uncertainty on online purchase intentions. The first is the variable privacy concerns, which captures an individual’s general disposition toward the provision of personal information to external entities (Malhotra et al. 2004). Given that the decision to use an app necessitates the provision of personal information, an individual’s privacy concerns can affect his or her intention to use an app. As a result, this variable is included in the model as a controlling variable so that we can estimate the effects of the uncertainty variables on the intentions above and beyond the inhibiting effects of one’s privacy concerns. The second control variable is trust, which can act as an uncertainty mitigator (Pavlou et al. 2007) and play an important role in determining one’s risktaking behavior (Malhotra et al. 2004). Hence, trust can affect the consumer’s willingness to be vulnerable, which accompanies downloading and using the app.

Given that mobile apps are developed to serve specific purposes, potential consumers are likely to exhibit different levels of interest in mobile apps and have varying levels of familiarity and experience with similar apps. Therefore, we also control for consumer interest in mobile apps in general and their experience with the specific category of apps. Given that the dependent measure of concern is the intention to download an app, it is important that the results are not confounded by the fact that some may not be interested in mobile apps altogether and therefore are not potential users. Furthermore, research has shown that low product involvement and interest can result in reduced search effort (Beatty and Smith 1987), which could affect how the consumer processes the information available on the app page. Hence, we measure subjects’ interest in mobile apps and use it as a controlling variable to clearly identify the relationship between the different types of uncertainty and the intentions to use an app.

Similarly, familiarity and experience with similar apps can facilitate domain knowledge and serve to create a conceptual benchmark against which the new app is compared. Furthermore, experience with similar products has been shown to affect the processing of available information in new purchasing contexts (Bettman and Park 1980), and product familiarity can lead to increased learning during purchase decisions (Johnson and Russo 1984). Hence, prior experience can potentially introduce a number of other external factors that affect consumers’ intentions to use the new app.

## 4. Method

To test our hypotheses, we employed the factorial survey method. It is a specialized version of the scenario method that applies an experimental orientation to the scenario approach (Rossi and Anderson 1982), and it has been used extensively in sociology research (Wallander 2009) and, more recently, successfully adopted by IS scholars (Vance et al. 2015). As a hybrid of the scenario and experimental methods, the factorial survey method is enriched by the qualities of both methodologies. Although the scenario method provides the richness of developing contextual situations, the factorial method allows us to manipulate the dimensions of theoretical interest. In essence, we can build a full factorial design that encompasses all the combinations of each dimension at its different levels, thereby giving us different treatment groups, ensuring an orthogonal design and reducing multicollinearity (Jasso 2006).

## 4.1. Scenario Design

To enhance ecological validity, it was important that subjects make their decisions in a regular mobile app purchase setting. Although the most realistic setting would be the actual platforms where mobile apps are sold, those platforms are neither available for research nor flexible enough to measure subjective responses. Thus, we developed a realistic mobile app–buying scenario by replicating the online app store experience. Specifically, we created two profiles for two fictitious apps that resemble app pages on a typical app store. The first app was a wellness app that helps users keep track of their diet and exercise. The second app is a personal finance app that helps users keep track of their spending, savings, and budgets. We used wellness and personal finance apps because they represent two of the most popular app categories and because of their continuous collection of users’ personal information.

The profile of each app consisted of all the relevant information potentially used to make an app-buying decision. It included (1) a logo for the app, which was designed specifically for this study; (2) information about the app’s features, which were integrated from multiple leading similar existing apps; (3) screenshots from the app, which were specifically designed for this study, highlighting some of its features; (4) privacy information, which constituted the treatment and was integrated from the privacy policies of existing leading apps; and (5) general information about the app, such as the app seller, device compatibility, and inapp purchases. (Figures A1 and A2 in Appendix A in the online supplement show the full app pages.)

Because we are interested in privacy-related information asymmetry and its impact on privacy uncertainty, we focused solely on this aspect. As discussed previously, there are three types of privacyrelated information practices of concern: information collection, use, and protection. Details on each of these practices (signal) can be either present or absent from the app-buying page. Consequently, we formed eight groups based on a 2 (collection) × 2 (use) × 2 (protection) combination of the presence (or absence) of these privacy information types. Figure A3 in Appendix A in the online supplement highlights the privacy section of the app page, which was manipulated for the study. The same section for each group had different privacy information depending on its treatment condition, whereas the rest of the profile remained the same.

## 4.2. Study Procedure

Although our research focuses on the effects of privacyrelated signals on privacy uncertainty, we did not wish to prime our subjects to the privacy context. Therefore, we informed subjects that we are an independent marketing company acting on behalf of WellnessMe (the fictional wellness app) and MyMoneyMia (the fictional personal finance app) and that we were seeking their help in evaluating the app to make sure that it is ready for launch. The instructions also informed subjects that they are expected to read all the information contained on the app page to be able to answer some questions later.

After the introduction, subjects were randomly assigned to one of eight treatments, which were followed by the questionnaire. The treatment app pages were embedded within Qualtrics, which was used for data collection. After carefully inspecting the app page, subjects answered an attention question that specifically asked them to select, out of nine possible answers, all the informational items that were contained on the app page. Their answers were validated against the treatment to which they were exposed. Subjects who answered incorrectly were shown the app page again and were allowed a second attempt, or they were otherwise screened out. To pass the attention question, a subject needed to correctly select all the informational items that his or her specific treatment page contained.

To reduce the effect of confirmation bias, we measured willingness to buy before the respondents rated the uncertainty measures. Furthermore, because the scenario page did not contain pricing information about the app, we also asked subjects about the price they would be willing to pay for the app. Subjects were subsequently asked in the form of an openended question to indicate any reasons they had for why they might or might not want to use the app. This was intended to help us ascertain whether privacy uncertainty is a salient belief when the consumer is exposed to a new mobile app (Research Question 1).

After capturing their intentions, subjects answered three Likert-type items that captured the extent to which they believed the app page contained information about the three privacy practices. These acted as manipulation checks to verify whether we were successful in signaling the intended privacy information. Subjects subsequently answered questions concerning their perceptions about information asymmetry, followed by prepurchase uncertainty and postpurchase uncertainty.<sup>6</sup> These were followed by items capturing their trust in mobile app sellers, privacy concerns as they relate to mobile apps, interest in mobile apps, experience with similar apps, items relating to the marker variable, and demographics. After completing the questionnaire, subjects were debriefed about the real purpose of the study.

## 4.3. Measures and Item Development

All constructs were measured using multi-item Likerttype scales ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree (listed in Table A1 in Appendix A in the online supplement). When possible, measurement items were adapted from scales that have been validated in earlier studies. The scales measuring product uncertainty, both before and after purchase, were adapted from Dimoka et al. (2012) after the items were modified to fit the mobile app context. Specifically, wording changes were made so that the items refer to mobile apps and mobile app sellers because their original context was car auctions. In the prepurchase scale, items addressing both adverse seller selection and seller moral hazard were used, whereas in the postpurchase scale, only the items for seller moral hazard were adapted. Similarly, the product uncertainty scales were adapted from

Dimoka et al. (2012). In the prepurchase scale, we included items that addressed both description and performance uncertainty. Because only performance uncertainty is relevant in the postpurchase stage, only the items addressing that dimension were included to measure postpurchase product uncertainty.

Scales measuring privacy concerns and trust were adapted from Malhotra et al. (2004). Minor wording changes were made to the items so that they refer to mobile app sellers rather than online companies. A new scale was also developed to measure the intention to use the app. The scale was modeled after similar measures of the intention-to-use decision aids (Al-Natour et al. 2011). Finally, two new scales were developed to measure interest in mobile apps and experience with similar apps.

Because they are relatively new in the literature, we gave careful attention to the development of the measures of privacy uncertainty and information asymmetry. As a first step, we used the definitions of each construct to generate a preliminary list of items for each of the three dimensions of information asym metry, the six dimensions of pre- and postpurchase privacy uncertainty, and an overall measure of privacy uncertainty. These measures were modeled after the seller and product uncertainty scales developed by Dimoka et al. (2012) and used similar phrasing. They were carefully inspected by the research team over a number of weeks to ensure face and content validity

To support construct and discriminant validity, we conducted a card-sorting exercise with the assistance of eight judges who are IS graduate students (Moore and Benbasat 1991). Each judge was presented with the definitions for all information asymmetry and uncertainty constructs, as well as a randomly sorted list of all the items used to measure those constructs. The judges were instructed to assign each item to one of the categories representing the constructs or to an “other” category if they were unsure of the best placement. This resulted in an average “hit ratio” of 92% and an average Fleiss’ kappa (Fleiss and Cohen 1973) of 0.86 (p < 0.01). The kappa for individual categories ranged from 0.94 for hidden characteristics and hidden protection action to 0.77 for postpurchase use and protection uncertainty. These results indicate an almost perfect interrater agreement (Boudreau et al. 2001) and suggest that all asymmetry and uncertainty constructs are discriminable.

## 4.4. Sample

The sample for this study consisted of 801 subjects, with 395 being exposed to the health app and 406 to the personal finance app. The study was conducted online using participants recruited from two online market research companies: Amazon Mechanical

Turk (M Turk) and The Sample Network (http:// thesamplenetwork.com/). The M Turk sample was drawn from a subset of M Turkers called masters. These are individuals who have completed a large number of M Turk work tasks and who maintain at least 99% approval. To ensure that the respondents were located in North America, we also inspected the geographic location of their latitudes and longitudes as recorded by Qualtrics. The Sample Network offers online market research solutions to clients desiring high-quality data at prices that are higher than M Turk or similar others. The relatively large incentives provided to panel members often guarantee more considerate responses. Using both samples allowed us to compare the quality of the responses between M Turk and another, more reliable source. Subsequent analysis indicated that there were no significant differences between the samples, and hence the pooled data set was used for the analysis.

Among the participants, 51.3% were male, with an age distribution that is similar to the population of mobile app users. All participants owned a smartphone (iPhone = 39.0%; Samsung = 36.4%; Google = 8.7%; and other = 15.9%). The characteristics of the subjects (summarized in Table A2 in Appendix A in the online supplement) did not differ across the eight treatment groups.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Analysis of the Qualitative Responses

A main objective of this research is to investigate whether privacy uncertainty is a salient concept in app consumers’ minds (Research Question 1). As a part of the questionnaire, subjects were asked to indicate any reasons they have for why they may or may not want to use the app. To analyze these open-ended responses, we first examined a sample of the responses to generate a list of potential reasons. We then categorized those reasons into four main categories for adopting the app (app-specific, personal reasons, privacy, and other) and five categories for not adopting the app (app-specific, personal reasons, privacy, alternatives, and other). Each of these main categories had multiple subcategories. Two independent coders were subsequently asked to read the open-ended responses and map them to one or more of the subcategories. The two coders had a substantial level of agreement (>71%).

The results are summarized in Table D1 in Appendix D in the online supplement. They indicate that although the most recurrent reasons for adopting the app concern its characteristics (accounting for 370 of the 600 reasons provided for using the app), the most commonly cited reasons for not intending to use the app concern privacy-related uncertainty and associated concerns (accounting for almost 50%, or 516 of the 1,023 reasons provided for not using the app). This indicates that although the app’s characteristics can induce adoption, uncertainty regarding privacy can inhibit it. As highlighted in Table D2 in Appendix D in the online supplement, subjects’ privacy uncertainty extends beyond being general in nature and addresses specific uncertainties about the collection, use, and protection of users’ data. Furthermore, in expressing uncertainty about privacy issues, subjects tend to address multiple dimensions at the same time. The results also attest to the importance of the uncertainty about the seller, especially in the case of the personal finance app. This is understandable, considering the significantly larger benefits that can be obtained by the app developer from abusing financial information compared with the kind of information collected by a wellness app. Overall, the analysis of the open-ended responses substantiates the salience of privacy uncertainty as an influencer of consumers’ app adoption.

## 5.2. Measurement Mode

Given that we collected data regarding two apps, we performed all the analyses of the measurement and the structural models separately for each app, as well as the pooled data set. Considering that all the results were similar for the data generated for both apps, in this section we report on the analysis using the pooled data set; some of the results for the two separate subsamples are included in Appendix B in the online supplement.

To support individual item reliability, we examined the loadings of the individual measurement items on their intended constructs (reported in Table A1 in Appendix A in the online supplement). The loadings for all items on their intended constructs exceed the recommended tolerance of 0.70 (Barclay et al. 1995). Table A3 (in Appendix A in the online supplement) depicts the bivariate correlations between the model’s constructs, whereas the diagonal elements, also provided in Table A4 (in Appendix A in the online supplement), represent the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE). A rule for assessing discriminant validity requires that the square root of AVE be larger than the correlations between the constructs (Barclay et al. 1995). As indicated by the results, this criterion is also met.

Another criterion for adequate discriminant validity requires that the loadings of indicators on their respective latent variables are higher than the load ings of other indicators on these variables and the loadings of these indicators on other latent variables. Considering the large number of items used in this study, we performed a number of exploratory factor analyses using a Promax rotation to discriminate between potentially related constructs. This is consistent with the approach followed in Dimoka et al. (2012). First, we explored whether the different dimensions of information asymmetry, the different dimensions of prepurchase privacy uncertainty, and the different dimensions of postpurchase privacy uncertainty are distinct. The results in Table A5 in Appendix A in the online supplement suggest that hidden characteristics, hidden use action, and hidden protection action are distinct and discriminable constructs. Similarly, the results confirm that prepurchase uncertainty perceptions regarding collection, use, and protection are distinct. Finally, the results suggest that the postpurchase privacy uncertainty dimensions of collection, use, and protection are also distinct.

Second, we performed three additional exploratory factor analyses to explore whether the same information practice—for example, collection—is distinct when it is measured as a dimension of information asymmetry, a dimension of prepurchase uncertainty, or a dimension of postpurchase uncertainty. The results in Table A6 in Appendix A in the online supplement confirm that hidden characteristics, prepurchase collection uncertainty, and postpurchase collection uncertainty are distinctive constructs. Similarly, the results suggest that hidden use action, prepurchase use uncertainty, and postpurchase use uncertainty are distinct constructs. Finally, the results indicate that hidden protection action, prepurchase protection uncertainty, and postpurchase protection uncertainty are also distinct and discriminable.

A third exploratory factor analysis was performed to confirm that the seller, product, and privacy uncertainties are distinct constructs in both the pre- and postpurchase stages. The results of this analysis, included in Tables A7 and A8 in Appendix A in the online supplement, confirm that overall privacy uncertainty is a distinct construct from pre- and postpurchase seller and product uncertainties. The results further suggest that prepurchase seller and prepurchase product uncertainties are unitary constructs (Dimoka et al. 2012). Composite reliability and Cronbach’s alpha are also reported in Table A4 in Appendix A in the online supplement. Because the values for these are all above 0.70 (Fornell and Larcker 1981), the internal consistency criteria are also met.

To address the potential concern of common-method bias (CMB), we first conducted an exploratory factor analysis and applied the Harman (1967) one-factor extraction test. Using a principal–component analysis for all items of the 19 latent variables measured in the study, we found 12 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, collectively accounting for 77% of the total variance. The first factor accounted for only 35% of the total variance, indicating a lack of a substantial CMB.

Second, we examined the potential for CMB using the marker-variable technique (Lindell and Whitney 2001, Malhotra et al. 2006). Specifically, we added theoretically unrelated variable that concerns beliefs in psi (Tobacyk and Milford 1983) and that was measured using a multi-item scale. Next, we examined correlations between the marker variable and other constructs in the nomologic model. The results of this analysis indicated that any CMB was not substantial because the average correlation coefficient was less than 0.01 (r = <sup>−</sup>0.002, not significant). Also, the effects of the marker variable were statistically nonsignificant when included in the structural model as another predictor of the intention to use. Thus, this study is relatively robust against CMBs (Son and Kim 2008).

Finally, to test for multicollinearity, we computed all the variance inflation factor (VIF) values for all the measures in our model. The results indicate that all VIF values are below the most conservative value of 4.0 reported in the literature (and all tolerance values exceeded 0.25), indicating that multicollinearity is inconsequential in this study (Hair et al. 1995). This, as suggested by Kock (2015), could also serve as further indication that the model is not contaminated by CMB.

## 5.3. Manipulation Checks

Manipulation checks were conducted for the three treatment factors as follows. After being exposed to the treatment pages, subjects answered three Likerttype items that captured the extent to which they believed the app page contained information about collection, use, and protection. As shown in Table B1 in Appendix B in the online supplement (mean scores for the manipulation checks for the eight treatment groups are shown in Table B2 in Appendix B in the online supplement), participants who were exposed to information regarding the collection of users’ information believed that the app page “describes what personal information will be collected by the app seller and the app” more than those who were not exposed to this information (M = 6.00 versus 3.38; t = 22.68, p < 0.01). Similarly, participants who received information regarding use reported higher perceptions that the app page “describes how the app seller will use the personal information collected” compared with those who did not receive that information (M = 5.64 versus 3.31; t = 19.17, p < 0.01). Finally, participants who were exposed to information about the protection of data reported higher perceptions that the app page “describes how the personal information collected will be protected” compared with those who did not receive that information (M = 6.10 versus $3 . 2 6 ; t = 2 4 . 9 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . These results indicate that the manipulations of collection, use, and protection information were successful.

## 5.4. Structural Model Results

We analyzed a structural model using partial least squares (PLS) with SmartPLS 3 (Ringle et al. 2015). In the model, the three privacy uncertainty dimensions (namely collection, use, and protection uncertainty) were modeled as second-order formative constructs formed by the mean scores of the respective pre- and postpurchase uncertainties. Likewise, seller and product uncertainties were treated as second-order formative constructs formed by the mean scores of their respective pre- and postpurchase uncertainties. All other constructs were modeled as reflective constructs.

The results of the structural model, including standardized path coefficients and corresponding significance values, are shown in Figure 2. Standard errors were computed via a bootstrap procedure with 500 resamples. The weights of the pre- and postpurchase formative indicators on the three dimensions of privacy and seller and product uncertainties are also shown. Table B3 in Appendix B in the online supplement summarizes the hypotheses and the level of support obtained for each. The results of the structural models for the two subsamples representing the wellness and personal finance apps are shown in Appendix B in the online supplement. The results concerning the two individual apps reveal a pattern of effects that is consistent with what is observed when analyzing the pooled data set; hence, these results are not discussed further.

In contrast to Dimoka et al. (2012), who showed that uncertainty about the seller and the product in the context of online auctions negatively impacts the intention to transact, our results show that only seller uncertainty has a statistically significant effect on the intention to use a mobile app, whereas the effect of product uncertainty is nonsignificant (seller: $\beta = - 0 . 1 7 ,$ $p < 0 . 0 1$ , and product: $\beta = 0 . 0 1 , p > 0 . 1 0 )$ ). Hence, although we find support for Hypothesis 1, we fail to find support for the related Hypothesis 2. By contrast, and in support of Hypothesis 3, overall privacy uncertainty has a negative and statistically significant effect on the intention to use $( \beta = - 0 . 2 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$

The results further reveal that the control variables included exert varying effects on the intention to use. Specifically, while trust exerts a significant effect $( \beta = 0 . 2 6 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , privacy concerns exerts a nonsignificant effect $( \beta = 0 . 0 1 , p > 0 . 1 0 )$ . Furthermore, prior experience with similar apps has a large effect on the intention to use $( \beta = 0 . 2 1 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ), whereas the effect of interest in apps on the intention to use is more modest $( \beta = 0 . 1 3 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). Collectively, the four control variables and the three types of uncertainties explain 43.4% of the variance in the intention to use.

If the control variables are removed from the model, the effect of privacy uncertainty on the intention to use significantly increases to <sup>−</sup>0.41 (from <sup>−</sup>0.22), whereas the effect of seller uncertainty increases only slightly to <sup>−</sup>0.19 (from <sup>−</sup>0.17), with 28% of the variance in intention being explained solely by privacy and seller uncertainty (the effect of product uncertainty remains negligible). Even if only trust is removed, which is a variable that is outside the realm of control of the app seller, the effect of privacy uncertainty increases to <sup>−</sup>0.33, whereas the effect of seller uncertainty only increases to <sup>−</sup>0.19, with 39% of the variance in intention being explained. If both trust and prior experience with similar apps are removed (both being outside the realm of the app seller’s control), the effect of privacy uncertainty increases to <sup>−</sup>0.36, whereas the effect of seller uncertainty remains at <sup>−</sup>0.19, with 36% of the variance in intention being explained. Collectively, these results suggest that privacy uncertainty is a dominant antecedent to the intention to use an app, even in the presence of influential control variables.

## 6. Additional Analysis

## 6.1. Privacy Uncertainty as a Distinct Construct

A main objective of this research is to investigate whether privacy uncertainty is a construct that is distinct from seller and product uncertainties (Research Question 1). As described earlier, the results of the card-sorting exercise as well as the exploratory factor analysis suggest that privacy uncertainty is clearly discriminable from the other two types of uncertainty. To further confirm this finding, we con ducted another study in which seller and product un certainties were manipulated in addition to privacy uncertainty (“Factorial study” in Table 3). The study employed a design similar to the one used in the main study. Subjects were exposed to a profile page for a wellness app, where the amount of information pro vided about the seller (first factor), the product (second factor), and privacy (third factor) was manipulated Specifically, in the low-information seller condition, no information about the app developer was offered. Alternatively, the high-information condition included additional information about the developer, such as its rating (from zero to five), a “top seller” tag, and a detailed section providing information about the seller (“About the developer” section). In the low information product condition, only basic information about the app was offered, such as the app’s name, a short description of the app and its features, and a single screenshot. In the high-information condition, additional information about the app was provided—namely consumers’ rating of the app, an “editor’s choice” tag, a ranking of the app as “#5 in health & fitness,” several screenshots of the app, a detailed description of the app features, a description of what is new in this version of the app, and customer testimonials. In terms of privacy, the low-information condition was the same as the control condition in the main study (no privacy information), whereas the high-information condition was similar to condition 8 in the main study (with full privacy information).

Similar to the main study, subjects completed a questionnaire after being exposed to the app page. The questionnaire included a number of manipulation checks and attention questions. Subjects also indicated their intentions to download the app and their perceptions of use risk and provided answers to the scales measuring the three types of uncertainty and the control variables. The scales measuring the seller and product uncertainties were adapted from the scales used in the main study (however, the items no longer refer to pre- or postpurchase uncertainty). The scale measuring privacy uncertainty was created by adding items from the three scales measuring uncertainty regarding collection, use, and protection that were used in the main study. In addition to these detailed scales measuring the three types of uncertainty, we developed three new shorter scales that measure uncertainty regarding seller (without distinguishing between adverse selection and moral hazard), product (without distinguishing between description and performance uncertainty), and privacy (without distinguishing between collection, use, and protection). All scales displayed acceptable levels of reliability (see Table E1 in Appendix E in the online supplement), and exploratory factor analysis indicated a high level of discriminant validity (see Table E2 in Appendix E in the online supplement).

Manipulation checks were conducted for the three treatments. After being exposed to the treatment pages, subjects answered three Likert-type scales that captured the extent to which they believed the app page contained information about the seller, the app, and privacy (the mean scores for the manipulation checks for the eight treatment groups are available in Table E3 in Appendix E in the online supplement, and the scores for each of the three manipulation checks per treatment factor are shown in Table E4 in Appendix E in the online supplement). Participants who were exposed to more information about the seller scored higher on the scale measuring the provision of seller information than those who were not exposed to this information $( M = 5 . 6 1$ versus 2.75; t = $1 6 . 1 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Similarly, participants who received more information about the app scored higher on the scale measuring the provision of app information than those who did not receive that information $( M =$ 5.64 versus $3 . 8 8 ; t = 1 6 . 5 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). Finally, participants who were exposed to information about privacy reported higher scores on the scale measuring the provision of privacy information than those who did not receive that information $( M = 6 . 0 9 $ versus 2.39; $t = 2 1 . 8 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . These results indicate that the manipulations of the seller, product, and privacy information were all successful.

The results from 231 subjects (selected from the same panel as the main study) confirm that privacy uncertainty is a distinct and separate construct from seller and product uncertainties. Specifically, the results of a MANOVA (see Table E5 in Appendix E in the online supplement), with the three treatment factors predicting the three overall uncertainty scores, indicate that the provision of privacy-related information only affects privacy uncertainty $( F = 7 2 . 0 7 ,$ $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , with no significant effects on seller uncertainty $( F = 1 . 1 1 ,  p > 0 . 0 5 )$ or product uncertainty $( F = 1 . 3 4 , p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . ## 7. Discussion

## 7.1 Discussion of the Results

The main objective of this research is to fully explicate privacy uncertainty, as well as understand its drivers and consequences. The main findings from this study are summarized in Table 3. The results from this research demonstrate that privacy uncertainty is a salient consideration in mobile app consumers minds and distinct from any uncertainty these consumers may have regarding the seller or the app. They further confirm that privacy uncertainty plays an influential role in affecting consumers’ initial decision to download an app (which is driven mainly by uncertainty about the use and protection of postpurchase information) and their continued intentions to use it. The large and consistent effects of privacy uncertainty on the intentions to use an app highlight the need to account for this type of uncertainty, as well as understand its antecedents. In fact, the results of the regression analyses in Appendix C in the online supplement indicate that privacy uncertainty by itself can explain a significant proportion of the variance in the intentions to use an app.

In contrast to other research focusing on physical goods, our research suggests that product uncertainty is not as serious a concern in the context of mobile apps. This could be due to the less complex nature of mobile apps and indicative of the sufficiency of the textual and visual app descriptions that can be found in app stores. Yet, as has been observed in other contexts, uncertainty regarding the seller remains an important consideration when deciding to use an app. This could be attributed to the fragmented nature of app markets, where most sellers represent small entities that lack the institutional trust that is attributed to major online sellers. Hence, the extent to which seller uncertainty remains to be an important consideration when deciding to use an app that is developed by a well-reputed entity (e.g., financial institution) is an important research question that deserves further investigation.

The results concerning the relative effects of preand postpurchase uncertainty are most intriguing. Prepurchase assessments of product uncertainty are significantly more important than postpurchase assessments. This may indicate that consumers believe that any uncertainty regarding an app’s performance, be that current or later in the future, can be resolved after downloading the app and having a chance to try it. Similarly, prepurchase uncertainty about the seller, which relates to both moral hazard and adverse selection, plays a more significant role. This could indicate that consumers are relatively less concerned about a seller’s postpurchase behaviors, given that the transaction has already taken place.

By contrast, with respect to the formation of overall perceptions of privacy uncertainty, we find that the uncertainty regarding the collection, use, and protection of postpurchase information contributes more than that of prepurchase information. This indicates that uncertainty in regard to information collected during use plays a more significant role in driving consumers’ purchase intentions. This, as discussed earlier, could be due to the more sensitive nature of such information or the abstract manner in which a consumer thinks about postpurchase uncertainty given its temporal distance.

The results regarding the relative contributions of the three dimensions of privacy uncertainty indicate that although consumers are worried about what information is collected by an app, they are more concerned about the uncertainty as to how that information is used and protected. The relatively smaller effect of the collection dimension on overall privacy uncertainty could indicate that the nature of the information that is collected by an app could be inferred from the description of its features. Hence, the consumer’s uncertainty about collection is lowered. By contrast, unless explicitly communicated by the app seller, it is difficult for consumers to infer the possible uses of their data or the mechanisms that the app seller has in place to safeguard those data. This highlights the importance of going beyond “what data are collected” to “how they will be used and protected” in existing “notice and consent” mechanisms.

As the results further show, uncertainty regarding these three dimensions is driven by perceptions of information asymmetry regarding hidden characteristics and hidden action. Hence, to alleviate consumers’ concerns and reduce their uncertainty regarding use and protection, app sellers need to reduce the perceived information asymmetry, whether that information asymmetry is based on actual differential levels of knowledge or is only perceived as such. As the results highlight, the provision of such information can help to reduce the perceived information asymmetry about actions relating to use and protection, but a significant component of these perceptions remains unexplained. Similarly, although information asymmetry can help explain consumers uncertainty regarding the collection, use, and protection of their information, a large proportion of these perceptions appears to be influenced by other factors. This is consistent with research showing that objective risk does not necessarily correlate with perceptions of risk, whereas the latter is a stronger predictor of subsequent behaviors (Adjerid et al. 2018).

The results regarding the control variables highlight that general privacy concerns, which have been extensively used in the literature as a correlate of the intentions to engage in various online behaviors, exert a negligible effect when privacy uncertainty is accounted for. By contrast, trust in app sellers and consumers’ interest and experience with apps affect the intentions to use an app. This is understandable because without trust, a consumer would not consider using the app regardless of its features, seller characteristics, or privacy protections. Similarly, if a consumer has no interest in apps, he or she will likely not be in the market for any app. Likewise, prior experience with a similar app could help mitigate the inhibitors/concerns regarding its use. Interestingly, as our additional analysis shows, when trust is removed from the model, the effects of privacy uncertainty increase significantly, whereas the increase in the effects of seller uncertainty is only modest. This indicates that trust is a more influential control variable in relation to privacy uncertainty than the other two types of uncertainties.

The results from the additional studies further corroborate our earlier findings regarding the distinctiveness of privacy uncertainty from seller and product uncertainties, as well as its significant influence on the intentions to use an app. The results suggest that the provision of additional seller and product information does not help in reducing the uncertainty regarding privacy. Similarly, the provision of privacy-related information does not affect consumers’ uncertainty regarding the app or the seller. The results of the survey of existing users highlight the potential for privacy uncertainty to act as an inhibitor of continued use. Consistent with our earlier findings regarding the relatively larger contributions of postpurchase assessments of the three dimensions of privacy uncertainty on the intentions to use an app, the survey results further suggest that although information about the product typically becomes less important after use, information regarding privacy generally remains equally important even after use.

## 7.2. Contributions

Our research makes several theoretical contributions. We extend the uncertainty literature in information systems to address information-collection-laden digital goods, and we introduce privacy uncertainty as an additional construct in the nomologic network explaining the intentions to purchase as well as continued use. We specifically show that privacy uncertainty significantly influences the willingness to use an app, even after controlling for seller and product uncertainties and a number of other controlling variables. This uncertainty is mainly driven by ambiguity regarding the use and protection of data that are collected during app use. These ex ante effects of privacy uncertainty extend to the perceived risk associated with using the app, as well as the price potential users are willing to pay for it. Furthermore, the effects of privacy uncertainty linger beyond initial adoption because ex post privacy uncertainty continues to drive continued use intentions and the perceived risk associated with that use.

Although earlier studies (e.g., Pavlou et al. 2007, Dimoka et al. 2012) focused on the role of seller and product uncertainties in affecting the willingness to buy physical goods in e-commerce, we underscore the importance of privacy uncertainty in relation to information-collection-laden goods, where sellers increasingly collect and use consumers’ information before and after adoption. Therefore, our results offer future researchers a theoretical model to identify the nature of privacy uncertainty in contexts where consumers’ information is part of the ongoing relationship with sellers. Furthermore, our theoretical model provides a granular understanding of consumers’ uncertainty and indicates that reducing the information asymmetry regarding prepurchase—and, more significantly, postpurchase—privacy practices, especially in relation to use and protection, increases initial and continued acceptance and reduces perceived risks.

The proliferation of information-collection-laden goods makes it necessary to understand how consumers’ decisions are affected by the collection of their personal data and the effects of the latter on the potential diffusion of these products and the prices consumers are willing to pay for them. As our results show, postpurchase information uncertainty is a prominent dimension, where both potential and existing consumers are concerned not only with what information is collected but also with how that information is handled in the future. Hence, sellers of such goods are advised to provide consumers (both potential and existing) full transparency as regards the continuing handling of their personal data because this could affect their ability to market their products and extract price premiums.

With the more than a few hundred billion apps that have been downloaded to date, app sellers have unprecedented control over consumers’ data. Although the transaction price may be low, the longterm privacy ramifications are extremely high given that the information collected is not limited to financial transactions but, in some cases, consists of consumers’ daily routines and other deeply personal and identifying data. Such concerns are especially amplified by the fragmented nature of mobile app markets, where most app sellers (who number in the tens of millions) represent small domestic and international development houses that lack the capability (and possibly the desire) to safeguard consumer information. Therefore, the role of intermediaries (e.g., app stores) in policing and monitoring app sellers becomes vital, and it raises a number of challenges for market regulators and policymakers. As this study’s results suggest, intermediaries and regulators need to implement consistent and clear policies that reduce the levels of information asymmetry that exist in the market while ensuring that disclosures of privacy practices, especially concerning future actions and behaviors, are mandatory.

Although the FTC promotes a “notice and consent” approach, this mechanism has been criticized because it focuses solely on informing consumers about what data an app is collecting (Tsai et al. 2011). Although some sellers explain why they are collecting information, and hence inform consumers of its use most do not make such disclosures, and almost all do not offer insights into data protection. Furthermore, in its commonly used form, the concept of notice and consent deals with prepurchase information asymmetry and does not tackle its postpurchase counterpart. Hence, in light of our findings regarding the importance of postpurchase assessments of the use and the protection of data, we view the practice of notice and consent to be an ineffective mechanism to reducing privacy uncertainty. Therefore, policymakers should enact policies in response to the need to inform consumers about sellers postpurchase actions to reduce consumers’ exposure to privacy loss after consent has been provided and develop an environment in which consumers are offered a recurring window into sellers’ privacy practices. Similar conclusions have been reached in the context of e-commerce transactions (Tsai et al. 2011) and mobile services (Adjerid et al. 2013), where it has been shown that privacy notices do not consistently impact disclosure behavior (Adjerid et al. 2013). This could be attributed to ineffective communication (Jensen and Potts 2004) or issues with how such privacy notices are presented (Felt et al. 2012, Almuhimedi et al. 2015), among other reasons. Therefore, by examining the nature, origins, and consequences of consumers’ pre- and postpurchase privacy uncertainties and their impacts, this study reaffirms such conclusions and significantly informs any efforts directed toward dealing with this issue.

The results of this study can be generalized to other contexts in which consumers’ data are continuously collected. This could include other types of informationcollection-laden digital goods or even physical goods and services that collect data during use, such as loyalty cards, fitness trackers (e.g., Fitbit), or conversational bots and intelligent technologies such as Amazon Echo. Of course, there are some differences as well as similarities between these products and mobile apps. In both contexts, there is the potential for the collection of sensitive data such as health and financial information. In addition, the manner in which the data are collected could be overt or covert, hence indicating varying levels of consumers’ awareness and control. Yet the extent to which privacy uncertainty is salient in such contexts, and its subsequent effects on the continued and future diffusion of these goods, remains understudied. This research goes a long way in contributing to this promising and growing research area.

## 7.3. Limitations and Future Research

One of this study’s limitations is that the proposed model was tested in the context of only two types of mobile apps. Our choice of the apps was motivated by a number of factors. First, wellness and personal finance are considered to be two of the fastest-growing categories of mobile apps. Second, such apps are often created and sold by independent developers, hence allowing us to control for higher perceptions of trust that may be attributed to more reputable organizations (such as a consumer’s own financial institution). Third, these types of apps have the main objective of tracking users’ behaviors and therefore are suitable for the study of postpurchase uncertainty regarding data collection. Future research should examine the extent to which the study’s results are generalizable to other types of mobile apps, as well as other types of information-collection-laden goods. As the results of the individual structural models for the two different apps indicate (as shown in Appendix B in the online supplement), there are some differences in the magnitudes of effects, but the pattern of effects is the same. This means that although the absolute effects of privacy uncertainty might change, its relative effects compared with other factors remain the same.

The focus of the study is on fully explicating the construct of privacy uncertainty and understanding its effects on the intentions to use an app. To do so, this research employs the factorial survey method to create an environment resembling that of existing app stores. Hence, this research does not focus on developing new mechanisms for manipulating or mitigating consumers’ uncertainties. Future research, however, should attempt to identify and examine design based mechanisms to mitigate privacy uncertainty in both pre- and postpurchase stages. A relevant stream of research has looked at designing and examining interventions that nudge users about privacy issues while using a mobile app (e.g., Acquisti et al. 2017) and providing them with the means to control their privacy (e.g., Almuhimedi et al. 2015). In light of the prominence of the two dimensions of use and protection uncertainty in our study, these efforts could be further extended to look at how an app can nudge users regarding possible uses of their data, as well as the data protection mechanisms in place.

As discussed earlier, the results of this study are generalizable to some other product contexts. However, unlike mobile apps, markets for these products may be dominated by reputable sellers offering heterogeneous goods with more complex attributes that allow for variations in the level and nature of data collected. Hence, our findings regarding the relative effects of the three types of uncertainties examined may differ in these contexts. Therefore, future research can test the proposed model in a more generalizable fashion, where the effects of privacy and other uncertainties can be measured for different types of applications and preferably at different price points.

## 8. Conclusion

This paper attempts to fully explicate the construct of privacy uncertainty and examine its position in a nomologic network that includes other types of uncertainties and concerns. We posit that overall privacy uncertainty is driven by the consumer’s uncertainty regarding information collection, use, and protection. We further hypothesize that different types of information asymmetry impact the dimensions of privacy uncertainty. Using a simulated mobile app– buying experiment, we find that privacy uncertainty is indeed a significant construct of paramount and broad effects. Moreover, our results show that postpurchase privacy uncertainty drives consumers’ decisions more than prepurchase uncertainty and that privacy uncertainty is a lingering factor that remains influential during use.

From a theoretical standpoint, this study extends the research concerned with uncertainty in online markets and introduces privacy uncertainty as another influential factor while also highlighting that within the ambit of postpurchase uncertainty, hidden action is a dominant factor. These conclusions have important implications for app sellers and the entities overseeing app markets. It signals that the notice and consent mechanism that allows users to determine what information is being collected is insufficient because it does not describe how the seller will use or protect the data, especially data collected during use.
