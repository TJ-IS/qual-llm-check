# Modelling corporate wireless security and privacy

Vasilios Katos, Carl Adams\*

Information Systems and Computer Applications, University of Portsmouth, Burnaby Terrace, Portsmouth PO1 3AE, UK

Available online 10 August 2005

## Abstract

As corporations adopt wireless technologies then both privacy and security landscapes change dramatically, causing a reassessment of how the wireless systems can be secured and at the same time ensuring privacy obligations to their customers, staff and shareholders are met. This paper explores the relationship between wireless security and privacy issues, and develops the foundation for metrics with which to develop and examine appropriate policies. The challenge is to get consistent and supportive security and privacy policies. In addition, the adoption of a wireless infrastructure will result in richer sets of information flows, requiring additional resources to achieve the same level of security as in a wired infrastructure. Richer sets of information are also likely to have a negative impact on privacy.

Keywords: Corporate privacy; Information richness; Wireless security and privacy policies

## 1. Introduction

Business, technology and societal changes have resulted in corresponding changes for corporations in how they develop and apply appropriate policies to conform to privacy and security obligations. One particular area where such changes seem imminent is with the current business and technology trends towards wireless technologies. A move towards wireless technologies is likely to have considerable strategic impact on a business (Barnes, 2003), as well as the security and privacy issues. For efficient operations, corporations have to ensure that consistent policies are developed, focusing the corporation in the same direction. Security and privacy are issues that seem to pull in different directions: security requires increasingly intrusive information about individuals, while privacy requires anonymity.

This paper explores this complex and seemingly contradictory relationship between wireless security and privacy issues. It also attempts to develop the foundation for metrics with which to develop and examine appropriate security and privacy policies. The paper uses information richness (Daft and Lengel, 1984) as a tool to explore privacy and security, then applies these concepts to the wireless arena.

The paper is structured as follows. First some background is given on security and privacy issues and trends affecting corporations. Then privacy and security attributes are defined within a corporate context and a model of the complex privacy/security relationship developed. The paper then explores the impact of wireless technologies for corporations, and further develops the privacy/security model. The paper also identifies particular challenges in achieving an appropriate balance between security and privacy obligations within a wireless environment. The paper concludes with an identification of interesting areas for further research.

## 2. Background

Information systems security continually evolves new issues and concerns, as technology, business needs and society change. As Bishop (2003, preface) notes “Key to understanding the problems that exist in computer security is a recognition that the problems are not new. They are old problems, dating from the beginning of the computer security (and, in fact, arising from parallel problems in the non-computer world). But the locus has changed as the field of computing has changed”. Bishop charts some of these changes of security focus, from the control of file access with the mainframes in the 1980s to more current business technologies and the associated computer viruses, worms and spam that wider access to the Internet brings. Changes in security focus are also influenced by events and changes in society, such as the increased focus on security since the terrorist attacks in the US on September 11, 2001. These societal changes are recognised internationally in the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development’s (OECD) guidelines on promoting a global ‘Culture of Security’ (OECD, 2002, 2003). The OECD’s voluntary guidelines respond to the ever-changing nature of security and the wider environment, specifically relating to information systems, networks and communicating technologies. It recognises the different roles and responsibilities for each ‘participant’ including governments, businesses and society. The implications for business, as owners and operators of information systems and networks, are explicit in that they are expected to address the principles of risk assessment, security design and implementation, security management, and reassessment (OECD, 2002, 2003, p. 6). Society and the business environment have changed, and security of information systems and networks has become even more paramount. “The Guidelines signal a clear break with a time when secure design and use of networks and systems were too often afterthoughts. Participants are becoming more dependent on information systems, networks and related services, all of which need to be reliable and secure.” (OECD, 2003, p. 2)

In the ‘culture of security’, corporations are expected to address security at an appropriate strategic level and with appropriate policies. The need for having practical policies, and associated procedures to address security, is demonstrated succinctly in McCarthy’s work (2003, p. 119): “Policies and procedures are the foundation for security. Without them it is impossible to maintain control of security.<sup>.</sup> Most importantly, make sure that your policies and procedures are followed. Consider that in 2000, US defence systems were subjected to 250,000 attempted cyber attacks. Of the 245 attacks that actually succeeded, 96 percent would have failed had users followed the security protocols already in place”. McCarthy also emphasizes the importance of giving someone responsibility for such policies, the challenges of internal politics in applying workable security policies, and the need to keep such policies current. “Outdated policies are worse than useless because they give the appearance of security where none exists” (McCarthy, 2003). Corporations increasingly need to collect, monitor and process vast amounts of information on individuals both to meet their security obligations and to conduct their business.

A corresponding societal trend affecting business use of technology is the increasing concern about privacy issues. Since the development of automatic processing of data, it has been increasingly important to consider privacy protection of personal data. In the 1980s, the OECD developed a set of guidelines governing the protection of privacy, yet at the same time enabling a free flow of information to support economic activity. “The 1970s may be described as a period of intensified investigative and legislative activities concerning the protection of privacy with respect to the collection and use of personal data. Numerous official reports show that the problems are taken seriously at the political level and at the same time that the task of balancing opposing interests is delicate and unlikely to be accomplished once and for all.” (OECD, 1980, p. 8). The guidelines identify the balance between privacy needs and the economic needs to use, store and transmit such personal data to conduct transactions and support commerce. As well as a response to privacy concerns, the guidelines also aimed to get some harmonisation in privacy legislation for member countries, while recognising that national laws, policies and philosophies may be different. The OECD guidelines have been instrumental in developing fairly consistent national and regional privacy legislation such as the Data Protection Directives in the EU or the FTC guidelines in the US.

Privacy policies pose challenges of their own. As O’Connor (2003, p. 315) notes with his study of privacy policies for international hotel companies “Although regulatory philosophies differ between Europe and the US, internationally there is general agreement on certain global privacy protection principles, namely notice, choice, onward transfer, access, security, integrity and enforcement. A content analysis of the privacy policies of the top 30 largest international hotel brands revealed that [only] 25% fully complied with these guidelines, 69% partially complied and only 7% failed to include a policy of any kind”. It seems the majority of these top hotels had adopted an ‘unsophisticated’ approach to the management of customer information. Further, as O’Connor notes, “the majority of policies displayed on hotel websites are limited in that they fail to address several key issues. Only one in four would be capable of meeting the requirements of the Safe Harbour Agreement negotiated by the European Commission and the United States Department of Commerce” (O’Connor, 2003, p. 325). The omissions noted were focused around choice, security and integrity: “a worrying combination given that their prime purpose is to reassure customers as to what will happen to personal data” (O’Connor, 2003, p. 325). This is of concern to other industry sectors since as O’Connor also notes “in comparison with other industries, the policies found on hotel websites tend to be more comprehensive” (O’Connor, 2003, p. 324).

Corporations then have to achieve a balance between meeting their security obligations on the one hand and their obligations to protect privacy on the other—security obligations resulting in increasingly intrusive data collection and monitoring activities, and privacy protection obligations requiring restrained and limited access to personal data.

Clearly some common metrics would be useful in developing and applying consistent policies. Being able to determine both objectively and accurately the amount of privacy within a certain scope, has become a crucial aim. This is mainly for two reasons. Firstly, as previously discussed, privacy is threatened more than ever. Secondly, it has become more difficult to detect whether our privacy has been breached, since it is virtually impossible to establish how many digital copies of a document have been made. The need for corresponding metrics for security has also been discussed, and there are many formal methods and activities to do this (e.g. Bishop, 2003; McCarthy, 2003; Fraser, 1997).

## 3. Concepts of security and privacy

As Belanger et al. identify, “Two problems with existing e-commerce literature include the extent to which privacy and security issues are conceptualised as distinct, and the lack of understanding of how they are related” (Belanger et al., 2002, p. 247). In this section we examine concepts of security and privacy, and try to identify common ground between these to enable consistent policies to be developed. First let us examine the concept of security.

As Hawker (2000, p. 4) identifies, “‘Computer Security’ was originally the preserve of the military, whose concern was to ensure the secrecy of information which might be helpful to an enemy. As a result, it was assumed that the key requirements were to build strong defences around the information system, and to keep the release of information to a minimum. It has taken some time for the commercial world to shake off these assumptions, and to develop new ones of its own”. Hawker’s work, mainly coming from an accounting and auditing perspective, makes two pertinent points about our understanding of security. The first is that our objectives and views on security change over time as the business environment and society changes. The second is that security plays an integral role in protecting a corporation’s resources and the associated need for controls and monitoring activity. Hawker identifies eight objectives of commercial Information Systems security and the associated controls, these being:

† Protect secrets

† Promote accuracy

† Prevent tampering

† Provide authorship

† Challenge repudiation

† Authenticate over time

† Ensure survival

† Maximise auditability

The concept of security changes over time due to changes in the environment, society and business practice. Panko (2004, p. 2) examines recent reports and studies of security incidents and highlights increasing trends in the number of reported incidents and the severity of the threats facing corporations. A clear contributory factor is increased problems with computer viruses, denial of service attacks, worms, other forms of hacking resulting in unwanted/illegal access, alterations to files and systems, and spam e-mail.

The increase in the variety of computer virus attacks and spam has changed the focus of security for corporations—indeed, in many organisations, security could be considered solely as protecting against hackers and reducing spam. McCarthy (2003, p. 33) makes a similar point in noting “far too many companies equate computer security with the firewall”, arguing that a wider remit is called for. The other objectives identified by Hawker also need to be considered for a security policy to be judged effective.

There have also been changes in the focus on privacy as a result of increased awareness of human rights issues. Privacy issues have arguably taken an equally prominent position within society as security. For instance, much of the anti-spamming legislation (SPAMHAUS, 2004a,b) has a clear focus on protecting people’s privacy and access to personal information.

Qualitatively, privacy is all about the right to be left alone without raising any suspicion (Warren and Brandies, 1890). Although this is an acknowledged attempt to define privacy, coming up an adequate definition is a challenging task, comparable to the intellectual and philosophical efforts involved in understanding concepts such as time and freedom (Brunk, 2003). The difficulty of arriving at a solid understanding of privacy (and consequently at an adequate definition of privacy) is partially attributed to our limited vocabulary on privacy, according to McLean (1995). Based on this observation, Brunk reacted by introducing the term exoinformation which is “the information leaving us, either semi-consciously or unconsciously, made available because of our interactions with computers, electronic equipment, or related machinery” (Brunk, 2003, p. 19). A similar concept described by Olsen (2000), was referred to as data exhaust.

A number of privacy frameworks have been developed, including the International Security, Trust and Privacy Alliance (ISTPA) Privacy Framework (ISTPA, 2002) and the Security Processes Framework (Schneier, 2000) that are based on the Principles of Fair Information Practices (HEW, 1973). Both of these frameworks adopt a user-centred view on privacy. The approach by the Canadian Government goes further than most in explicitly addressing privacy in the form of the ‘Privacy Impact Assessments (PIA) guidelines’ (TBCA, 2002). These are based upon ‘universal privacy principles’ defined in the Canadian Standards Association’s model code for the protection of personal information, which is itself consistent with Canadian privacy legislation. The guidelines require organisations to conduct appropriate analysis to assess any privacy implications raised by the adoption of any new technology or system.

## 4. Defining corporate privacy

Drawing on the above, we choose to adopt the user-centred approach and combine it with Brunk’s concept of exoinformation to provide a definition of privacy. The definition will be used as a basis for presenting the modelling concepts proposed in this paper.

Therefore we define privacy as follows:

Definition 1. An informal definition of privacy. Given an entity A and a set of possible actions B, the privacy of an entity A (PRI) relates to the inability to answer efficiently the following questions:

q1. Who is A?

q2. What is A’s action (i.e. which element from set B did A do)?

q3. When did A perform the action?

q4. Where did A perform the action?

q5. Why did A perform the action?

q6. How did A perform the action?

Although this definition does not completely quantify privacy, it can be used as an effective bridge between the empirical (qualitative) and relational (quantitative) world. Proposing a model of a quantifiable privacy is beyond the scope of this paper, yet we believe that this is an interesting field for research. Crucial to defining privacy is the amount of information needed to answer the questions q1–q6, and there may well be more specific questions related to particular privacy contexts. There are clear information richness (Daft and Lengel, 1984) perspectives in understanding privacy—the richer the set of information required, the less the privacy.

There are corresponding security interpretations for each of these privacy attributes. For instance, question q1 relates to the ability to confirm A’s identity or, in security terms, addresses ‘authentication’. Similarly, question q2 relates to the success of selecting the correct action (defined as the action that A did indeed perform) from the set of all possible actions, which relates to ‘access control’. We can accept that the most important questions are q1 and q2, since the correct answer to the former affects the validity of the remaining questions, and the correct answer to the latter affects the validity of questions q3–q6. This is the main reason for formally defining q2 by defining its set of answers.

We argue that the more questions that can be answered, the less the privacy. An important aspect that needs to be considered is that these questions can also be addressed indirectly from side channels. A side channel is often neglected in an analysis, but is crucial when investigating privacy. Side channels are ‘unofficial’ channels of information that unintentionally allow information leakage. For example, a large takeaway order placed by a Pentagon office late at night to a nearby pizzeria, or a large number of cars parked at the building after normal hours, suggest that military operations are taking place (Diffie and Landau, 1999). Journalists, among others, are well aware of such practices.

To make the above definition of privacy quantitative, one could incorporate the effort required to answer the questions, or alternatively the probabilities of answering the questions correctly. The existence of side channels would mean that the respective probabilities are conditional, as answering one question will influence the probability to answer another.

## 5. Security—privacy relationship

The security process is inherently related to establishing a balance between security safeguards and system usability. Traditionally, this is expressed as risk analysis, as shown in Fig. 1. Considering the cost of the security investment, we can accept that it increases as the level of security increases (curve SEC). We also accept that the higher the security, the lower the cost of damage due to a security breach (curve BRE). The balance—or equivalently the optimal security level—is where the total cost is minimised (curve TC, point M).

Although the above is a neat academic description of balancing costs, in reality it is virtually impossible to approximate the shapes of the cost curves and thus to determine the optimal security level. This is due to the inherent limitations of risk analysis and more specifically of the risk assessment stage, where the primary data is biased, inappropriate or incomplete (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 1997). However, the risk analysis process is valuable because it conveys the concept of balancing opposing interests. In addition, the curves are more likely to contain discrete steps as, say, a particular security investment and measure ‘notches up’ the level of security across several fronts. The curves show a general relationship to both the level of security investment and the cost of security breaches.

Similarly, security and privacy seem to aim at opposing goals. More specifically, privacy opts for protecting information as expressed in the questions q1–q6 of Definition 1.

Security on the other hand includes the service of accountability, which has exactly the opposite aim, i.e. to answer questions q1–q6.

From the above it can be seen that the key factor that privacy and security deal with is information and its protection. Information richness can be used to define perspectives in privacy and has corresponding security perspectives. Therefore by parameterising on information richness, we could argue that the balance between security and privacy can be expressed as in Fig. 2. The information richness refers to the availability of information— the opposite of privacy (Definition 1). The more information is available, the higher the level of security could be offered, as better access control decisions can be made. We emphasise could, suggesting that the SEC curve is the boundary of attainable security— just because information is available does not imply that the system will be secure. Yet, if there is not enough information available, then the level of security cannot be high. Therefore security is dependent on the availability of information, or information richness.

These claims can be demonstrated with a series of security examples and scenarios. Access control is a security service that typically consists of three stages: identification, authentication, and authorisation. During the identification stage, a user reveals his identity to the system.<sup>2</sup> A typical example of identification information is the username. During the authentication stage the user needs to verify or prove that he is the legitimate possessor of the identity he has presented to the system during the identification stage. The most popular means of authentication is the password. Although passwords are accepted in many environments, they yield low security. In environments where a higher level of security is required, other authentication technologies are considered, such as tokens (e.g. smartcards, magnetic stripe cards) or biometric authentication mechanisms. It is obvious that from an information richness perspective, a password carries less information than a biometric pattern. If two or more authentication technologies are combined, then the resulting security level would be much greater than each one alone, and furthermore the resulting security would be greater than the security sum of its parts. This justifies the exponential-like shape of the SEC curve.

From a privacy perspective, the higher the information richness, the higher the potential for privacy violations; therefore, privacy decreases. However, this model is not entirely accurate, since security is also required for protecting privacy. For low levels of information richness, privacy is ‘bounded’ by security, as there cannot be high privacy with no or low security. This corresponds to the privacy legislation discussed earlier which requires a minimum level of data security. This is true for the area on the left hand side of breakeven point M in Fig. 2 and security is positively correlated with privacy. To the right of M, security increases at the expense of privacy and therefore security and privacy are negatively correlated. Consequently, to complete the model in Fig. 2, the rule PRI%SEC should be applied, which leads to the revised model of Fig. 3(a). The correlation can be obtained from the plot of SEC against PRI in Fig. 3(b).

The above model assumes the same metrics for security and privacy. In order to allow different metrics for security and privacy, the model can be represented as a triple (s, p, i), for security, privacy and information richness.

The maximum level of security capable of preserving privacy is at point M. If a higher level of security is required then more information should be available and vice versa; more available information can contribute to a higher security level.

An efficient privacy policy can be found on the curve between O and M. Such a privacy policy specifies what and how information is used for both business processes and security enforcement. A security policy can be found at any point on the SEC curve. It should be noted that a security policy is considered as a good security policy if it meets certain criteria. However, in many cases the list of criteria does not include explicit privacy considerations (e.g. Fraser, 1997). Consequently, a good security policy may still exist beyond point M. Therefore in order to preserve privacy, from all good possible policies an organisation must select the policy closest to M. There may of course be other specific concerns that call for a deviation from point M, such as a specific security need, in which case it can be considered as a normal strategic decision: an increase in security away from point M will result in a corresponding decrease in privacy.

## 6. Wireless implications

A very prominent trend in business technology is the move towards wireless systems. That is likely to have an impact on security as well as a corresponding impact on privacy issues. Wireless technologies raise a different set of security issues, for instance as Panko (2004, p. 83) notes about wireless LANs, “WLANs spread their signals widely, allowing attacks to read messages easily. In addition, in [such] drive-byhacking, an attacker reads frames and can even send attack frames without entering the premises”. Wireless technologies are redefining corporate space, expanding beyond previous physical and wired boundaries. This expansion of the corporate space will also have a direct impact on privacy issues since potentially private information will be transmitted and collected wirelessly. As a corporation’s corporate space expands it may also overlap other corporations’ corporate space. It may also encroach on individual’s personal space, or personal trust space defined by their personal mobile technologies (Adams et al., 2003). Such overlapping of corporate space and encroachment of personal space is likely to result in increased security concerns and privacy protection obligations.

In addition, such wireless systems increasingly have wider strategic implications, as Barnes (2003, p. 2) notes “mobile(m-) business is likely to have a tremendous impact on organisations, as wireless technologies and applications begin to challenge the existing processes, strategies, structures, roles of individuals, and even cultures of organisations”. Consequently, wireless systems are likely to be a concern for policy makers for many corporations in the coming years. Clearly, as corporations adopt wireless technologies, both privacy and security landscapes change dramatically, causing a reassessment of how wireless systems can be secured and at the same time ensure privacy obligations to their customers, staff and shareholders. The challenges in achieving consistent policies on security and privacy will also be relevant to the wireless domain.

From a security perspective, wireless systems inherently increase potential security weak points. It is unlikely that most corporations will adopt a purely wireless infrastructure (at least for the foreseeable future) since corporations already have considerable investment, expertise and processes tied up in the existing wired infrastructure. Consequently, most corporations will operate both wireless and wired systems and so increase the potential channels and points for security breaches.

The wireless infrastructure itself is inherently less secure on two main points. The first is related to increased scope (Ciampa, 2002; Panko, 2004), where wireless technologies expand security issues and potential access points outside the existing corporate physical space. The second area is the medium itself and concerns wireless security protocols. Take for example the 802.11 wireless standards. The 802.11 Working Group initially developed the WLAN security standard Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP) which was supposed to provide an equivalent level of protection as wired security protocols (Panko, 2004). Unfortunately, WEP had some serious security holes including it not being turned on as ‘default’ for WLANs, a limited password length for devices to obtain connection through access points, and even shared passwords for multiple users on the same access point (Panko, 2004, p. 87). Panko charts the development and improvement of the 802.11 wireless security protocols to the 802.11i EAP (Extensible Authentication Protocol) which is likely to take some time before it is widely adopted, and the stop gap TKIP (Temporal Key Integrity Protocol) which address many of the WEP weaknesses (Panko, 2004, p. 90). TKIP itself is part of the umbrella Wi–Fi Protected Access (WPA) method that addresses previous weaknesses in WLAN transmissions.

By the time wireless security catches up with the current level of security in the wired world, the wired world is likely to have moved on to further levels, addressing different security needs of the time. Even if the level of security offered in the wireless protocols and services were the same as the wired world, there is extra overhead in reaching the same security level; extra resources will be needed in terms of equipment, training, maintaining and managing wired and wireless systems concurrently. Wireless security can be improved to reach (something equivalent to) wired networks but, as Ciampa (2002) identifies, extra technologies and processes will be required. There is the added dimension that wireless infrastructure is increasingly involving not just one wireless system, but an array of competing wireless systems each with their own unique characteristics, capabilities, protocols and issues. For instance, as well as a 802.11 WLAN, a corporation may use a variety of devices throughout the organisation involving Bluetooth, infrared, RFIDs, smart phones, PDAs, wireless smart cards or a mix of any of these or other devices. The level of support increases with each new wireless device, along with the resource implications of maintaining comparable levels of security.

Further concerns relating to wireless technologies relate to location information that is embedded in the protocols or within system attributes. Typically, many wireless devices will be locatable by means of their interaction with access points and with each other. It may be possible to monitor movement of such devices, and their users, through the corporate space. The same monitoring capabilities are possible outside the corporate space. For instance, since 1996, wireless carriers in the USA have been required to incorporate wireless phone locators in their networks for ‘safety’ reasons (WLIA, 2001b). In reality, the motivation for this may have been strongly influenced by law enforcement needs to locate drug traffickers and other criminals; nonetheless it initiated much activity in location-based technologies for wireless. Similar location-based developments took place in Japan and Europe, though driven more from commercial reasons rather than legislation (WLIA, 2001b). The current state of technologies is that most wireless telecommunications infrastructure incorporates location information about users. With the global move towards 3G technologies, with always-on capabilities, such location information will become more accurate and potentially more intrusive.

The Wireless Location Industry Association (WLIA) identify the main challenges for many wireless technologies: “Without question, the single most important issue confronting the new industry of wireless signal location technology and applications is the issue of personal privacy. As with any other issue, there are two sides to this issue. This does not mean that there are two sides as in ‘for’ or ‘against’ privacy, nor two sides as in ‘for’ or ‘against’ wireless signal location. Reasonable people will look for solutions that will maintain both the value of protecting individual privacy and the value of achieving the benefits of new wireless location technologies. The issue is how to strike the right balance between these values.” (WLIA, 2001a, p. 3)

The WLIA has been involved in developing a set of self-regulation policies for operators. These include the Fair Location Information Practice (FLIP) (Barnes, 2003, p. 144; WLIA, 2001a):

a. inform customers about collection practices

b. give the customer choice regarding any use of the information

c. allow for access to the data so that customers can ensure that it is correct

d. maintain the data securely

e. comply with enforcement and auditing of the FLIP policies

These are fairly generic and similar to the data protection and privacy rules discussed earlier. Clearly there is scope for opt-in and opt-out approaches in applying such selfregulation. However, there are likely to be practical challenges in applying the FLIP guidelines not encountered in the wired world, particularly in how to inform customers about collection practices, how to give choice regarding any use of such data and how to enable access to such data, since most of the data collection activity is likely to be automatic and required for wireless activity. Even if choice could be communicated to individuals it is unlikely to be able to be exercised.

It is clear that the wireless domain is moving towards the similar set of data protection and privacy rules that corporations currently have to adhere to. Interestingly, the issues are relevant to any organisation adopting wireless technologies, not just telecommunication companies. As more corporations adopt wireless technologies to support their businesses operations, then the more corporations will be expected to adhere to privacy and security rules with appropriate policies.

## 7. Corporate privacy in wireless environments

We conjecture that the introduction of a wireless environment results in a move beyond point M of Fig. 3(a), due to the substantial increase in information richness. Additional features, such as the Wired Equivalent Privacy WEP protocol discussed earlier, are a typical attempt to rectify the anticipated drop of security. A wireless environment inherently provides information for answering privacy questions q1–q4. Furthermore, this information is used not only for security, but also for operational purposes, resulting in low privacy.

This caveat is represented graphically in Fig. 4. The security curves both for the wired and the wireless environment are superimposed on the privacy curve. ‘Wired’ security indicates an environment with no wireless technologies present. Since wireless security is less than wired security (in the sense that in order for wireless security to attain the same level of security as in a wired environment more effort would be required), the wireless security curve intersects the privacy curve at a lower point. Consequently, the maximum privacy level in a wireless environment is $P _ { 2 }$ instead of $P _ { 1 }$ , i.e. there is a loss in privacy. If the security level is not accepted, or alternatively if the wireless security level is to be maintained to the same level as the wired security $( P _ { 1 } )$ , then the privacy would drop further to $P _ { 3 }$

The following example demonstrates the principle that in order to maintain a certain security level in the wireless environment, an increase of information richness takes place. Consider the Wired Equivalent Privacy protocol. The purpose of this protocol is to encrypt all wireless traffic, since the risk of eavesdropping in a wireless environment is considerably higher than the risk in a wired one—a wired private network may have well defined physical boundaries, whereas a wireless private network may be accessible also from outside the physical boundaries. In this example, WEP is responsible for the increase in information richness, as the cryptographic keys introduced must be securely managed.

Corporations need to acknowledge that there is likely to be loss of privacy when introducing a wireless environment and develop efficient strategies for dealing with the increased relevant risks. It seems that SEC can be described as a monotonous increasing function and PRI as a monotonous decreasing function with respect to information richness. Also, PRI cannot be higher than SEC, since the latter is necessary for supporting the former.

It should be stressed that the described model is still in an early stage of development. However, it opens up new avenues for investigating security and privacy in a consistent framework. The hypotheses and assumptions stated above with regard to the relation between SEC and PRI, as well as the shapes of the curves, need to be further investigated. Alternatively, further empirical research would provide more evidence of the validity of the proposed model. This would be ideally carried out in the context of an organisation that has mature and clear-cut privacy and security policies, such as in the healthcare industry, for example.

## 8. Conclusion and areas for further research

This paper has contended that corporations have to adopt appropriate policies to conform to privacy rules and to meet security concerns. It explored the relationship between security and privacy issues, and used information richness to develop the foundation for metrics with which to develop and examine consistent privacy/security policies.

As corporations adopt wireless technologies, both privacy and security landscapes change, requiring reassessment of how privacy and security levels can be maintained.

The information richness perspective, along with the specific attributes of wireless systems, indicates that adoption of wireless infrastructure will result in a richer set of information flows, increased system complexity and increased potential for security breaches. To maintain the same level of security in a wireless system will require more investment in the form of specific additional security technologies, working practices and associated security policies. The result of these technology and process changes is that a richer set of information about individuals and their activities will be collected, much of which is likely to be automatically collected, collated, stored and processed as part of the technology protocols. This collection and processing of a richer set of information about individuals is also likely to have a corresponding negative impact on privacy. Further research is necessary to support our preliminary developments. In addition, we believe research on tools and metrics for establishing the relationships between privacy, security and information richness will prove to be an interesting and valuable field. A possible direction towards defining privacy metrics could be to adopt an Information Theoretic approach, where privacy can be related to the success probability on answering the privacy questions stated in Definition 1, which in turn will associate privacy with the uncertainty measures originally defined by Shannon (1949).
