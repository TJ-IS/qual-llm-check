Abstrac t: Information technology (IT) innovations follow a diverse set of diffusion patterns. Early diffusion models explaining technology diffusion patterns assumed that there is a single homogeneous segment of potential adopters. It was later shown that a two-segment model considering two groups of adopters explains variations in diffusion patterns better than the existing one-segment models. While the two-segment model considers a group of adopters promoting adoption by exerting a positive influence on prospective adopters, it does not consider the members of society who aim to inhibit the adoption process by exerting a negative influence on prospective adopters. In fact, most IT innovations face opposition. Yet it is not clear how opposition affects the diffusion process. In this paper, we model the diffusion of an IT innovation through its target population with three types of actors: influentials, who are autonomous in adopting new technology and promote its adoption; opponents, who are opposed to the technology and inhibit its adoption; and imitators, who are information seekers, thus affected by both influentials and opponents. We show that opponents play a crucial role in determining the diffusion path of an innovation. The empirical tests using real as well as simulated data sets demonstrate the ability of our model to fit the data better and to identify the segments of adopters correctly.

Key word s and ph rases: diffusion of innovation, information technology diffusion, technology opposition.

In th e past few d ec ad es, the information technology (IT) marketplace has been inundated with new hardware, software, protocols, and standards [4]. Despite potential advantages of new technologies,<sup>1</sup> their long-term viability is often unclear. Hence, before adopting an innovation, decision makers have to understand how the innovation spreads through its target population to assess the potential effects of the innovation.

Prospective adopters who value the innovation and will benefit from it are key actors and have been the focus of the extant literature. However, many innovations face resistance. Opposition to new technologies is not new [23]. More often than not, certain stakeholders aim to stop or slow down the diffusion of an innovation. These opponents do not perceive the innovation in the same way as the adopters. They are concerned about the risks associated with innovation. For example, some people are concerned about the health risk of wireless phones, privacy issues related to search engines storing and using users’ search histories, and the potential privacy violations brought on by radio frequency identification (RFID) technologies. It is not difficult to extend the list of innovations encountering opposition. Regardless of the context—whether the innovation is a consumer product targeted at a particular segment of the population or a technology for businesses—in each of these cases, certain actors are opposed to the innovation. At the organizational level, opponents can be rivals of the technology provider, competitors of technology users, customers of the firm using the technology, nonprofit organizations, or government or technology experts. At the individual level, opponents can be people who totally reject the innovation, environmental or health organizations, or public interest groups.

Opponents in the context of almost all innovations, particularly in the context of IT, are significant actors. They primarily have two objectives. First, they want to increase the number of actors concerned about the innovation (i.e., increase the size of the opposition group). Second, they want to influence the diffusion process (i.e., restrain the adoption from reaching a certain level). They do not adopt the innovation. However, existing literature is silent about the role that opponents play in the diffusion process of an innovation. Our research aims to fill this void.

We extend Van den Bulte and Joshi’s [33] two-segment diffusion model by including the role of the opponents in the diffusion process. The three-segment diffusion model considers two groups of heterogeneous adopters: influentials are more in touch with the new innovation and are able to affect the other group of adopters, namely, imitators, whose own adoptions do not affect the influentials. The third segment of actors is opponents, who will not adopt the innovation and negatively influence the diffusion of innovation. With this setup, we address two questions:

1. How does the existence of opponents influence the diffusion process?

2. How good is the proposed three-segment model in its ability to fit the data and its predictive power, compared to other existing diffusion models?

The first question is important because a better understanding of the diffusion process enables decision makers to devise their strategies to obtain more benefit from a new technology/innovation. The second question is important as well because a new diffusion model is useful only if it is superior to existing diffusion models.

We develop a mathematical model for technology diffusion and investigate the role of the opponents in the diffusion process. The model is validated in three empirical ways. First, we test how well it fits the real data composed of nine diffusion data sets. Second, we create simulated data sets to demonstrate that our model overcomes the segment misidentification problem that the existing two-segment model may result in. Third, we use our model to predict the future diffusion of cell phones.

An opponent group can affect the diffusion process in three ways: (1) delaying peak adoption time, (2) reducing the peak adoption rate, or (3) changing the shape of the diffusion curve. Our analysis reveals the importance of the influence factor, which measures the effect of the opponent group on imitators’ adoption behavior. When the influence factor is significant, opponents can effectively delay or weaken the diffusion process, even if the opponent community is small. However, if the influence factor is small, the negative aspect of the opponents is marginal, even if the opponent community is large or able to grow quickly. Our findings offer useful insights. In order for the influential group to reach a target adoption rate (for the opponent group to control the adoption under a certain level), the influentials (opponents) should either make an effort to improve their respective influence power on imitators or focus on speeding up the growth of their own groups. Although the choice depends on concrete market parameters, in most cases the first strategy (i.e., improving the influence power on the imitators) is more effective.

An empirical analysis of nine diffusion data sets demonstrates that our three-segment diffusion model fits the data better than the two-segment model. Although empirical research in the diffusion literature by and large overlooked the existence of opponents, our analysis provides evidence that opponents are significant actors in many diffusion processes. Furthermore, we show that if the opponents influence the diffusion process as described herein, the estimation of the aggregate diffusion data based on the two-segment model may lead to misidentification of the segments of adopters. That is, the two-segment model may incorrectly identify influentials as imitators and imitators as influentials. This problem could be overcome with our three-segment model. Understanding the nature of adopters’ heterogeneous segments is important for decision makers who need to tailor their marketing strategies for these segments.

Hence, our model is preferred to the two-segment model not only for its ability to fit the data better but also for its ability to identify the segments correctly.

The paper is organized as follows. We first review the relevant literature in the next section. Then, we propose our three-segment diffusion model and present mathematical solutions to it. Subsequently, we present our main findings as well as their empirical validations. Finally, we analyze the diffusion process for cell phones. We conclude the paper with some final remarks and future research directions.

## Literature Review

Innovation d iffusion th eory [27] is a popular reference theory used in information systems (IS) literature [8]. Numerous IS studies have built on the diffusion theory. The following discussion provides an overview of the diffusion literature and builds the theoretical foundations for our model.

## Diffusion Studies

How, why, and at what rate a new idea, innovation, or technology spreads through the society have been of interest to researchers in various fields. Attewell [1] classifies diffusion studies into two groups—macro diffusion studies and adopter studies. The former group deals with mathematical characterization of the rate and the pattern of adoption of a technology among potential adopters, and the latter group deals with the identification of factors that facilitate or hinder the adoption of the technology [8]. Examples of macro diffusion studies include Taga and Isii [31] in statistics, Mansfield [19] and Williams [35] in economics, Coleman [6] in sociology, and Bass [2] in marketing. These studies developed diffusion models measuring the adoption rate over time, which is influenced by two forces at all times: the intrinsic tendency to adopt (i.e., adopters’ inner motivation) and social contagion (i.e., existing adopters’ influence on potential users). The former factor represents the time-invariant internal drive that persuades the potential user to adopt an innovation (often called the innovation factor), whereas the latter factor represents how a potential user considers the existing adopters of the innovation in his or her adoption decision (often called the imitation factor). The existing literature often assumed that innovation and imitation factors ex ante affect all the potential users equally [2, 6, 19, 20, 31, 35].

Among macro diffusion studies, Bass [2], for example, proposed a growth model for the timing of initial purchases of a new product. Assuming that consumers’ initial purchases are influenced by the number of previous buyers, Bass predicted that exponential growth of initial purchases peaks and then exponentially decays after the peak. However, such a pattern cannot explain the diffusion process of many innovations. Van den Bulte and Joshi [33] recently recognized the discrepancy between observed and theoretical diffusion patterns of some innovations, highlighting the importance of heterogeneity among adopters. Drawing on various theories, Van den Bulte and Joshi conceptualized two groups of heterogeneous adopters—influentials and imitators.

They argued that innovation factors and imitation factors differ for these heterogeneous adopters. Their work shows that new technology/innovation diffusion can follow various paths, which may indicate a dip between the earlier and later parts of the diffusion curve. Our model is closest in spirit to Van den Bulte and Joshi’s [33] model, and builds on the heterogeneous adopters (for a comprehensive review of technology/ innovation diffusion models, see [9, 18]).

Most diffusion studies in IS literature can be classified as adopter studies. Some IS/IT initiatives have encountered serious resistance from prospective users; thus, IS literature has focused on understanding issues in IT implementations. Cooper and Zmud defined IT implementation, from the perspective of technology diffusion, as “an organizational effort directed toward diffusing appropriate information technology within a user community” [7, p. 124]. As most implementations require some changes in the organization, drawing on Lewin’s change model [17], Cooper and Zmud [7] and Kwon and Zmud [16] presented a staged model of IT implementation in which the new IT diffusion process goes through six stages—initiation, adoption, adaptation, acceptance, routinization, and infusion. Kwon and Zmud [16] argued that contextual factors such as community characteristics, organizational characteristics, technology characteristics, task characteristics, and environmental factors affect the six stages of new IT diffusion within a user community. Fichman [8] further argued that factors facilitating or hindering IT diffusion critically depend on classes of technology (e.g., whether the new IT creates a knowledge burden on users or not) and locus of adoption (e.g., whether diffusion of a new IT is considered within a single organization or a group of organizations).

Diffusion of new methodologies among IS practitioners has been of interest to IS researchers. Gibson pointed out that innovations in software development processes have not been widely diffused [10]. The diffusion of an IS development method is not a purely rational event; rather, it is a broader social one [3]. For example, developers’ perceptions about usefulness, social pressure, compatibility, and organizational mandates are significant determinants of software developers’ intentions to follow formalized methodologies [13]. The IS literature has also investigated the hurdles in propagating software development tools. For example, regarding computer-aided software engineering (CASE) tools, instability and performance gaps in the IS department have been found to not directly promote CASE tools adoption [25].

Furthermore, the IS literature studied innovation diffusion regarding IS and business processes. In the context of IS process innovations, factors from diffusion theory (e.g., need recognition, availability of technological infrastructure, past experience, trials, autonomous work, ease of use, learning by doing, and standards) affect the adoption process [21]. In the context of business processes, Grover et al. [12] found that a complex relationship exists among IT diffusion, perceived productivity improvement, and process redesign. In addition, a substantial amount of research focused on diffusion of particular IT. Karimi et al. [14] identified the contextual conditions under which enterprise resource planning (ERP) implementations have the greatest effect on business process outcomes. Grover and Goslar [11] found that environmental uncertainty and decentralization of decision making significantly influence the use of telecommunications technologies in U.S. organizations. Reinig et al. [26] argued that affective reward in promoting group support systems (GSS) is crucial in the diffusion process. Premkumar et al. [24] examined the relationship between characteristics of electronic data interchange (EDI) and diffusion success.

Although most studies related to IT diffusion are adopter studies, a few macro diffusion studies exist in IS literature. One notable example is Tam [32], who studied adopters’ reactions to changes in technology prices. Kocas [15] later formalized a dynamic competitive pricing model that considers the diffusion process in price-comparison shopping. The fact that IT diffusion studies have mainly focused on adopters highlights the need for macro diffusion studies in the IS literature. Hence, our paper attempts to advance knowledge at the macro level in the IT diffusion process.

## Theoretical Foundations

Although adoption decisions are made by potential adopters individually, their decisions are often shaped by social dynamics surrounding the innovation. The idea of an innovation does not emerge overnight; rather, it is an outcome of highly contentious discourse in a community consisting of a variety of participants with different interests in the innovation. Such communities do not have to be formal. Discourse communities in IS/IT innovations are active and highly visible. Consortia and committees dedicated to defining technical standards are examples of those communities [29]. They often highlight the benefits of their technologies. Hence, all existing diffusion models focus on the positive aspects of social contagion only, paying no attention to the negative aspects. They implicitly assume that innovation per se is a good thing [27].

Although firms may develop a rationale in favor of adoption, mindful firms may develop a rationale against adoption [30]. Those who reject the innovation may subscribe to a different version of the future and oppose the diffusion of the innovation. However, all existing diffusion models ignore the existence of opponents, who may impose a strong negative influence on potential adopters. In fact, in many IT diffusion processes, the existence of opponents (such as privacy advocates, corporate watchdogs) is an important feature that should not be overlooked. Many critics of IT are concerned about the legal, social, and economic implications of new technologies [22]. Going beyond the passive reluctance to use the technology, these critics often show overt op position to the technology and aim to influence the innovation’s diffusion process.

Rogers’s innovation-diffusion theory [27] offers theoretical justification for our three-segment diffusion model, which explicitly considers three kinds of actors— influentials, opponents, and imitators. Rogers conceptualized diffusion as a process in which an innovation is communicated through certain channels among members of a social system. A new innovation is often associated with some degree of uncertainty. Members of the social system use information about consequences of innovation in deciding whether to adopt or reject the innovation. Therefore, the innovation-decision process is essentially an information-seeking and information-processing activity in which individuals gather information about advantages and disadvantages of the innovation.

Rogers [27] defined opinion leaders as members of the social system who have the ability to influence other members’ attitudes or overt behaviors by communicating favorable or unfavorable information about the innovation. According to Rogers, “many systems have both innovative opinion leaders and leaders who oppose change” [27, p. 27]. Innovative opinion leaders favor the innovation and aim to spread its adoption throughout the system. On the other hand, opposing opinion leaders do not favor the innovation and often promote active opposition. In our model, we refer to innovative opinion leaders as influentials and opposing opinion leaders as opponents. According to Rogers, not all members of the social system are opinion leaders. These members form their attitudes toward innovation based on information about the consequences of innovation, as provided by opinion leaders. They then decide whether to adopt or reject the innovation. In our model, members who are not opinion leaders and who eventually adopt the innovation are called imitators. In short, Rogers’s innovationdiffusion theory provides us with the theoretical foundation for considering three kinds of actors in our technology diffusion model.

We propose a mathematical model for technology diffusion to capture relationships among three kinds of actors in the diffusion process. Because influentials and opponents are opinion leaders who disseminate information about consequences of innovation, they affect imitators’ adoption decisions in positive and negative ways, respectively. Although opinion leaders exert influence on those who are not opinion leaders, they are not influenced by the other members of the society. Therefore, influentials are not affected by either opponents or imitators. Similarly, opponents are not affected by either influentials or imitators. Our work contributes to the literature by extending the existing mathematical models of diffusion to include interactions of the three actors inherent in many innovations. This new modeling approach allows us to better understand how a technology diffuses through its target population.

In our diffusion model, adopters and opponents can be organizations or individuals. It is not necessary that adopters and opponents be the same kind; adopters can be organizations while opponents can be some of their customers. We do not impose any restriction on the units of actors in our model as long as the definitions of segments hold. Our diffusion model is general in that previous diffusion models serve as our special cases.

## Model

We m od el th e d iffusion of an IT -related innovation with adopters and opponents. Adopters are those who are in favor of the innovation and will eventually adopt it; opponents are those who are against the innovation and will not adopt it. The set of eventual adopters of the innovation has a constant size and can be categorized into two heterogeneous groups—influentials and imitators. Although both are inner directed in adopting the new technology, imitators are more risk averse. The adoption decision of influentials and imitators is influenced by the size of the population who have already adopted the innovation. However, the existing adopters affect influentials and imitators differently. Influentials are only influenced by the adoption behavior of their own, whereas imitators are influenced by both types of adopters. Consequently, the former are called influentials—they can affect the adoption of imitators in a positive way; however, imitators cannot influence influentials’ adoption behaviors. In addition, a third group of actors advocates concerns about the innovation’s adoption. We call this opposition group opponents. Opponents do not adopt the innovation. They negatively influence imitators’ adoption behaviors. Opponents also display a diffusion pattern. Prospective opponents are not only inner directed but they are also influenced by existing opponents. Similarly, the ultimate size of the opponent population is constant.

Thus, the adoption rate (i.e., fraction of the population adopting) at time t is

$$
f _ {m} (t) = \theta f _ {1} (t) + (1 - \theta) f _ {2} (t).\tag{14}
$$

## Peak Times and Peak Adoption Rates

This section investigates two important statistics characterizing a diffusion process: (1) the peak adoption time $t ^ { * }$ , by which the adoption rate $f _ { { \scriptscriptstyle m } }$ reaches its maximum value, and (2) the peak adoption rate $f _ { m } ^ { * }$ . Because influentials and opponents aim to influence the diffusion process, useful insights as to how they can do so can be obtained by understanding these two statistics. Analytical solutions of $t ^ { * }$ and $f _ { m } ^ { * }$ can be obtained by solving $d f _ { m } ( t ) / d t = 0$ , where $f _ { m } ( t )$ is given in Equation (14). We present the numerical results in Figures 3 and 4.

Figure 3 demonstrates how opponents could affect the peak adoption time and rate. A threshold value for opponents’ influence factor divides the parameter space into two regions. In each region, the peak adoption rate behaves differently. For small values of $w ^ { \prime } .$ , an increase in $w ^ { \prime }$ leads to an increase in the peak adoption time. In the same region—that is, when the opponents’ influence factor is smaller than the threshold value—the peak adoption time is delayed for each diffusion process as $w ^ { \prime }$ increases.

At the threshold value, the negative effect exerted by opponents cancels the positive effect by influentials; therefore, the imitators’ adoption follows an independent path. A further increase in $w ^ { \prime }$ beyond the threshold value will no longer have any effect on the peak adoption time. This finding suggests that it is not wise for the opposition group to further invest in increasing its influence factor after a certain point. For example, the solid line in Figure 3 shows that the canceling point of $w ^ { \prime }$ is around $0 . 1 . ^ { 4 }$ The peak time curve drops suddenly right after the threshold of $w ^ { \prime } .$ , indicating that—once the influence from the influentials and the opponents cancels out—the adoption is purely from the influentials, whose behavior is independent and not affected by the opponents. However, the figure further shows that the peak adoption rate continues to drop as the opponent group increases its influence factor before the critical point and remains flat after that. From our analysis, we find that strengthening the influentials’ diffusion (by increasing $p _ { 1 } , q _ { 1 } , \operatorname { o r } w )$ increases the threshold value of $w ^ { \prime }$

Figure 4 shows how influentials affect the peak adoption time and rate. Like the opponents’ influence factor, a threshold value exists for the influentials’ influence factor that divides the parameter space into two regions. For small values of w, the peak adoption time is constant, as seen in Figure 4. This represents cases in which the positive effect of influentials is canceled out by the negative effect of opponents. After the threshold value of $w ,$ the peak adoption time decreases in the influence factor. The greater the value of w, the faster the adoption process reaches its maximum. Interestingly, we find that the influentials’ influence factor affects the peak adoption rate nonmonotonically. As shown in Figure 3, the peak adoption rate is monotonically decreasing in the opponents’ influence factor. However, Figure 4 demonstrates that the peak adoption rate is a nonmonotonic function of w. It may increase or decrease in w because the influentials influence factor, w, is negatively correlated with the imitators’ self-influence factor, 1 – w. An increase in w means a decrease in 1 – w; consequently, it does not result in monotonic $f _ { 2 } ( t ^ { * } )$ and $f _ { m } ( t ^ { * } )$ functions (see Equations (5) and (14)).

## Implications for Opponents

Opponents are looking for effective strategies to restrain the imitators’ adoption from reaching a certain level. To provide insights, we plot the imitators’ cumulative adoption function $F _ { _ 2 } ( t )$ with respect to parameters of opponents’ diffusion process: the influence factor $w ^ { \prime }$ , time-invariant factor $p _ { _ 3 } { \mathrm { : } }$ , and social contagion factor $q _ { 3 } ,$ as seen in Figure 5.

Figure 5 shows that the imitators’ cumulative adoption at $t = 3 0$ is a nonincreasing function of $w ^ { \prime } , p _ { 3 } ,$ and $q _ { 3 } .$ . Interestingly, in some cases (i.e., solid curves in the figures), the cumulative adoption could be reduced to zero. The zero-adoption point corresponds to the canceling point. However, in other cases, the canceling point does not exist. The cumulative adoption from imitators is always positive regardless of what the opponents do. In Figure 5b, for example, when $w ^ { \prime } = 0 . 0 1$ , the imitators adoption cannot decrease below 60 percent at time $t = 3 0$ irrespective of how large ${ { p } _ { 3 } }$ is. A similar pattern can also be observed in Figure 5c: no matter how large $q _ { 3 }$ is, the imitators’ cumulative adoption rate cannot be lower than a certain value when $w ^ { \prime } = 0 . 0 1$ . In these cases, the influence factor of opponents is small when compared to that of influentials, meaning that imitators’ adoption is not sensitive to the change in the opponents’ diffusion parameters. Overall, we conclude that focusing exclusively on its own diffusion process (by increasing $p _ { 3 }$ and $q _ { 3 } )$ may not be an effective strategy for the opponent group to exert the largest influence on imitators.

## Implications for Influentials

Influentials aim to increase imitators’ adoption. As shown in Figure 6, the imitators’ cumulative adoption rate is a nondecreasing function of the influentials’ diffusion parameters—the influence factor w, innovation factor $p _ { 1 } ,$ , and social contagion factor $q _ { 1 } ^ { \phantom { - } }$

When the influentials’ influence factor w exceeds the canceling point, they can positively influence the imitators’ adoption. As shown in Figure 6a, the cumulative adoption is always positive when w is greater than the canceling point. At any given time, an increase in innovation factor $p _ { 1 }$ or social contagion $q _ { 1 }$ of the influentials leads to an increase in the imitators’ cumulative adoption. However, such a positive effect is limited; an upper-bound value exists for the adoption level. Figures 6b and 6c demonstrate that the adoption stops when $p _ { 1 }$ or $q _ { 1 }$ are large enough. Thus, our quantitative study can help the influential group in practice. Practitioners can use our figures to analyze whether the imitators’ cumulative adoption has reached the target level, decide whether they are able to improve the adoption effectively, and quantify their promotional efforts.

## Data Descriptions

We obtained adoption data related to nine new products and services.<sup>5</sup> The data consisted of adoptions of high-technology products such as mammography, ultrasound, and scanners; home appliances such as air conditioners and clothes dryers; and agricultural products such as hybrid corn. The data represent quite a diverse set of products and services, thereby enabling us to evaluate our model’s performance in various settings and contexts. Furthermore, using the same data set as in the literature [34] allows us to compare our model with other existing models reliably. In order to draw conclusions regarding the relative performance of our model, we also adopt the same parameter estimation methods. Therefore, following Van den Bulte and Joshi [33], we used the Proc Model procedure in SAS. Various measures (criteria) can be used to gauge a model’s fit to data, including the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), mean absolute deviation (MAD), mean squared error (MSE), and the squared Pearson correlation. We used the MAPE value as our criterion in the performance comparison because the MAPE calculation is straightforward and the MAPE value is easy to interpret.<sup>6</sup>

## Model Performance Comparison

MAPE is calculated by averaging the percentages of the deviation of the estimation from the actual observation and is used as an indication of how well the estimation model fits the data. A small MAPE value indicates that the estimation produced by the model follows the actual data very closely. When two estimation models are compared, the model with a lower MAPE value provides a better fit to the data. Table 1 reports the MAPE comparison of our three-segment model (i.e., the model with influentials, imitators, and opponents) and the two-segment model (i.e., the model with influentials and imitators). Our model has lower MAPE values in eight out of nine series of the data set. The results clearly indicate that our three-segment model performs better than the two-segment model in terms of fitting the data.

Because our model has three segments (i.e., influentials, imitators, and opponents), it would be interesting to see how well the model fits the diffusion of each segment respectively. Next, we provide a detailed analysis on the CT scanners (50–99) data set. The CT scanners (50–99) data set reports the penetration of CT scanners among hospitals with 50 to 99 beds from 1979 to 1993. We focused on CT scanners because it is reasonable to expect the existence of strong opponent forces in their diffusion due to health concerns associated with the CT scanners.

## A Case Study: CT Scanners

Figure 7 plots the actual CT scanner adoption data and the estimated adoption of our model. Our model is able to track the bimodal distribution pattern in the original data precisely. By incorporating the existence of opponents, our three-segment model attains an MAPE value of 8.81 percent, representing a more than 60 percent performance improvement over the two-segment model of Van den Bulte and Joshi [33]. Figure 7 also plots the estimated adoption for three segments. The influential segment reaches its peak adoption rate around period 7. All influentials have already adopted the CT scanner by period 12; thereafter, the adoption is only composed of imitators. The new opponents reach their peak adoption at the beginning. As time passes, the number of new opponents decreases. Although more than 60 percent of opponents emerge within the first three periods, new opponents consistently exist 20 periods later. Hence, the CT scanner adoption is a battle between the fast diffusion of influentials and a long-standing opponent force, which results in a bimodal diffusion pattern for the total adopters.

## Further Evidence: Simulation Analysis

In the previous subsection, we demonstrated that our three-segment model fits the data better than the two-segment model of Van den Bulte and Joshi [33]. In this subsection, we show that this is not the only reason for the superiority of our model. By using simulated data, we show that our model corrects the two-segment misidentification problem, which an estimation using the two-segment diffusion model could produce. We resort to simulated data to validate our model performance since any statistical test is a joint test of model assumptions (in our case, whether the underlying data-generating process is a two-segment model or three-segment model) and model estimations. After fixing the underlying theoretical data-generating process, we can compare which model performs better in characterizing the segments of adopters.

We simulated data for a diffusion process by assuming that three segments of actors exist in the diffusion process. Accordingly, the numbers of influentials, imitators, and opponents were generated over time assuming a set of parameter values (see Table 2). We also added a random error term to each segment. After completing the simulation, we applied both the two-segment [33] and our three-segment models to the simulated data and estimated the model parameters. We then compared the original parameter values used to generate the simulated data with the estimated parameter values in both models (using the direct integration method implemented by the Proc Model procedure in SAS). Surprisingly, the parameters estimated based on the two-segment model may result in erroneous diffusion curves—that is, in some circumstances, the two-segment model mistakenly identified the influentials as imitators and vice versa.

Identifying segments of adopters is important in practice. In many business settings, organizations strive to determine the right characterization of the segments of adopters as this would give them an edge over others. Imagine that a new social networking site wants to promote its services among potential adopters. Because different segments of adopters may require different kinds of persuasions and targeting, it is essential for the site to identify the segments of adopters correctly in order to devise its promotion strategies. The failure to identify the segments of adopters could undermine promotion efforts and may even lead to a market failure. Given the significance of segment misidentification, it is important to determine conditions under which the use of the two-segment model is likely to result in such a problem. Analysis reported in the $\mathrm { A p } \cdot$ pendix reveals that the two-segment model is more likely to cause the misidentification problem when the ratio of $q _ { 1 }$ to $p _ { 1 }$ or that of $q _ { 3 }$ to $p _ { 3 }$ is high. ## The Diffusion of Cell Phones

In th is sec tion, we apply our th ree-segm ent m od el to analyze the diffusion of cell phones. Based on real data, we aim to glean insights into the diffusion process and, hence, predict the future diffusion trend.

The debates over the health risks caused by mobile devices have been active for decades. Some people are opposed to using cell phones in real life. Therefore, we believe that our three-segment model is appropriate for analyzing the cell phone diffusion process. We considered the adoption of a cell phone at the individual level. We obtained data on cell phone usage in the United States from 1984 to 2006 from the U.S. Census.<sup>8</sup> The number of cell phone users grew exponentially over this period, from 92,000 in 1984 to more than 233 million in 2006. We used data from 1984 to 2001 to estimate the model parameters and then used the data from the last four years (i.e., 2002 to 2006) to validate the predictive power of our model. The MAPE value for the model estimation for the last four years is 5.85 percent, showing that our model provides a good fit to the real data. Figure 9 depicts both the actual and predicted diffusion curves as well as the curves for each segment. For ease of exposition, the left vertical axis represents the scale for influentials and imitators, while the right vertical axis represents the scale for opponents. Note that there is no influential group; hence, the curve for predicted adopters overlaps the one for imitators. Yet a strong opponent group came into the market in 1990 and reached its peak in 1992; the rate at which new opponents emerged diminished after 1994.

The last four data points on the predicted data curve are our forecast of cell phone adoption from 2002 to 2006. Comparing these four data points to cell phone adoption from 2002 to 2006, we concluded that our model is able to predict the trend of cell phone adoption correctly. We also compared the two- and three-segment models in terms of their abilities to fit the cell phone diffusion data set. We found that the MAPE value of the two-segment model is 0.298 and that of our three-segment model is 0.200.

Therefore, we conclude that the three-segment model fits the cell phone diffusion data better than the two-segment model.

## Conclusion

In th is paper, we proposed an IT d iffusion m od el with influentials, opponents, and imitators. The model considers asymmetric influence among these three groups of actors. The first two groups—influentials and opponents—have a positive and negative effect, respectively, on imitators’ technology adoption decisions, while imitators have no effect on the other two groups’ behavior. Mathematical solutions to the model and numerical analyses were presented. The proposed three-segment model is a general diffusion model in which traditional diffusion models serve as its special cases. Our model is appropriate for describing the diffusion process of new technologies, especially when significant anti-adoption forces exist. Our findings show that when the influence factor of opponents is large (i.e., exceeding a critical level), it will always cancel out the positive effects from influentials. As a result, imitators’ adoption is completely stopped. In general, the existence of opponents will reduce the maximum adoption rate of technology, delay the adoption process, and change the shape of the diffusion curve significantly.

We further demonstrated the importance of the influence factor through a sensitivity analysis. For both influential and opposition groups, in most cases, investing in improving their influence power over imitators (e.g., through education and knowledge sharing) is much more effective than speeding up the growth of their own groups. Our model can be useful for practitioners. Software and hardware vendors, who are typically technology influentials, can use our model to estimate and control the diffusion of a technology through its prospective adopters. Opposition groups, who are against the new technology/innovation, can use our model to restrain the adoption to a target level. Our model can potentially be used to describe the diffusion of all technologies where significant anti-adoption forces exist.

We provided three kinds of empirical evidence to justify the importance and superiority of our three-segment model. First, using nine real diffusion data sets, we proved that, in general, our model fits the real data better than models that do not consider the opponent group in the diffusion process. Second, we showed the risks of not recognizing the existence of opponents in a diffusion model. In 17 out of 39 simulated data sets, we found that the two-segment model resulted in the misidentification problem— that is, it incorrectly characterized the real influentials as imitators and vice versa. Misidentification of segments of adopters can lead to serious managerial mistakes. However, this problem can be overcome using our three-segment model. Finally, we applied our model to the diffusion of an IT-related innovation—cell phones. We identified the diffusion patterns of three segments for the technology and predicted its future adoption trend.

Our work leaves interesting avenues for future research. First, with better data sets, more rigorous empirical validations of our model should be performed in the IT domain. Second, as our analysis shows, influentials and opponents are able to use various strategies to control the diffusion process in opposite ways. The strategies available to the actors in the diffusion process are not endogenous in our current model. Therefore, one would expect that choices of strategies can be modeled in a game theory framework. Third, our model can be extended to dynamic settings. Currently, model parameters are assumed to be constant. It would be rewarding to allow some of these parameters to vary over time, which may lead to a better prediction of the future. Finally, we assumed that each actor in our model is ex ante associated with a particular segment. Future research can relax this assumption and investigate how a change in an actor’s role over time affects the diffusion process.
