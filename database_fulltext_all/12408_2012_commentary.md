# Commentary

Jeffrey James

Economics Department, Tilburg University, The Netherlands.

E-mail: m.j.james@uvt.nl

Journal of Information Technology (2012) 27, 342–343. doi:10.1057/jit.2012.21;

published online 2 October 2012

C <sup>haudhuri</sup> <sup>has</sup> <sup>written</sup> <sup>a</sup> <sup>provocative</sup> <sup>and</sup> <sup>timely</sup> <sup>paper.</sup>His main thesis is that there are very few rigorous His main thesis is that there are very few rigorous evaluations of individual ICT projects in developing countries and that one should be correspondingly sceptical of promoting these technologies in such countries (It is inconsistent though also to assert that Internet projects mostly fail for without some sort of evaluative framework how can failure be established?). What I will argue below is that the author somewhat overstates his case and more tellingly looks in the wrong place for support of the contention that this technology promotes growth.

Although Chaudhuri is correct in bemoaning the lack of hard evidence at the project level,<sup>1</sup> it is crucial to note that this level itself tells us little or nothing about the overall economy-wide impact of ICT. And it is this more aggregate level that ultimately determines the success or failure of the technology (the more so since ICT is a so-called general purpose technology that spreads across all sectors of the economy). Individual projects have a macro impact only if they are widely replicated across the country. One such case of Grameen Telecom in Bangladesh helps to validate my claim that the author has somewhat overstated his case about the lack of project evaluation because this model has been very widely diffused and evaluated several times (e.g., Bayes et al., 1999).<sup>2</sup> Grameen Telecom can be considered a major success because it has managed to place a rental mobile phone in at least half of Bangladeshi villages, involving tens of millions of villagers, and respondents are apt to claim that they are better off with the phone than without it. It is odd that the author fails to mention this project given that it is one of the world’s most successful IT endeavours, with benefits accruing even to the rural poor.

A simple if crude measure of macro success or failure is the degree of IT adoption in a developing country (of course, adoption does not necessarily translate into individual benefits, especially in the case of the Internet, where user capabilities are usually low, but this is much less of a problem for mobile phones that are far easier to use). On this measure, it is well known that mobile phones have recorded unprecedented rates of growth while the Internet has lagged somewhat behind.<sup>3</sup>

These rates of adoption indicators of IT impact, however, are likely to be quite heavily understated. For, with respect to both mobile phones and the Internet sharing is common in developing countries, yet this form of behaviour is not recorded in official statistics. Notable examples of Internet sharing are Peru, where there are at least 32.000 cybercafe´s and Brazil with 58.000 (Coward et al., 2008). With regard to mobile phones sharing takes place predominantly between family and between family and their friends. It is not unusual in Africa, for example, for a single phone to be shared by four or five non-owners of the technology (James, 2011).

More compelling macro evidence comes from models that devote themselves specifically to the task of estimating the impact of IT penetration on growth in developing countries. Qiang and Rossotto (2009), for example, are the first to have attempted to measure the economic impact of broadband in developing countries. They begin by noting that examining the overall impact is a logical way to assess the implications of broadband diffusion because it takes a more comprehensive view than looking only at impacts on individuals, firms, or communities (p. 39). What they find is that the growth benefit that broadband provides for developing countries is of similar magnitude as that for developed economics – about a 1.38% point increase for each 10% increase in penetration (p. 45). Their main conclusion provides powerful support for those who believe in the transformative power of IT in developing countries. In particular, ‘broadband has a significant impact on growth and deserves a central role in country development and competitiveness strategies’ (p. 35).

The study by Waverman et al. (2005) is on mobile phones rather than the Internet. Using a sophisticated macro-model they find that:

Mobile telephony has a positive and significant impact on growth and this impact may be twice as large in developing countries compared to developed countries. The result concurs with intuition. Developed economies by and large had fully articulated fixed-line networks in 1996 y In developing countries, we find that the growth dividend is far larger because here mobile phones provide, by and large, the main communications networks, hence they supplant the information gathering role of fixed line systems (p. 10; emphasis in original).

As in the study of the effect of broadband on economic growth, it is helpful to provide some sense of the magnitudes involved. Thus, a ‘developing country which had an average of 10 more mobile phones per 100 population between 1996 and 2003 would have enjoyed per capita GDP growth that was 0.59 higher than an otherwise identical country’ (p. 11).

The final macro study, by Jorgenson and Vu (2005), is of IT in general and estimates the contribution (between 1995 and 2003) of that technology to growth rates in a range of countries, developed and developing. From my point of view, the most striking of their findings is that for Latin America IT investment contributed nearly 20% to growth rates of countries in the region. As one would expect from penetration rate differences, the contribution to growth is less in poorer countries, amounting to 10% in the case of Sub-Saharan Africa. During the past 10 years or so, these estimates are likely to have increased substantially as IT has spread widely in the developing countries.

My conclusion is thus that far from having to rely on an admittedly scarce and anecdotal micro studies (as described by the author), policymakers can point instead to the macro studies cited above as serious support for policy intended to promote IT and growth in developing countries (though of course there remains a need for many more micro studies to learn what is successful and replicable at this level).<sup>4</sup>

## Notes

1 There are, however, more micro studies and evaluations than the author allows. Some of the micro-evidence is reported in Best and Kenny (2009) and Day and Greenwood (2009) while international institutions such as the World Bank routinely evaluate their projects, including those with an IT component.

2 One reason is that penetration tends to ignore quality differences in IT. Another reason is of course that adoption is only part of the overall welfare effect, which, at least in the case of the Internet, is heavily conditioned by the way in which the technology is used.

3 Chaudhuri spends quite a lot of time trying to explain this difference but he does not consider the role of leapfrogging in the mobile success story. This concerns the ability of developing countries to bypass previous stages of technology (most notably the fixed-line infrastructure) and jump directly to mobile telephony. The ability of developing countries to leapfrog in this way is reflected in a high ratio of mobile to fixed-lines.

4 The point is not that the macro models are beyond criticism. Like all models they make assumptions that may or may not be realistic.
