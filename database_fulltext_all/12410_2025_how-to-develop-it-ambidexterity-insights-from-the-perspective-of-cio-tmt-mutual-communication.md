# How to Develop IT Ambidexterity: Insights from the Perspective of CIO-TMT Mutual Communication

Nianxin Wang,<sup>1</sup> Hao Hu,<sup>2</sup> Huigang Liang,<sup>3</sup> Yajiong Xue<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>College of Economics and Management, Jiangsu University of Science and Technology, China, nianxin.wang@gmail.com <sup>2</sup> School of Digital Economy and Management, Wuxi University, China, huhao\_just@163.com <sup>3</sup>Fogelman College of Business and Economics, University of Memphis, USA, huigang.liang@gmail.com <sup>4</sup>College of Business, East Carolina University, USA, xuey@ecu.edu

## Abstract

Although IT ambidexterity can improve organizational agility and firm performance, limited attention has been paid to how it is developed. This article theoretically elaborates and empirically examines the effects of two types of communication modes between the chief information officer and the top management team, formal mutual communication (FMC) and informal mutual communication (IFMC), on IT ambidexterity, or the proportional balance between IT exploitation and IT exploration and how these effects vary across organizational and environmental contexts. Based on fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis of the survey data from 256 dyads of business and IT executives from the Chinese shipbuilding industry, we found that all configurations for achieving IT ambidexterity are observed in dynamic environments where both FMC and IFMC can play enabling roles, depending on the specific firm size. In addition, both modes can serve as enablers of IT exploitation. While IFMC always enables IT exploration, FMC may be irrelevant in some contexts or even counterproductive in other contexts. This paper expands the literature on IT ambidexterity by investigating FMC and IFMC as its antecedents, which makes a significant contribution to both IS research and practice.

Keywords: IT Ambidexterity, IT Exploitation, IT Exploration, Mutual Communication, Configuration, Fuzzy-Set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA)

## 1 Introduction

In today’s turbulent business environment, organizations strive to achieve competitive advantage by investing in information technologies (ITs) to seize fleeting opportunities and respond to impending threats (D’Aveni et al., 2010; Overby et al., 2006; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). Besides using existing IT resources, firms are exploring IT innovations to meet their changing business needs. This dualistic IT behavior is termed by Gartner (2014) as bimodal IT, consisting of Model 1, which emphasizes stability and exploitation, and Model 2, which focuses on innovation and exploration. <sup>1</sup> Many companies have embraced bimodal IT (Haffke et al., 2017). According to Gartner’s (2014) survey of more than 2,800 CIOs worldwide, 38% of enterprises have implemented bimodal IT, and 26% plan to implement it within three years (Chen, 2016). In the post-pandemic era, bimodal IT has become even more crucial, playing a pivotal role for firms to pursue digital transformation (Kusanke & Winkler, 2022) and other digital enterprise goals (Jayadev, 2023). Recognizing this trend, consulting companies such as Accenture and Deloitte have begun providing services to help firms plan and implement bimodal IT.

Like the concept of bimodal IT used by practitioners, academics refer to IT ambidexterity (ITA) to describe a firm’s ability to balance both IT exploitation and exploration (Chi et al., 2017). IT exploitation stands for the efficient refinement of existing IT resources, whereas IT exploration denotes experimentation with new IT resources (Liang et al., 2022). ITA is defined as the firm’s ability to simultaneously pursue exploitation and exploration in managing IT resources and practices (Lee et al., 2015). Information systems (IS) scholars have confirmed that ITA can enhance organizational agility (Lee et al., 2015) and performance (Syed et al., 2024).

Despite the value of ITA, limited attention has been paid to understanding its development (Appendix A), except for Syed et al. (2024), which examines how directive and participative managerial activities enable ITA. In contrast, the antecedents of organizational ambidexterity have been extensively studied (Raisch & Birkinshaw, 2008), including structure (Benner & Tushman, 2003; Tushman & O’Reilly, 1996), context (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004), leadership characteristics (Jansen et al., 2008; Lubatkin et al., 2006), and contingency factors like firm size and environment (Park et al., 2020). In particular, the top management team (TMT) has been found to play a critical role in achieving organizational ambidexterity (Heavey & Simsek, 2017) by building a supportive context, reconciling implicit tensions (Smith & Tushman, 2005), and making appropriate decisions between exploration and exploitation (Syed et al., 2019).

Considering that ITA involves managing IT knowledge and asset portfolios in uncertain environments, two key points warrant attention when investigating its antecedents. First, the chief information officer (CIO) is pivotal. In modern firms, CIOs are responsible not only for managing existing IT resources to ensure short-term operational efficiency (Peppard, 2010) but also for exploring new ITs (Weill & Woerner, 2013) for longterm competitive advantages. With increasing involvement in strategy formulation (Applegate & Elam, 1992; Grover et al., 1993; Karahanna & Preston, 2013), CIOs shape the TMT’s understanding of IT implications (Chatterjee et al., 2001; Li et al., 2021). By convincing the TMT of the strategic value of IT, CIOs can effectively contribute to digital innovation success (Chen et al., 2021).

Second, IT exploitation and exploration are distinct activities, each with its own focus and goals, requiring different knowledge management strategies. IT exploitation prioritizes efficiency and control, relying on users to apply current knowledge, whereas IT exploration promotes innovation and creativity, demanding that users acquire new knowledge. Thus, firm-wide information exchange and knowledge sharing are crucial to enable these activities, notably in sensemaking—the process wherein the CIO and TMT negotiate and give meaning to their collective experiences. Literature suggests that the interconnection and sharing of distinct knowledge and perspectives among TMT members can influence the development of ambidextrous orientation (Heavey & Simsek, 2017). Thus, mutual communication between the CIO and TMT can help nurture ITA by enabling executives to share knowledge and handle ambiguity and conflicting demands (Karahanna & Preston, 2013) associated with IT exploitation and exploration.

According to Armstrong and Sambamurthy (1999) and Preston and Karahanna (2009), there are two CIO-TMT communication modes: formal mutual communication (FMC) and informal mutual communication (IFMC). FMC is based on formal working relationships, allowing structured, official interactions (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998), while IFMC is based on informal social relationships for easy and frequent communication (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Karahanna & Preston, 2013). Research shows that formal mechanisms are effective in creating a shared understanding (Preston & Karahanna, 2009), and informal interactions can build trust-based relationships to promote consensus (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Thus, the deliberate integration of formal and informal communication modes could help alleviate the contradiction and paradox inherent in ITA (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; Jansen et al., 2008; Smith & Tushman, 2005).

Despite the theoretical link between FMC/IFMC and ITA, empirical research on their roles in achieving ITA remains scant. FMC and IFMC entail different organizational arrangements and may exert distinct influences. FMC reflects a hierarchy culture that values routinization, stability, and process formalization (Hartnell et al., 2019), which is suitable for streamlining existing business operations and exploiting known resources and practices, while IFMC emphasizes easy and relaxed interactions without hierarchical control and bureaucratic barriers, which can stimulate creativity. Given that ITA involves IT exploitation and exploration (Lee et al., 2015), FMC and IFMC are both essential for its development.

It should be noted that the effects of FMC and IFMC may vary in different contexts. Researchers have called for increased recognition of contingencies in ambidexterity research (Gupta et al., 2006; Jansen et al., 2006). It is necessary to examine the organizational and environmental conditions under which FMC and IFMC enable firms to achieve ITA. Externally, environmental dynamism determines whether firms should exploit or explore (Lavie et al., 2010). Internally, a firm’s resources, often indicated by its size (Josefy et al., 2015), dictate its IT practices. Syed et al. (2024) demonstrate that specific firm sizes are associated with distinct managerial activities affecting ITA. Environmental dynamism is also widely recognized as a crucial factor in this process (Park et al., 2020; Raisch & Birkinshaw, 2008). Moreover, firms of varying sizes operating in diverse environments require different CIO-TMT communication modes. For example, larger firms tend to rely more on FMC, while dynamic environments often benefit more from IFMC. Therefore, we incorporate firm size and environmental dynamism as contingency factors and explore how they interact with FMC and IFMC to enhance ITA.

Furthermore, the proportional balance view of ITA (Liang et al., 2022) suggests that simultaneous high levels of IT exploitation and exploration may not always be beneficial. Rather, specific capability needs to be high or low to produce benefits for a given situation. To gain deeper insights, it is imperative to explore how to configure different antecedents to develop IT exploitation and exploration separately. Thus, we endeavor to answer the question: How do FMC and IFMC, under varying firm size and environmental dynamism, affect ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration in a configurational manner?

We address this inquiry using a configurational approach (Fiss, 2011; Ragin, 2008), which is suitable for analyzing how combinations of antecedents influence outcomes in various contexts (Park et al., 2017). Specifically, we explore how FMC, IFMC, environmental dynamism, and firm size combine into multiple configurations to achieve ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration. Fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) is used to analyze the survey data from 256 dyads of business and IT executives from the Chinese shipbuilding industry. Based on the findings, we uncover the contingency effects of environmental dynamism and firm size and propose theoretical propositions.

This study makes several contributions to IS research. First, it extends the existing literature on ITA by exploring its antecedents from the perspectives of mutual communication and knowledge sharing. We examine how FMC, IFMC and other contingency elements combine to produce ITA. Our results reveal multiple paths to ITA and the boundary conditions that determine the effectiveness of FMC and IFMC. Second, building on the proportional balance view of ITA (Liang et al., 2022), we go one step further to examine the development of IT exploitation and exploration. Since these two capabilities may not need to be strong simultaneously, their antecedents should be understood separately. Results indicate that both FMC and IFMC can be configured in various ways to achieve IT exploitation. For IT exploration, IFMC consistently plays a core and enabling role, whereas FMC may be irrelevant or even counterproductive under specific conditions. Finally, we extend the research on mutual communication between the CIO and TMT (Preston & Karahanna, 2009) by showing the potential effects of FMC and IFMC on ITA. We examine the configurations of FMC and IFMC after conceptualizing their differences in terms of content, purpose, and outcome. Although Preston and Karahanna (2009) did not find that IFMC enhances shared understanding and IT alignment, we demonstrate its significant role in developing ITA.

## 2 Theory Background and Research Model

## 2.1 IT Ambidexterity and its Antecedents

Literature suggests that firms need high levels of both exploitation and exploration (Gupta et al., 2006; Jansen et al., 2006). According to March (1991), exploitation is incremental, aiming to broaden existing knowledge through the efficient leveraging and refinement of current resources using known processes. Exploration is radical and requires new knowledge or a departure from existing knowledge, involving the discovery and management of resources in experimental and novel ways to create new opportunities. Studies have confirmed the synergistic value of pursuing both fundamentally different processes to achieve ambidexterity (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; Im & Rai, 2008).

Consistent with this ambidextrous perspective, IS scholars introduced ITA, defining it as a firm’s ability to simultaneously pursue exploitative and exploratory management and use of its IT resources and practices (Lee et al., 2015). Specifically, IT exploitation involves efficiently leveraging and refining existing IT resources through established practices, such as utilizing current IT portfolios, appropriating and assimilating them in business processes based on proven methods, and acquiring complementary technologies to enhance effectiveness. In contrast, IT exploration entails experimenting with new IT resources or practices, investigating emerging ITs, evaluating the pros and cons of alternatives, and selecting one that is most likely to bring operational and strategic benefits to the firm. Developing ITA thus requires managing both current and emerging ITs to realize their potential business values. Given today’s turbulent business environments, ITA is particularly desirable as firms face increased pressure to promptly sense and respond to market changes (Gupta et al., 2006; O’Reilly & Tushman, 2013).

While prior research has shown that ITA can enhance organizational agility (Lee et al., 2015; Liang et al., 2022), its antecedents have received less attention. IT exploitation builds on existing IT resources and practices to strengthen capabilities and streamline business processes, whereas IT exploration pursues new IT resources and practices to create innovative capabilities and competitive advantages. These approaches have drastically different goals and may induce paradoxical tensions (Gregory et al., 2015). IT exploitation emphasizes efficiency and control, prioritizes safety, and requires executives to be proficient in applying their existing knowledge, whereas IT exploration encourages innovation and creative thinking, tolerates failures, and requires new knowledge and skill sets (O’Reilly & Tushman, 2008). Research shows that, through a combination of directive and participative managerial activities, executives can not only clarify role definitions and assign responsibilities for IT exploitation but also encourage creative information discussions and facilitate novel knowledge sharing for IT exploration (Syed et al., 2024). As such, developing ITA needs diverse knowledge (new/existing) and coordination of distinct activities to accommodate the underlying uncertainties and ambiguities.

Research on organizational ambidexterity highlights the crucial role of TMT (Jansen et al., 2008; Syed et al., 2020). Firms need to skillfully integrate new/existing and internal/external knowledge to balance exploitation and exploration (Raisch et al., 2009). TMT is in the best position to broker between the firm and its environment and coordinate organizational units (Smith & Tushman, 2005). Studies suggest that generating, distributing, and integrating knowledge within TMTs enhances a firm’s ability to differentiate and consolidate strategic agendas for ambidexterity (Heavey & Simsek, 2017). When strong cohesion and synergy are achieved, both exploitation and exploration strategies can be successfully implemented (Chandrasekaran et al., 2012).

These findings also apply to ITA. In a complex digital landscape, enterprise IS are tightly enmeshed with business operations. In such cases, firms cannot rely solely on employees to decide when to exploit existing ITs or explore new ones. Instead, it is typically the responsibility of TMTs to make strategic decisions at the organizational level, determining when, where, and how to retain, renew, or replace critical IT resources and capabilities (Wang et al., 2012). Nevertheless, TMT members lack sufficient technical IT knowledge to effectively manage IT exploitation and IT exploration. Thus, the CIO responsible for IT strategy (Karahanna & Preston, 2013) should be involved in the decision-making process for ITA.

Research has demonstrated that the presence of CIOs can enhance the identification of managerial opportunities and emphasize the strategic application of innovative IT (Carter et al., 2011). With extensive, diverse IT knowledge, CIOs can effectively leverage existing IT resources or introduce new ones to align with business strategies. Specifically, the CIO’s IT expertise can inform TMT in managing IT resources (Simons et al., 1999; Smith et al., 1994), fostering a common understanding of IT’s roles within the firm (Johnson & Lederer, 2005). Through effective interaction and knowledge exchange, executives can reconcile various information demands in pursuing ITA, leading to consensus and well-informed decisions.

In sum, we propose that mutual communication and knowledge sharing between the CIO and TMT can synergize IT exploitative and exploratory activities, thereby facilitating ITA. Prior research has also emphasized the role of contingency factors, such as external environments and internal resources, in determining firms’ IT activities (Gupta et al., 2006; Jansen et al., 2006). Thus, to further investigate ITA’s boundary conditions (Fourné et al., 2019), we explore how different types of mutual communication, combined with environmental dynamism as a proxy for external contingency and organizational size as a proxy for internal contingency (Josefy et al., 2015), affect ITA.

## 2.2 Mutual Communication between TMT and the CIO

For mutual communication and knowledge sharing to occur and subsequently contribute to ITA, effective communication modes or organizational arrangements should be built. Preston and Karahanna (2009) proposed that firms provide two distinct communication modes: FMC and IFMC. Both are mechanisms for establishing interaction, sharing ideas, and developing shared understanding between the CIO and TMT. From the issue-selling perspective (Chen et al., 2021), the CIO-TMT partnership is critical for enhancing the effectiveness of CIO issue selling by capturing the attention of senior executives towards digital initiatives, emphasizing the significance of formal and informal interactions. Additionally, these close CIO-TMT work or social relationships can enable continuous knowledge-sharing and consensus about current or new IT resources, thereby paving the way for the initiation of exploitative and exploratory activities.

Literature has confirmed that formal and informal communication mechanisms (Jansen et al., 2006) and senior management team integration mechanisms (Jansen et al., 2009) are key contributors to organizational ambidexterity. By the same token, we posit that FMC and IFMC can be juxtaposed to achieve ITA. By enhancing information exchange and knowledge sharing, FMC and IFMC enable the CIO and TMT to collectively understand how existing IT resources can support the current business strategy and how emerging IT can drive new strategies (Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999). The resulting common understandings can facilitate the alignment of current/emerging IT portfolios with existing/new opportunities (Liang et al., 2017), thus helping to develop ITA.

Nevertheless, as shown in Table 1, FMC and IFMC differ in content and purpose and generate distinct outcomes. FMC includes the hierarchical level of the CIO (e.g., the direct reporting relationship from the CIO to the CEO), formal cross-functional interface (e.g., the CIO participation in the TMT), and formal interaction channels (e.g., official meetings or workrelated phone calls). It aims to enable organized, controlled, and official interactions that help increase executives’ IT and business knowledge (Preston & Karahanna, 2009) and develop a shared view of the firm’s vision, environments, strategies, and goals from a holistic perspective (Jansen et al., 2006, 2009). Specifically, FMC embodies a hierarchical culture, emphasizing formal controls and embedded processes to streamline business operations or resource utilization with existing knowledge and skills. Hence, compared to enhancing consensus on the exploration of new IT opportunities, FMC is more conducive to formulating formal plans and routines to exploit current IT resources.

In contrast, IFMC delineates the informal interactions and exchanges characterized by casualness and flexibility. IFMC is not intended to execute predefined tasks; rather, it accentuates comfortable and relaxed communication between the CIO and TMT. According to Jansen et al. (2009), social interaction and open exchanges can positively moderate the work environment to spark innovation and new ways of thinking and learning. Thus, the goal of IFMC is to eliminate hierarchical constraints and language barriers, facilitating the timely exchange of new insights and inspiring creative ideas. Thus, compared to refining existing IT assets, IFMC is more conducive to creating new knowledge and experimenting with new IT resources.

## 2.3 Decomposition of IT Ambidexterity

Previous literature has assumed that IT exploitation and IT exploration are equally important (Lee et al., 2015). However, Liang et al. (2022) introduced the view of proportional balance and empirically demonstrated that IT exploitation and exploration do not necessarily need to be at the same level. Depending on the degree of environmental dynamism, varying levels of IT exploitation and IT exploration (e.g., high or low) can engender optimal benefits. This view offers novel insights into ITA, highlighting that IT exploitation and exploration should be treated separately.

Given the distinct natures of IT exploitation and exploration, they may have different antecedents. For instance, exploration is associated with organic structures like improvisation and loosely coupled systems, whereas exploitation is associated with mechanistic structures, characterized by path dependence and routinization (Asif, 2017; Benner & Tushman, 2003). To better understand the antecedents of ITA, we differentiate between IT exploitation and IT exploration and examine the effects of FMC/IFMC on each.

## 2.4 Achieving IT Ambidexterity with Two Mutual Communication Modes

CIOs are crucial in helping TMTs understand a firm’s IT capabilities and digital resources. Drawing on upper echelons theory, Li et al. (2021) demonstrated that CIOs significantly influence firms’ AI orientation, especially when interacting with board members with diverse knowledge and experiences. Chen et al. (2021) proposed that the CIO-TMT partnership, along with their formal and informal interactions, is essential for establishing common views on digital solutions and promoting the effectiveness of CIO issue selling.

## 2.4.1 FMC and IFMC with IT Ambidexterity

Organizational advantages can be achieved from top executives’ collective ability to exchange, combine, and integrate knowledge (Karahanna & Preston, 2013). Both FMC and IFMC can facilitate information exchange and knowledge sharing between the CIO and TMT (Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999). By constructing effective interaction channels and organizational arrangements, CIOs and TMT can engage in thorough discussions regarding the exploitation and exploration of IT resources and practices. Specifically, FMC can enhance shared understanding, helping to identify firm paradoxes (Preston & Karahanna, 2009), and IFMC is pivotal in constructing the trust-based CIO-TMT relationships that foster common views (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). These understandings and common views can create opportunities for resource exchange and combination across exploitative and exploratory activities (Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998).

Mutual understanding and information sharing between the CIO and TMT create a pool of diverse ideas and knowledge about ITs (Somech, 2006; Syed et al., 2024). Executives can leverage this knowledge to refine their existing IT resources and discover new IT opportunities (Jansen et al., 2006, 2008). Continuous feedback and information flow incorporate diverse knowledge, improve existing IT resources application, and enhance executives’ exploration capabilities. Thus, knowledge sharing and mutual communication between the CIO and TMT help resolve ambiguity and conflicts underlying ITA.

Both FMC and IFMC can contribute to ITA, but they have different foci and expected outcomes. FMC emphasizes routinization, stability, and process formalization (Hartnell et al., 2019), making it suitable for streamlining existing business operations and exploiting known resources. In contrast, IFMC values easy and relaxed interactions that generate new knowledge and ideas, aiding IT exploration. By integrating and coordinating these two modes, firms can achieve synergistic effects that enhance ITA (Lee et al., 2015).

## 2.4.2 FMC and IFMC with IT Exploitation

March (1991, p. 85) asserted that “the essence of exploitation is the refinement and extension of existing competencies, technologies, and paradigms” where its goal is to reuse and renew existing IT resources and capabilities. The key to successful exploitation is ensuring efficiency, implementation, and execution. FMC between the CIO and TMT is most effective for facilitating IT exploitation, given that the associated tasks tend to be familiar, well-defined, predictable, and oriented toward existing IT knowledge, thus requiring more structure and routinization (Daft & Lengel, 1986). Hierarchical control and formal communication are intended to streamline business operations by using existing resources and knowledge. FMC embodies a hierarchy culture that values order, control, and stability, characterized by routinization, process formalization, and uniformity (Hartnell et al., 2019). In this mode, TMT and the CIO collaborate to develop processes, policies, and rules to ensure IT exploitation. Over time, the most efficient routines become best practices through a positive feedback loop, making these IT exploitation activities more entrenched (Sydow et al., 2009). However, this can somewhat reduce the firm’s adaptability and openness to new initiatives.

In contrast, IFMC creates a relaxing and accessible communication environment that fosters rich interaction between the CIO and TMT, facilitating the flow of information and knowledge. Such informal social communication can occur both inside and outside the workplace, offering various opportunities for executives to acquaint themselves with one another and cultivate social connections. As work is often discussed in casual settings, the firm benefits from these more relaxed interactions, where bonding among executives creates a social “glue” and sense of clan that can help them overcome workplace challenges. Thus, the social relations and IFMC between the CIO and TMT encourage adequate exchanges on business operations and strategies, including how to better leverage existing IT knowledge and resources to reap their full benefits (Dyer & Nobeoka, 2000; Wang et al., 2011). These exchanges and knowledge sharing can result in organizational routines or policies that directly enhance IT exploitation.

## 2.4.3 FMC and IFMC with IT Exploration

According to March (1991, p. 85), “the essence of exploration is experimentation with new alternatives.” Due to its experimental nature, the benefits of exploration are harder to accrue and assess. Researchers have long noticed that exploration payoffs are more difficult, time-consuming, and uncertain to obtain (Benner & Tushman, 2015). Similarly, IT exploration involves adopting new IT resources or practices with new knowledge that can introduce great ambiguity or insecurity. Galbraith (1974) stated that “the greater the uncertainty of the task, the greater the amount of information that has to be processed among decision makers during the execution of the task” (p. 28). Given the emerging nature of internal and external changes, it is essential for the TMT and the CIO to share knowledge and information through open-ended exchange and reconcile conflicting views through sensemaking, defined by Weick (1995) as “the ongoing retrospective development of plausible images that rationalize what people are doing” (p. 409).

FMC, by providing formal communication channels, enables interaction between the TMT and CIO for knowledge sharing and integration (Armstrong &

Sambamurthy, 1999; Preston & Karahanna, 2009), which is essential for firms to address uncertainty and avoid equivocality in IT exploration. FMC can also enable more focused discussions, ensuring rigorous and purposeful communication of knowledge. However, its formal nature may limit creativity and the exchange of ideas. Since IT exploration requires nonroutine problem-solving and the deviation from existing knowledge, FMC is a misfit. Moreover, firms often favor conservative approaches such as exploitation over the less controllable form of exploration due to the tendency of uncertainty avoidance, resistance to change, or challenges from dynamic environments (Benner & Tushman, 2015). Influenced by this mindset, the information and knowledge transferred through FMC are less likely to nurture new ideas for experimenting with new IT, especially when the current ITs are seemingly doing well. In this sense, FMC’s impact on IT exploration may be indefinite.

IFMC, on the contrary, is largely free from the constraints of formal hierarchical structure, enabling more exchange of information and knowledge among executives. This informal communication helps the CIO and TMT overcome language barriers, reduce costs, and increase mutual appreciation and credibility (Chen et al., 2021), allowing them to integrate their domain knowledge to effectively address challenges and seize opportunities related to IT exploration (Liang et al., 2017). Additionally, IFMC can boost timely communication without the delay common to formal reporting procedures (Hu et al., 2023). Research has shown that formal structures, standard procedures, and planned responses are too slow, disconnected, and insufficient to deal with the speed and volatility of environmental change (Faraj & Xiao, 2006). IFMC is flexible enough to bypass bureaucracies and red tape to accelerate and reap benefits from IT exploration.

Moreover, IFMC fosters interpersonal relationships and social capital between the CIO and TMT (Karahanna & Preston, 2013). In the organizational setting, the “clan” atmosphere of social exchanges promotes interpersonal closeness and trust, which can encourage risk-taking and inspire creative ideas for IT exploration (Hartnell et al., 2019). Subramaniam and Youndt (2005) have also confirmed that social relations help establish the legitimacy of creative IT solutions. Therefore, the informality of these relationships, typical in IFMC but absent from FMC, is conducive to IT exploration. Nevertheless, the lack of formal interaction can reduce decision-making efficiency, potentially compromising IT exploration.

## 2.5 Configurational and Contextual Perspective for IT Ambidexterity

This paper explores how FMC and IFMC interact with other organizational and environmental factors to affect the development of ITA, as well as its two different dimensions, IT exploitation and IT exploration based on the notion of proportional balance (Liang et al., 2022). Given that the effects of FMC and IFMC are interrelated and contingent on contextual factors (Park et al., 2020), we apply a configurational approach (El Sawy et al., 2010; Fiss, 2011) to explore how these two communication modes combine into multiple2 configurations to affect ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration under different levels of environmental dynamism and organizational size.

## 2.5.1 Environmental Dynamism

Environmental dynamism refers to the instability or changes in customer preferences, rivals’ competitive actions, and the innovation rates of new products and technology. Many studies have examined it as a contextual factor and explored its roles in firms’ achieving ambidexterity (Halevi et al., 2015; Mammassis & Kostopoulos, 2019). For example, Soto-Acosta et al. (2018) confirmed that environmental dynamism is positively associated with innovation ambidexterity. Wamba et al. (2020) demonstrated that the effects of big data analytics on supply chain ambidexterity are contingent on the level of environmental dynamism. Thus, we argue that environmental dynamism interacts with FMC and IFMC to jointly influence ITA.

In dynamic environments characterized by unpredictable changes in technology iterations and product updates, firms require more information and knowledge to seize fleeting opportunities and make timely decisions about ITA. As environmental dynamism introduces risks to firm decision-making (Dess & Beard, 1984; Hu et al., 2023), firms’ development of ITA depends not only on the construction of different communication modes but also on the surrounding environments. To address these uncertainties, both FMC and IFMC are necessary to enhance information sharing and knowledge exchange. However, in stable environments, the need for ITA diminishes, leading firms to prioritize maintaining the stability of their existing IT resources and practices. Thus, the requirement for different communication modes to develop IT exploitation or IT exploration varies, due to their distinct foci and goals. To fully understand the effects of FMC and IFMC on ITA, we view environmental dynamism as a contextual factor.

## 2.5.2 Organizational Size

Prior studies have highlighted the importance of firm size for organizational ambidexterity (Chandrasekaran et al., 2012; Fourné et al., 2019). Different-sized firms possess varying levels of resources, structure, and hierarchical complexity, influencing information processing, knowledge sharing, and decision-making (Park et al., 2017). Following Syed et al. (2024), we use the number of employees to represent firm size.

Large firms typically have a substantial workforce and more slack resources dedicated to IT. However, as firm size increases, bureaucratism, complexity, and information overload may slow down the decisionmaking process. To mitigate these challenges, various communication modes, both formal and informal, should be constructed to facilitate interaction and knowledge exchange between the CIO and TMT. In contrast, small firms have fewer employees and tend to have a more flexible and flatter hierarchy (Dean et al., 1998). As a result, mutual communication is easier to achieve and requires fewer organizational arrangements. In light of this, we consider firm size as another contingency factor.

## 2.6 Research Model

Figure 1 presents the nomological framework for this research. We applied qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) to identify multiple equifinal pathways in which FMC and IFMC may play different roles under different contexts. Based on set theory and Boolean calculation, QCA addresses causal complexity (Rihoux & Ragin, 2008), suggesting that outcomes are better explained by the simultaneous combinations of antecedents rather than by any individual element. We applied fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) to analyze how multiple elements combine into various configurations to produce ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration, demonstrating the concept of equifinality. FsQCA has been widely adopted by IS scholars to study digital phenomena (El Sawy et al., 2010; Park & Mithas, 2020; Park et al., 2020), and it can dissect the diversity and individuality of the particular dataset, enabling the nuanced impacts of antecedents to be comprehended (Fiss, 2011).

## 3 Data and Methodology

## 3.1 Data Collection

We collected survey data on the Chinese shipbuilding industry. Since 2009, China has become the world’s largest shipbuilding country (Jiang et al., 2013).

Affected by the international financial crisis, the continual downturn in international transportation has brought great pressure to the industry (Liang et al., 2017). Faced with turbulent environmental changes, Chinese shipyards have exhibited different responses. For example, some of them pursue new developmental paths—entering the high value-added ships market, applying SCM, CRM, ERP, and other enterprise IT to improve their business operation efficiency (e.g., cloud computing, internet of things (IoT), and other emerging ITs) and explore new business models. Meanwhile, others who have stuck with their existing IT and business strategies have often struggled, which suits our research contexts well.

Although selecting a single Chinese shipbuilding industry can prevent cross-industry confounding effects (Schmalensee, 1985; Sharp et al., 2013), this industry, in fact, has many subsectors, such as ship-related materials, equipment, devices, and marine development. Since 2018, these subsectors have been influenced by the US-China trade war, thus making their competitive environments more diverse and heavily dependent upon the status of each sector, as well as domestic and foreign economic and political situations. As a result, the various firms in the Chinese shipbuilding industry tend to have different responses according to their distinct business and IT strategic focuses.

We conducted a matched-pair survey to collect data from business and IT executive dyads to reduce the threat of common method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Two different questionnaires were administered, with one targeted at IT executives (e.g., chief information officers, IT directors, and IT managers) to assess IT ambidexterity, and the other aimed at business executives (e.g., chief executive officers, chief financial officers, and vice presidents of manufacturing) to gather business-related information such as firm size and environmental dynamism. Furthermore, FMC and IFMC were assessed by IT executives based on their position within the firm or interactions with the TMT (Preston & Karahanna, 2009).

## 3.2 Measurement and Validation

All the constructs were measured using existing scales from the literature, as shown in Appendix C, with modifications to suit our research contexts. IT exploitation and IT exploration were based on scales from Lee et al. (2015) and Liang et al. (2022). IT exploitation was measured to reflect exploitative strategic posture and existing IT reuse, while IT exploration was measured to reflect explorative strategic posture and new IT experimentation. These align with Gartner’s (2014) concept of “bimodal IT,” in which Model 1 emphasizes exploiting what is known (e.g., existing IT reuse) and Model 2 is exploratory (e.g., new IT experimentation). IT ambidexterity was operationalized using item-level interactions of IT exploitation and IT exploration (Chandrasekaran et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2015). By multiplying the six items of IT exploitation with the six items of IT exploration, 36 interaction items were created, and their average provided the measurement of IT ambidexterity. FMC and IFMC were measured according to Preston and Karahanna (2009). Environment dynamism was assessed using items from Karimi et al. (2004) that indicate not only the rate of change and innovation in production and service but also the unpredictability of customer taste and actions of the firm’s principal industries. The natural logarithm of the number of fulltime employees was used as the measure of organizational size (Syed et al., 2024).

SPSS 20.0 and AMOS were used to analyze the reliability and validity of the scales. We first conducted exploratory factor analysis (EFA); the results show that Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) is 0.833, greater than the threshold of 0.8. Bartlett’s value is 6434.285, significant at the 0.001 level. The cumulative variance contribution rate is 84.89%, indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. We then examined the reliability and validity of the measurements. As shown in Appendix D, CR is greater than 0.9 for all constructs, indicating sufficient internal consistency (Nunnally, 1978), and all Cronbach’s α values are greater than 0.8, indicating high reliability (Bagozzi & Edwards, 1998; Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Convergent validity and discriminant validity were tested using AVE and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). As Appendix D shows, the AVE values of the five latent variables are greater than 0.8. CFA results show a high goodness of fit $( \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { D f } = 2 . 3 8 2$ RMSEA = 0.074, GFI = 0.884, CFI = 0.958, NFI = 0.930), exhibiting convergent validity (Hu & Bentler, 1999). As Appendix E shows, the square root of each construct’s AVE is much greater than its correlations with any other construct, suggesting sufficient discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Also, each item’s factor loading is higher than its crossloadings on other constructs, confirming the sufficiency of discriminant validity (Hair et al., 1998) (Appendix F).

Finally, we tested common method variance (CMV) using the unmeasured latent methods factor (ULMC) method. A method latent factor was added to the confirmatory factor analysis model so that all the measurement items not only loaded on the associated constructs but also on this latent method factor. Results (χ<sup>2</sup>/Df = 2.209, RMSEA = 0.069, GFI = 0.906, CFI = 0.967, NFI = 0.943) show that compared with the CFA model, the decrease of $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { D f }$ is not significant. After adding the common method factor, the model fit did not improve, suggesting that there is no obvious CMV in the measurement (Richardson et al., 2009).

## 3.3 Set-Theoretic Analysis with fsQCA

## 3.3.1 Calibration

When using fsQCA, calibrating variables into setmembership scores is necessary. Calibration assigns set membership to a case (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012), defining the extent to which a given case has membership in the set, such as a high level of IT ambidexterity. According to Ragin (2008), direct calibration relies on three qualitative anchors—full membership, full nonmembership, and a crossover point—which are defined by the researchers’ empirical and theoretical knowledge of the context. The resulting membership scores range from 0 to 1, with 0 indicating full nonmembership and 1 full membership.

Except for firm size, other constructs were measured using a 7-point Likert scale. Following Ragin’s (2008) direct calibration method in fsQCA 3.0, we applied the qualitatively defined scale, setting 6 as the anchor for full membership, 2 for full nonmembership, and 4 as the crossover point, which we believe better reflects reality.

For IT ambidexterity, we set 34 as full membership, 4 as full nonmembership, and 17 as the crossover point, based on its maximum, minimum, and mean values. Organizational size was calibrated using the 95th, 50th, and 5th percentile. See Appendix G for detailed statistics on the constructs and calibration thresholds.

## 3.3.2 Truth Table Analysis

After calibration, we used the truth-table algorithm (Ragin, 2008) to identify combinations of elements that produce the desired outcome (Park & Mithas, 2020). Appendix H presents truth tables for ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration, listing all logically possible combinations of elements. Each row represents one combination, with the number column indicating the frequency of cases for that combination. We set the minimum acceptable frequency at 4 for ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration, as a higher sample size requires a higher frequency threshold (Rihoux & Ragin, 2008). The raw consistency threshold was set to 0.8, following Fiss (2011) and Greckhamer (2016), complemented by a PRI score threshold of 0.65 to avoid simultaneous subset relations in both the outcome and its absence. Taking ITA as an example, we only considered combinations with at least four empirical instances, with a raw consistency of at least 0.8 and a PRI consistency of at least 0.65, reliably resulting in ITA. In the ITA column, a value of 1 indicates that the row consistently produces ITA, while 0 indicates otherwise.

According to Fiss (2007, 2011), fsQCA can identify multiple equifinal configurations for an outcome, meaning that the same result can be achieved through various paths from different initial conditions. In these configurations, mutual communication may play different roles as part of a causal recipe for the outcome. This implies that the impact of FMC or IFMC on developing ITA depends on the other elements in a configuration. This analytical approach helps identify which elements are a necessary or sufficient condition for the outcome, providing a deeper understanding of the core aspects of causality (Ragin, 2008).

## 4 Configurational Analysis Results

## 4.1 Necessary Conditions Analysis

Before analyzing the fuzzy set truth table program, it is necessary to check the necessary conditions (Rihoux & Ragin, 2008). To identify these conditions, we drew a fuzzy-set membership plot depicting the distribution of cases in terms of FMC/IFMC and ITA. If the set membership value of an element is always equal to or higher than the set membership value in the outcome, it is likely to be a necessary condition.

As Appendix I shows, all the consistency values of FMC and IFMC for ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration are less than 0.9, indicating that they do not constitute the necessary condition. Similar conclusions were drawn from the set-membership plots, where the pattern is consistent with a necessary condition unless most cases appear below the diagonal (Park et al., 2017). In summary, neither FMC nor IFMC passed the necessary condition test, suggesting that a single antecedent condition has a weak explanatory power for ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration. Consequently, these antecedent conditions will be integrated into fsQCA to identify sufficient solutions for developing high levels of ITA.

## 4.2 Sufficient Conditions Analysis

We used truth table analysis to identify causal recipes sufficient for ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration (Ragin, 2008). fsQCA yields three types of solutions: complex solutions (excluding logical remainder), parsimonious solutions (including logical remainder), and intermediate solutions (only including logical remainder consistent with theoretical and practical knowledge). Appendix J presents the intermediate and parsimonious solutions of the fsQCA results in Boolean expression form. Elements in the parsimonious solution are embedded in the intermediate solution (highlighted in bold), representing core conditions for the outcome. Elements that solely appear in the intermediate solution, not in the parsimonious solution, are peripheral conditions, indicating a weaker relationship with the outcome (Park et al., 2017). We graphically depict the results from Appendix J using the notation system suggested by Ragin (2008). As shown in Table 2, large circles represent core elements and small circles represent peripheral elements. Dark circles indicate that elements serve as enablers for the outcome, while crossed-out circles indicate that a high degree of the element can be an inhibitor for organizations to achieve the outcome.

Under the combinatorial impacts of FMC, IFMC, and organizational and environmental elements, firms can adopt two configurations to achieve ITA, showcasing equifinality. In dynamic environments, FMC and IFMC together effectively support firms to achieve a high degree of ITA (AM1). However, for smaller firms, only IFMC is enough, and a high degree of FMC does not matter (AM2). Among these configurations, IFMC is a core element, and the role of FMC is contingent on the context. In addition, our results do not yield any solution for ITA in stable environments.

## 4.2.2 Configurations for Achieving High IT Exploitation

Three configurations are available for firms to develop high IT exploitation. Configuration EI1 shares the same structures as AM2, where IFMC serves as a core element helping small firms in dynamic environments achieve IT exploitation. For large firms in stable environments, FMC proves more effective in enabling them to attain this capability. Additionally, based on Fiss (2011), we found that the neutral permutations within configurations EI3 point to the existence of second-order equifinality because EI3a and EI3b share the same core elements. Specifically, in a dynamic environment (EI3a) or for large organizations (EI3b), the core presence of FMC and IFMC is sufficient to achieve high IT exploitation.

## 4.2.3 Configurations for Achieving High IT Exploration

Finally, the results show that two configurations can produce high IT exploration, indicating multiple equifinal pathways where IFMC is always the core element, while FMC plays different roles depending on the particular context. Specifically, in dynamic environments, IFMC can sufficiently support firms to achieve high IT exploration, and a high level of FMC does not matter (ER1). However, as core conditions, IFMC should be present, and FMC should be absent for large firms to develop IT exploration (ER2).

Overall, these results reveal how different configurations can produce high levels of ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration, with specific communication modes exerting distinct effects in varying contexts. Notably, ITA can be achieved exclusively in dynamic environments, where IFMC consistently plays a pivotal role, while FMC is essential for larger firms to address their hierarchical complexities. In addition, both FMC and IFMC play core and enabling roles in achieving IT exploitation, whereas for IT exploration, a high degree of FMC may be irrelevant or even counterproductive under specific conditions. This is because IT exploitation involves well-defined decisions and existing knowledge that FMC can facilitate, while IT exploration probes open questions and new knowledge regarding whether, what, and how to adopt new ITs and requires flexible information exchange beyond the capacity of formal reporting structures. IFMC is thus effective for both IT exploitation and exploration, as it allows for frequent and rich knowledge sharing, which is essential for solving both routine and novel questions. In contrast, FMC, with its emphasis on formal and structured interactions, tends to restrict exploratory activities and the flow of innovative ideas related to experimenting with new ITs, particularly in large firms with more hierarchical levels and bureaucratic procedures.

Interestingly, we found that both ITA and IT exploitation can be developed using the same configuration, AM2 or EI1. This is because ITA was measured by multiplying IT exploitation and exploration. Thus, any configuration producing high ITA can also be used to develop its two dimensions. In other words, only the intersection of configurations for IT exploitation and exploration can lead to ITA. Given that firm size does not matter in ER1, AM2 can also produce IT exploration. Thus, in specific contexts, it is possible for the same configuration to develop ITA and IT exploitation. In view of this, we will attempt to examine how to configure FMC and IFMC to develop these three outcomes while investigating various contextual effects.

## 5 Theoretical Propositions

Based on configurational theory and fsQCA, our investigation of the combined effects of two types of mutual communication, alongside organizational and environmental elements, on ITA suggests multiple equifinal pathways. Firms can adopt one of these configurations, tailored to their specific contexts, to achieve a high degree of ITA, IT exploitation, or IT exploration. By creating a 2×2 contingency table with organizational size (large vs. small) and environmental dynamism (dynamic vs. stable) and mapping the results from Table 2 into each cell (as shown in Table 3), we propose a set of theoretical propositions.

## 5.1 FMC Effect

FMC is based on the formal working relationship between the CIO and TMT, facilitating organized, controlled, and formal communication and knowledge sharing. Through these interaction channels, CIOs and TMT can partake in in-depth discussions and share insights on both the exploitation and exploration of IT resources and practices. Prior research has demonstrated that FMC is a crucial determinant of shared understanding between the CIO and TMT (Preston & Karahanna, 2009), helping them manage uncertainty and avoid equivocality.

Thus, FMC plays an enabling role in enabling firms to achieve ITA.

However, the impact of FMC on IT exploitation and IT exploration is different, particularly considering that exploitation is associated with mechanistic structures involving path dependence, routinization, control, and certainty, whereas exploration aligns with organic structures characterized by improvisation, loosely coupled systems, autonomy, and discovery (Benner & Tushman, 2003; O’Reilly & Tushman, 2013). As mentioned above, FMC prioritizes hierarchy culture, structure, and formalization (Hartnell et al., 2019), aiming at streamlining business operations and exploiting known IT resources. Thus, FMC appears more conducive to IT exploitation than exploration. Nevertheless, we believe that by facilitating more condensed and focused discussions and offering formal institutional assurances—especially in large firms with more hierarchical levels—FMC still holds potential value for IT exploration.

## 5.2 IFMC Effect

IFMC is rooted in the informal social relationship between the CIO and TMT, enabling an easy and relaxed flow of information. Literature has suggested that IFMC is crucial for building trust-based CIO-TMT relationships, which can foster shared understanding (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). These shared ideas promote discussions on both exploitative and exploratory activities related to IT resources and practices. By facilitating the communication and integration of new and existing knowledge about the strategic implications of IT exploitation and exploration, IFMC is instrumental in solving both well-defined and novel questions and can thus motivate executives to pursue ITA.

Unlike FMC, IFMC is free from the constraints of formal hierarchical structures and allows informal interaction. This informality helps to reduce communication costs, remove language barriers, and ensure agile communication that is flexible in time and space (Liang et al., 2017). By enhancing timely information exchange and knowledge sharing between the CIO and TMT, IFMC can improve their shared understanding of managing and utilizing IT resources, especially in dynamic environments that require quick decisions. Therefore, compared to FMC, IFMC is flexible enough to circumvent bureaucracies to accelerate the decision-making processes. Specifically, IFMC encourages risk-taking and inspires novel ideas and creative knowledge, making it more inclined to drive IT exploration (Hartnell et al., 2019).

## 5.3 Environmental Dynamism and Organizational Size

Although both FMC and IFMC can enhance ITA, their exact effectiveness is dependent on different environmental and organizational contexts (Fourné et al., 2019). In turbulent environments characterized by rapidly changing customer preferences, competitor activities, and the introduction of new products and services, flexible organizational arrangements are crucial for facilitating timely communication and integration of business and IT knowledge between the CIO and TMT. This helps firms understand how to exploit existing IT resources to streamline business operations and explore new IT resources for more longterm benefits simultaneously. IFMC is thus essential for firms in achieving ITA in dynamic environments. Furthermore, according to Park et al. (2017), large firms usually exhibit higher levels of hierarchy, complexity, and interdependence. Thus, in such cases, IFMC alone is not necessarily sufficient. FMC, characterized by formal rules and procedures, can facilitate the focused and precise transmission and communication of information and knowledge regarding IT exploitation and exploration. Therefore, collectively, in dynamic environments, FMC and IFMC work together for large firms to achieve ITA.

IT exploitation aims to reuse and renew existing IT resources (Liang et al., 2022), requiring a high degree of structure and routinization due to the tasks being familiar, predictable, and oriented towards existing IT knowledge. FMC, reflecting a hierarchical culture that values order, control, and stability, is suitable for firms to develop this capability. Additionally, research suggests that exploitation involves certainty and variance reduction (O’Reilly & Tushman, 2013). Thus, in dynamic environments, IFMC also plays a crucial role by offering informal and timely communication to reduce uncertainty, enabling more adaptive responses and ensuring that emerging issues are addressed promptly to maintain stability. Thus, FMC and IFMC complement each other to assist large firms to achieve IT exploitation in turbulent environments.

However, IT exploration typically requires more informal communication channels to ensure timely interaction for interpreting and sharing new knowledge. Thus, IFMC is important for firms to develop IT exploration in dynamic environments. Furthermore, exploration advocates discovery and autonomy (O’Reilly & Tushman, 2013), and formal communication mechanisms and organizational arrangements characterized by rules and control are not compatible under this condition. Our results support this argument. That is, in dynamic environments, IFMC may be sufficient for large firms to develop IT exploration and FMC is likely dispensable.

Compared to large firms, small firms usually have fewer hierarchical levels and complexities (Hu et al., 2023). Consequently, they do not require formal communication modes, as informal organizational arrangements are often sufficient for them to achieve ITA. Based on our empirical findings and theoretical logic, we posit the following proportions for firms of different sizes to develop ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration in dynamic environments.

Proposition 1: For large firms in dynamic environments, both FMC and IFMC are required to develop IT ambidexterity and IT exploitation. However, IFMC is sufficient and FMC is dispensable for large firms to develop IT exploration in dynamic environments.

Proposition 2: For small firms in dynamic environments, a high level of IFMC can help to achieve IT ambidexterity, IT exploitation, and IT exploration effectively, whereas the extensive use of FMC is not necessary.

In stable environments, companies face well-defined and predictable events with fewer uncertainties. In such situations, the priority for firms, especially large ones, is to enhance the exploitation of existing resources to maximize benefits from current information systems given the high sunk costs (Gupta et al., 2006). FMC enables organized information exchange between TMT and the CIO, streamlining existing business operations and IT resources for IT exploitation. In addition, despite stable conditions and low innovation rates, firms still endeavor to explore new IT resources for competitive advantage. In this case, IFMC can inspire executives in the use or experimentation of new IT resources. Conversely, FMC is less effective in nurturing ideas for exploring new IT, particularly when the current IT systems are doing well. Thus, FMC disables while IFMC enables large firms to achieve IT exploration in stable environments.

Additionally, we found no specific configuration for firms to achieve ITA in stable environments. While there is still potential for large firms to develop IT exploitation and exploration, the conflicting effects of FMC on these two capabilities may contribute to this outcome. This implies that developing ITA in stable environments may require less enablement of communication channels and organizational arrangements. Thus, we offer the following proposition:

Proposition 3: For large firms in stable environments, FMC alone is sufficient to achieve IT exploitation, while IFMC needs to be strengthened and FMC weakened to achieve IT exploration.

## 6 Discussion

In this study, we theorize that developing ITA needs different types (new/existing) of knowledge and thus mutual communication and knowledge exchange between the CIO and TMT. We conceptualize and differentiate FMC and IFMC and adopt a configurational theory approach to examine how these two modes are combined with other organizational and environmental factors to jointly influence ITA. Furthermore, we consider IT exploitation and IT exploration as independent outcomes in light of the proportional balance view of IT ambidexterity (Liang et al., 2022). Based on the solutions of fsQCA, we propose three theoretical propositions to illustrate how firms can construct FMC and IFMC to develop ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration under specific conditions.

## 6.1 Theoretical Implications

This study contributes to IS research in three ways. First, it extends the literature on the development of ITA by introducing a CIO-TMT mutual communication perspective. This paper theoretically articulates and empirically examines how FMC and IFMC and other organizational and environmental factors (Fourné et al., 2019; Park et al., 2020) combine into multiple bundles to affect ITA. The fsQCA results indicate that the effects of these two interaction modes on ITA are contingent on organizational and environmental contexts. By examining how organizational size and environmental dynamism influence the effectiveness of FMC and IFMC on ITA, our study responds to the call for more research on boundary conditions (Jansen et al., 2006) in ambidexterity research.

Second, to better understand the antecedents of ITA, it explores the roles of FMC and IFMC in developing IT exploitation and exploration separately. Unlike prior research, which often treats ITA as a single construct with perfect balance or simultaneous strong IT exploitation and exploration (Lee et al., 2015), thus overlooking the distinctiveness of these two dimensions, we examine how FMC and IFMC and organizational and environmental elements jointly influence IT exploitation and exploration in line with the proportional balance view of ITA (Liang et al., 2022). In doing so, this research not only enhances the understanding of the antecedents of IT exploitation and exploration but also suggests how to apply different types of communication modes to achieve the desired balance between the two.

Third, it expands the research context of mutual communication modes. Although previous studies on IT alignment have proposed that mutual communication can influence strategic IT alignment, they have reported mixed findings (Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999; Preston & Karahanna, 2009). Our study complements this literature and shows that mutual communication plays an important role in developing ITA. While IFMC has been found to have no effect on CIO-TMT shared understanding and IT alignment (Preston & Karahanna, 2009), it could facilitate ITA. According to Gibson and Birkinshaw (2004), alignment pertains to exploitation, which values agreement and congruence. Different from the goal of alignment, the goal of ITA is to integrate diverse knowledge and contradictory demands among exploitative and exploratory efforts. IFMC may not be needed to achieve alignment, but it is instrumental in helping executives integrate knowledge and balance competing demands. Research indicates that informal socialization among executives is associated with high levels of ambidexterity (Jansen et al., 2009; Raisch et al., 2009). Additionally, IS scholars have suggested the necessity of less-formalized approaches for successful IT exploration (Chen et al., 2010). In line with these findings, we highlight the significance of IFMC in developing ITA.

## 6.2 Practical Implications

Our paper offers several practical suggestions. First, the effects of FMC and IFMC on the development of ITA are based upon various contingencies. Firms should carefully assess environmental dynamism and their size before selecting or prioritizing the modes. Recognizing the core enabling role of IFMC, firms should create opportunities for informal interactions both at work (e.g., lunch and coffee breaks, spontaneous discussions in hallways, ridesharing) and outside of work (e.g., social events, happy hours, sports, etc.). In addition, some virtual spaces (e.g., social media) can also be used for personal connections and experience sharing.

Second, a proportional balance view of ITA suggests that high levels of IT exploitation and IT exploration simultaneously may not always yield optimal benefits (Liang et al., 2022). Therefore, it is crucial for managers to understand how to enable IT exploitation and IT exploration independently so that they can strengthen either or both when needed. Our analysis indicates that in specific contexts, either FMC or IFMC can play a core role in enabling firms to achieve IT exploitation. However, for IT exploration, IFMC always plays an enabling role, whereas FMC sometimes hinders it. Thus, firms should recognize the significance of IFMC while also implementing practical organizational arrangements that leverage the strengths of FMC and mitigate its negative effects after evaluating the internal and external contexts.

Third, for small firms in dynamic environments, IFMC alone enables the development of ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration, implying that firms only need to establish sufficient informal interactions. However, for large firms in stable environments, FMC plays conflicting roles in developing IT exploitation and exploration. Executives should thus exercise caution when using this mode. In addition, given that no configuration for ITA was found in this context, we suggest that certain approaches, such as structural or sequential ambidexterity (Sun et al., 2023), may be helpful in addressing these conflicts.

Finally, the multiple equifinal configurations of high ITA, IT exploitation, and IT exploration imply that firms can select a unique configuration to achieve ITA and its two distinct dimensions based on their specific contexts, rather than imitating other successful cases or following their previous success history (Park et al., 2017). Executives have various options for achieving ITA, taking advantage of their firm’s capabilities in different organizational and environmental conditions to minimize the risk of failure.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Research Directions

There are several limitations in our study. First, we collected data from the Chinese shipbuilding industry. Although this setting can eliminate aggregation bias, the generalizability of our findings may be constrained. Moreover, this industry possesses unique characteristics, potentially impacting Chinese executives’ decision-making through cultural values and institutional pressures (e.g., gender relations, local/regional politics, Confucian philosophy, etc.). Although our study did not examine these specific influences, future research should explore the development of ITA in diverse contexts while taking these factors into account.

Second, our emphasis on the impacts of mutual communication modes was primarily informed by the perspective of mutual communication and knowledge sharing between the CIO and TMT. Other factors, such as shared knowledge, IT assets, and IT application capability, may also impact IT ambidexterity (Tai et al., 2019). Additionally, contextual factors like strategic orientation, firm type, and IT spending should be considered to explore their contextual effects on the firms’ achievement of ITA. Thus, future research could develop a more comprehensive framework to identify and elaborate on other key antecedent candidates to gain a deeper understanding of how to achieve such goals. Specifically, our analysis found no configuration for developing ITA under stable contexts. Future work examining the antecedents or outcomes of ITA should pay particular attention to the contingency of environments.

Third, fostering ITA is a complex process influenced by various organizational factors. The cross-sectional data in our study has limitations in addressing processoriented issues. Therefore, employing a longitudinal design would be desirable to empirically reveal the causal dynamics between ITA and its antecedents.

## 7 Conclusion

We adopted a configurational approach to examine how FMC and IFMC combine with other organizational and environmental elements into multiple configurations to enable ITA, as well as its two distinct dimensions, IT exploitation, and IT exploration. By integrating the literature on mutual communication and ITA, our paper provides novel theoretical and methodological insights into the development of ITA, which makes a significant contribution to the IS literature and opens new avenues for future research to further enrich our understanding of how to develop ITA.
