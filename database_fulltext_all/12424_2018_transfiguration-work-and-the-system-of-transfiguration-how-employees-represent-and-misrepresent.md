# TRANSFIGURATION WORK AND THE SYSTEM OF TRANSFIGURATION: HOW EMPLOYEES REPRESENT AND MISREPRESENT THEIR WORK<sup>1</sup>

Joao Cunha IÉSEG School of Management (LEM-CNRS 9221), 3 Rue de la Digue, 59000 Lille, FRANCE {j.cunha@ieseg.fr}

Andrea Carugati

School of Business and Social Sciences, Aarhus University, Fuglesangs Allé 4, 2628-215, 8210 Aarhus V, DENMARK {andrea@mgmt.au.dk} and IÉSEG School of Management (LEM-CNRS 9221), 3 Rue de la Digue, 59000 Lille, FRANCE

The quality of an organization’s decisions depends on the quality of the data in its information systems. When technology records employees’ work automatically, information quality is ensured by algorithms that produce electronic representations of work. What happens when employees report their own work? We show that the quality of this self-reported information depends on how managers direct their employees to report their work in their organization’s information systems. We refer to constructing this representation as transfiguration work. We draw on a 15-month ethnography to specify the process of transfiguration work along with the characteristics of the system of transfiguration that supports it.

Our specification of transfiguration work supplements the model of representation implicit in research on management information systems. We show how managers can take over information technology in enforcing the practices that employees follow to report their work. By doing so, we theorize a broader role for agency in the representation of work.

We conclude that organizations can improve the quality of data in their information systems by shaping how managers enforce transfiguration work, rather than by changing the information systems where employees report their work.

Keywords: Transfiguration work, informating, representation theory, CRM, IT appropriation, ethnography

## Introduction

What happens to the quality of information when employees, rather than technology, report their own work? The answer to this question can provide new insights into how information systems get used and abused to represent ongoing work practices. The answer can also help organizations improve the information that they use to decide and implement strategy.

Several studies suggest that the quality of information does not change when employees report their own work (e.g., Gray 2013, Orlikowski 1991). Information technology (IT) may no longer keep an automatic record of how people do their work. Nevertheless, IT still keeps an automatic record of how people report their work, so it encourages them to report these data as accurately as possible.

However, a previous article, based on the fieldwork that grounds the study presented next, shows that the quality of information can deteriorate when employees report their own work. That study shows that managers may have too much at stake in the representations of their employees’ work to simply let IT enforce accurate records of work (Cunha 2013). Instead, managers may take advantage of how employees represent their work to impress their leaders (managers of managers). In doing so, managers replace IT in being the primary influence on how employees report their work. Managers enact a process of representation that contrasts with the model of representation of information systems research that has been influenced by Zuboff’s (1988) theory of informating. To assess the quality of information when employees report their own work, it is first necessary to specify this new process of representation. However, neither Cunha (2013) nor previous research on IT use in jobs where people report their own work, have developed such a model (see Burton-Jones 2014; Sewell and Taskin 2015). The study presented next develops this model of representation when managers decide and enforce how their employees report their own work.

Our new model of representation shows that employees report their actions and achievements in their organization’s IT by doing transfiguration work. Transfiguration work is the work done to transform the data that employees use to do their job into the information that they report in their organization’s IT. We show that transfiguration work is a job that can, and should, be managed because it is burdensome and difficult. This means that the quality of information when employees report their own work depends on the goals of managers who enforce transfiguration work. If organizations want their information systems to provide high-quality information, they need to ensure that their managers enforce transfiguration work designed to inform rather than impress their leaders.

In the next section, we argue that we cannot extend the model of representation when IT reports work automatically to jobs where employees do their own reporting. Instead, the process of representation when employees report their own work can only be developed from new empirical research. The methods section describes the steps we took to specify this process of representation from ethnographic data. We then explain how managers in a desk-based sales unit, where we collected the data for this research, had their employees report compliance with sales targets without doing any sales. We show that these salespeople did service work for their customers to get orders that they could report as sales. We also describe the representation work that employees did to transform the data that they used for service work into records of sales work.

We use our analysis of desk salespeople’s representation work to outline a new model of representation: representation through transfiguration work. We show how this new model of representation extends the specification of the quality of information and offers organizations new opportunities to improve the data that feed their strategy process.

## Toward a Model of Representation When Employees Report Their Own Work

Developing a model of representation that explains how employees’ work is recorded in IT is one of the core and persistent problems in information systems (IS) research (Weber 2003). There is a body of research that explains the process of representation when IT records work automatically (e.g., Burton-Jones and Grange 2012; Stanko and Beckman 2015; Zammuto et al. 2007). In this section, we assess whether we can explain how employees report their own work by extending the research on representation when IT reports work automatically.

## The Model of Representation When IT Reports Work Automatically

IS research has developed the model of representation by examining how automatic representations of work affect the quality of data in an organization’s IT (Bhimani 2015; Kallinikos 2009). This research shows that IT has an informating property:

The devices that automate by translating information into action also register data about those automated activities, thus generating new streams of information.…The same systems that make it possible to automate office transactions also create a vast overview of an organization’s operations, with many levels of data coordinated and accessible for a variety of analytical efforts (Zuboff 1988, p. 9).

IT has an informating property because it records and reports work automatically as employees do their jobs (Zuboff 1988). This property allows IT to produce accurate, detailed, and comprehensive representations of work.

In this model, employees cannot decide how their work will be reported in their organization’s IT, nor do they need to put any effort into reporting their work. In turn, managers need not exert any effort to compel employees to report their actions and achievements, nor do they have any power over the data related to employees’ work. Organizations can only improve the quality of information by changing how IT collects data (Wagner et al. 2011).

This informating model of representation was developed by studying IT that reports work on its own (Kallinikos 2009). However, many information systems require employees to self-report their own work (e.g., Jensen and Aanestad 2007). Next, we consider whether the informating model might be extended to this type of information system, which informates the representation of work instead of informating work.

## Applying the Informating Model to IT Where Employees Report Their Own Work

When employees report their own work, the informating property of IT affects their representation of that work, not the work itself (Sewell 2005).

## IT Can Informate the Representation of Work

Employees have to report their own work if it cannot be recorded automatically by IT. In jobs such as sales (Sinisalo et al. 2015) and medical care provision (Doolin 2004), employees do not need IT to do their work. They may use IT only to create a representation of that work. They may also be required to report information that they can collect during the course of their everyday work, but that is unrelated to their job (Gray 2013). Organizations make employees collect this supplemental information because employees have easy access to data that organizations need to make decisions, but which would be otherwise difficult or expensive to collect. For example, in sales organizations, employees collect information about competitors and details about customers (Carayannis 1999). We use the phrase representation work to refer to the practices that employees follow to report their work and to provide supplemental information in their organization’s IT.

The technologies that employees use to report their work still have an autonomous power to informate. These technologies record the steps that employees take to enter data about their actions and achievements. The informating property of these technologies then exposes employees’ compliance with reporting procedures. However, IT that requires employees to report their own work provides no independent information about employees’ compliance with work procedures.

In these cases, employees must make an effort to report their own work, but they often cannot decide how they represent their work because any deviation from prescribed reporting procedure will be detected by their organization’s information system. Workers’ foreknowledge of observability induces their conformity, not only with work procedures but also with reporting procedures (Doolin 2004; Sewell and Taskin 2015). Similarly, managers have no more power over how their employees report their own work than they do when IT reports employees’ work automatically. However, managers do not have to exert any effort to make sure that employees report their work in their organization’s IT.

## Managers May Object to IT Informating the Representation of Work

Managers may refuse to let IT enforce accurate representations of work. Research on IS appropriation shows that managers may impose their own reporting practices to overcome the limitations of their organization’s IT (the functional mismatch of IT, e.g., Berente and Yoo 2012). Moreover, Cunha (2013) shows that managers may also impose their own reporting practices so that they can use the data that employees report to impress their leaders (the dramaturgical potential of IT).

The functional mismatch of IT is the gap between employees work practices and the work procedures prescribed by their organization’s IT (e.g., Nan 2011; Strong and Volkoff 2010). Research on IS appropriation shows that managers may care more about how well employees do their job than about how well they comply with prescribed procedures (Grover et al. 2014). This may lead managers to exempt employees from reporting their work in their organization’s IT and allow those employees to improvise their own work practices, without fear of scrutiny (Rivard and Lapointe 2012). When employees do not use their organization’s IT to work, they may improvise a shadow information system. This shadow information system keeps the data that employees need to do their improvised work practices without exposing them to the rest of their organization (Lyytinen and Newman 2015). When employees use shadow information systems, they under-report their work in their organization’s IT, and only provide the minimum data needed to comply with reporting requirements that their managers cannot allow them to avoid. Berente and Yoo (2012, p. 932) found that

While the data in an enterprise system is intended to represent certain organizational activity … it is fairly commonplace for the data to bear little resemblance to the activity it is intended to make visible.…Even when there is system use, the enacted practice may be loosely coupled from the aspects of practice represented by the data in the system. That is, just because the system is used and data is populated does not mean that the process prescriptions associated with the ERP implementation are necessarily complied with.

When managers tolerate this functional misrepresentation, they trade information quality for employees’ ability to address their everyday challenges at work (Lapointe and Rivard 2005). This trade-off may make organizations more effective, but it also hinders their ability to learn from employees’ work or to use employees as sources of information for developing their corporate strategy (Ferneley and Sobreperez 2007). For employees, the functional misrepresentation reduces the burden of reporting their work. However, employees do not decide on their own which data to report. They need to negotiate that with their managers. Managers get more power over what employees report in their organization’s IT. However, managers only use that power to allow employees to report less data, rather than change the data that employees report. Therefore, managers continue to have to put very little effort in enforcing representation work.

The dramaturgical potential of IT is the extent to which managers (managers of employees) can use the data that employees report to impress their leaders (managers of managers). Cunha showed that leaders use the data in their organization’s IT to build an image of each manager’s ability and commitment. It is the image that managers present through data about their employees’ work which leaders use to decide which managers merit their protection and support. Managers who are better at making employees report flattering performance data are those who are awarded more resources and that are rewarded with faster promotions (Jackall 1989).

In these conditions, managers may care much more about how their employees report their work, rather than how effectively their employees perform their work. Managers may still accept deviations from prescribed work procedures, but they cannot let employees under-represent their work, even if employees need to hide their improvised work practices. Instead, managers make employees report embellished representations of work, which managers then use as evidence of their own competence and commitment.

Managers can take over their organization’s information systems in enforcing representation work to tap into this dramaturgical potential of IT. Managers may require employees to report their compliance with prescribed work procedures, even if these are unrelated to their everyday practices. Managers may also have employees misrepresent the data that they collect for their organization, to be consistent with the embellished representations of the employees work. When they do so, managers use their organization’s IT as a “store window to show achievement upward instead of [using it as a] transparent window to monitor compliance downward” (Cunha 2013, p. 728).

When managers leverage this dramaturgical potential of IT, they trade off the quality of information for their ability to use representations of their employees’ work to impress their leaders. However, they also allow their organization to make decisions based on embellished (and therefore distorted) records of employees’ actions and accomplishments and inaccurate data about competitive conditions.

Representation work becomes a burden for employees. Employees must comply with the extra reporting requirements that managers impose, yet they have little say over what they report in their organization’s IT. Representation work also becomes a burden for managers who now have to monitor and enforce the data that employees report. However, taking on this burden allows managers to control how employees record their work in their organization’s IT.

Together, the functional mismatch and the dramaturgical potential of IT suggest that the informating model cannot be extended to IT where employees report their own work. Specifying how employees report their work in their organization’s IT (and the quality of information they produce) in these conditions requires a new model that explains representation work as a task imposed on employees by managers, not by technology. We develop this new model from the ethnographic observations that Cunha used to build a dramaturgical model of the production of performance data. We use these ethnographic observations to address the following, new research question:

How do employees comply with their managers’ orders to produce electronic representations of work that differ from what they actually do in the course of their everyday jobs?

## Methods

Data for this study come from a 15-month ethnography at DeskSales, a desk sales unit at a global telecommunications company called M-Tel (all names are pseudonyms). M-Tel created DeskSales to sell cheaper, simpler products to corporate customers, while its field sales force continued to be responsible for more expensive and complex products. Desk salespeople were led by desk sales managers, but they also had to report their activity to the field salespeople associated with their accounts, which granted field salespeople some authority over desk salespeople.

Desk salespeople’s work was to sell. They were not supposed to do any service work for their customers because M-Tel had a set of dedicated service units. According to an internal training presentation, DeskSales took the lead when M-Tel was selling, because “the customer doesn’t know what they want.” Service units took the lead when the customer was buying, because “the customer knows what they want.” To manage its sales force, M-Tel implemented Siebel. Siebel is a customer relationship management system that keeps information about customer contacts, salespeople’s activities, sales opportunities, and closed sales. It also allows managers to scrutinize and supervise salespeople.

DeskSales was overseen by a steering committee that was tasked with evaluating the unit and its performance. This committee created two sets of targets for desk salespeople: annual sales targets to sell \$300,000 on their own and \$200,000 in collaboration with field salespeople; and sales work targets to make seven calls to existing customers every day, write quarterly account plans, and report their sales and sales calls in Siebel. Sales managers in turn were evaluated according to the proportion of their salespeople who reached these targets.

The study presented next is part of a broader research on the use of Siebel at DeskSales. Table 2 lists the data analytical procedures we used to specify the representation of work at DeskSales. In analyzing our data, we took a practice perspective because of its advantages for revealing and learning how work is actually undertaken in organizations (Feldman and Orlikowski 2011). We began by extracting practice–artifact chains from field notes. We read our data for chains of practices (rather than individual practices) to analyze the links between employees’ work and employees’ representation work. We read our data for the artifacts that people used to do work and to report work, to analyze the links between shadow information systems and the company’s IT. Next, we grouped practice–artifact chains that we found in our data according to their function. We arranged these practice–artifact chains sequentially and used them to build a model that integrates the role of managers in the process of representation when employees report their own work.

## Managing the Representation of Work at DeskSales

At DeskSales, managers instructed desk salespeople to report sales and sales work in Siebel that were at odds with the service work that managers told desk salespeople to perform. M-Tel implemented Siebel at DeskSales to monitor the unit’s performance. Frank, a member of the committee that managed the implementation, described Siebel as

Our central nervous system....It’s not just a monitoring tool, we need them [i.e., salespeople] to keep Siebel accurate because that’s the information we use to bill customers and to generate [marketing] campaigns.

However, desk sales managers presented Siebel as a stage for impression management, describing it to desk salespeople as their “shop window.” In a team meeting, sales manager Ferdinand explained: “[Sales and sales work] numbers are fed back to senior managers.” He also stressed that “not only are poor performers mentioned, but so are over-achievers.… [Siebel] measures the way we do business and how successful we are….[It’s] completely transparent.”

These descriptions present Siebel as a medium for supervision that exposes both the failures and the achievements of sales managers and salespeople. Sales managers used their team meetings to take advantage of this exposure. Sales managers began their team meetings by telling each salesperson their gap to targets for sales and sales calls and, at the end of each quarter, the account plans that salespeople still needed to complete. Then, sales managers asked each salesperson about the orders that customers had placed but that salespeople had not yet reported in Siebel. Sales managers added these orders to those that were already reported in Siebel. They wrote the total in a table that listed the salespeople’s names, the total revenue, and the number of customer calls salespeople had reported. Some displayed this table on a whiteboard in their team’s area. Others kept it in a spreadsheet which they e-mailed to their salespeople at the end of each team meeting. These tables often showed that the total orders and sales calls that salespeople had reported were far below their targets. Sales managers used three practices to address these gaps.

First, in team meetings, they taught salespeople how to report customer orders in Siebel as if they were sales that the salespeople had made. Sales manager Nathan told his salespeople that “the best way to get revenue is to get customers to call you when they need to buy something.” Other sales managers issued similar advice. ## Improvising Information Systems to Do Service Work

Desk salespeople performed some service work on their own, and some in collaboration with field salespeople and service representatives. They improvised an information system to keep the information that they needed for the service work that they could do by themselves. They created a different information system to share information with service representatives and field salespeople so that they could work together on customers’ requests.

## Individual Service Work

The service tasks that desk salespeople performed on their own were requests and problems that could be addressed without sending service representatives to the customer. These could be simple or complex tasks, which were supported by different improvised IT artifacts.

## Simple Individual Service Work

Simple service work included processing small orders and reporting faults. It only required salespeople to remember a single product reference, an error code, or a price. Salespeople kept an ongoing record of this information in an artifact we refer to as a running log. This could be a notebook or a calendar that salespeople kept until all the pages had been used. Running logs could also be a few scraps of paper, such as leaflets and supermarket receipts that salespeople discarded throughout the day as they ran out of space. Jeanne used her running log as follows: She received a call from a customer asking for a quote on a private circuit. She asked for the phone numbers of the two ends and jotted down “555-4554 to 633-1234” on the back of a supermarket flyer that she had taken from her bag. She then called the private circuits service unit. When her call was answered, she asked for help pricing the request, reading the phone numbers from her note on the supermarket flyer. She was told a price and jotted it down on the supermarket flyer. She then wrote an e-mail message to her customer with the price, asking to confirm the purchase.

Used this way, running logs were representations that kept information that desk salespeople needed for their current tasks but that was difficult to commit to memory.

## Complex Individual Service Work

Complex service work consisted of processing orders for multiple products with many configurable parameters or dealing with equipment problems that were difficult to diagnose. It required salespeople to remember detailed information about a variety of equipment and service requirements and calculate complex pricing structures. Salespeople kept this information in an artifact that we refer as a to-do pile. For some, this was a folder on their computer where they saved the e-mail messages that customers had sent reporting a fault or placing an order. Desk salespeople saved these e-mails as text files, which they could annotate on their computers. For most, it was a stack of printed e-mail messages that they annotated with pen or pencil. Jeremy used his to-do pile thus: He picked up a pile of annotated printed e-mails from the top drawer of his desk and said, “This is my to-do pile.” He picked up the topmost printed e-mail in the pile. It was an order for multiple ISDN lines connecting multiple customer sites. At the top of the right margin, there was a list with four tasks, “(1) A and B numbers; (2) References; (3) Price; (4) Order form.” Tasks (1) and (2) were crossed out: “Right, now I need to figure out how much we’re charging them.” He calculated ISDN prices next to the data for the ISDN lines on the printed e-mail message. He then sent an e-mail with an order form to the ISDN service unit and discarded the printed e-mail message, complaining: “One down, a whole pile to go.”

To-do piles allowed salespeople to store and access, at a glance, all the information that they needed to complete their complex service tasks.

## Collaborative Service Work

Collaborative service work consisted of simple or complex service requests for which desk salespeople needed the help of either field salespeople to interact directly with customers or service representatives to work at the customers’ premises.

## Simple Collaborative Service Work

Desk salespeople needed the help of service representatives for some of their simple service tasks. This type of service work required salespeople to provide information to the service units which carried out service work at the customers premises. This type of service work also required salespeople to remember all of the service work they had requested from each service unit, to communicate those tasks to each service unit, and to monitor the extent to which the tasks had been completed. Salespeople kept all this information in an artifact called a job list. A job list was a spreadsheet in which desk salespeople recorded information about all the service requests that they had placed with each service unit. Desk salespeople shared these job lists with service representatives to inform them about pending service requests. They also annotated their job lists to keep a record of completed requests. Paul used a job list for ADSL installations, explaining “The first thing to do in the morning is to make sure that all the ADSL appointments are going OK.” He went through his spreadsheet and looked up the people whose installations were assigned for the day and then called the ADSL service unit to make sure those installations were still going on as planned, relying on his spreadsheet to check what customers are getting things installed that day. His spreadsheet was color coded: one line was green for “order sent,” five lines were yellow for “returned to the customer to get more details.” Others were purple and gray for orders in different stages of progress.

These spreadsheets were collaborative versions of running logs because they allowed salespeople to coordinate their work with other parts of the company.

## Complex Collaborative Service Work

Desk salespeople’s worked together with field salespeople in complex service tasks such as pricing orders for large-scale telecommunication infrastructures. These tasks required desk and field salespeople to share information. Desk and field salespeople used an artifact that we call an “invisible Siebel record” to keep and share this information. This was an opportunity recorded in Siebel as a low revenue and low probability of closure, to escape Siebel’s automatic reporting algorithms. Desk salesman Tom explained:

1K (\$1,000), 10%, opportunities are never picked up [in management reports], you can keep everything there…it builds a picture on Siebel of everything that happened that nobody else can see.

Tom used Siebel to work with field saleswoman Deborah to set up a call center. Tom received an e-mail from Deborah asking him to place orders for all the equipment necessary to set up a new call center for Northcorp. Deborah wrote “I created a \$1000/10% opportunity in Siebel to keep all the paperwork...you can find all the information you need there.” She asked Tom to “put all the AROs [order forms] there, instead of sending them via e-mail.” Tom opened the opportunity that Deborah had created in Siebel and found a spreadsheet listing all the equipment that had to be ordered for Northcorp’s call center. He sent an electronic copy of the spreadsheet to the service unit that installed and repaired telecommunications equipment for call centers. He asked service representatives to “please confirm when each order is first billed” to the customer. Whenever Tom received a billing confirmation for Northcorp’s call center, he updated the spreadsheet that Deborah had placed in Siebel.

These Siebel records were a collaborative version of to-do piles because they collated all the information that field and desk salespeople needed to complete a service task together.

Desk salespeople performed all of this service work to get orders from customers, as well as some sales from field salespeople. Salespeople achieved their targets by reporting that they sold the orders that customers placed and the sales that field salespeople handed them in return for their help with reporting work in Siebel. Creating these records of sales in Siebel was difficult and burdensome. Next we explain how desk salespeople used an information system of their own to help them misreport their service work as sales work.

## Improvising Information Systems to Report Sales Work

Desk salespeople complained about the admin work they had to do in addition to their service work. This admin work consisted of reporting sales and sales work in Siebel for their managers and reporting service work to their field salespeople. Admin work is an instance of representation work: the work of reporting data in an organization’s IT. While having breakfast with Jack and Christine, Frances complained about the burden of reporting sales and sales work: “With Siebel there’s a lot of paperwork to do,” but it is just “for management to be able to control you.” People called it “feeding the monster,” because it took so much of their time. Jack complained, “This is what I hate about this job, having to do all this admin crap.” Christine concluded that, “We’re working for Siebel instead of Siebel working for us…the measure becomes the goal!”

However, some desk salespeople were happy to do substantial admin work to earn their quarterly bonuses. Salesman Luke explained that some desk salespeople were even willing to do their field salespeople’s admin work:

The trick is to get your account team to give it [one of their sales] to you. Because they don’t want to do Siebel. What is Siebel all about? …Siebel allows a [desk salesperson] to do everything on Siebel because the account team [field salespeople] doesn’t want to do it.…The account team does nothing on Siebel. We do everything on Siebel.… “Can I have that opportunity?” “What are you going to do for me?” “I’ll put it on Siebel for you.” You listen to every [desk salesperson] and that’s what they’ll say. They’ll never say, “I’ll manage it for you,” “I’ll talk to the customer for you.” They’ll say “I’ll put it on Siebel and make sure it’s not wrong.” Why? Because we have incentives to do that.

The representation work (admin work) performed by desk salespeople was solely for the sake of others. In that sense, all of this representation work was collaborative. What distinguished the two sets of information systems that desk salespeople improvised to support their representation work was their purpose. Desk salespeople improvised one information system to report data that was meant to impress others, but they improvised a different information system to report data that was meant to inform others.

## Dramaturgical Representation Work for Managers

Desk salespeople created simple and complex representations of sales and sales work in Siebel, under instruction from their sales managers to leverage the dramaturgical potential of their company’s IT.

## Simple Dramaturgical Representation Work

Managers imposed some representation work that required desk salespeople to record information but not to process it. This representation work included sales calls, account plans, and opportunity updates that desk salespeople reported in Siebel to make their customer service tasks appear like legitimate sales work.

Desk salespeople improvised various artifacts to help them record sales calls. Some salespeople recorded service calls as sales calls in Siebel shortly after hanging up, which created an extra burden of admin work for each service task. Just before reporting two calls in Siebel, desks salesman Terry protested:

Now I have to log all of this crap [in Siebel] and I don’t even need to keep a record of it! It actually makes me feel really frustrated and quite violent, Siebel. I’d say that on a day to day basis it’s my biggest cause of stress in the office. I spend most of my day dealing with bureaucracy, instead of being customer-facing.

Another practice was to record service calls in an artifact which we refer to as a call list because it kept a record of the calls that salespeople made throughout the day. Salespeople introduced these calls into Siebel when they found some free time, often at the end of their workweek.

Still, other salespeople created a generic sales call record in Siebel and copied it the number of times necessary to reach their target. After being admonished by his sales manager for not having recorded a single customer call in Siebel for a whole week, David complained: “I’m just going to copy all of them.” He confessed “I feel I’m abusing the system.” He entered a call to one of his customers in Siebel with the description “Spoke with customer RE: account” and copied it 34 times (the target is 7 calls a day so these are all his calls for the week). He said, “Before I used to write different descriptions on the calls and change the time on each one, but no one does it, so I simply copy and paste.” He argued “Otherwise I’d do nothing, only this admin crap!”

Desk salespeople followed a similar practice to produce their quarterly account plans. In a conversation with Paul three days before the deadline for submitting account plans. He stated that Peter complained that he had seven account plans to do. “My plan is to copy and paste everything from one [sales] plan to the others. [Peter] says that he is only going to change the wording of the first paragraph ‘because that’s what everyone checks.’”

Finally, desk salespeople adopted similar practices to report opportunity updates in Siebel. Some salespeople kept a text file with several generic descriptions of account activities. When they needed to update the probability of closing a sale, they would copy one of the descriptions in their file to Siebel’s notes field. Monica’s text file included activity descriptions such as, “called customer re: proposal,” “customer interested,” and “updated opportunity.” Others improvised directly in Siebel by entering impromptu, generic descriptions in the notes field of each opportunity record. To update two opportunities, Rick opened the notes view in one of his opportunities in Siebel and typed: “customer now expected to sign up for service.” He confessed, “I put that exact same note on all my open opportunities last week.” In the notes view of another opportunity, he wrote, “checked with customer what they wanted.” He said that some people just entered “……..” as a note because managers only saw if there was any activity, not the actual content of the activity. However, “somebody accidentally found out” and this practice had been all but abandoned now.

Desk salespeople improvised an information system to know which opportunities they needed to update. Some used a paper-based or electronic calendar to schedule their opportunity updates in Siebel. James explained his own system thus:

I have a small list, it’s in my diary. I use the Outlook calendar for my list....So what I do is that I’ll put an Outlook entry in my calendar, say 10 days from now, and even if I forget, it will flag up that I need to update it [an opportunity record], [that] I need to add some information. And then I will possibly look through the [paper] notes that I’ve got to find out what I can put in because I just find it easier to read from paper than to read from the screen.

Other salespeople improvised with Siebel to remember when to update their sales opportunities by running a query that listed all the opportunities that had not been updated for some specific period of time.

## Complex Dramaturgical Representation Work

Managers also imposed some representation work that required desk salespeople to process information before reporting it. This representation work consisted of reporting orders as sales in Siebel. Desk salespeople improvised two artifacts to help them report orders as sales in Siebel. Some salespeople used an invoice list that provided a summary of all the invoice references for the orders that salespeople processed throughout the day, whether on a blank page in a notebook, a Post-It note, or a scrap of paper. Most salespeople used an order table: an artifact in the form of an electronic spreadsheet or printed table that listed customers’ completed orders in rows, with separate columns to hold invoice references and all the other information necessary for a complete sales record in Siebel. Jeanne described using her invoice list thus:

I’ve been doing a lot of ISDN lately and I’ve been using this spreadsheet to keep all of my orders. Then, I only need to put them in Siebel in one go. Sometimes, I combine all of them into a single order.…It’s probably not as accurate as it should be, but it saves me a lot of work.

With the order references, salespeople could find the information in M-Tel’s order tracking system that they needed to transform customers’ orders into completed sales records. Salespeople who used invoice lists recorded this information in running logs and in the printed e-mails in their to-do piles, which also supported their service work. Salespeople who used order tables recorded this information there.

Much of the information required for a complete sales record could be copied from M-Tel’s order tracking system. However, salespeople had to produce two types of information themselves: setup fees and quarterly payments. The setup fees and quarterly payments for simple orders could be calculated directly from M-Tel’s electronic pricing manual, so salespeople used their running logs to support these simple calculations. Sarah transformed an order for three ISDN lines into a sale in Siebel as follows: She received an e-mail from a customer ordering ISDN lines for three new stores. She opened the ISDN section of M-Tel’s electronic pricing manual, which included a table listing the set-up fee and quarterly payment for a single connection. She jotted these two numbers in a notebook that she kept on her desk in front of her computer’s keyboard. She then used her calculator to multiply both numbers by three (the number of ISDN connections the customer had ordered) and wrote down the result in her notebook. She created a new opportunity in Siebel and entered the total set-up fee and the quarterly payment schedule, together with the order quantity and the product information, which she also copied from her notebook.

The setup fees and quarterly payments for complex orders were more difficult to calculate, though. Salespeople had to process product requirements, deduce discounts according to a prescribed schedule that changed for each product, and accrue service fees based on individual service-level agreements with customers. Some salespeople used the e-mail messages in their to-do piles to scaffold these calculations. Most people used an artifact that can be called a disposable aid to do so. Disposable aids were improvised from pages in a notebook or a calendar, blank sheets of paper, or even scraps of paper where salespeople wrote a sequence of procedures that helped them remember and process all the information necessary to calculate setup fees and quarterly payments for complex orders. A few salespeople encoded these procedures in an electronic spreadsheet to calculate the two types of revenue automatically. Mark reported a complex order as a sale in Siebel this way: The next e-mail in his to-do pile was an order for 27 MaxMeridians, a type of private telephone circuit. Mark drew a table in a sheet of paper with three headers: “distance,” “fee,” and “payment.” He searched the pricing manual for the set-up fees and quarterly payments for each of the private circuits that the customer had ordered and jotted this information in his table. He then read the discount information in the pricing manual and found that there was a 10% discount in set-up fees for any private circuit shorter than 50 miles, for orders of 15 or more private circuits. Mark circled the setup fees of the three private circuits that were less than 50 miles long and then used his calculator to find the discounted prices. He wrote these new prices on the table next to the original prices.

## As Mark explained,

I have to use paper because there’s no way to do this in Siebel. It just throws this massive table at you and you’re supposed to figure it all out [calculate the revenue schedules] on your own.…MaxMeridians are my bread and butter [the product that Mark’s customers order more frequently] and that’s a 5-year contract: 20 quarters worth of revenue!…Maybe I’m really stupid, but I can’t do that [calculate a revenue schedule for 20 quarters] just using my brain.

Together these artifacts became an improvised information system that helped desk salespeople report the sales and sales work that their managers demanded without exposing desk salespeople’s inability to sell or their resistance to perform any of the sales work that their company prescribed.

## Functional Representation Work for Sales Managers and Field Salespeople

Desk salespeople reported their own representation work for two audiences: desk sales managers, who monitored and led their representation work, and field salespeople, who coordinated their service work and used desk salespeople to collect information about customers. Desk sales manager Tom explained, ironically, why he required this information from desk salespeople:

Don't tell me that we could have done everything we do with Siebel with an Excel spreadsheet and that instead of paying millions…we could have just bought Excel off a store shelf?!

Tom added in a more serious tone: “I don’t trust Siebel, that’s why I run a parallel Excel spreadsheet.”

Some of these spreadsheets were simple representations that only required desk salespeople to share information. Others were more complex and required desk salespeople to collect and process information.

## Simple Functional Representation Work

The most important piece of information that desk sales managers required relating to desk salespeople’s representation work was about orders that desk salespeople had received but not yet reported in Siebel. Desk sales managers needed this information to assess the gap between desk salespeople’s targets and the sales and sales work that those salespeople reported in Siebel. Then sales managers used this information to decide how much to enforce the representation of sales and sales work on each salesperson in their team. Desk sales manager Tim asked his desk salespeople to report this information in a forecast spreadsheet: While filling her forecast spreadsheet, desk saleswoman Theresa, who was new to the team, asked out loud how they were supposed to fill in this information. Desk salesman Matt explained, “You have to say how much you are going to close for the next three months and put it there.” Theresa complained, “They should be able to figure this out themselves from Siebel!” A while later, Daniel asked Matt how many opportunities they were supposed to use in the forecast. Matt replied, “I’m going to put in as little as I can. I don’t want to over-exaggerate and put an amount that I won’t be able to close.”

Field salespeople required desk salespeople to keep them updated about specific service tasks for customers. Rick sent a list of his service work to his field salespeople every day. He was updating a matrix with all of the premises of one of his customers and all of the products that M-Tel sells. The intersection between rows and columns shows the number of items of a certain product that this customer has at each location. There are some rows highlighted in green; these are the customers that he has closed, meaning that he has solved any problem that they had. Rick explains that he sends this file to his field salesman everyday so that the salesman can see what he did. This was a file that his field salesman had provided to him.

Rick only used this file to share his work with field salespeople. He explained that although he used his Excel files, what he really used were his paper notes. He had ruled sheets of paper he ripped from a notepad which he used for his service work. The first line in each page had the company name in big letters. Then he wrote the phone number, contact name and full address. The rest of the page was filled with the same notes that he shared in his Excel file. He confessed that he liked paper better.

Desk salespeople also used an artifact, which we refer to as a job report, to share the progress of their service work with field salespeople. Job reports were similar to the job lists that the salespeople used to coordinate their service work with field salespeople, but instead of sharing information about open tasks for cooperation, they provided data about closed tasks for monitoring.

## Complex Functional Representation Work

Occasionally, sales managers asked desk salespeople to provide specific data about their representation of work, which required desk salespeople to process, rather than simply share, information about their work.

John had received a spreadsheet from his sales manager Peter with a column where he was supposed to enter “1” when a sale was for a specific category of products. Desk saleswoman Martha, who had to fill in the same spreadsheet, complained, “I have to do f\*\*king hundreds!” She protested, “I’ll just do the same thing for all of them.” Desk salesman Sam complained, “I have too much to do today, I’m not going to do it today.” John asked Peter if this was just so that “someone can keep their job” and Peter replied that it was to measure this “exactly” so that when they’re implementing this model in other parts of the company they can show that “it actually had results instead of just saying we had desk salespeople work on accounts and we liked it.” After spending a whole morning filling this spreadsheet, John complained, “The only reason we are doing this is to be monitored.”

Field salespeople also occasionally had desk salespeople collect data about customers for their own purposes. Isa did such a report for one of her accounts. She was doing a spreadsheet to collect information about all of the products and services that had been purchased by one of her customers. It was her account team that put her up to it. She opened Siebel to show two opportunities for another account that were worth more than \$400,000 put together. She explained that those opportunities were a direct outcome of a similar spreadsheet she did about another customer. She said that building these spreadsheets for her field salespeople “takes a lot of work but it's worth it.”

These representations helped sales managers lead their salespeople’s representation work and helped field salespeople do their own sales work better.

Considering all the work that desk salespeople performed to transform orders into sales reveals that reporting work can require even more effort than doing work. Moreover, all of the artifacts that desk salespeople used to help them produce records of sales in Siebel show that reporting work may require an information system that is as intricate as that which employees use to do their job. In the next section, we use these gathered insights into desk salespeople’s admin work to develop a transfiguration model of representation.

## A Transfiguration Model of Representation

How do employees comply with their managers’ orders to produce electronic representations of work that differ from what they actually do in the course of their everyday jobs?

Our research shows that employees can do this by carrying out what desk salespeople called admin work—transforming the data that they used to do service work into evidence of sales work. Admin work is an instance of what we call transfiguration work, which consists of transforming the data that employees use to do their job into representations of work in their organization’s IT.

In this section, we develop a transfiguration model of repre sentation from our analysis of desk salespeople’s transfiguration work (Table 3 compares the informating model and the transfiguration model of representation). We begin by explaining how managers direct employees’ representation work, then describe how employees do that representation work through transfiguration. In addition to explaining the tasks that constitute this transfiguration work, we describe the system of transfiguration that employees use to support these tasks. Finally, we explain the effect of transfiguration work on the quality of information that employees provide.

## The Role of Managers in Representation Through Transfiguration

Our research shows that managers may have far more power than IT over their employees’ representation work. The informating model postulates that when IT keeps an automatic record of representation work, the mere possibility of surveillance is enough to ensure compliance with reporting procedures (Burton-Jones 2014). However, in the transfiguration model that we develop, managers have too much of a stake in their employees’ representation work to let IT enforce reporting procedures. Instead, managers try to impose their own reporting practices on employees. Next we outline the types of reporting practices that managers enforce, the factors that specify which reporting practices are necessary, and the effect of those practices on employees’ representation work.

## Types of Reporting Practices Enforced by Managers

Our research shows that managers can impose three types of reporting practices that are different from their organization’s reporting procedures. First, managers may demand that employees report data that present an image of achievement, even if employees do not reach their targets. With these inaccurate, but embellished, data, managers improve the image that they present through their organization’s IT and protect their employees’ improvised work practices. However, this requirement also opens a gap between the information that employees need to do their job and the data that employees report in their organization’s IT. This gap turns the representation of work into a job that is separate from, and that competes with, employees’ everyday work.

Second, managers may require employees to report data that help other parts of their organization, but that are not required by their organization’s IT. Employees have to offer data that others need, but which employees would not have provided if they followed prescribed reporting procedures. Managers do not benefit directly from this representation work. However, they help other parts of the organization deal with the gap between the data that the other people need and the data that IT provides.

Third, managers can ask employees to provide them with the data that they need to monitor representation work, including data that employees keep in their improvised information systems, which they use to perform their work but do not report in the organization’s IT. Managers need these data to monitor representation work without spoiling the image that managers present through their organization’s IT, and without exposing their employees’ improvised work practices.

By replacing the reporting procedures encoded in the organization’s IT, managers weaken the informating property of IT (see Zuboff 1988). IT still records data about employees’ representation work, but managers rob these data of their informating property because they do not use these data for surveillance. By enforcing their own reporting practices, managers replace the informating property of IT in directing how employees report their work. The mix of these three types of reporting practices defines the transfiguration work that employees perform.

## Costs and Benefits Managers Use to Decide on Reporting Practices

The mix of reporting practices that individual managers impose depends on several factors, which can be grouped into costs and benefits. Our research highlights two costs of enforcing representation work and three benefits.

First, the effort that managers put into enforcing their own reporting procedures represents a cost. Employees may refuse to do the representation work that managers require. Managers can overcome this resistance by taking on the job of leading representation work in addition to leading their employees’ work. This requires managers to review the data in their organization’s IT to assess how much representation work employees have done. Managers also need to collect data from their employees’ improvised information systems to assess how much representation work employees can still do. Then they must develop and enforce reporting practices to close the gap between the data they want their employees to report and the data that their employees have actually reported. Managers cannot delegate any of these tasks to the informating property of IT, if they want to use data about their employees’ work to impress their leaders. At DeskSales, managers were happy to take on this burden so that they could take advantage of Siebel to impress their leaders.

Second, another cost of enforcing representation work stems from the trade-off between allocating employees’ efforts to doing work versus reporting work. Representation work may require so much effort that it reduces the time employees have to do their jobs. Managers can turn representation work into employees’ main job and may even impose work tasks just to improve the data that employees report. At DeskSales, managers imposed service work on salespeople so that they could report enough orders as sales to reach their targets.

Managers are willing to endure the costs of representation work in return for profiting from at least one dramaturgical benefit and two functional benefits. First, representation work can improve the image that managers present to their leaders. Leaders rely on the data in their organization’s IT to assess managers’ performance and to make decisions about managers’ careers (Boland 1993). If managers can make their employees report data consistent with an image of commitment and success, they can improve their career chances without improving their employees’ job performance. At DeskSales, managers only monitored their employees’ representation work in Siebel. They put no effort into making employees close any sales or do any kind of sales work.

Second, the façade that dramaturgical representations of work provide can protect improvised work practices that serve functional ends. This means that functional and dramaturgical goals complement, rather than hinder, one another. At Desk Sales creating a façade of successful sales work allowed salespeople to do their service work unimpeded. It was this service work which allowed desk salespeople to find customer orders that they reported as their own sales.

Third, representation work can improve the quality of information. Prescribed reporting procedures are a compromise between the multiple goals of various people in the organization (McLeod and Doolin 2012). Managers can help their organization by making employees report data that is necessary for people in other parts of the organization, but which is not required by reporting procedures. Field salespeople had desk salespeople provide data about service work that was not required by Siebel but which field salespeople needed to coordinate service work.

## Role of Leaders in Enabling Reporting Practices Enforced by Managers

The IT practices enacted by leaders affect the reporting practices that managers can impose. Our research surfaced three IT practices of leaders that allow managers to enforce their own reporting practices.

First, leaders may accept, rather than scrutinize, data that confirm their expectations and further their own interests. This allows managers to enforce their own reporting practices as long as employees report compliance with prescribed targets and prescribed procedures (even if employees do not do any of the necessary work to do so). Leaders may use information systems thus because they are more worried with showing success to their organization’s board or to the stock market than with monitoring employee compliance (Cunha 2013).

Second, when leaders do use IT to monitor compliance, they may only look at data about work, but not look at data about representation work. When leaders do not monitor employee compliance with reporting procedures, they weaken the informating property of IT over the representation of work. IT still records data about how employees report their work, but employees and their managers know that their leaders do not use these data to enforce compliance with reporting procedures. This opens a gap between what managers can have employees report and what employees actually do at work.

Third, when leaders do use IT to monitor compliance, they may rely on IT artifacts that are prepared by their managers (such as written reports or presentations) and that prevent leaders from accessing and analyzing data directly. Research has emphasized the opportunities for misrepresentation when there is a disconnect between the artifacts that employees use to work and their organization’s IT (Berente and Yoo 2012). Our study adds that there are also opportunities for misrepresentation when there is a gap between the organization’s IT and the IT artifacts that leaders use to monitor work.

## The Tasks of Transfiguration Work

The reporting procedures that managers can impose on employees entail four different tasks: selecting, collecting, manufacturing, and sprucing information. We use the label transfiguration work to describe this set of tasks. This label highlights that employees produce representations of work in their organization’s IT by transforming the data that they use to do their job.

## Selecting Information

Transfiguration work is a sequential process that begins with either selecting or collecting information. Selecting is defined as carefully choosing something as being the best or most suitable. In transfiguration work, selecting consists of choosing the data that are most suitable for producing information that impresses or informs others. These are data that managers have employees collate from the data that employees use to work. These data are the raw material for the representations of work that managers ask their employees to produce in their organization’s IT.

Specified thus, selecting separates the data that managers want employees to report from the data that they want employees to keep hidden. Having employees report only part of the data that they use to work serves two purposes. First, it reduces the burden of representation work. Employees provide only the data that others need to work or that helps managers impress their leaders. Second, employees are able to hide any data about their work that might jeopardize the image that managers want to present to their leaders or that might invite sanctions for any deviation from prescribed work procedures.

## Collecting Information

When managers have employees provide supplemental information, employees collect data that they do not need to work, rather than select data that they use to work. Collecting is defined as bringing or gathering together things, typically when scattered or widespread. In transfiguration work, it consists of finding and collating data that are accessible to people doing specific jobs, but that are unrelated to those jobs. These are data that organizations need and which employees are conveniently located to collect. Collecting these data provides other parts of the organization and its leaders with information that would be difficult to obtain otherwise. Having employees collect supplemental information also allows managers to present an image of compliance with reporting procedures.

## Manufacturing Information

Manufacturing has a double sense. It is synonymous with producing, assembling, and processing. However, it is also synonymous with making up, inventing, and fabricating. In transfiguration work, manufacturing consists of performing a set of operations on the data that managers have employees select and collect. These operations transform those data into information that employees may not need to work, but that managers want them to report.

The double sense of manufacturing highlights that managers may have employees produce representations of work for functional and dramaturgical purposes. They can ask employees to produce representations of work that improve coordination with other parts of the organization or help others do their own job. Managers may also have employees produce representations of work to impress their leaders. In this case, employees have to process the data that they previously selected or collected to produce representations of work that give the appearance of compliance with work procedures. By doing this, managers enlist employees to present an image of ability and commitment.

## Sprucing Information

Sprucing can mean to improve in appearance, but it can also imply pretense and deception. In transfiguration work, sprucing consists of reporting data—which can be accurate, but also distorted or fabricated—to meet the expectations of people who use those data. Employees may engage in sprucing in response to demands from other people in the organization or at the direction of their managers.

When other people in the organization get employees to spruce information to help them do their job, employees provide information according to a set of shared reporting norms. These norms may define the artifacts that employees should use to provide information, in addition to defining the procedures for reporting that information. Field salespeople asked desk salespeople to share information about their service work in a set of spreadsheets that were kept in Siebel records that were invisible to their managers.

When managers have employees spruce information to impress their leaders, they enforce their own reporting practices. These improvised reporting practices produce representations of work that are similar to those that employees would produce if they followed their organization’s prescribed reporting procedures. However, these representations may be built from data about work practices that differ greatly from prescribed work procedures (much like desk salespeople reported their service work as sales work). This ensures that employees produce embellished representations of their actions and achievements.

Together, these four tasks of transfiguration show that reporting work can be far more difficult and burdensome than simply transcribing data from one information system to another.

In the informating model, people report their work as they do their job because they use their organization’s IT to keep some of the information that they need to work (Burton-Jones 2014).

In the transfiguration model, reporting is a burdensome and difficult job that employees must do in addition to their everyday work. Some employees may be happy to take on this extra effort because they benefit from presenting an image of achievement and compliance. They can also use transfiguration work to earn bonuses for results that they have not really achieved. However, at DeskSales many salespeople also resented their transfiguration work. They only produced representations of sales work in Siebel to appease their managers.

## The System of Transfiguration

Our research shows that transfiguration work may be burdensome enough to require an information system of its own. We call this improvised information system the system of transfiguration. At DeskSales, the system of transfiguration was a chain of six sequential artifacts: four used by employees and two by managers.

## Employees’ Artifacts to Keep Information

The system of transfiguration starts with the artifacts in which employees keep the data that they transform into representations of work in their organization’s IT. Three artifacts can constitute this first link in the system of transfiguration. First, there are artifacts where employees keep the data that they need to work, such as desk salespeople’s to-do piles. These artifacts are part of the system of transfiguration because employees select some of these data, which they need to do their job, to produce representations of their work in their organization’s IT. Second, in other artifacts, employees keep data that they collect for the sake of their organization but that they do not need to perform their work, such as the reports that desk salespeople did for field salespeople. Third, organizations provide employees with some artifacts that contain the information needed to carry out prescribed work procedures, such as M-Tel’s pricing manual. Employees may use this information to manufacture representations of their actions when their everyday job is different from this prescribed work.

## Employees’ Artifacts to Process Information

The system of transfiguration also includes artifacts that employees use to manufacture and spruce the information about their work. Employees use these artifacts to help them carry out the reporting practices that managers impose on them, or the reporting norms that they negotiate with people in other parts of their organization. Employees may use the artifacts that support their everyday jobs to do their transfiguration work. However, employees may also improvise artifacts specifically to process information for transfiguration work, especially if they need resources or functionalities that are not available in the artifacts they use for their everyday jobs, such as the spreadsheets that some desk salespeople created to calculate revenue automatically.

## Employees’ Artifacts to Shortcut Reporting

Employees can replace the artifacts to process information with artifacts that simplify, abridge, or dispense with this process. Employees shortcut reporting by creating a generic record of each of their tasks, which can be duplicated to achieve the number of records that managers require them to produce. Employees can also keep a list of generic information that they copy into the records of their work in their organization’s IT. These are data that employees need to comply with the reporting practices that their managers impose, but which are not part of the data that they use to work.

## Employees’ Artifacts to Structure Reporting

Employees can use artifacts to monitor and pace their own transfiguration work. They may improvise their own artifacts, such as calendars and electronic reminders that prompt them to do their transfiguration work. Alternatively, employees can also use their organization’s IT to get reports of their pending transfiguration work, then check these reports regularly to ensure that they comply with their managers’ reporting requirements.

## Managers’ Artifacts to Monitor Reporting

Managers can also improvise a system of transfiguration for themselves. Managers use one set of artifacts to make transfiguration work more visible and therefore more amenable to supervision and leadership (e.g., desk sales managers’ forecast spreadsheets). With these artifacts, managers can monitor employees’ representation work as it unfolds. They can also use these artifacts to assess employees’ representation work before it becomes visible to the rest of the organization.

## Managers’ Artifacts to Enforce Reporting

Another set of artifacts helps managers enforce transfiguration work (e.g., desk sales managers’ team whiteboards). Managers use these artifacts to show employees the gap between prescribed and achieved targets and to discipline employees who lag behind. Such artifacts also tap into the motivational effects of exposure, as documented in a previous study of DeskSales (Cunha 2013) that revealed that exposure induces compliance with representation work, because employees are ashamed of unflattering performance data.

## Summary

Our description of the system of transfiguration offers a very different approach to the common discussion of shadow systems in organizations (e.g., Behrens 2009). Research has opposed shadow systems (the information systems that employees can improvise to do their job) to mandated systems (the information systems that organizations provide employees to do their job). However, our transfiguration model shows that the shadow systems that employees use to work are the source of data for mandated information systems. Shadow and mandated IS actually are links in the same chain, which connects the data that employees use to work with the data that they report. Specified in this way, shadow information systems can almost be considered the primary information system. Shadow systems not only keep the data that employees use to work but also the data that they use to report their work. Mandated information systems only keep the portion of those data that managers want their employees to share. The system of transfiguration and the transfiguration work that it supports suggests a specification of the quality of information that contrasts with that in the informating model.

## The Quality of Information under Transfiguration Work

Transfiguration work has contrasting effects on the quality of information. Transfiguration work provides high-quality information for some people, but it simultaneously produces poor-quality information for others. At DeskSales, sales managers had salespeople produce embellished representations of their work. For sales managers, this was highquality information because they could use it to impress their leaders. However, for the rest of M-Tel, it was low-quality information, filled with distorted records of employees’ actual actions and achievements. This contradictory effect of representation through transfiguration suggests a new specification of the quality of information, marked by three main differences from the specification implicit in the informating model (Zuboff 1988, see also Burton-Jones 2014).

## Quality of Information Is Subjective

Our research suggests a subjective specification of the quality of information. In this view, high-quality information provides a useful representation of employees’ work, rather than one that complies with prescribed reporting procedures. Employees provide high-quality information when they follow the improvised reporting practices that others request, instead of following prescribed reporting procedures imposed by their organization’s IT. These may be representations of work that managers enforce. However, these may also be representations of work that people in other parts of the organization convince employees to produce for them.

This subjective view contrasts with the objective view implicit in the informating model (Zuboff 1988). The informating model suggests that the quality of information should be assessed by the extent to which it provides an accurate, thorough representation of employees’ work. According to this view, when managers enforce reporting procedures other than those encoded in their organization’s IT, they reduce the quality of information that employees report (Burton-Jones 2014).

This is an objective, decontextualized view of the quality of information. It assumes that the data that organizations gather from their employees’ work is sufficient for people to do their jobs, including the managers who are evaluated on the data that their employees report (Carayannis 1999, Petter et al. 2013).

For leaders deciding about strategy, high-quality information may indeed require accurate representations of work that comply with prescribed reporting procedure (Arnott and Pervan 2005). However, for managers who want to impress their leaders, representations of work are better if there is a favorable gap between reality and what employees report (Cunha 2013). Moreover, for other people in the organization, high-quality information requires another kind of representation. Other people need representations that provide the data that they need to do their jobs even if these data are not required by their organization’s IT (Nan 2011). Even people doing the same job might require different representations of employees’ work, because of their different goals. At M-Tel, different field salespeople required different types of data from desk salespeople. At DeskSales, different sales managers imposed different reporting practices on their salespeople.

## Quality of Information Is Decided by People

Our subjective specification introduces the role of agency in the quality of information. The quality of information that other people receive depends on the representations of work that they require of employees. At DeskSales, some sales managers only required salespeople to report customer orders as sales in Siebel. Others imposed a specific sequence of steps for salespeople to follow. Still others demanded that salespeople report supplemental data that further improved the image that managers gave through their organization’s IT.

However, the quality of information is ultimately decided by the employees as they report their work. Employees appropriate, rather than simply adopt or accept, the reporting practices that managers (or other members of the organization) impose on them. Some employees may report their work as managers intend. However, other employees may improvise their own reporting practices. In so doing, they may produce representations of work that do not provide all of the data that others need. Our research suggests that employees may improvise their own reporting practices for two reasons.

First, they may be unwilling to comply with the reporting practices that others impose. These reporting practices may require efforts that employees are not willing to accept. Alternatively, these reporting practices may hinder employees’ own interests and goals.

Second, employees may be unable to comply with the reporting practices that others impose. Some salespeople at DeskSales were unable to establish a relationship with customers and with field salespeople that provided enough orders to report compliance with their sales targets.

## Quality of Information Is Improved Through Organizational Factors

Our subjective specification shows that organizational factors can take over technological factors when it comes to specifying the quality of information. Our research highlights the impact of three organizational factors on the quality of information.

The first is the balance between functional and dramaturgical goals for representation work specified by an organization’s context. The organizational context of desk sales managers, including their relationship with their leaders and their unit’s culture, had them enforce representation work to impress their leaders (see also Cunha 2013). The organizational context of field salespeople had them enforce representation work to learn about customers.

The second organizational factor that shapes the quality of information is employee motivation. Our research highlights that representation work can be so burdensome that employees may resist it, even if it benefits them. Furthermore, employees’ motivation to perform representation work is different from their motivation to do their jobs. Representation work competes with employees’ everyday work, so if organizations or managers do not motivate representation work directly, information quality might decline, due to laziness or the pressures of everyday work.

The third organizational factor that shapes the quality of information is the power of other parts of the organization over employees’ representation work. Other people in the organization can use employees’ representation work to address their own functional mismatch of IT. They can ask employees to report data that they need but that is not available in their organization’s IT. This allows people to improve the quality of information that they receive even if they have no power to change prescribed reporting procedures.

In summary, our transfiguration model improves the model of representation implicit in IS research. It also offers new avenues for organizations seeking to improve the quality of the information that gets reported into their IT, which they then use to make strategic decisions.

## Conclusion

Overall, our contribution to research is to provide a broader conceptual apparatus to study the representation of work in organizations. Our transfiguration model makes two contributions to current research on representation work. First, we extend the specification of agency in the representation of work. We show that agency in the representation of work should not be limited to the employees who report their work. Instead, agency needs to be broadened to people who impose their own representation procedures on employees, such as managers and people in other parts of the organization.

Second, we offer a broader specification of the process of representation work. We show that reporting data in an organization’s IT is but the last step in a sequence of practices that together constitute representation work. We also show that an organization’s IT is but the last link in a chain of information systems that support each step in representation work through transfiguration. This specification of representation work contrasts with the split between research on the shadow IT that employees use to do their work, and research on the mandated IT where employees have to report their work (Behrens 2009). We underscore the benefit of studying the network of practices and the network of artifacts that link these two types of information systems.

Our transfiguration model challenges managers to reflect much more carefully on how they manage the representation of work. Managers must be mindful of the burden that transfiguration work imposes on employees. The effort that employees devote to transfiguration work needs to be motivated just as much as the effort that they put into work. To motivate employees to take on this burden, managers should present transfiguration work as a valuable part of the job. Otherwise, employees may dismiss representation work as a marginal task that has no value other than appeasing the leaders of the organization.

Our transfiguration model suggests that researchers need a much better understanding of transfiguration work if they are fully to appreciate how IT is used, and how work is performed in practice.

First, future research should build a model of the organizational antecedents of the representation of work. Research has focused on the technological factors that shape the representation of work. These are the most important factors when IT reports work automatically (see Burton-Jones 2014; Stanko and Beckman 2015; Zammuto et al. 2007). However, we show that when employees self-report, organizational factors such as incentive structures and employee motivation, take precedence. Future studies can link macro-organizational processes (such as power dynamics and corporate culture) with employees’ reporting practices. Future studies can also explain how formal and informal relationships affect the production of electronic representations of work. We showed how managers imposed their own reporting procedures on employees. However, more research is needed to build a full relational account of the representation of work by showing how other members of an organization can shape the data that employees report in their company’s IT.

Second, future research should explain how representation through transfiguration changes the consequences of information systems. Transfiguration produces representations of work that are different from those that IT produces when it records work automatically. Therefore information systems that require transfiguration work will have different organizational consequences than information systems that record work automatically. We showed the effect of transfiguration work on the quality of information. Future research can discover and explain new consequences of information systems which have hitherto remained under-theorized because of the focus on IT that reports work automatically.

Many corporate scandals can be attributed to the misrepresentation of a company’s actions and achievements. Specifying transfiguration work and using it to research the organizational antecedents of representation allows scholars to play a role in attenuating and preventing such misrepresentations which have done much harm to the global economy.
