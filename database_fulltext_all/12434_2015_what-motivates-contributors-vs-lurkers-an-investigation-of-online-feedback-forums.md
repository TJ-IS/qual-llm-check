These findings are useful for understanding and promoting participation through differential strategies for contributors and lurkers in OPDFs in particular, and by extension, other feedback or online forums.

## 1. Introduction

Online forums are increasingly being established by organizations to solicit inputs and feedback from key stakeholders. For instance, firms are setting up forums to obtain employees’ inputs on corporate policy and other aspects (Hrastinski et al. 2011, Liang et al. 2012, Van Dyne et al. 2003), an example being IBM’s crowdsourcing jam forum to source incubator business ideas from its employees. In the consumer context, brand communities are enabling businesses to garner customers preferences and ideas on new products or services (Di Gangi and Wasko 2009, Goh et al. 2013, Jeppesen and Frederiksen 2006); e.g., MyStarbucksIdea gathers customer inputs on improving Starbucks’ products. Similarly, in the public sector, government organizations are building forums for citizens to participate in policy making (Olphert and Damodaran 2007, Phang et al. 2014), referred to as online policy deliberation forums (OPDFs).

Instances of OPDFs can be found around the globe, such as Estonia’s Osalusveeb (www.osale.ee), Singapore’s REACH (www.reach.gov.sg), and the United States’ eRulemaking program (www.regulations.gov), and may operate at different levels of government (e.g., city, state, national) where specific policies are formulated.

While empowering the respective stakeholders, i.e., employees, customers, and citizens, these forums also allow the host organization to collect inputs that can lead to improved decisions and actions. For instance, in the corporate context, employees’ constructive expression of concerns or ideas about work-related issues (referred to as voicing behavior) can help companies innovate and successfully adapt to dynamic business environments (Liang et al. 2012, Van Dyne et al. 2003). OPDFs, too, can offer benefits of significantly enhancing democratic practice and societal welfare by incorporating citizens’ inputs in policy making (Kumar and

Vragov 2009, Phang and Kankanhalli 2008). Through enabling civic engagement, these forums allow governments to respond to citizens’ increasing demand for democratic participation (Coleman and Gøtze 2001, Kumar and Vragov 2009).

OPDFs aim to promote the exchange of views on policy issues and allow citizens to build on each other’s ideas (Dahlberg 2001, Kumar and Vragov 2009). Compared to other public participation technologies such as microblogs and live chat rooms, OPDFs allow the storage of information (e.g., policy materials and discussion history) in a structured and persistent manner, so that participants can interact with each other and access information to collectively engage in policy deliberation. These forums thus facilitate policy deliberation that could, in turn, contribute toward the formulation of more inclusive and better accepted policies. Yet as with other feedback forums, promoting and sustaining participation in OPDFs remains a critical issue (Stromer-Galley and Wichowski 2011). For instance, the response to an online forum set up for the discussion of a local UK government project was described as a “real disappointment” (Olphert and Damodaran 2007, p. 502). Similarly, low participation rates have been reported in various OPDFs initiated under the UK Ministry of Justice’s Digital Dialogues program (Chadwick 2009).

The issue of how participation in online forums can be promoted has been a salient topic in information systems (IS) research (Ma and Agarwal 2007, Phang et al. 2009). However, prior research has mostly examined contributors’ behavior (e.g., Ma and Agarwal 2007, Wasko and Faraj 2005), with less attention paid to lurkers, i.e., individuals who only read and observe but do not contribute posts in online forums (Preece et al. 2004, Ridings et al. 2006). Although encouraging the participation of contributors is essential, it is also important to stimulate lurkers’ participation in feedback forums such as OPDFs for at least three reasons. First, a key objective of such forums is to promote the inclusion of feedback and ideas from more members (Dahlberg 2001). Eliciting inputs from lurkers can enhance the reach and range of contributions. Second, lurkers typically constitute a large percentage of visitors to online forums, e.g., as high as 90% (Emergence Marketing 2010). Thus, they present a large pool of potential contributors from whom ideas and feedback can be obtained. Third, the conversion of lurkers to contributors may serve to replenish the loss of contributors that is typically seen in online forums. For instance, Ridings and Wasko (2010) estimated an average attrition rate of over 50% of contributors per month in the forum they studied.

Whereas the understanding of what could motivate lurkers to contribute remains limited (Marett and Joshi 2009), the knowledge gap is even more pronounced for research that compares contributors and lurkers (a review of the literature is provided in Table A1 in the online appendix (available as supplemental material at http://dx.doi.org/10.1287/isre.2015.0599)). Such comparative research can lead to a more holistic strategy for sustaining online feedback forums, such as OPDFs. Additionally, prior research on online participation has investigated other contexts of online forums, e.g., knowledge sharing (Wasko and Faraj 2005), open-source software (OSS) development (Roberts et al. 2006), and social support (Ridings and Wasko 2010). With online forums not being monolithic (Ridings et al. 2002), there could be both similarities and differences in the participation drivers for feedback forums, such as OPDFs, compared to other forums. Similar to other online forums, participating in OPDFs (i.e., providing policy inputs) is a form of collective action where members voluntarily contribute their views and knowledge even when they do not know each other (Ma and Agarwal 2007). Thus, their participation leads to the creation of public goods (e.g., feedback to public agencies) that can be enjoyed by all, including those who do not contribute.

However, feedback forums, such as OPDFs, differ from other online forums in several ways. As mentioned before, the primary purpose of these forums is for organizations to gather inputs and feedback from their stakeholders. This purpose differs from those of other online forums, e.g., those for knowledge sharing or Q&A (e.g., Kankanhalli et al. 2005, Wasko and Faraj 2005), for particular interests or leisure (e.g., Ma and Agarwal 2007), or for social support (e.g., Ma and Agarwal 2007, Ridings and Wasko 2010). Thus, participants in feedback forums such as OPDFs expect the host organization to consider and potentially adopt their inputs (Coleman and Gøtze 2001), whereas participation in the other forums typically does not come with such expectations (e.g., Peddibhotla and Subramani 2007). In OPDFs it is considered good practice for the public agency to consolidate and summarize the inputs collected and explain how they have been taken into account (Coleman and Gøtze 2001). Similar expectations of adoption of submitted inputs have been noted in forums that collect feedback from employees (Hrastinski et al. 2011) and customers (Di Gangi and Wasko 2009). These unique aspects of OPDFs and feedback forums warrant separate study. Yet, even with the challenges agencies face in eliciting public participation, there is a lack of research about the participation antecedents of contributors and lurkers in feedback forums such as OPDFs that serves to motivate our study.

Because OPDFs specifically aim to facilitate public participation in policy discussions, to derive the antecedents of participation in OPDFs, we find it relevant to draw from public participation theory from the public administration literature. This enables us to identify factors that can more specifically explain public participation compared to alternative theories applied in other online community contexts, such as identity, social exchange, and social capital theories (e.g., Kankanhalli et al. 2005, Ma and Agarwal 2007, Wasko and Faraj 2005). In our study, we draw on the civic voluntarism model (CVM) (King et al. 2009, Verba et al. 1995), which is comprehensive in considering all three classes of participation antecedents, i.e., motivations, resources, and mobilization (Seyd et al. 2001). Other public participation theories focus on a subset of participation antecedents, e.g., the general incentives model (Seyd and Whiteley 2002) and resource-mobilization theory (Snow et al. 1986), or address community-level properties, e.g., social capital theory of participation (Putnam 1993), rather than individual-level antecedents that we are interested in.

Nevertheless, because prior public participation theories were mainly formulated in the off-line context, they did not consider how information technology (IT) can facilitate individuals’ participation. In this study, we are also interested in understanding the role of IT in facilitating collective action, i.e., participation in OPDFs to influence policy. For this purpose, we employ the public goods perspective of IT (Fulk et al. 1996, Yuan et al. 2007) that identifies two such characteristics of interactive virtual spaces, connectivity and communality. This perspective offers advantages over other possible theories such as the technology acceptance model and the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology, which explain individuals’ IT usage rather than the collective action through IT investigated in this study.

Building on the antecedents derived from the public participation and IT-enabled public goods theories, we aim to explain what drives contributors’ versus lurkers’ participation in OPDFs. The participation antecedents of the two groups are expected to differ due to their defining difference, i.e., contributors have direct experience of contributing, whereas lurkers read posts in the forum without contributing inputs. How this difference implicates the participation antecedents of contributors and lurkers can largely be explained through construal level theory (CLT) (Trope and Liberman 2010). As per this theory, lurkers, who have not yet contributed, should have an abstract mental construal of OPDF contribution. This makes them less sensitive to the concrete, incidental details (Trope et al. 2007) of participating, and leads to an idealized view of OPDF participation. However, once individuals have started contributing, they form a concrete mental construal about OPDF participation. This sensitizes them to the immediate situational constraints and opportunities of contributing and leads to a pragmatic view (Trope et al. 2007) of OPDF participation. Thus, CLT serves as an appropriate lens to understand differences in the antecedents of contributors’ and lurkers’ intention to participate.

Thus, we seek to address the following research questions: What are the drivers of participation (and their differences) for contributors versus lurkers in online feedback forums, in particular OPDFs? And how do the IT-related factors of perceived connectivity and communality influence contributors’ and lurkers’ participation in such forums? To answer these questions, we develop a research model of the antecedents of participation in OPDFs, which incorporates the differences between contributors and lurkers and takes into account the role of perceived connectivity and communality. We tested the proposed model through field data from a nationwide OPDF. By developing and validating the model, this research contributes to both research and practice in this area.

## 2. Conceptual Background

In this section we first review studies that compare contributors and lurkers in online forums and identify unexplained aspects that our study seeks to address. This is followed by a discussion of the theories that are employed as the foundations for our research model (CVM, IT-enabled public goods perspective, and CLT).

## 2.1. Review of Literature That Compares Contributors and Lurkers

Among the limited research that compares contributors and lurkers (see Table A1 in the online appendix, which also shows how this research compares to the previous work), earlier studies were primarily interested in understanding the differences in their actual behaviors rather than their motivations (Nonnecke et al. 2006, Preece et al. 2004, Ridings et al. 2006). Lurkers were found to be less enthusiastic in seeking information from others and in obtaining formal membership of forums compared to contributors (Nonnecke et al. 2006, Ridings et al. 2006). Later research by Mo and Coulson (2010) also supported such findings.

Among the few studies examining lurkers’ motivations, subsequent work showed that lurkers can be motivated to contribute to the community and become active once they start to participate (Farzan et al. 2010). Regarding the comparison of motives of the two groups, this was addressed to some extent by Marett and Joshi (2009), who explored the motivations of contributors and lurkers to share information in sports discussion forums. They found that lurkers are driven by normative influence (measured as the admiration of contributors), but not by enjoyment and status benefits, whereas contributors are motivated by enjoyment and normative influence. Their study adds to our understanding of the differences between lurkers and contributors in terms of their perceived importance of several motivational factors (status, enjoyment, and normative influence). However, there are other motivations for public participation not included in their study, such as political career benefit and political efficacy (Verba et al. 1995), that could be relevant for OPDFs.

Additionally, other types of drivers such as resources may affect individuals’ participation (Bishop 2007, Verba et al. 1995). Research on off-line public participation suggested that the possession of relevant resources can promote individuals’ participation decisions (Verba et al. 1995). This was echoed by Bishop (2007), who proposed a conceptual framework to explain contributors and lurkers’ online participation decisions. As per the framework, lurkers choose not to participate because they lack the resources (e.g., ability) for participation, in addition to the desire to do so. However, the framework was not empirically validated, and, more fundamentally, the differences between contributors and lurkers were inferred from previous findings without explicit theoretical bases. To advance our understanding in this area, this study seeks to develop a more comprehensive, theoretically grounded model of the participation antecedents of contributors versus lurkers and validate the model using a field survey. We now describe the theories that serve as foundations for our model.

## 2.2. Public Participation Theories and the Civic Voluntarism Model

Citizen participation has long been studied in public administration research (e.g., Barber 1984), which has given rise to several theories to explicate individuallevel antecedents of participation (Pattie et al. 2003, Seyd et al. 2001, Verba et al. 1995). Among them, the CVM (Verba et al. 1995), which proposes three broad classes of participation factors, i.e., motivations, resources, and mobilization, is considered the most comprehensive (Seyd et al. 2001). Its comprehensiveness is due to the inclusion of all three classes of participation drivers compared to other participation theories, such as the general incentives model (Seyd and Whiteley 2002), resource-mobilization theory (Snow et al. 1986), and social capital theory of participation (Putnam 1993). Whereas the general incentives model focuses on the motivations of participation, resource-mobilization theory explains the roles of resource and mobilization factors. The social capital theory of participation examines community properties, rather than individuals participation antecedents that are the focus of this study. Because of its empirical support (e.g., Barkan 2004, Pattie et al. 2003) and comprehensiveness, we employ the CVM as the primary theoretical foundation for this study.

The CVM (King et al. 2009, Verba et al. 1995) seeks to address why people do not participate in civic and political activities and then proposes motivations, resources, and mobilization as three classes of factors that make people participate. Specifically, motivations include benefits for individuals and groups, as well as a sense of political efficacy (Verba et al. 1995). Political efficacy refers to a person’s belief that political change is possible, i.e., government is responsive, and that citizens can play a part in bringing about this change (Anderson 2010). Resources include time, money, and civic skills that an individual possesses. Civic skills refer to an individual’s organizational and communication abilities that can facilitate participation, which may be acquired through practicing different skill acts (e.g., chairing meetings) in institutions such as the workplace, voluntary organizations, and religious institutions. Mobilization refers to the request from others for the individual to participate (Verba et al. 1995). For instance, an individual may be asked by her friends or family members to sign a petition or join a political party. We now expand on each of these classes of factors with reference to our research model.

The CVM highlights benefits for individuals and the group as participation motivations. Benefits for the group have been conceptualized as collective benefits, which refer to positive outcomes that can be enjoyed by all members of the community regardless of whether they participate (Seyd and Whiteley 2002, Sturmer and Simon 2004). Regarding benefits for individuals, furthering one’s political career is commonly considered as a motivating factor for public participation (Granik 2005, Seyd and Whiteley 2002, Verba et al. 1995). Specifically, people may participate in civic and political activities (e.g., campaigns, lobbying, local community efforts) to enhance their prospects of taking up more salient political roles. Additionally, as people engage in debates with each other on policy viewpoints in OPDFs (Kelly et al. 2006), we consider persuasion benefit as another participation motivation. Furthermore, as per the CVM, we also include political efficacy as a motivation for public participation in OPDFs.

In terms of resources, civic skills<sup>1</sup> have been highlighted as a key antecedent in the CVM (Verba et al. 1995) as well as in the public participation literature (Theiss-Morse and Hibbing 2005). Participating in OPDFs requires people to articulate their opinions and communicate with other participants; thus, civic skills may be an important driver for an individual’s participation. Last, mobilization, representing requests from others asking an individual to participate (Verba et al. 1995), is considered as a participation antecedent in our model. Although the CVM and participation literatures help us to identify the above factors that may influence contributors’ and lurkers’ participation in OPDFs, the public participation theories have mainly been confined to off-line participation. Thus, we make use of the IT-enabled public goods theory to explain the role of IT in OPDF participation.

## 2.3. IT-Enabled Public Goods Perspective

The role of IT in facilitating collective action by online forum members has been examined through the public goods perspective (Wiertz and de Ruyter 2007). Public goods theories address the problem of how to induce collective action among a group of actors who share some common goals (Samuelson 1954). They explain why and how people contribute voluntarily toward the generation of public goods in online forums that can be enjoyed by all (i.e., nonexcludable), and where the consumption of the goods by one person does not reduce its utility to others (i.e., nonrival; Marwell and Oliver 1993). Among these, Fulk et al. (1996) identified Markus’ (1990) critical mass theory of interactive media and Connolly and Thorn’s (1990) formulation of discretionary databases as two variants of public goods theories that can explain the role of IT in online communities. Markus’ (1990) theory highlights the need for IT to facilitate communication among community members for collective action. Connolly and Thorn’s (1990) research underscores the importance of IT that allows access to shared information by all community members. Building on these two views, Fulk et al. (1996) derived two IT-enabled factors, one related to communication (connectivity) and the other related to shared information access (communality), that can facilitate collective action in online forums.

Connectivity refers to the ease of reaching others who share similar interests or concerns (Fulk et al. 1996, Kumar and Benbasat 2002), whereas communality reflects the ease of accessing a common pool of information to perform generalized and productive exchanges (Fulk et al. 1996, Monge et al. 1998). Generalized exchanges occur when an individual contributes to and receives resources, e.g., knowledge, from different people (not a dyad). Productive exchanges build on generalized exchanges such that new resources, e.g., a knowledge base, may be generated from the exchange (Fulk et al. 1996). In an online forum, IT such as a bulletin board may contribute toward connectivity and communality perceptions in several ways. Related to connectivity, an online bulletin board records messages in a structured format where the identity information of contributors, e.g., their nicknames, is tagged with the message. This can allow individuals to easily identify others with similar interests and communicate with them, e.g., by clicking on “reply” or “message” buttons. Related to communality, an online bulletin board acts as a repository of information and discussion contents contributed by both policy makers and participants. Such a repository can reduce members’ cost of accessing relevant information (Kumar and Vragov 2009), which could facilitate generalized and productive information exchanges among them.

In the context of OPDFs as well as other feedback forums (Di Gangi and Wasko 2009, Hrastinski et al. 2011, Phang et al. 2014), it is vital for participants to communicate with each other and gain access to relevant information to deliberate and provide feedback on a focal issue. Previous literature examining the use of technologies for public participation (Kumar and Vragov 2009; Shah et al. 2005, 2007; Vedel 2006) has suggested that IT should offer related capabilities (similar to connectivity and communality), though they were not conceptualized and tested. For instance, Shah et al. (2005) emphasized the role of technologies as both a source of information and a sphere of communicative action for sharing political perspectives and concerns. Similarly, Vedel (2006) proposed three dimensions of e-democracy, i.e., information (instantaneous access to politically relevant content), discussion (communication and exchange among citizens), and how the preceding two may support the third dimension of online decision making and participation. Thus, although the literature has suggested that related concepts influence citizen participation, there is a lack of conceptualization and empirical study of the role of connectivity and communality, including in conjunction with other participation antecedents, as we aim to do. Next we discuss the CLT, which is used to explain the differences between contributors’ and lurkers’ participation antecedents.

## 2.4. Construal Level Theory

The CLT proposes that people may form concrete or abstract mental construals about an object or event that guide their preferences and behaviors. The formation of the mental construals is tied to individuals’ subjective perception about their distance from the object or event (Trope and Liberman 2010). The theory initially focused on the extent to which people perceive an event to be temporally distant or near and how this shapes their mental construals (Trope and Liberman 2003), but it was later extended beyond temporal distance to account for a general psychological distance that includes “remote locations, another person’s perspective, and counterfactual alternatives” (Trope and Liberman 2010, p. 440). Building on this notion of psychological distance, the theory posits that when people perceive a low psychological distance from an object or event, they tend to form low-level mental representations or concrete mental construals that focus on contextual and incidental details about the object or event. Conversely, when a high psychological distance is perceived, people are likely to form high-level mental representations or abstract mental construals about the object or event that are more general and decontextualized (Kivetz and Tyler 2007, Trope and Liberman 2010).

CLT has been employed in several IS and computermediated communication studies to explain phenomena such as consumers’ acceptance of online recommendations (Koehler et al. 2010), the tendency to search for information that confirms individual’s decisions (Shani et al. 2009), and the intention to use Internet services (Shen and Chiou 2009). For instance, Koehler et al. (2010) found that consumers accept product recommendations from interactive decision aids based on whether the need representations are abstract or concrete and whether the recommendation delivery is immediate or delayed.

Applying the theory to our study context, contributors, having direct contribution experience and thus a lower psychological distance from the act of contribution, are expected to form a concrete mental construal about it. Conversely, lurkers, who only observe and have not yet contributed in the forum, should perceive a higher psychological distance to contributing, which leads them to form an abstract mental construal about it. Thus, lurkers should be less sensitive to the incidental details of OPDF contribution compared to contributors when deciding to participate in the future. This is consistent with the notions of “current self” and “future self” suggested by Pronin et al. (2008); i.e., lurkers, whose “current self” has not contributed may foresee their “future self” to be a contributor in the OPDF. Indeed, such a prediction of future experiences by lurkers is likely to suffer from prediction biases that stem from underweighting the contextual and incidental details (e.g., constraints and opportunities) of the event when deciding their behavior (Libby and Eibach 2002, Pronin et al. 2008).

Building on the above logic, when an individual is a lurker, the abstract mental construal could lead her to adopt an idealized view of OPDF participation and be less sensitive to the pragmatic aspects when considering when to participate. With appropriate motivations, resources, and mobilization, she may decide to start contributing (Verba et al. 1995, Bishop 2007). However, once the individual starts contributing, situational constraints and incidental details are likely to become immediate and apparent. The concrete mental construal formed may then orient her to be more sensitive to pragmatic aspects when considering OPDF participation in the future. Our overall intuition above is consistent with prior technology acceptance literature; e.g., Venkatesh and Morris (2000, p. 122)

state that “[a] significant body of research 0 0 0 , including recent IS research 0 0 0 , has suggested that in the absence of direct behavioral experience with the target object, individuals anchor their perceptions to general/abstract criteria. 0 0 0 With increasing experience, user judgments reflect specific/concrete criteria that result from the interaction with the target object.” Thus, CLT (Trope and Liberman 2010) allows us to systematically explain and hypothesize differences between contributors and lurkers in terms of their motivational antecedents of participation in OPDFs. (A summary of the differences between contributors and lurkers in terms of CLT is provided in Table A2 in the online appendix.)

## 3. Research Model and Hypotheses

In this section, we develop our research model (see Figure A in the online appendix), which compares the drivers of contributors’ and lurkers’ OPDF participation intention,<sup>2</sup> i.e., motivations (collective benefits, political career benefit, persuasion benefit, political efficacy), resources (civic skills), and mobilization, derived above from the CVM and prior literature. Apart from these drivers, the two IT-related factors (perceived connectivity and communality) are also incorporated into the model based on the public goods perspective of IT. Based on CLT and the CVM, we then hypothesize whether an antecedent is important for contributors or lurkers. (For a summary of the rationales, refer to Table A3 in the online appendix.)

## 3.1. Motivations for Participation

3.1.1. Collective Benefits. The traditional rational choice perspective of participation contends that individuals are rational actors who will participate only if it leads to self-benefits. However, previous research (Seyd and Whiteley 2002) has noted that this view is too narrow in that it cannot account for why individuals sometimes participate despite the lack of benefits for themselves alone. Indeed it has been found that individuals’ willingness to participate is related to achieving benefits for the group or community (Sturmer and Simon 2004). Such benefits have been referred to as collective benefits or incentives (Seyd and Whiteley 2002), i.e., benefits that can be enjoyed by all people regardless of whether they participate. For instance, collective benefits could occur in the form of better policies that result from the participation of some citizens (Seyd and Whiteley 2002). Such benefits are similar to the ideology motive in online innovation forums (Paulini et al. 2014) and the community interest motive in previous online community literature (Wasko and Faraj 2000).

Based on CLT, we expect collective benefits to appeal to lurkers for their future participation, but not contributors. Lurkers, with their abstract mental construals, are likely to hold an idealized view (Kivetz and Tyler 2007, Trope et al. 2007) of OPDF contribution as it should be, i.e., citizens providing inputs for the formulation of better policies. Thus, collective benefits, in the form of improved policies enjoyable by all, should be salient for lurkers when deciding to participate in OPDFs in the future. This is consistent with findings from recent research indicating that lurkers could value community benefits (Farzan et al. 2010) and are not merely selfish free-riders as previously thought (Preece et al. 2004). However, contributors, due to their concrete mental construal and emphasis on the pragmatic aspects of participation (Kivetz and Tyler 2007, Trope et al. 2007), may be less motivated by such idealistic benefits. Consequently, collective benefits may not be important in determining contributors’ participation intention.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> Collective benefits are positively related to the intention to participate in OPDFs for lurkers, but not for contributors.

3.1.2. Political Career Benefit. As noted previously, people may participate in civic and political activities to enhance their prospects of gaining access to more salient political roles (Granik 2005, Seyd and Whiteley 2002, Verba et al. 1995). For instance, participants may become a grassroots leader in the local community or establish connections with political figures that can lead to better political career opportunities. The desire to further one’s political career parallels the desire for career advancement in corporate feedback forums (Paulini et al. 2014) and in other communities, e.g., for programmers contributing to OSS development (Roberts et al. 2006).

However, we expect political career benefits to be salient for contributors but not lurkers when deciding to participate in the OPDF. Based on CLT, contributors low-level pragmatic view (Trope et al. 2007, Kivetz and Tyler 2007) of OPDF participation makes them sensitive to the instrumental opportunities available through contribution, such as political career benefits. This resonates with prior research (e.g., Constant et al. 1994, Jarvenpaa and Staples 2000) that individuals are concerned with gaining private benefits when contributing knowledge. Conversely, as lurkers may be governed by a high-level and idealized view (Kivetz and Tyler 2007) of OPDF participation, they may not be motivated by such instrumental benefits in forming their participation intention. Thus, political career benefit should not be salient for them when deciding to participate.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> Political career benefit is positively related to an intention to participate in OPDFs for contributors, but not for lurkers.

3.1.3. Persuasion Benefit. Another participation motive that may be salient in OPDFs is the benefit of persuading others, because people with opposing views carry out debates in such forums (Kelly et al. 2006). In the context of our study, persuasion benefit implies that members think they can persuade others to adopt their own views through contributing to the forum. For instance, an individual may express his view that increasing taxes would be the right thing to do and try to shape others’ opinions to align with his view (Ryfe 2005) in an OPDF.

Deriving from CLT, we expect that the persuasion benefit will be salient for lurkers but not contributors when deciding about their intention to participate in OPDFs. Specifically, lurkers, with their abstract and idealized view (Trope et al. 2007) of participating in an OPDF, are likely to find a persuasion benefit important when considering their future participation intention, because this is an intended purpose of such forums. For instance, on www.nidirect.gov.uk, a UK e-government website, it is explicitly highlighted that “To promote a cause, no matter how great or small, you need to convince people that your ideas will work.”<sup>3</sup> On the other hand, with their concrete and pragmatic orientation (Kivetz and Tyler 2007) toward participation, contributors may not perceive the benefit of persuading others about their views, after being sensitized to the challenges of doing so on the OPDF.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> Persuasion benefit is positively related to an intention to participate in OPDFs for lurkers, but not for contributors.

3.1.4. Political Efficacy. In addition to the participation benefits discussed above, political efficacy has been suggested as a motivator of citizen participation as per the CVM (Verba et al. 1995). Political efficacy refers to individuals’ perception that government bodies will respond to citizens’ inputs and that the individual is qualified to contribute relevant policy inputs (Anderson 2010). Such a perception is formed jointly by individuals’ internal beliefs about their competence to contribute policy views and their external beliefs about the responsiveness of government authorities to citizen inputs (Dyck and Lascher 2009, Niemi et al. 1991). An individual’s efficacy, in general, has been recognized as an important determinant of their behavior (Bandura 2012). In the context of off-line citizen participation, previous studies have suggested a positive relationship between political efficacy and participation (Anderson 2010, Brady et al. 1995).

Based on CLT, we expect political efficacy to matter for contributors but not for lurkers in forming their OPDF participation intention. This is because contributors have a concrete mental construal (Trope and Liberman 2010) and experience of contributing, whereby internal efficacy may motivate their participation (Bandura 2012). The external belief that government is responsive to citizen inputs should also be important for contributors or else they may not participate in OPDFs again (with their pragmatic concerns). By contrast, for lurkers, who have an abstract mental construal and lack contributing experience, internal efficacy may not be salient in determining their participation intention. Furthermore, even though lurkers may have an idealized view of OPDF participation, this does not imply that they will be convinced of external political efficacy, i.e., a general belief in a government’s responsiveness to citizens’ concerns. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4).</sup> Political efficacy is positively related to an intention to participate in OPDFs for contributors, but not for lurkers.

## 3.2. Individual Resources for Participation

As per the CVM, civic skills may be a particularly relevant resource for public participation. Civic skills refer to individuals’ organizational and communication abilities that can facilitate their participation (Theiss-Morse and Hibbing 2005, Verba et al. 1995). Participating in OPDFs requires people to articulate their opinions and engage in discourses with other participants. Individuals with better civic skills should be able to communicate their views well during the deliberation process and organize inputs, thus facilitating their participation in OPDFs.

However, we expect civic skills to be a driver of the participation intention of lurkers, but not that of contributors. In fact, a lack of civic skills has been noted as a salient reason in the CVM that may explain why an individual has not participated (Verba et al. 1995). By contrast, with the experience gained from communicating their views in the OPDF, contributors are less likely to consider civic skills as important in determining their intention to contribute again in the forum. Prior research notes that once individuals start to perform a behavior, they may not be concerned about their ability to perform it (Venkatesh 2000). Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5</sup> <sup>(H5).</sup> Civic skills are positively related to an intention to participate in OPDFs for lurkers, but not for contributors.

## 3.3. Mobilization

Mobilization refers to a request from others to ask an individual to participate (Verba et al. 1995). Mobilization is distinct from but related to the concept of subjective norms that has been recognized as a significant determinant of individuals’ behavior in well-established theories such as the theory of planned behavior (Ajzen 1991) and social cognitive theory (Bandura 1989). Specifically, Whiteley (1995) argues that mobilization works because people are influenced by subjective norms, in this case the pressure to conform to others’ requests to participate. This argument is consistent with prior studies of knowledge sharing communities (e.g., Bock et al. 2005) where subjective norms have been identified as an antecedent of individuals’ participation. Mobilization for citizen participation typically occurs in the form of requests to participate from an individual’s relatives, friends, acquaintances, or sometimes strangers (Verba et al. 1995).

As per the CVM, mobilization is important for encouraging lurkers (or nonparticipants in general) to participate (Verba et al. 1995). This logic is consistent with previous arguments (Venkatesh and Morris 2000) that subjective norms may be critical when people have little or no prior experience with a target object. Thus, mobilization is expected to influence lurkers intention to participate. However, having participated in the OPDF, contributors may not find requests to participate as salient. Once they have already begun to contribute, mobilization pressure may not be perceived as important by contributors. Hence, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>6</sup> <sup>(H6).</sup> Mobilization is positively related to an intention to participate in OPDFs for lurkers, but not for contributors.

## 3.4. Connectivity and Communality

In this study, we are interested in understanding how individuals’ perceptions about two IT-related factors, i.e., connectivity and communality (Fulk et al. 1996, Yuan et al. 2007), may influence their participation in OPDFs. Although other IT-related factors such as ease of use could be examined, we limit our attention to these two factors because they are particularly relevant to participation in online forums for collective action (in this case OPDFs), as per the IT-enabled public goods perspective (Connolly and Thorn 1990, Fulk et al. 1996, Markus 1990).

Broadly, connectivity and communality can be seen as IT facilitating conditions (for the collective action of policy deliberation in this context) according to the technology acceptance literature (Venkatesh 2000). IT as a facilitating condition may influence behavioral intention and/or the motivations of the behavior (Schifter and Ajzen 1985, Venkatesh 2000). Accordingly, we hypothesize perceived connectivity and communality as having a direct effect on participation intention and/or an indirect effect through influencing participation motivations, based on the differences in their roles as explained in §§3.4.1–3.4.3. (For a summary of these effects, refer to Table A4 in the online appendix.)

3.4.1. Effects of Perceived Connectivity on Participation Intention. Perceived connectivity refers to the ease of reaching others who share similar interests or concerns through IT (Fulk et al. 1996, Kumar and Benbasat 2002). Zhang (2008) notes that the ability of IT to enable individuals to connect with others can motivate their use of IT. In a similar vein, we expect perceived connectivity to directly influence participation as it enables people to easily connect with those who are interested in similar issues through the OPDF. However, we propose that perceived connectivity will appeal differently to contributors and lurkers.

Specifically, with their concrete mental construal and pragmatic view (Kivetz and Tyler 2007) of OPDF participation, contributors may desire acknowledgment of their postings from others as reinforcement. Previous literature has confirmed that getting a response will increase a contributor’s likelihood of posting again to the group (Joyce and Kraut 2006). This will be more likely if the IT is perceived as able to reach people with similar interests who tend to respond and thereby reinforce contributor’s future participation. By contrast, lurkers, with their idealized view of OPDF participation, may not appreciate this capability because they may not have experienced the necessity to get such acknowledgment from others. Hence, they may not value the connectivity in the OPDF. Similarly, differential effects of IT-related factors on individuals behavioral intention based on their mental construals have been demonstrated in the literature. For instance, Shen and Chiou (2009) show that perceived ease of use is not significant for individuals’ use decision when they have an abstract mental construal, although this factor is important for system use in general (Venkatesh 2000). Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>7</sup> <sup>(H7).</sup> Perceived connectivity is positively related to an intention to participate in OPDFs for contributors, but not for lurkers.

3.4.2. Effects of Perceived Communality on Participation Antecedents. Perceived communality refers to the ability of IT to provide a common repository that allows easy access to information for better policy deliberation. We do not expect perceived communality to directly influence participation intention consistent with the previous literature (Shah et al. 2005), which found that access to and use of relevant information did not directly affect citizen participation. Rather, by facilitating access to policy information, we expect communality to play an indirect role by helping people to obtain and perceive other benefits from participation. In this way, communality is similar to the concept of information accessibility in the technology acceptance literature (Teo et al. 2003) that has been found to indirectly influence usage through increasing the perceived usefulness of a technology.

Specifically, by facilitating access to information (e.g., policy details and discussion contents) through a common repository such as an online forum, communality may enable productive exchanges. Productive exchanges involve assembling and analyzing information to create something new (Fulk et al. 1996), in this case synthesizing different ideas for improved policy formulation. When members perceive that the forum system allows easy access to such information, they will consider it more likely that their participation could contribute toward better policy formulation for all, i.e., collective benefit (Verba et al. 1995). Thus, perceived communality should be related to the collective benefits that members perceive from participating in the OPDF. Given the primary role of communality in facilitating collective policy deliberation in OPDFs, we do not anticipate a difference in its effect for contributors and lurkers. Although contributors could perceive its importance firsthand, lurkers should also consider communality important for perceiving collective benefits because they can observe information exchanges in the forum. This leads us to hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>8</sup> <sup>(H8).</sup> Perceived communality is positively related to the collective benefit perceptions of both contributors and lurkers.

Previous research has also shown that having access to relevant information is important for individuals when they attempt to persuade others (Chaiken 1980). Because persuading people involves paying attention to what people say and how they say it, IT such as an online bulletin board may facilitate these generalized exchanges by providing a persistent record of conversations (Erickson and Herring 2005). This can help members understand the policy-related information as well as the positions held by others, thus facilitating and highlighting this benefit of OPDFs. Hence, perceiving a high communality in the forum system may enhance the persuasion benefit perception of both contributors and lurkers (who can observe such exchanges in the OPDF).

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>9</sup> <sup>(H9).</sup> Perceived communality is positively related to the persuasion benefit perceptions of both contributors and lurkers.

3.4.3. Perceived Connectivity and Communality. Additionally, we expect perceived connectivity to lead to an enhanced perception of communality. Specifically, when perceived connectivity is high, individuals may perceive relevant information to accumulate easily in the forum because participants can readily engage in discussions on topics of interest together.<sup>4</sup> This may, in turn, make it easier to perform generalized and productive exchanges, i.e., based on the accumulated information of discussion contents (Fulk et al. 1996, Monge et al. 1998). Consequently, individuals (both contributors and lurkers) may perceive a high communality in the forum, when their perception of connectivity is high.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>10</sup> <sup>(H10).</sup> Perceived connectivity is positively related to perceived communality for both contributors and lurkers.

Although we do not hypothesize a direct effect of connectivity on collective and persuasion benefits, given the central role of information in attaining these benefits,<sup>5</sup> we expect perceived connectivity to indirectly influence these benefits via perceived communality as per H10. However, both perceived connectivity and communality are not expected to influence the other participation antecedents (political career benefit, political efficacy, civic skills, mobilization) based on their role and the nature of those antecedents. (For further details, refer to Discussion A in the online appendix.)

## 4. Research Methodology

## 4.1. Research Context

Our research model was tested through a field survey of members of a nationwide OPDF. The OPDF under study is an ongoing initiative that invites citizens to provide their feedback on a wide range of national policy issues, covering areas such as the community, economy, education, environment, health, transportation, and urban planning. Examples of these issues include whether to extend a smoking ban to more public places and how the public transportation system can be improved. For each policy issue listed in the forum, the public agency responsible provides the policy’s background information, related documents, and links to relevant resources. At the end of the policy deliberation exercise, inputs from citizens and their effects on policy making are summarized and published.

When participating in the forum, individuals may use their real name or a nickname. When this study was conducted, there were 1,061 unique valid contributors’ names that were identified in the forum in total (to minimize duplicate counting, we verified the contributors’ names using their email addresses). There were, on average, 177 contributions made by 44 participants per week in the forum. The forum has been implemented using an online bulletin board that stores and organizes the discussion of different policy issues into threads, in which the responses are attached to their respective thread topics in a chronological order. Each response is tagged with the contributor’s name as well as the date and time when the response was submitted.

## 4.2. Construct Operationalization

The survey measures for the model constructs were generated or adapted based on relevant instruments from the public administration and IS literatures wherever possible. Because the study employs several new measures (specifically, persuasion benefit, perceived connectivity, and perceived communality), a thorough instrument validation was done as per procedures given in DeVellis (2003). Face validity and content validity of the items were assessed in the following ways. First, item pools were generated for the constructs based on their conceptual definition and description, which were then examined by three experts on online community and participation. They identified minor problems in the framing and phrasing of the items, which were rectified. Second, all items were tested for conceptual validity by conducting sorting sessions with four judges. Third, a pilot test of the questions was administered to 65 undergraduate students who were members of an online political forum. Minor modifications were made to the items based on the pilot results. All items were measured using a seven-point Likert scale (from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”), except civic skills (five-point Likert scale from “never” to “often”) and mobilization (single-item variable from Verba et al. 1995 with a “yes” or “no” response). The civic skills and mobilization items were based on existing measures from the previous literature (Brady et al. 1995, Verba et al. 1995). Table 1 summarizes the construct operationalization.

It should be noted that civic skills and political efficacy were modeled as formative constructs, whereas the other constructs (except mobilization, which is a singleitem measure) were modeled as reflective. Formative con structs are a composite of multiple measures, whereas reflective constructs have observed measures that are affected by an underlying latent, unobservable construct (Petter et al. 2007). By contrast to reflective measures where a change in the construct affects the underlying measures, changes in the formative measures cause changes in the underlying construct (Cenfetelli and Bassellier 2009). Below we briefly describe the operationalization of the formative constructs in this study, i.e., civic skills and political efficacy (the reflective constructs are evident from Table 1).

The civic skills construct is modeled as a secondorder formative construct that is composed of three first-order formative constructs, each capturing a set of skill acts practiced in different societal institutions (see Table 1). Per the established conceptualization from Verba et al. (1995), individuals’ civic skills can be developed through practicing a set of five different skill acts, i.e., writing to express views, taking part in decision making in a meeting, chairing a meeting, planning for a function, and giving a presentation or speech. These skill acts were found to be particularly relevant for individuals’ organization and communication abilities to participate in political activities (Barkan 2004, Pattie et al. 2003). Furthermore, civic skills can be honed by practicing these skill acts in three major societal institutions, i.e., the workplace, voluntary organizations (e.g., rotary clubs, volunteer associations), and religious institutions (Verba et al. 1995). Hence, the first-order civic skills formed by individuals’ practice of the five skill acts at the respective societal institutions, together contribute toward their overall (second-order) civic skills. Such a comprehensive measurement of civic skills is considered more appropriate than asking the respondents to self-describe their skills, because the latter may confound with the respondents’ efficacy belief (Brady et al. 1995).

Political efficacy is operationalized as a second-order formative construct consisting of two first-order reflective constructs, i.e., internal political efficacy (IPEF) and external political efficacy (EPEF) dimensions (see Table 1). The internal dimension captures individuals beliefs about their competence to discuss policy issues, whereas the external dimension captures the responsiveness of government authorities to citizens’ inputs (Dyck and Lascher 2009, Niemi et al. 1991).

Apart from the hypothesized factors, we also measured the following control variables: gender, age, education, income, Internet experience, off-line participation, and duration in the forum, which may influence individuals’ participation in the OPDF. Previous research noted that males tend to be more active in public participation than females (Herring 1996). Age, income, and education represent three basic socioeconomic factors that have been argued to influence participation, with those who are older and have higher income and educational levels expected to show a higher inclination to participate (Verba and Nie 1972). Internet experience concerns individuals’ familiarity with using the Internet, which should facilitate their online participation (Garramone et al. 1986). Additionally, those who have been active in off-line participation (e.g., attending public meetings) may have a greater propensity to participate using electronic channels (Garramone et al. 1986). Last, individuals who have been members of an online forum for a longer duration are more likely to be active in the forum (Bryant et al. 2005).

## 4.3. Data Collection

The field survey was administered through an online questionnaire. The URL of the online survey was published on the OPDF website with agreement from the public agency responsible for administering it. When respondents clicked on the URL, they were directed to the survey website. A background of the study that stated the research objective was first provided to the respondents. The respondents were assured of the confidentiality of their responses and that only aggregated responses would be reported. At the start of the survey, respondents were first presented with a filtering question that asked whether they had posted any message to the forum, to differentiate between contributors and lurkers. As a further check, we included a question toward the end of the questionnaire that asked the respondents to state example(s) of the policy issues they had contributed to in the forum (contributors should state at least one issue, whereas lurkers should state none), which was verified based on the forum discussion log. All respondents received a token of appreciation in the form of a shopping voucher sent to the mailing address that they provided.

Altogether, 225 completed responses were received, of which 118 were from contributors and 107 were from lurkers. However, of the 118 responses from contributors, 17 were not used for our analyses because the usernames could not be identified in the forum or they were duplicate entries based on verification using both the email and mailing addresses provided. This resulted in 101 usable responses for contributors, with a response rate of 9.5% based on the 1,061 valid contributors’ names identified in the forum.<sup>6</sup> The rate is typical for online surveys (Sills and Song 2002). Table A5 in the online appendix presents the demographic information of the respondents.

To test for possible nonresponse bias, we compared the means of all variables and demographics for early and late respondents (Oppenheim 1966), as done in previous studies (e.g., Ma and Agarwal 2007). The results of t-tests showed that one demographic variable, i.e., gender, was significantly different between early and late respondents, with males tending to respond earlier than females. Gender was included in the model as one of the control variables to account for its potential influence. Additionally, we compared the means of the dependent variable, participation intention, for the contributor and lurker samples. The t-test showed that their participation intentions were significantly different (p < 0001), i.e., contributors had a higher intention than lurkers, as expected. The difference remained significant even when contributors who posted only once (N = 20) were compared with lurkers $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$

## 5. Data Analysis and Results

Partial least squares (PLS) analysis was used to validate the model because it allows for the incorporation of both reflective and formative constructs, i.e., political efficacy and civic skills, present in our model (Chin 2010). Though possible to analyze, formative measurement poses challenges for covariance-based techniques like LISREL (Gefen et al. 2011); e.g., identification of estimates can be more complex than under PLS (Cenfetelli and Bassellier 2009).

## 5.1. Measurement Model Analysis

Convergent and discriminant validity were assessed for the reflective measures (MacKenzie et al. 2011). Table A6 in the online appendix shows the descriptive statistics, Cronbach’s alpha (CA), composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE) of the reflective constructs. It can be seen that all reflective constructs exhibited acceptable convergent validity, i.e., CA > 007, CR > 007, and $\mathrm { A V E } > 0 . 5$ (MacKenzie et al. 2011). The constructs also demonstrated acceptable discriminant validity. The results of factor analysis (see Tables A7 and A8 in the online appendix) show that the factor loadings of all items on their intended construct are above the minimum recommended level of 0.5, and each item loads higher on its intended construct than on any other construct (Hair et al. 2010). Discriminant validity was also assessed by comparing the square root of AVE of each construct against its correlations with other constructs (MacKenzie et al. 2011). The results (see Tables 2 and 3) show that the diagonal elements (square root of AVE) exceeded other entries in the same row or column, thus satisfying the validity requirement.

For the formative constructs (civic skills and political efficacy), we assessed the weights of their measures. As shown in Table A9 in the online appendix, all of the items contributed substantially to their intended constructs, indicating that they are valid and important formative measures for their respective constructs (Cenfetelli and Bassellier 2009).

## 5.2. Structural Model Analysis

Prior to testing the hypotheses, all measures were standardized<sup>7</sup> to avoid scale issues. Subsequently, we tested the hypotheses by estimating the model parameters for contributors and lurkers separately (see Table 4). Because the links (except for H8–H10 that are common to both models) were significant in one model and not in the other, it was strictly not necessary to compare the group coefficients. However, as an extra step, we performed a between-groups significance test (Hsieh et al. 2008),<sup>8</sup> whose results are also reported in Table 4.

From the table it can be seen that lurkers are motivated by collective benefits, but not contributors (i.e., H1 is supported). By contrast, contributors are motivated by political career benefits and political efficacy, but not lurkers (i.e., H2 and H4 are supported). The remaining motivation, i.e., persuasion benefit, influences lurkers participation intention only at the 0.10 level, while having no effect on contributors (i.e., H3 is weakly supported). The resource factor, civic skills, is important in determining lurkers’ participation intention, but not contributors’ (i.e., H5 is supported). Furthermore, mobilization affects lurkers’ participation intention, whereas it has no effect on contributors’<sup>9</sup> (i.e., H6 is supported). For the IT-related factors, participation intention of contributors but not lurkers is influenced by perceived connectivity as expected (i.e., H7 is supported). Perceived communality, on the other hand, significantly enhances both contributors’ and lurkers’ perceptions of collective and persuasion benefits (i.e., H8 and H9 are supported). Additionally, perceived connectivity significantly influences perceived communality (i.e., H10 is supported).

Among the control variables, gender, education, and income were found to affect contributors’ intention to participate, whereas education influenced lurkers’ participation intention. Specifically, contributors who are female, have a lower education level, or have a higher income level were more likely to participate. For lurkers, those who have a higher education level expressed a lower intention to participate. Overall, the explanatory power (R<sup>2</sup>5 for contributors’ participation intention is 42.5%, whereas it is 41.6% for lurkers participation intention. Additionally, perceived communality explained 16% and 9% of contributors’ and lurkers’ collective benefit perceptions, respectively, and 11% and 12% of contributors’ and lurkers’ persuasion benefit perceptions, respectively. Furthermore, perceived connectivity explained 18% and 28% of perceived communality, respectively, for the contributor and lurker samples.

Last, to ensure the robustness of our results, we assessed whether there is common method bias, which is a potential threat to internal validity for research using surveys that collect responses in a single setting (Malhotra et al. 2006). A series of four tests were conducted: Harman’s one-factor test, correlating contributors’ participation intention with an objective measure of their number of postings, the common method latent variable test, and the modified marker variable test (Pavlou et al. 2007, Pavlou and Gefen 2005, Podsakoff et al. 2003). The results show that such bias is unlikely to be a concern for our study (for details about the tests, refer to Test A in the online appendix).

## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

The objective of this study was to explain the drivers of participation intention in OPDFs for contributors versus lurkers. The antecedents were derived based on the CVM (Verba et al. 1995, King et al. 2009) and IT-enabled public goods perspective (Fulk et al. 1996, Monge et al. 1998), from which different hypotheses for contributors and lurkers were formulated primarily through the CLT (Trope and Liberman 2010). The model was well supported empirically, with only one hypothesis being weakly supported (the effect of a persuasion benefit on lurkers’ participation intention is significant at the 0.10 level).

## 6.1. Research Contributions

Our study offers several important research implications with respect to the theories employed (CVM, IT-enabled public goods perspective, CLT) and participation in OPDFs, feedback forums, and online forums in general. First, we contribute toward a more holistic theory of participation in OPDFs by deriving participation antecedents from the CVM (Verba et al. 1995) and related literature and integrating them with the IT-related factors from the IT-enabled public goods perspective (Fulk et al. 1996). Specifically, although the CVM proposes participation antecedents in terms of motivations, mobilization, and resources, it limits its attention to resources possessed by an individual, e.g., civic skills. Resource factors in the online environment that may directly or indirectly facilitate participation have not been considered in the CVM. Through the IT-enabled public goods perspective, we identify two such factors, i.e., connectivity and communality. These IT factors serve as facilitating conditions that may alleviate constraints in organizing mass collective action in online settings and thereby promote participation. In this way, our study advances a theory for explaining public participation in OPDFs, which are becoming increasingly prevalent (Phang and Kankanhalli 2008).

Second, through the CLT and CVM, our research identifies the contrasting antecedents that influence contributors’ and lurkers’ participation in OPDFs. Here, our study advances previous literature (e.g., Marett and Joshi 2009, Mo and Coulson 2010, Ridings et al. 2006) by systematically theorizing and validating the different antecedents of contributors’ versus lurkers’ participation. Additionally, our findings provide a theoretically grounded explanation about the nature of lurkers, who are far less understood than contributors in the existing literature (Bishop 2007, Mo and Coulson 2010). Particularly, previous research has tended to view lurkers as selfish free-riders (Kollock and Smith 1999), or individuals who “morally justify” their lurking behavior as not wanting to add to information overload problems to the community. Our research adds to a few other studies’ position (Farzan et al. 2010, Preece et al. 2004) that these views about lurkers warrant a change. Specifically, through understanding lurkers as observers with an abstract mental construal, our study finds that lurkers can be motivated by higher-order benefits, e.g., the collective benefits of producing more comprehensive policies in OPDFs.

Third, this study extends our understanding of what drives participation in online feedback forums where participants provide inputs to influence organizational decision making, a manifestation being OPDFs. Our focus on OPDFs led us to employ the CVM (Verba et al. 1995) to derive participation antecedents that are relevant to this context. For instance, collective benefits were conceptualized in terms of public policy-related benefits for the community; political efficacy was modeled as a specific form of efficacy tailored for OPDFs; and political career benefit was included as a specific form of career advancement benefit in OPDFs. Nonetheless, given their common objectives (i.e., gathering stakeholders’ inputs to aid decision making), these antecedents may be applied to other online feedback forums with appropriate adaptations. For instance, in the context of customer feedback forums, Di Gangi and Wasko (2009) observed that customers desire to exert a greater influence over product development and have their feedback accepted by the host firm. Like wise, Hrastinski et al. (2011) found that the employees they interviewed in an organizational feedback forum desired greater consideration of their contributed ideas. In light of these findings, future research may test the importance of participants’ efficacy in other feedback forums, in terms of the organization’s responsiveness to their feedback (external dimension), as well as the confidence in their own competence to participate (internal dimension). Table A10 in the online appendix provides a comparison of our model constructs with those examined in the prior literature and an overview of how the constructs may be applied to other online feedback forums. It also highlights additional constructs, i.e., persuasion benefit and civic skills, introduced to the online participation literature by this study.

As can be seen from Table A10, our study also provides implications for the investigation of other types of online forums and communities beyond feedback forums. The table shows that some of the antecedents in this study, such as collective benefits (similar to community interest) and political career benefit (similar to career opportunities), may be appropriately adapted for investigating participation in other online communities, such as knowledge sharing groups. Individuals in these online communities participate to create a repository of knowledge beneficial to all members, while pursuing possible career advancement when their contributions are noted by employers or prospective employers. In addition, the civic skills construct investigated in this study should be relevant for research on other online communities in which the ability to clearly articulate and communicate ideas or views is important, such as for providing effective emotional support in social support communities. Mobilization may also be a useful construct for research on online communities in general where individuals can be triggered to participate by requests from others, such as influential community members. Nonetheless, the relevance of some antecedents may be more limited, e.g., political efficacy, in which the external dimension may be less applicable for online communities where the main focus of participants is not to contribute feedback to influence organizational decision making.

Fourth, our research also contributes to the voicing literature in the corporate context (Liang et al. 2012, Van Dyne et al. 2003) by extending our findings about individuals’ behavior of expressing constructive opinions, concerns, or ideas in OPDFs. Although our study context differs from the typical corporate context of voicing in which people fear negative consequences (such as losing their job position) due to the tightly organized and managed environments (Liang et al. 2012), voicing research is also interested in understanding why people express opinions or remain silent (Van Dyne et al. 2003). Relevant to this research problem, our study unveils the different factors that influence people who have already contributed (contributors) versus those who remain silent (lurkers) in the OPDF context. Additionally, the role of IT is also highlighted because participation behavior is IT mediated in OPDFs.

Last, this research affords a more nuanced understanding of the role of perceived connectivity and communality (Fulk et al. 1996, Monge et al. 1998) in facilitating participation. Particularly, our findings suggest that perceived connectivity has a direct impact on the participation intention of contributors, but not lurkers. This finding complements Zhang’s (2008) work that highlights the ability to connect with others via IT as a salient motivational affordance of IT. We show that the connectivity afforded by IT is indeed important in motivating participation, but its importance is contingent on whether the individual possesses a concrete mental construal regarding participation. Additionally, connectivity has indirect effects on lurkers’ participation intention via communality, which enhances both the collective and persuasion benefit perceptions of contributors and lurkers. The conceptualization and operationalization of connectivity and communality developed here can be employed in future research to model the role of IT in influencing online participation. The two IT-enabled factors may be relevant for research on online communities in general wherever there is the potential for generating public goods through members’ contributions.

## 6.2. Implications for Practice

Our study informs practitioners to devise more comprehensive and customized interventions to improve participation levels in OPDFs. Specifically, the findings highlight the need for OPDF managers to adopt different approaches for encouraging contributors to participate again versus converting lurkers into contributors.

To motivate contributors to participate again, OPDF managers need to recognize that these individuals are particularly influenced by the pragmatic and instrumental opportunities of the forum. Thus, one approach is to increase their political career benefit perception by creating networking opportunities for contributors to connect with well-known political representatives and by recruiting contributors for more active political involvement (e.g., allowing them to serve on a citizen panel). Additionally, OPDF managers need to improve contributors’ confidence that their inputs can indeed bring about desired changes in policy, i.e., political efficacy. This may be achieved through active efforts to convince contributors that their inputs matter for policy making. Newsletters and other materials that detail how citizen inputs have been incorporated in policy making can be published for this purpose. Furthermore, perceptions about connectivity in the forum could be improved through features that allow for easy identification of individuals’ interests (e.g., by allowing individuals to indicate these in their profile) and for greater interaction between members.

To appeal to lurkers to start contributing, OPDF managers need to recognize that lurkers subscribe to an idealized view of participation. Specifically, OPDF managers may proactively highlight to lurkers the collective benefits they can attain through contributing to the community. It is important to recognize that as lurkers may perceive a high psychological distance from participation, the collective and persuasion benefits of OPDF participation should be made evident to them. To urge lurkers toward participation, requests to participate can also be sent to mobilize them. As a longer-term measure, there is a need to inculcate the civic skills of citizens to facilitate their participation in OPDFs. One way to achieve this is through providing resources to support the formation of civic voluntary organizations, e.g., study circles (Rubenson 2000). These organizations could serve as avenues for citizens to develop their organizational and communication abilities needed for OPDF participation.

However, although converting lurkers to contributors can serve to replenish the loss of contributors in the forum (Ridings and Wasko 2010) and increase the reach of the forum, managers may want to be cautious in doing so.<sup>10</sup> This is because lurkers may need time to observe and learn in the forum before they can make useful contributions. Trying to convert lurkers too aggressively may lead to lower quality inputs from the lurkers-just-turned-contributors. Furthermore, it is important for OPDF managers to note the salience of communality in enhancing contributors’ and lurkers’ perceptions of participation benefits (specifically collective and persuasion benefits). To enhance communality perceptions, OPDF managers may choose an IT system for the forum that can comprehensively archive and organize information (e.g., policy information and previous discussion history) in an easily accessible manner. An overview of the information in the forum can be provided to users with appropriate menus and displays. Various visualization techniques can be tried out to examine which can provide participants with a clear idea of how the policy discussion has evolved over time. Also, OPDF managers may take note that communality can be enhanced by improving the connectivity of the forum system.

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Work

The limitations of the study need to be recognized when interpreting its findings. First, our study was conducted in a nationwide OPDF. As institutional contexts vary across nations (Rubenson 2000), this may limit the generalizability of our findings. However, with the inclusion of political efficacy in our model that is closely linked to the institutional context (Verba et al. 1995), this limitation may be less critical. Nonetheless, future research may investigate OPDFs in other countries with differing institutional contexts to further validate our results.

Second, although our model offers considerable explanatory power, future research may attempt to further improve the R<sup>2</sup> by considering additional antecedents. Among other motives, enjoyment may influence individuals’ participation in online feedback forums if they perceive participation to include hedonic elements (e.g., Marett and Joshi 2009, Nov et al. 2009). Other motivators, such as status, image (e.g., Marett and Joshi 2009), or issue relevance may also be considered in future research. Individuals’ value systems (Koh et al. 2007) may also play a part in view of the participation antecedents found in this study. For instance, individuals who are more collectivistic, i.e., emphasize group over individual goals, may find collective benefits to be particularly salient, and this may even apply to contributors if they have a strong collectivist orientation. Additionally, future studies may consider incorporating factors beyond the individual level, e.g., community social capital (Putnam 1993), in building a multilevel model.

Third, future research could improve the measures of constructs such as civic skills (Brady et al. 1995, Verba et al. 1995) to make them more specific and directly applicable to the online interaction context. The current measures may be relatively indirect, i.e., they tap on the fundamental organizational and communication skills that may facilitate one’s online interaction. For instance, one who often engages in planning efforts in the offline context may be better able to organize online interactions with others around issues of common interest.

Fourth, future research may examine specific design aspects of online forum systems that can enhance the perceptions of connectivity and communality. For instance, profile pages for members to present comprehensive information about themselves (Ma and Agarwal 2007), such as policy areas of interest, may help promote connectivity. On the other hand, structural layouts of forum pages that can provide participants with a clear overview of the information available may help improve communality. Future work can investigate the role of these features and their effectiveness in contributing toward connectivity and communality perceptions.

Fifth, the cross-sectional design of this study may not allow for possible bidirectional effects to be captured. For instance, contributors’ political efficacy and persuasion benefit beliefs may change with their increased participation in the forum over time. Also, individuals may lurk because they are still in the initial stages of visiting the online forum. Future research could employ a longitudinal panel design to capture the dynamics of changes in lurkers’ antecedents as well as bidirectional effects for contributors. Nonetheless, our study unveils the different participation antecedents for current contributors versus lurkers. As per CLT, although a lurker may foresee their “future self” as a contributor, their “current self” is still a lurker and requires a different perspective as a hypothetical contributor (Pronin et al. 2008).

Last, this study focuses on one specific kind of IT platform for online public participation, i.e., OPDFs, which afford both high connectivity and communality. With the emergence of various IT platforms, such as online chat rooms and microblogs, future research may investigate how their characteristics can facilitate online participation. For instance, although microblogging platforms such as Twitter may not be useful for gathering participants to collectively deliberate on a policy, they enable more spontaneous interactions that could increase participant involvement within a short time (e.g., a quick vote for different policy choices). Considering their different strengths and weaknesses, a combined use of different IT platforms for encouraging citizen participation could be fruitful.

With the increasing prevalence of OPDFs and other feedback forums in general, understanding how to promote participation in such forums that can improve organizational decision making is imperative for government agencies, firms, and societies as a whole. Our model, which is theoretically driven and empirically supported and considers contributors and lurkers as well as the role of IT, could provide a more holistic understanding of this important issue.
