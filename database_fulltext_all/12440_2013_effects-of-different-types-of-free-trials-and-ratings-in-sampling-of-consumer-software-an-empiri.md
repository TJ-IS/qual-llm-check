# Effects of Different Types of Free Trials and Ratings in Sampling of Consumer Software: An Empirical Study

Yo un g-Jin Le and Yon g Tan

Young-Jin Lee is an assistant professor in business information and analytics at the Daniels College of Business, University of Denver. He received his B.S. in business administration from the Ohio State University, M.S. in information systems management from Carnegie Mellon University, and Ph.D. in information systems from the University of Washington. His research interests include the economic and marketing aspects of online social media, mobile IT markets, and adoption and diffusion of IT innovation. His work has appeared or is forthcoming in academic journals and conference proceedings, including Journal of Management Information Systems, International Journal of Strategic Management, and the International Conference of Information Systems.

Yong Tan is the Neal and Jan Dempsey Professor of Information Systems at the Michael G. Foster School of Business, University of Washington. His research interests include electronic and social commerce, economics of information systems, social networks, and software engineering. His research has been published in Management Science, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Operations Research, INFORMS Journal on Computing, IEEE/ACM Transactions on Networking, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, IEEE Transactions on Knowledge and Data Engineering, and other journals. He served as an associate editor of Information Systems Research and is an associate editor of Management Science.

Abst ract : Giving away trial software is a common practice for software developers to maximize the exposure of their products to potential consumers and to minimize the consumers’ uncertainty about software quality. There are two types of free trials: (1) freeware, which consists of very basic features of focal software without a time lock, and (2) trialware, which has the full functionality of focal software with a time lock. In this paper, we study what factors make some free-trial software attract more potential adopters than others. Our empirical model under the traditional Bass-type diffusion examines the effects of the different types of free-trial software and ratings on consumer software sampling and reveals the dynamics of sampling over time. Using free-trial software downloading data on Download.com, we observe that the consumer software sampling process can be described by the theory of information diffusion. We find that user ratings affect sampling performance positively and that third-party ratings need to be positive to be effective. Finally, our results do not show any discernible differences between freeware and trialware with regard to their impact on sampling performance. This study contributes to the understanding of software free-trial practice from the perspective of consumer sampling growth of different types of free trials. Our findings can help design free-trial strategies to extrapolate the extent of consumer awareness of focal software and effectively convey its quality information to potential customers.

Key words and phrases: freeware, Hausman–Taylor estimation, information diffusion, online user and third-party ratings, software commercialization, software free trials, software sampling, trialware.

Product sampl ing has been described as an excel ent way t o buil d a consumer franchise quickly because a consumer’s perceived credibility of other information sources such as word of mouth, independent product reviews, direct salesperson, and traditional advertisements for competing brands is usually lower than that of direct experience [44]. Despite the positive aspects of product sampling, it is an expensive strategy for durable goods producers to pursue. Also, one-time product sampling typically does not have the reinforcing effects of multiple advertising exposures [37]. In contrast, software developers can implement the sampling strategy effortlessly by distributing free-trial copies of their focal software over the Internet. It is also easy for consumers to download and try free-trial software more than once. Hence, offering free trials is a common marketing strategy for new entrant software in the market. Furthermore, this practice has recently evolved into the “freemium” business model in mobile application markets that monetizes free mobile applications through in-advertising and in-app purchases.

Another reason for the popularity of consumer sampling in the software market is attributed to the different nature of software adoption from that of durable goods. From the perspective of the diffusion of innovation, the generic attributes of innovation that influence the rate of consumer goods adoption are relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability [39]. However, technology adoption requires individual users to consider not only their initial decision to adopt but also later stage actions such as implementation, adaptation, and routinization— the so-called adoptability [24]. Hence, because of the risks in the later stages of technology adoption, software trialability is especially important to potential users to evaluate their adoptability without undue effort and expense. By giving away a trial period or limited functionality of commercial software to potential consumers, software developers can promote their software and provide the adopters positive network benefit, which is one of the sources of increasing returns to adoption. As such, it is critical to investigate what factors make some free-trial software copies perform better than others on software free-trial sites because the performance of a free-trial software copy may directly influence the rate of actual adoption of its commercial counterparts. Despite the importance and common practice of consumer software sampling, previous studies have not directly analyzed this question but, rather, have focused on the quality differentiation between a free trial and its commercial software [13, 14, 31].

Software developers’ strategic reasons for offering free-trial software include reducing potential customers’ uncertainty about the quality and features of their commercial software [14]. Publicly available quality information such as user and expert reviews can directly affect the consumer sampling process because the quality signal is an important and influential attribute in the technology adoption process. Positive reviews from users or credible third parties is regarded as an effective way to achieve this goal because such positive reviews give potential users a more objective measure of software quality. Integrating free trials with reviews from users or third parties may become an effective way to reduce consumers’ uncertainty about the quality of the software in a consumer sampling or adoption process, and consequently enhances their propensity to purchase. In fact, recent studies have examined the effects of online user reviews [15, 17, 34] and third-party reviews [12, 16, 41] on product adoption and success and the strategic responses of manufacturing firms to the reviews. The situation may be different, and hence more interesting to investigate, when consumers form their beliefs on quality and generate corresponding ratings and reviews based on free-trial versions.

The objective of the current research is to empirically examine how consumers respond to different types of free-trial software and product ratings in terms of product sampling. In such an effort, we can better understand how consumer software sampling evolves over time, given the quality and licensing control in free-trial software and the expressed opinions by users and a third party. Specifically, we consider the following research questions using an explanatory empirical framework based on the data from an Internet download Web site, Download.com:<sup>1</sup>

RQ1: Can we describe consumers’ online software sampling process as a general adoption process in the theories of information diffusion (i.e., any aggregate level of social influence)?

RQ2: Do online product reviews from anonymous users and a third party still influence consumer software sampling response even though consumers can evaluate all the available products directly?

RQ3: Which type of free-trial software, freeware (limited functionality without a time lock) or trialware (full functionality for a limited time), is better to improve the consumer sampling responses?

It has been difficult to understand the strategic components of product sampling because of the lack of available data. A unique aspect of our research that differentiates it from previous studies is that we model the variation in consumer sampling with a large set of software products over time. We use a data set obtained from a popular Internet downloading site that contains longitudinal downloading data, which enables us to examine the changes in consumer software sampling from period to period. We implement econometric techniques to address potential endogeneity problems in our model, which may bias our findings.

Across free-trial software copies, our results show that consumer software sampling evolves under the social influence between users who have sampled because of external influences and potential trial users who can be influenced by previous users (Bass-type information diffusion [6]). Importantly, to the best of our knowledge, our finding provides the first evidence that consumer sampling, like adoption, of digital goods can be explained by a traditional type of diffusion process. Previous studies found comparable results in repeated purchases or trials of durable goods only theoretically [25, 31]. In particular, this is a very useful ancillary finding that can help software developers to extrapolate the level of awareness of their products in the market. We also show that third-party ratings need to be greater than or equal to four stars on a five-star scale to be effective in consumer software sampling, but user ratings are in general positively related to sampling performance. In other words, consumers still refer to a free trial’s quality information created by peer users or a third party even when the consumers sample free trials. In addition, our analysis reveals that there is little difference between freeware and trialware with respect to consumers’ sampling preference. To check whether this finding is robust, we run analyses within each software category and observe that some software categories could be more suitable for distributing freeware rather than trialware. Overall, the current study sheds light on the understanding of the popular free-trial practice of information goods such as software, e-books, music, movies, and mobile applications, which has not been studied extensively in academic research.

In the remainder of the paper, we first review the related literature. Then we discuss the uniqueness of consumer software sampling and develop our hypotheses. This is followed by the formulation of estimation model and the description of our data set. After we outline the estimation procedure, the estimation results are presented, and research implications are discussed. Finally, we offer some directions for future research.

## Literature Review

Our research is rel at ed t o new product growt h model s, online word of mouth, and marketing issues in the context of software applications, which falls into the broader category of e-commerce. The articles in these fields have considered various models and explained how the metrics of their interest affect consumer product adoption and in turn product performance. However, few researchers have focused on the effects of such metrics on consumer product sampling.

## Adoption and Diffusion

Theories of the diffusion of innovation have allowed researchers to investigate how new innovations spread among groups of people. Diffusion research seeks to understand the spread of innovations by modeling their entire life cycle from the perspective of communications and consumer interactions. Traditionally, the main thread of diffusion models has been based on the framework developed by Bass [6]. The Bass model considers the aggregate first-purchase growth of a category of a durable good introduced into a market with potential m. The social network into which it diffuses is assumed to be fully connected and homogeneous. At each point in time, new adopters join the market as a result of two types of influences: external influences (p), such as advertising and other communications initiated by the firm, and internal market influences (q), which result from interactions (e.g., word of mouth) among adopters and potential adopters in the social system [38]. The diffusion modeling literature has attempted to extend the Bass framework to reflect the increasing complexity of new product growth. Recent contributions to the diffusion modeling literature have reexamined the interpretation of the internal parameter (q) to identify and discuss other types of social interactions, such as network externalities [8, 40, 46], which present increasing consumer utility as more consumers adopt the new product, and social signals [48, 49], which relate to the social information that individuals infer from adoption of an innovation by others (for a complete review of this field, see [38]). However, to the best of our knowledge, there has been little research on the diffusion of consumer product sampling, which is quite different from the consumer product adoption process.

## Effects of Online Word of Mouth

Our paper is related to a growing body of research that examines the effects of online word of mouth on product performance measures (e.g., sales, rank, and growth). The articles in this field have considered various forms of online word of mouth, including user-created ratings and reviews with respect to valence, variance, and volume [3, 19, 20, 35], and have found varying empirical results. Two articles [21, 36] examine the impact of posted user reviews on movie box office sales. Although the effects of valence and volume were modeled in both cases, only the volume of online word of mouth was significant. By examining the patterns of sales growth, Dellarocas et al. [21] found that both valence and volume of online word of mouth have significant effects on movie box office sales. A study by Clemons et al. [17] focused attention on the craft beer category, finding that the valence and the variance of ratings affect sales growth. Lee et al. [33] found that social biases can exist in the online user rating generation process and that socially biased online user ratings can positively affect movie box office sales. Overall, the existing research has identified several important rating metrics that can influence sales.

A growing number of commercial Web sites (e.g., CNET, ZDNET, PCWorld) offer online third-party product reviews. Through those reviews, consumers can easily obtain software information as well as expert reviews of the software of interest. Especially in online commercial environments, obtaining high ratings from third parties has become a critical marketing consideration for firms [2]. Clemons [16] showed empirically that referring to an expert rating from a third party is one way for consumers to reduce the risk associated with product quality if the credibility of the third party is high. Chen and Xie [12] showed that a new product using advertisements containing third-party award logos can hurt a winning product in the market. However, although there is ample research on the effects of such ratings on sales, the question of whether there are similar effects of user and third-party ratings on consumer software sampling has not been answered.

## Product Sampling

Because free-trial copies of commercial software are mostly used for marketing purposes, our research is related to product sampling in the marketing literature. Although understanding the prepurchase advertising effects for new brands is very important and well documented, these conditions can be short-lived in the marketplace. Indeed, most real advertising for brands is not only from advertising but also from direct product experience [44]. Shoemaker and Shoaf [43] argued that product sampling is one of the most effective ways to alleviate a consumer’s risk in a series of stages of the consumer adoption process. With a cross-sectional analysis using pharmaceutical products in a repeated purchase model, Hahn et al. [25] showed that the effectiveness of firms’ communication activities to promote consumer trial experiences is related mainly to product quality attributes and market growth, whereas that of word of mouth is associated with product class characteristics and market competitiveness. Kelly [32] showed that a consumer’s trial experience can be a plausible external cause for the consumer’s product evaluations, and his or her product trial experience can discount the causal role of internal prejudice or preoccupation. Jamieson and Bass [30] developed a trial purchase prediction model and found that as affordability increases, consumers’ propensity to buy a product as an initial trial is increased for a repeat purchases product. Therefore, free trials may eliminate any restriction of an initial trial purchase because of affordability. Another dimension of advantages of free trials is that it is conducive to developing consumer learning and loyalty. Bain [4] showed that with free trials consumers can effectively gain information regarding how well a product fits their needs. In a future period, which that may be the purchasing stage, there is an informational advantage that a consumer may prefer a product that the consumer knows more about because of the initial trial experience. The impact between trial and advertising is different because of the risk-reducing feature of trial strategy. In Smith and Swinyard’s [45] experimental study, they found that attitude development is significantly stronger for trial subjects in terms of both affective extremity and confidence. In addition, trial subjects display higher levels of consistency than do advertising subjects. In general, in order to reach the same number of people, free-trial strategy is less expensive for sellers as compared to advertising. Our work contributes to the studies on product sampling in the marketing literature by examining different and implementable free-trial strategies for software developers.

In t his sect ion, we discuss t he uniqueness of consumer soft ware sampl ing in which a consumer can try one or more free copies of commercial software with reduced features or for a limited time, given publicly posted opinions about the free copies by peer users and a third party. In this research, we observe consumer free-trial downloads and product ratings in the data for two different types of free-trial software, and we model the growth of consumer software sampling as a Bass-type diffusion process, which is widely used to explain the sales growth of products and services. We do not explicitly model how consumer software sampling can affect future sales of the software, which is out of our research scope and a limitation of our research.

## Awareness and Diffusion

The growth of consumer software sampling can be explained with the theories of the information diffusion of a new product. The theories usually describe that the potential adopters of an innovation are influenced by pressure from the social system. In particular, the Bass-type diffusion models show that the potential adopters’ timing of adoption can be influenced by two groups, “innovators” and “imitators.” The first group of adopters is assumed to be influenced by mass media, whereas the second group represents the later adopters who are influenced by the previous adopters through social influence. Also, traditional models of technology adoption describe that a potential adopter generally considers five attributes (as shown in Figure 1; see also [39]) to make his or her adoption decision of an innovation. In contrast, consumer software sampling is quite different from consumer software adoption because consumers can download and try, in theory, all the available free-trial software multiple times. However, in practice, because of consumers’ adoptability of a product [24], several costs such as learning, maintenance, and time are bound to occur even after a consumer downloads a free copy of software. Because of such costs, the growth of consumer software sampling may exhibit similar processes of the information diffusion of adoption.

Information diffusion theories may explain one of the main mechanisms for the consumer software sampling process, which allows an application of the Bass-type diffusion. However, empirical evidence of information diffusion in the sampling process has not been found in prior studies. Using a data set similar to ours, Duan et al. [22] considered other alternative mechanisms than information diffusion, including informational cascading [5, 7] and word of mouth, and showed the existence of the informational cascading effect in consumer software adoption. We aim to examine the salience of the information diffusion effect in consumer software sampling. Thus, we follow Bass [6] to formulate the probability of an initial download for free trial i made at time t, given that no download has yet been made, as $P _ { _ { i , t } } = p + ( q / m ) y _ { _ { i , t - 1 } } ,$ where p and q/m are constants and $y _ { i , t - 1 }$ is the number of previous downloads for freetrial software i; p, the coefficient of innovation, is the probability of initial sampling at time $t = 0 ;$ m is the total number of potential downloads at the end of product life cycle; q is the coefficient of imitation; and $( q / m ) y _ { i , t - 1 }$ reflects the pressure operated on imitators as the number of previous free-trial downloading increases. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: The adoption and the diffusion of free-trial copies of software do not significantly differ from the adoption and the diffusion of other goods described by the Bass diffusion model.

## Attractiveness

Before any sampling experience, it is not clear whether a consumer may construct a presampling evaluation based on several software quality inputs. As mentioned earlier, because the choice of free trials is not a consumer purchase at a certain price, the rate of consumer software sampling may not be related to software quality information, which is one of the most important attributes in consumer product adoption (see Figure 1). In other words, a consumer can download and try more copies, when they are free, to evaluate without considering too much publicly available quality information. However, because of the costs associated with the actual utility of free-trial software, such as learning, maintenance, and time, a consumer’s software sampling decision can be greatly affected by his or her expected quality derived from the opinions expressed by others. To examine the previously mentioned contradicting arguments, we first assume that a consumer who intends to sample a free copy of software weighs the costs in his or her expected utility from the free copy. Therefore, instead of sampling multiple times, the consumer would rely on product reviews for quality information.

As Duan et al. [22] noted, there are mixed findings in the literature on how online user reviews influence consumer adoption decisions. The positive effect of user reviews on book sales for online retailers such as Amazon.com or Barnes & Noble is empirically verified [15]. However, a study by Chen et al. [11] reported no sales impact of user ratings on other types of products using Amazon.com’s data. Duan et al. [22] found contrasting evidence of the impact of user ratings on software adoption using a data source similar to ours. Their results suggest that user ratings have no affect on online users’ choice of popular products but have an increasingly positive effect on the adoption of less-popular products. We propose a similar hypothesis to test the overall impact of user ratings on consumer software sampling when we control for the effects of information diffusion:

Hypothesis 2: Online user ratings are positively related to consumer software sampling.

We are particularly interested in the extent to which different types of online reviews can provide consumers with information about the superiority of free-trial software. Before users download to try software, some attributes such as complexity and compatibility in their adoption process are under consideration even in their choice of software sampling, and then a credible third-party recommendation with a significant degree of technical expertise can transfer the quality information more effectively than user ratings because there could be a large variation of user ratings as well as their anonymity [3, 16]. In practice, third parties usually select software to review based on their own policy resulting from to a large set of available software products, and only selected software products for review therefore can receive a third-party review.<sup>2</sup> Hence, a third-party rating may not be very influential if a product is selected for review after it has already gained significant popularity among users or if the rating is created based on the relationship (e.g., sponsorship or partnership) between the developer of the software and the third party. Therefore, it is worth examining the effect of thirdparty reviews on the consumer software sampling process because the greater impact of the ratings may bring more users and advertisers (developers) to the third-party sites [12]. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3: Third-party reviews are positively related to consumer software sampling.

## Trialability

The possibility of consumer prepurchase evaluations with actual software is an important attribute of software adoption (as shown in Figure 1). Unlike durable goods sampling, software developers can differentiate their free-trial software from the focal software with respect to quality or license period. First, the developers can give away their software as a form of freeware,<sup>3</sup> which usually consists of reduced features or functionalities of the focal software without a time lock. Second, a trialware<sup>4</sup> is a free-trial version that incorporates all the same functionality of the focal software but with a time lock. Because both types of free-trial software are priced at zero, they act as product samples but provide an unlimited supply of free-trial software, unlike durable goods.

The consumer may regard freeware and trialware as “free stuff” or perfectly substitutable software to the commercial counterparts. In any event, there is a huge difference between cheap and free. Charging even \$0.01 for a product places a provider in an entirely different business. This has to do with the powerful psychology of “free,” which means that zero is one market and any other price is another [1]. When people are faced with a choice between two products, one of which is free, they overreact to the free product as if zero price means not only a low cost of buying the product but also an increased valuation [42]. However, once a consumer observes the differences in quality and license period, not in price, the consumer’s perception of the value of the “free stuff” can be influence, and this can affect his or her preference of ownership. If a consumer values the perpetual license of the basic software more than the temporary license of its advanced version, the consumer is more likely to download the freeware version than the trial version, and the purpose of trial for evaluation can be debased. Hence, a freeware with minimum quality differentiation from its commercial counterparts may have a cannibalization effect, unless the freeware has a freemium model, which relies on other sources such as in-software advertising or item purchase. A trialware version can make more downloads if more potential adopters come for solely evaluation or one-time-use purpose. Hence, it is important to examine which type of free-trial software performs better in the growth of consumer software sampling. For the convenience of testing, we set up a hypothesis that consumers would choose a free-trial option with a higher cost-benefit difference by weighing more the option’s usage period. Therefore, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 4: Freeware is positively related to consumer software sampling due to the perpetual period of usage, whereas trialware is negatively related to consumer software sampling.

The software market is fragmented and has many categories based on common function, type, or field of use. The consumers’ needs for free-trial software may differ by categories, and there is heterogeneity across different software categories. Thus, we further analyze whether the previously mentioned results can persist within each category.

## Data

Download.com is a virtual marketplace where consumers can experience as many products as they want with related product information provided by competing developers, users, and CNET. The Web site lists most of the active software products in the market. Hence, the data from the Web site are ideal for exploring our hypotheses because Download.com is well representative and reflective as a subset of the entire software marketplace. We collected product characteristics, numbers of downloads, and user and expert reviews for the listed software products on the Web site.

At the time of data collection, there were 22 software categories for Windows downloads in Download.com. To ensure that our data cover a wide range of software products, we chose the seven most popular software categories out of all the categories in terms of the number of actively listed software in each category. During the ten weeks of our data collection period, October 20–December 22, 2007, we extracted the following information on listed software products in the seven categories: software names, dates added in Download.com, weekly numbers of downloads, total numbers of listing products in its category, CNET ratings, average user ratings, file sizes, and types of free trial. After removing irrelevant products, our data set has 1,653 products that provide a complete time series of observations during the ten-week period. Table 1 gives the descriptive statistics for the pooled data and Table 2 shows the differences across categories. The number of software listed in each category varies from 153 to 529. Such a variation reflects the idiosyncratic environment in a specific software category, which can be defined as a single market [22]. Hence, further analysis of the category level would be useful.

## Dependent Variable

Download.com keeps track of the total number of downloads of any freeware or trialware from the time the software is listed on the site. Whenever a user actually downloads the software from Download.com, the cumulative number of downloads of the software is increased by one to reflect the user’s sampling experience. Although a freeware has no limit on the license period with limited functionality, downloading a freeware induces sampling for its higher-quality focal software. A trialware is exactly the same copy of the focal software but with a time lock. Therefore, the total number of downloads is a reliable indicator for consumer sampling experiences for the focal software. If the version of the software changes, the older one is dropped from the site and the downloading count restarts. Therefore, our dependent variable for Equation (1), $S _ { i , t ^ { \prime } }$ is the instantaneous number of downloads of freeware or trialware i at week t.

## Independent Variables

The developer’s choice of providing freeware or trialware is one of the most interesting variables. We set a sampling strategy variable $T R _ { i }$ to 1 if the listed software i is a trialware and 0 if the software is a freeware. This variable also includes the difference in price information between these two types of free trial. A freeware does not list the price of its focal software for users to induce quality, whereas a trialware is identical to its focal software with available price information. Nevertheless, we do not include the price variable in the set of independent variables because of its high correlation with $T R _ { i }$ . In addition, the effect of price is eliminated when we examine our hypotheses with the FE model because price is a time-invariant variable. A developer’s strategic decision to position a commercial software product may link to the type of their sampling strategy. For example, a developer may choose the level of quality differentiation between the commercial software and its freeware version. However, the developer’s decision on the licensing period of its trialware version is critical when they pursue giving away the commercial software product for free. From a consumer software sampling perspective, a downloader’s choice of a free-trial version is closely related to the characteristics of the software’s commercial version. As a consequence, a developer’s choice of the type of free trial is closely related to the characteristics of the commercial software product, and the aspects of the product can influence a user’s free-trial choice among competitive software products in a software category. Therefore, we assume that $T R _ { _ i }$ is endogenous with respect to software product heterogeneity, the fixed effects $( a _ { i } )$ in the model.

The problem of self-selection bias is minimized in our data set although users who choose to download have already decided to try or use a software product. First, a developer can list freeware, trialware, or both for their focal software. Thus, if both types of the software exist on Download.com,<sup>5</sup> a user’s choice of the types can be random. In addition, if only one type of free trial of the software exists, it is still possible that users may try the other type from other products because there are many comparable products in each software category. Second, a user can download multiple different free-trial copies of a certain kind of software of his or her interest. After the user has used one free copy for the purpose of software trial to evaluate, one-time use, or free adoption, he or she can download another substitutable one for the same reasons. Each time the user downloads a free-trial copy, the $\mathrm { c o p y } ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ surrounding information such as user or third-party ratings and the types of the free trials can influence his or her choice. Hence, the availability of both types of free trial for a focal software and the possibility of a user’s multiple choices of free trials can avoid the selection bias in our analysis.

The zero marginal production cost and an unlimited supply in the software industry allow software developers to offer an unlimited quantity of a free-trial product. Thus, the product availability for sampling is not an issue here. However, the quality information to potential users may be an issue because price does not truly represent the relative quality of free-trial software. Download.com provides CNET’s laboratory rating on the listed software based on their technical testing. This rating reflects a professional assessment of software quality [10]. In our data set, this expert rating observation is one of two, three, four, or five stars. Eighty-eight percent of the listed free-trial software in our data set do not have CNET’s rating. Hence, we make five different dummies for CNET rating. NR indicates whether the free-trial software i is rated $( N R _ { i } = 0 )$ or not $( N R _ { i } = 1 )$ by CNET. The dummies $C R _ { { _ 2 i } } , C R _ { { _ 3 i } } , C R _ { { _ 4 i } } ,$ and $\mathrm { C R } _ { \mathfrak { s } _ { i } }$ are correspond to each rating value. CNET reviews only selected software products that enter their top 100 most popular ranking or new products selected based on CNET’s judgment. Therefore, CNET ratings can be exogenously given to selected software products based on CNET’s selection criteria. However, $N R _ { _ i }$ could also be correlated with software heterogeneity $a _ { _ i }$ in the model as the aspects of the commercial software product can still affect CNET’s selection for review and consumer’s free-trial choice.

The average user ratings of the free trials are posted on the Web site. Hence, the average user rating in a previous week, $U R _ { i , t - 1 } ,$ is used as a proxy for quality information as well as the CNET rating. We assume that $U R _ { i , t - 1 }$ may also be correlated with the heterogeneity $a _ { _ i }$ as the endogenous assumption of the CNET rating. $U R _ { i , t - 1 }$ varies over time, whereas the CNET rating is scored only once.

Our model should control for different competitive structures in different software categories. $C P _ { i , t - 1 }$ is the total number of actively listed free-trial copies within each category one week before current week $t ,$ and its quadratic term is another independent variable to capture the nonlinear effect of competition. In addition, we add categorical dummies $( C _ { j , i } = 1$ if software i is in category j) when we estimate Equation (1) with the pooled data. The category dummies control for different characteristics across categories. We assume that the category dummies are not associated with the softwarespecific unobservable factors, $a _ { _ i \colon }$ , because software categories are defined exogenously based on software functionalities in Download.com.

According to software engineering theories, the size of software is a proxy for its functionality [47]. Users may be more attracted to software with a larger file size because it may yield greater value than software with a smaller file size. However, the software with more functionality may contain more uncertainties about the quality of the software; therefore, it is helpful to try it out before the final purchase. Hence, we control for file size, log(FS ). We assume that $\log ( F S _ { i } )$ is not correlated with the heterogeneity $a _ { _ i }$ because a developer can upload an installer file instead of a complete package of the listed free-trial software for user downloading. For example, an installer file of a free-trial copy is listed on Download.com, and it is downloaded first when a user chooses to download. Then, the installer connects the developer’s server to download additional files to complete the installation (e.g., the listed file size of the installer of Acrobat Reader X is 957 kilobytes, but the file size of the complete package is 51.95 megabytes).

As new product diffusion theories suggest, the information diffusion of a new product and its awareness rates moderate or go down after its peak point of the process time [6, 25]. Jain et al. [29] demonstrated that a product becomes familiar to the public over time and the need for sampling decreases. Hence, taking into account an entire product life cycle is necessary to examine the information diffusion in the model. Fortunately, we can identify when each free-trial product has been added and listed on Download. com although our data collection period is relatively short. We construct the variable Age to describe how long a free trial i has been listed on Download.com in the current observation week t to control for a product life cycle and the different stage of the diffusion process in the model. Nevertheless, our ten-week data collection period is one of the limitations of our study because a product’s peak in the diffusion process can be reached well after ten weeks from its listing. Also, the $A g e$ variable may not capture completely an entire life cycle because the shapes of diffusion for different software products may be quite different from each other. We assume that $\log ( A g e _ { i , t } )$ is uncorrelated with $a _ { \scriptscriptstyle i } ,$ which can affect consumer software sampling because the change of a free trial’s listing duration is related to the versioning of its commercial software rather than the developer’s strategies for product sampling.

## Estimation

Our est imat ion procedure fol ows t hat of Boulding and Christen [9] so as to obtain the consistent estimates of all the variables of interest in our model. We consider that a developer’s choice of listing freeware or trialware for their focal software product on Download.com and having a third-party rating for the product are potentially endogenous because of the correlation with the unobservable firm- or softwarespecific strategic factors such as marketing efficiency, resources, or positioning. This potential bias has centered on the unobserved fixed errors, $a _ { _ i } .$ This may be the most relevant assumption because the sampling strategies are time invariant once free trials of software are listed on the site, and thus can be correlated with only the omitted time-invariant factors.

Including all of the omitted variables in our model is impossible, and using only cross-sectional data with the instrumental variables cannot give the consistent estimates of the parameters of interest unless the instruments are uncorrelated with unobserved components. With panel data, the FE estimation procedure gives only the consistent estimates of the parameters on the time-varying variables after addressing potential endogeneity in the contemporaneous shock and the serial correlation. However, the estimation cannot identify the effects of the time-invariant variables, such as the type of free trials and the third-party rating in our model, because all the time-invariant components in the model are removed in the FE estimation procedure, along with the fixed effects $a _ { i ^ { \ast } }$ The RE estimation procedure can estimate the parameters of all the time-variant and time-invariant variables in the model under the assumption that all the independent variables are independent of the fixed effects. Hence, we test for the assumption to decide whether the RE estimation is appropriate for our model. If the Hausman [26] specification test rejects the assumption that all the independent variables are exogenous, the HTIV estimation procedure [27] can provide a surmountable and alternative way to correctly identify the parameters of interest using a valid subset of instrumental variables in all the time-varying independent variables in the model (see Figure 2). Therefore, a subset of the time-varying independent variables in our model should be independent of the fixed effects if HTIV estimation becomes necessary.

There are three appropriate instrumental variables in the set of our time-varying independent variables in the model: log $( A g e _ { i , t } ) , C P _ { i , t - 1 } ,$ and $C P _ { i , t - 1 } ^ { 2 }$ . The listing duration, $\log ( A g e _ { i , t } )$ , of the free-trial software can be a good instrumental variable for the type of free trials because of our focus on software sampling. Freeware and trialware are not actual software on sale, and the availabilities of free trials on Download.com may not be related to the unobserved fixed factors other than the presence of the current versions of their commercial software. In other words, when developers upgrade their commercial software, new versions of free trials for the commercial software will replace the older ones and reset all the clocks and their counts in Download.com. Therefore, the listing duration of free trials in Download.com is exogenous in the model and is more likely associated with the versioning of their commercial software. From a developer’s perspective, the decision on how long their freeware or trialware is listed on Download. com can be closely related to their decisions on the commercial counterparts because the main purpose of providing free trials is to promote consumers’ trial experiences for the commercial software. In addition, upgrading a commercial software product requires the developer to relist its trialware version in Download.com, which restarts the listing duration. Therefore, a developer’s free-trial choice between freeware and trialware can relate to the versioning of their commercial software, which affects the free trial’s listing duration. In contrast, when a consumer chooses a free copy to use or simply try, the consumer does not have to necessarily consider any other factors related to the free trial’s listing duration. Also, a free trial’s longer listing duration is less likely to increase potential users’ software awareness and propensity to download the software because the listing (availability) duration is not usually considered a channel to increase product awareness [23]. Hence, this implies that log $( A g e _ { i , t } )$ is correlated with replacing the freeware or trialware $( T R _ { _ i } )$ of the commercial software in Download.com but less likely correlated with software-specific unobservables affecting consumer software awareness and propensity to download the software.

As a competitive structure of each category, $C P _ { i , t - 1 }$ is another good instrumental variable. Given a certain level of competition among developers in a software category, the developers can strategically choose one type of free trial versus the other to attract more potential consumers. For instance, developers may tend to list a freeware version of their commercial software to increase product awareness and base users when the competition level is high in its category. In contrast, the needs of providing trialware become greater if there are only a few competitive products in a category. For instance, the operating system category usually has several products and developers, and the developers commonly provide trial versions or beta versions for free. Hence, the developers’ choice of listing freeware or trialware can be closely related to the competitive structure of a software category. In addition, the effort to gain a high rating from a third party can also vary across different competition levels of categories. However, the competition environment of a certain category is more likely formed exogenously with respect to consumer software sampling.

In addition, the relevance of the instrumental variables can be directly assessed based on the regression of $T R _ { _ i }$ on all the exogenous variables. The result shows that our instrumental variables are significant based on the F-statistic of a joint test $( F ( 3 , 1 6 5 2 1 ) = 1 3 1 . 0 5 )$ . Also, when we estimate the first-order autocorrelation coefficient, ρ, in the following subsection, a specification test reveals that these potential instrumental variables are generally uncorrelated with the fixed effects, $a _ { i } ,$ statistically.

However, our adjusted Lagrangian multiplier test (Lagrangian multiplier statistic = 4,130.62) indicates that we should remove the serial correlation in the model before using FE, RE, or HTIV estimations.

The autocorrelation effect $\boldsymbol { \mathsf { \Pi } } _ { \mathsf { I } _ { i , t - 1 } }$ is removed, and only the contemporaneous shock ${ { \bf { \Theta } } _ { { i , t } } }$ and the fixed effects $a _ { \scriptscriptstyle i } ( 1 - \mathsf { \rho } )$ as errors are left as

$$
\begin{array}{l} S _ {i, t} = \rho S _ {i, t - 1} + \delta_ {1} y _ {i, t - 1} - \delta_ {1} \rho y _ {i, t - 2} + \delta_ {2} y _ {i, t - 1} ^ {2} - \delta_ {2} \rho y _ {i, t - 2} ^ {2} + \beta_ {1} \log \left(A g e _ {i, t}\right) \\ \quad - \beta_ {1} \rho \log \left(A g e _ {i, t - 1}\right) + \beta_ {2} C P _ {i, t - 1} - \beta_ {2} \rho C P _ {i, t - 2} + \beta_ {3} C P _ {i, t - 1} ^ {2} - \beta_ {3} \rho C P _ {i, t - 2} ^ {2} \\ \quad + \beta_ {4} U R _ {i, t - 1} - \beta_ {4} \rho U R _ {i, t - 2} + (1 - \rho) \gamma_ {1} T R _ {i} + (1 - \rho) \sum_ {r = 2} ^ {5} \gamma_ {r} C R _ {r, t} \\ + (1 - \rho) \gamma_ {6} \log (F S _ {i}) + (1 - \rho) \sum_ {j = 2} ^ {7} \kappa_ {j} C _ {j, i} + (1 - \rho) \delta_ {0} + (1 - \rho) a _ {i} + \omega_ {i, t}. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Now, except $\log ( A g e _ { i , t } )$ , all the other time-varying variables are uncorrelated with the contemporaneous shock ${ \boldsymbol { \Theta } } _ { i , t }$ as they are in a higher order than ${ \boldsymbol { \Theta } } _ { i , t }$ . The first-order autocorrelation coefficient $\rho$ can be estimated by the fixed effects instrumental variable estimation [28], with the one-lagged variable, $\log ( A g e _ { i , t - 1 } )$ , as an instrumental variable for $\log ( A g e _ { i , t } )$ . The estimated value of $\rho$ is 0.652, and it is significant at the 1 percent level. We use this estimate, $\hat { \rho } ,$ , in the ρ-differencing procedure to remove the serial correlation in the model. This yields a data set with the variables, for example, $\Delta S _ { i , t } = S _ { i , t } - \hat { \mathsf { p } } S _ { i , t - 1 } , \Delta y _ { i , t - 1 } = y _ { i , t - 1 } - \hat { \mathsf { p } } y _ { i , t - 2 } ,$ and $\Delta y _ { i , t - 1 } ^ { 2 } = y _ { i , t - 1 } ^ { 2 } - \hat { \mathsf { p } } y _ { i , t - 2 } ^ { 2 } ,$ which are corrected for the serial correlation. Therefore, the estimation model for the FE, RE, or HTIV estimations becomes

$$
\begin{array}{l} \Delta S _ {i, t} = \delta_ {1} \Delta y _ {i, t - 1} + \delta_ {2} \Delta y _ {i, t - 1} ^ {2} + \beta_ {1} \Delta \log \left(A g e _ {i, t}\right) + \beta_ {2} \Delta C P _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {3} \Delta C P _ {i, t - 1} ^ {2} \\ + \beta_ {4} \Delta U R _ {i, t - 1} + (1 - \rho) \gamma_ {1} T R _ {i} + (1 - \rho) \sum_ {r = 2} ^ {5} \gamma_ {r} C R _ {r, i} + (1 - \rho) \gamma_ {6} \log \left(F S _ {i}\right) \\ + (1 - \hat {\rho}) \sum_ {j = 2} ^ {7} \kappa_ {j} C _ {j, i} + (1 - \hat {\rho}) \delta_ {0} + (1 - \hat {\rho}) a _ {i} + \omega_ {i, t}. \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

## Obtaining Benchmark Estimated Coefficients with Fixed Effects Estimation

With the corrected data set after carrying out the above ρ-differencing procedure, we now proceed to the second part of our estimation procedure. We use the FE model for Equation (4) to obtain the benchmark estimated coefficients of the time-varying variables in Equation (4), namely, $\hat { \bf 8 } ^ { F E } \mathrm { \bf S }$ and $\hat { \beta } ^ { F E } \mathrm { \bf S }$ . The Hausman specification test is used to perform and produce a test statistic, $, { \hat { m } } .$ , to test whether the estimated coefficients of the time-varying variables of the RE model $( \hat { \bar { \boldsymbol { \delta } } } ^ { R E } , \hat { \boldsymbol { \beta } } ^ { R E } )$ or the HTIV model $( \hat { \ S } ^ { H T I V } , \hat { \mathsf { \ B } } ^ { H T I V } )$ for Equation (4) differ statistically and significantly from the benchmark estimates $( \hat { \bar { \boldsymbol { \delta } } } ^ { F E } , \hat { \bar { \boldsymbol { \beta } } } ^ { F E } )$ of the FE model [26].

Except $\log ( A g e _ { i , t } ^ { } ) , C P _ { i , t - 1 } , \log ( F S _ { i } )$ , and all the category dummies, we assume that the other variables may be correlated with the unobserved fixed effects, $a _ { i ^ { \ast } }$ To check this, we first fit Equation (4) using the RE model and then perform the Hausman specification test by comparing $( \hat { \ 8 } ^ { F E } , \hat { \mathsf { \ B } } ^ { F E } )$ with $( \hat { \mathbb { S } } ^ { R E } , \hat { \mathbb { \beta } } ^ { R E } )$ ). If the test result shows statistically that the two sets of estimates are different, we run the HTIV estimation for Equation (4) using the mean differences of all the time-varying variables $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \Delta y _ { { } _ { i , t - 1 } } - \Delta y _ { { } _ { i , t - 1 } }$ , where $\Delta y _ { i , t - 1 }$ is the mean of all $\Delta y _ { { } _ { i , t - 1 } } )$ as instrumental variables for the time-varying variables and the cross-sectional means of the three variables, $\overline { { \Delta \log ( A g e _ { i , t } ) } } , \overline { { \Delta C P _ { i , t - 1 } } } .$ , and $\overline { { \Delta C P _ { i , t - 1 } ^ { 2 } } } .$ which are exogenous in our model with respect to the unobserved fixed effects, as instrumental variables for the strategic variable $T R _ { i }$ [27].

We also checked the endogeneity of the dummy variable $N R _ { i }$ with respect to the fixed effects. We include only $N R _ { i }$ without the CNET rating category dummies, $C R _ { r , i } ,$ when running the HTIV estimation for Equation (4) with the same set of instrumental variables. Our test indicates that $N R _ { i }$ is uncorrelated with $a _ { i } . \mathrm { A l s o }$ , in practice, CNET ratings are given only to those software products that are selected for review based on CNET’s own review policy. Therefore, we consider $C R _ { r , i }$ as exogenous in Equation (4). Hence, $T R _ { _ i }$ is the only possible endogenous time-invariant variable in the model.

## Information Diffusion of Consumer Software Sampling

On the basis of the model solution and its empirical test in a typical type of Bass diffusion, the estimated parameter of $\ S _ { \nu }$ should be positive, whereas the estimated parameter of $\ S _ { \gamma }$ should be negative if the evolving process of consumer software sampling is similar to that of consumer adoption [6]. The respective estimates of $\ S _ { \nu }$ and $\ S _ { 2 }$ are $\hat { \delta } _ { \scriptscriptstyle 1 } = 0 . 0 7 9$ and $\hat { \tilde { 0 } } _ { { } _ { 2 } } = - 0 . 0 9 9 \times 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ in the FE results in Table 3, and they are significant at the 1 percent level. Hence, we can conclude that the growth of consumer software sampling can be similarly explained with the growth of consumer product adoption in terms of information diffusion. The information obtained here is particularly useful in understanding how the consumer software sampling process evolves in terms of the degree of consumer awareness of new software over time. For example, using the estimated coefficients of $\delta _ { \scriptscriptstyle 0 } , \delta _ { \scriptscriptstyle 1 }$ , and $\ S _ { _ 2 }$ of FE in Table 3 we can see that the consumer software sampling rate for a free trial is expected to reach its maximum at about the fortieth week of listing on Download.com based on the prediction of the timing of the sales peak described in Bass [6]. A period of 40 weeks seems similar to the typical life cycle of versioning of the commercial software. Therefore, this result draws the software developers’ special attention because monitoring the sampling process for their free trials is conducive to understanding the degree of potential consumers’ awareness of their focal software at a given time. Consequently, developers can effectively tailor their other promotional campaigns for the focal software according to the state of the process.

## Effects of Different Types of Ratings

The estimate of parameter $\beta _ { 4 }$ is shown in the FE column of Table 3: $\hat { \beta } _ { { } _ { 4 } } = 0 . 1 1 6$ and is significant at the 1 percent level. Therefore, this reveals that user ratings positively influence consumer software sampling, as described in H2. This evidence is consistent regardless of different model specifications in RE, HTIV(1), and HTIV(2) in Table 3. Therefore, the higher user ratings of free trials induce more users to download the free-trial copies, which is similar to the effect of user ratings on product adoption. This demonstrates that sampling users still consider quality signals from other users although they can obtain multiple copies of free-trial software. Similarly, the effect of the CNET rating, CR, becomes significant and positive, but only if the value of CR is 4 or greater. Users find a free trial with a very positive rating from CNET more attractive than other free trials with a less positive rating $( \hat { \gamma } _ { 4 } = 3 . 1 3 3$ and $\hat { \gamma } _ { 5 } = 2 8 . 6 3 8 )$ .

Hence, both types of rating are still influential references when potential users decide to download a free-trial copy of commercial software.

This brings forth useful implications to developers and third-party recommenders. First, developers should carefully consider the ratings of their free-trial software from users or third parties because those ratings directly link to quality information of their focal software. For other types of products such as durable goods, online ratings usually provide the quality information of a product itself (not from its free trial), which sells on the markets. However, on software markets, these types of online ratings from free trials can be too noticeable for consumers to correctly recognize the corresponding focal software. For example, although freeware does not have complete functionality as its focal software, the freeware’s online ratings may act as the quality information for the focal software on the market. However, to gain high ratings of free trials from users or third parties, developers cannot simply increase the quality of their free trials because, then, the free trials cannibalize potential sales of their focal software.

Second, the impact of a third-party rating is of great interest not only to developers but also to third parties whose business models are mainly advertising [12, 41]. The greater impact the rating has, the more users and advertisers (developers) participate in the third-party sites. To achieve this, third parties should maintain their credibility and integrity in product reviews, which in turn improves their sustainable reputation.

## Effects of Different Types of Free Trials

To understand the role of different types of free trials, we test H4 based on the result in Table 3. The result of the RE model indicates that H4 is supported $( \hat { \gamma } _ { _ 1 } = - 0 . 3 2 1$ significant at the 5 percent level) in the pooled analysis. However, the Hausman specification test between the FE estimation and the RE estimation $( \hat { m } = 1 3 . 1 2 )$ indicates that there are potentially biasing effects due to the correlations with the fixed errors in the model assumption of the RE model. After running HTIV(1) and HTIV(2) (assuming $T R _ { i }$ as either exogenous or endogenous in each respective model), the signs of $\hat { \boldsymbol { \gamma } } _ { 1 }$ in both results in Table 3 are still negative, but they become insignificant. Therefore, we cannot draw a definitive conclusion that the difference between freeware and trialware on consumer software sampling is significant. In addition, consumers may not prefer freeware over trialware (or vice versa) for trial experience purposes.

Our finding of consumer’s indifference between the two types of free-trial software particularly implies that software developers should be cautious with the free-trial sampling strategy. If software developers just follow an intuition that giving away freeware would be better than providing trialware to improve consumer sampling, it may seriously cannibalize the sales of commercial software because consumers may find it sufficient to use only the functionalities provided in the freeware instead of purchasing the commercial product [13]. Therefore, software firms should carefully decide the optimal amount of functionalities offered in freeware.

However, each software category has very different functionalities, and we should check whether consumer’s indifference still holds within each category. Also, the endogeneity of $T R _ { i }$ may come from the correlation with category-specific rather than product-specific unobservable components. Therefore, we analyze the same model in each software category next.

## Category Differences

In the previous subsections, we analyze the overall effects of the independent variables on consumer software sampling using the pooled data, including category dummies to control for category-specific effects. However, the inherent nature of each category differs significantly from each other, and only the competitive relationship within each category may matter. Therefore, by conducting the same analysis within each category, we can completely rule out any category-specific unobserved component in the pooled data analysis. As such, our category-level analysis considers each software category as an independent product class in the entire software market. Table 4 shows the overall results of each category by performing the same estimation procedure and endogeneity tests as in the pooled analysis.

As Table 4 shows, H1 of the Bass-type diffusion process in the consumer sampling process is still supported in most software categories except for the E-mail Client and Desktop Publishing categories. For the categories supporting H1, Table 5 shows the predicted average peak times of consumer software sampling and magnitudes of peaks. The predicted timing of download peak in Table 5 shows the different aspects of consumer sampling growths across categories. For instance, the Antivirus category is the slowest to reach its growth peak, whereas the Presentation Tools category is the fastest. Antivirus software products usually update virus definition files or deploy patches frequently while new versioning is relatively infrequent. Also, the quality of antivirus software is more likely to depend on the ability of up-to-date protection against current viruses rather than functional advantages.

In the pooled analysis, the overall effect of user ratings is statistically significant, but it becomes statistically insignificant within most categories except for the Antivirus and the File Compression categories. The effect of CNET’s rating becomes statistically insignificant in three categories, E-mail Client, Desktop Publishing, and Presentation Tools, but it continues to have a significantly positive effect on consumer software sampling if the rating reaches 4 or 5. The effects of both user and third-party ratings are still significant in the Antivirus and File Compression categories. This may demonstrate that users in the two categories are more sensitive to rating information than those in the other categories.

Like the findings in the pooled analysis, five of seven categories that we studied do not support H4 that freeware outperforms trialware. However, the CD and DVD Burner and Digital Photo Tools categories support that freeware strategy prevails. Freeware versions for CD and DVD Burner led to 934 more downloads on average during a week. There is a difference of only 47 downloads between freeware and trialware in Digital Photo Tools; hence, the performance difference is practically nonsignificant in this category. Therefore, we can conclude that it is not necessarily disadvantageous to distribute trialware of the focal software to improve sampling performance.

Although freeware may improve the number of downloads by users, developers should consider carefully the use of this strategy because of the aforementioned cannibalizing effect by the failure of quality differentiation between freeware and the focal software [14]. In other words, a freeware strategy would be successful only if the quality difference is sufficient. For example, users satisfied with a freeware version of a CD and DVD burner to perform a core function of burning a CD or DVD would not purchase the focal software. Trialware would be a better practical marketing strategy when a developer cannot sufficiently differentiate freeware from its focal product because consumer software sampling preference does not depend much on the type of free trials in the overall software market.

## Conclusion

In t he current st udy , we il uminat ed t he useful impl icat ions of actual strategies aimed at improving consumer software sampling, such as the different types of free trials and ratings. We found that overall consumer software sampling growth follows a traditional type of information diffusion process, from which software developers can effectively extrapolate the extent of consumer awareness of their focal software in the market. Ratings of free-trial software from users and third parties can send strong signals that users may use to infer the quality of the focal software. Unlike other experience goods such as movies, music, and books, third-party ratings can effectively reduce evaluating users’ difficulties, such as certain requirements of technical knowledge and readiness in software adoption. Hence, a higher rating by a third-party is more conducive to users if it is credible. As such, a third party’s public trust is a driving force to lead more software developers to list their products and more users to search information about products on third-party sites. Therefore, our findings are important not only to software developers but also to third parties. In addition, we show that the common practice of freeware is not always a better approach than trialware to attract potential users in the software market. We found that freeware does not induce more sampling activities by users, and potential users do not have a distinctive preference of freeware over trialware although freeware can be used for an unlimited amount of time. Software developers should not choose freeware for the sole purpose of increasing the number of downloads by users. This observation serves to caution software developers because freeware can seriously cannibalize the sales of focal software.

Because of the lack of the sales information for software in our data, we were unable to examine whether higher sampling performance leads to higher sales or revenue. An integrated framework for software sales and sampling would be of great interest. In particular, future research could estimate the effect of freeware versus trialware on software sales. The estimation of the marginal value of user or third-party ratings on sales through software sampling could raise great attention as well. An important observation in this paper is that consumer software sampling reflects Bass-type information diffusion. With actual software sales and sampling performance data, future research could test the difference between corresponding diffusion processes. It could also be possible to set up a more accurate software sales prediction model using sampling data as previous information, and this model could give software developers a better idea to choose the optimal timing of the next generation of their software. In addition, the current analysis considered only one third-party site and its rating. It would be interesting to identify competing effects of several third-party ratings based on their characteristics, such as company size, volume of traffic, and advertising power. This would help software developers’ advertising budget allocation when third parties charge fees for banner or sponsored search advertising.
