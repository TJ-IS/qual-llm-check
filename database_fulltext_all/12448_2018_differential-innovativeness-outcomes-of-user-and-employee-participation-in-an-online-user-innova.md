# Differential Innovativeness Outcomes of User and Employee Participation in an Online User Innovation Community

JIE (KEVIN) YAN, DOROTHY E. LEIDNER, AND HIND BENBYA

ABSTRACT: Firm-hosted online user innovation communities (OUICs) have emerged as a vital source of knowledge and expertise for innovation and new product development. Prior research on OUICs has primarily focused on participation of external product users and benefits obtained from their contributions. However, the role of internal employees of the host firm, albeit essential for the community’s longterm success, has not yet received much attention. This study attempts to bridge this gap by investigating the innovation outcomes of employees participating in OUICs. By analyzing a longitudinal data set collected from Salesforce.com’s IdeaExchange community, we find that accessing product user ideas with diverse and well-codified content drives idea promotion and generation of participating employees. Moreover, ideas contributed and promoted by employees are more likely to be implemented than those contributed and only promoted by product users. We discuss important implications of our study for the online innovation community literature and for the development of overall employee engagement in OUICs.

KEY WORDS: AND PHRASES: Online user innovation communities, employee innovativeness, crowdsourcing, co-creation, online communities, online ideation.

## Introduction

Firm-hosted online user innovation communities (hereinafter OUICs) <sup>1</sup> refer to firmsponsored initiatives designed to co-create value with firms’ external product users. In OUICs, participants (i.e., product users and internal employees of host firms) can interact with each other for new product development. As the potential value of these communities has become more apparent, an increasing number of firms are leveraging such communities to gain access to suggestions of product users and to capture distant knowledge, thereby increasing their ability to innovate [6, 22]. Despite the potential value of OUICs, recent evidence from both academic research and practice suggests that many OUICs suffer from the “Empty Bar Syndrome” because they lack active participation [23, 67]. Having sustainable participation from both external product users and internal employees is imperative for the long-term success of any OUIC because it serves as the foundation upon which firms can further design, invest, build and grow their communities [23, 58].

While both employee and product user participation are required to build a successful OUIC, the extant research has tended to focus primarily on product users. Many scholars have studied the participation of product users in OUICs and the conditions under which their participation might lead to successful innovations (e.g., [27, 70]). Although these studies provide valuable insights into how product users contribute to organizational innovation, they provide an incomplete account of the role of employees in OUICs. Acquiring knowledge related to employee participation is important because a thriving OUIC depends on participation and contribution from both sides of this external/internal duality, an issue that has received limited empirical research attention to date.

Scholars have thus called for empirical studies to better understand employees participation in OUICs (e.g., [14, 40, 59]). Nambisan and Baron [59], for example, highlighted that an important organizational decision relates to the choice of employees assigned to participate in an OUIC and the nature of their interactions with product users. Other studies emphasize the importance of proactive and reactive attention from employees for eliciting more suggestions and ideas from product users [19, 23, 38]. Overall, the few existing studies in this area confirm the key role of employee participation in building a successful OUIC and indicate a lack of understanding of outcomes related to such participation. This study therefore seeks to add to the existing literature by focusing on internal employees who participate in OUICs. We examine how participating in an OUIC affects employee innovativeness. We define employee innovativeness as the extent to which an employee actively promotes and generates new ideas [30].

We believe that examining employee innovativeness in an OUIC is important and valuable for the following reasons. First, employees are still considered by firms as the most important innovation partners followed by product users and other external entities [14]. OUICs hold the potential to enhance participating employees’ innovativeness because the communities offer participants a digital social space to discuss new ideas and learn from each other [60, 63]. Therefore, research that investigates the innovation benefits employees might obtain from OUICs has the potential to advance both research and practice. Second, and more importantly, many host firms still lack the requisite knowledge and motivation to mobilize their employees to engage in OUICs [82]. Increasing numbers of firms hold only the community managers and some customer-facing positions responsible for participating in the communities. Because achieving long-term community success requires employee participation from various job levels and functions [82, 85], it is difficult for these firms to continue to build and grow their communities. Thus, by investigating and demonstrating the innovation benefits of employee participation, our study underscores the importance of encouraging and enabling overall employee engagement in OUICs.

We review extant literature on online innovation communities and employee innovativeness in organizations. We then develop our hypotheses drawing upon organizational knowledge creation and attention theories. To test our hypotheses, we collected data from IdeaExchange, an OUIC hosted by Salesforce.com. We analyzed both quantitative and qualitative participation data of 122 employees of Saleforce.com over a two-year period. The data includes 8,088 product user ideas accessed by the employees, 337 ideas contributed by the employees, and 5,402 employee comments associated with ideas of product users. Overall, our study shows that accessing ideas with diverse and well-codified content influences participating employees’ idea generation and promotion behaviors. Furthermore, ideas contributed and promoted by employees are overall more likely to be implemented than those contributed and only promoted by product users. With these findings, our goal is to catalyze research on the role of employees in OUICs in order to help address the failure of many innovation communities to yield solutions that provide value to organizations.

## Literature Review

We review representative studies in two research streams to highlight the gaps that motivate our investigation: participation in online innovation communities and employee innovativeness in organizations.

## Participation in Online Innovation Communities

Extant studies on online innovation communities have largely examined the dynamics related to community participation. Participation has been conceptualized to include both passive (e.g., viewing) and active (e.g., posting) behaviors of community members. Both types of activities are needed and they reflect different commitment levels [54]. Table 1 provides a comparison of different members activities and participation levels in four major forms of online innovation communities. In this study, we investigate active participation and focus particularly on employee innovative behavior in OUICs.

In the context of OUIC, a large body of research explores the factors that motivate or demotivate product users to participate and share ideas with the host firms. The motivations include, among others, product users’ sense of responsibility and commitment to the community [47, 59], the degree of community support from peers [21, 47], trust in the host firms [47, 70], and individual attributes (e.g., online interaction propensity) [86]. Demotivation, however, emerges when negative perceptions of fairness and dissatisfaction with the outcomes exist among community members [27]. Recent research has also moved beyond the motivation-participation link to investigate the influence of social interactions and community network structures on product users’ innovativeness [e.g., 12, 18, 40, 41]. For instance, Dahlander and Frederiksen [18] find that product users’ position in the core/periphery network structure of an OUIC is likely to explain why some product users are more innovative than others. Likewise, Chen et al. [12]’s research shows that product users’ innovativeness is affected not only by the degree of interaction with peers in the community but also by product users’ level of network connectedness.

While these studies contribute to our in-depth understanding of participation in OUICs, the specific role of employees and outcomes related to their community participation has been neglected. In addition, although there are a handful of studies focusing on various roles for participants in OUICs (see [41, 53]), these studies fall short in explaining and differentiating between the roles of product users and employees. Motivated by this knowledge gap, the present study seeks to focus on internal employees and to examine their role and participation in OUICs. We investigate the innovation outcomes arising from employee participation and compare the outcomes of participation between employees and product users. Benefits associated with employee participation may not only relate to employee innovativeness, but also to the overall sustainability of OUICs.

## Employee Innovativeness in Organizations

Although few studies specifically examine employee innovativeness in using OUICs, the topic of employee innovativeness has received considerable attention in the traditional (offline) organizational context. Organizational studies conceive of employee innovativeness as a multi-dimensional concept, consisting of activities pertaining to idea generation as well as idea realization or implementation [68].

Employee innovativeness is a function of individual ability, motivation and organizational context [2, 3]. Different aspects of social context including organizational climate, leadership, and work group relations have been linked to employee innovativeness at work (see [3, 68] for a recent review).

In the online organizational setting, early research on group support systems (GSS) [74] and electronic brainstorming [20] relied on an experimental approach to investigate how different stimuli or cues affect employee idea generation. The techniques studied to enhance an employee’s idea generation range from cognitive priming [9], external stimuli [36], and divergent and convergent thinking [44]. Recently, a growing body of research has examined how using digital technologies (e.g., wikis [4], social media and innovation platforms [8, 49]) within an organizational setting can contribute to employee innovation. Gray et al. [30], for example, investigate how the use of social bookmarking systems can help employees bridge structural holes to access novel information and, as a result, become innovative. Leonardi [49] offers a theory of communication visibility, which suggests that enterprise social software improves employee metaknowledge, leading to less knowledge duplication and new products and services. These studies are limited to intra-organizational communications among employees, rely on self-reported levels of individual innovativeness, and do not examine either the characteristics of content accessed by employees or how employees allocate attention among different content accessed. Nevertheless, these studies suggest that digital technology enhances interactions between employees and endows them with informational advantages that can drive individual innovativeness [8, 11].

Our study offers several extensions to the existing literature on employee innovativeness. First, we reveal how organizations can harness the OUIC not only as a platform to build social relations and connections among employees and product users, but also as a platform to facilitate employee innovativeness. Second, we focus on the content of product user ideas accessed by employees and examine how the characteristics of content may affect employee innovativeness. And third, our study highlights that the benefits of accessing idea content will be subject to decreasing returns. Overall, compared to existing studies mainly focusing on intra-organizational communications among employees, this study advances both theory and practice by investigating how interactions with an external entity in an online setting can benefit employees. ## Theoretical Background

This study integrates two theories (organizational knowledge creation theory and attention theory) to derive our conceptual model. In this section, we do not include a detailed discussion of the theories because these have been the subject of multiple reviews in the literature (see [63] and [65]). Rather, we provide a discussion of the theories’ main tenets and explain how they are related to our study.

## Organizational Knowledge Creation Theory

The theory of organizational knowledge creation was first introduced by Nonaka [60] and subsequently extended in his later works (see [61, 62]). The theory indicates that a core dimension of new knowledge creation in organizations is the level of social interaction between individuals [60]. Specifically, because knowledge is created by individuals, organizations should provide a context, or “communities of interaction,” wherein interactions between individuals can contribute to the sharing and development of new knowledge and ideas [60]. Nonaka’s later studies have highlighted that such a knowledge-creating place, or “ba,” can be formal or informal, physical or virtual, and inside or outside organizational boundaries [61]. Apart from the context of new knowledge creation and innovation (i.e., “communities of interaction”), organizational knowledge creation theory highlights the relevance of information content in innovation processes [60]. Nonaka points out that in terms of innovation, the content of knowledge is more relevant than the form in which the knowledge is embodied. Thus, he views the conversion and combination of different types of knowledge (i.e., tacit and explicit knowledge) as central for innovation.

In this study, we treat firm-hosted OUICs as virtual communities of interaction spanning organizational boundaries. In OUICs, employees from various functions, levels, and regions of the host firm can interact with external entities such as product users to create and share knowledge. Exchanging knowledge and information between employees and product users in such communities of practice, versus the formal taskrelated procedures specified by the organization, may facilitate employee innovativeness by linking employees’ routine work to active learning and innovation [61, 63].

## Attention Theory

We adopt Ocasio’s [64] view and definition of attention which considers attention as a process involving time, energy and effort resulting in particular attentional selections that then drive behavior and outcomes. According to this perspective, the attention of both individuals and organizations is a scarce resource and any allocation of attention has an opportunity cost [64]. At the individual level, because of human limits, attention cannot be dedicated to all issues and answers. Rather, individuals selectively allocate more attention to some stimuli over others [56, 77]. Such human limits with respect to attention allocation have been described as the ecology of attention. It highlights the competitive nature of attention between alternative targets seeking the attention of individuals [65]. The ecological perspective primarily views attention as salience and treats selective attention as a process driven by the characteristics of the attention targets [e.g., 34, 37, 77]. Therefore, salient problems that stand out more relative to alternative ones are likely to attract attention. In addition, more recent research on the ecology of attention in a digital environment considers attention allocation as a matching process between individuals and problems [e.g., 7, 33, 43]. It focuses on the dyadic nature of the attention process and examines how particular individuals allocate attention to particular problems. Overall, the matching process views attention allocation as an individual-problem match that is largely decided by individual/problem characteristics.

The aforementioned attention perspective fits with our study for several reasons. First, most host firms are limited in terms of their capability to process all the ideas contributed by product users [69, 84]. This implies that in OUICs, ideas compete for participating employees’ attention. Second, the notion of problem salience suggests that the characteristics of idea content can impact attention allocation in the community. Finally, the view of matching process emphasizes the importance of taking individual characteristics into account when examining attention allocation and its effects. Such characteristics, for instance, include the knowledge and expertise possessed by product users and employees.

## Conceptual Model and Hypotheses Development

Organizational knowledge creation theory emphasizes that interaction content, rather than interaction forms (e.g., voting and commenting), plays a relatively critical role in explaining individuals’ innovativeness. In addition, attention theory indicates that employees, while participating in the community, cannot devote their attention to all the ideas accessed. Rather, they tend to attend to ideas that are salient. We thus focus our analysis on two characteristics of idea content that may influence employee innovativeness via different cognitive mechanisms and processes: diversity and codifiedness. We refer to diversity in this study as how diverse the content of an idea is and to codifiedness as how well a product user has converted his/her tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge as idea content. Specifically, we examine 1) how accessing diverse and well-codified content of product user ideas may stimulate the generation and promotion of ideas by participating employees.

Furthermore, we differentiate employees from product users because of the different roles of the two entities in OUICs. In comparison to product users, employees may allocate their attention to a different set of ideas because of the different characteristics of the two groups. We thus take such role and characteristic differences into account by focusing on idea implementation, a key construct examined in the OUIC literature (e.g., [6, 22, 50]). We seek to investigate whether participation of employees would lead to different innovative outcomes than those of product users. Specifically, we examine 2) how the innovative behavior of idea promotion and generation by employees, compared to that by product users, has a different impact on idea implementation. ## Idea Content Characteristics and Employee Idea Promotion and Generation

As employees browse product user ideas, well-codified ideas with contextual elements such as pictures and figures may attract their attention by dominating employees’ field of vision and crowding out other stimuli [33, 87]. In addition, because of its lower level of inherent difficulty and higher level of presentation, a well-codified idea reduces the cognitive load for an employee to read and comprehend it, compared to an idea that is vague and/or lacks contextual explanations. For example, ideas using images for idea description are more persuasive because of higher levels of vividness and comprehension [84]. Such ideas, as a result, draw more attention from community members [50]. Likewise, an idea with diverse content is more likely to capture an employee’s attention by providing information outside the employee’s existing knowledge base [55]. Alternately, the idea may offer a hook that captures the employee’s attention by touching on an expertise domain of interest to the employee [55]. Such an idea may connect other expertise domains to the domain of expertise that originally attracted the employee, making it more engaging to read. In sum, we suggest that compared to ideas that are vague, incomplete, and/or plain, ideas with diverse and well-codified content overall draw more attention because they are more engaging and provide broader expertise and more contextual details.

Prior research has shown that drawing an individual’s attention to information that is relevant to an individual’s interests influences the level of evaluation and subsequent action toward the information or opportunity [5, 66]. And attention facilitates perception and action towards the object or idea being considered [64]. We thus argue that as an employee attends to ideas, the employee’s promotion behavior may be triggered. This can be especially true for ideas with diverse and well-codified content in light of the following reasons. First, well-codified ideas usually receive better evaluation and full consideration because of the contextual details provided by the ideas [23]. Second, ideas with diverse content are often valuable because they suggest changes that can be potentially applied to multiple products/services [6]. Overall, we suggest that ideas with diverse and well-codified content draw more attention from an employee and are likely to trigger the employee’s promotion behavior.

However, we further suggest that the positive effect of accessing diverse and wellcodified idea content on employee idea promotion diminishes after an employee has attended to and evaluated numerous ideas. At this point, continuous access to diverse and well-codified content may have only a minimal or negative impact on employee idea promotion. This is because a wealth of information creates a substantial information-processing burden and cognitive load [64, 75]. As stated by Koput [46], too much high-quality information can be harmful to innovative performance because it creates greater cognitive load and requires an individual to utilize more complex schemas to process it. As a result, as an employee accesses increasing idea content, a great amount of content may remain unabsorbed or only receive cursory attention and exploitation, resulting in decreasing idea promotion. Taken together, we expect that there will be a curvilinear relationship between accessing diverse and well-codified idea content and idea promotion of an employee. Thus, we predict:

Hypothesis (H1a): The amount of diverse content of product user ideas accessed by an employee has an inverted U-shaped relationship with employee idea promotion.

Hypothesis (H1b): The amount of well-codified content of product user ideas accessed by an employee has an inverted U-shaped relationship with employee idea promotion.

Apart from idea promotion, employees may generate their own ideas arising from accessing diverse and well-codified idea content. According to Nonaka [60], when sharing their ideas, individuals convert their tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge as idea content. By attending to ideas with diverse and well-codified content, employees are able to acquire the explicit knowledge contained in the idea content and absorb it to create new tacit knowledge. As an employee continues to access diverse and well-codified content, the accumulated tacit knowledge may inspire the employee to generate his/her own ideas [74].

More specifically, accessing ideas with diverse content can facilitate idea generation by offering individuals the potential to create novel links and associations among different types of knowledge acquired [16]. A wide variety of studies have recognized the importance of accessing diverse knowledge for individual idea generation [e.g., 11, 25, 41, 76]. Faniel and Majchrzak [25], for example, find that engineers who have access to knowledge from other functional departments are more likely to become innovative than engineers who do not. Likewise, Sosa [76] indicates that in new product development teams, employees who possess strong ties connected with diverse information generate more ideas than those who do not. We therefore expect that accessing diverse idea content will provide employees the potential to process (via modifying and/or integrating) the knowledge acquired and to establish novel linkages among it, facilitating the generation of new ideas. Similarly, accessing well-codified ideas is likely to facilitate individual idea generation because such ideas are less ambiguous and more readily integrated into one’s existing knowledge [62]. In addition, well-codified ideas present an opportunity to link one’s ideas to those of others or to combine some of the shared ideas into more complete, novel or useful ideas [45]. We thus expect that well-codified ideas, from the recipient’s cognitive perspective, are easy to absorb, process, and combine into new ideas.

Meanwhile, because of the potential information overload problem discussed above, employees are unlikely to benefit from the knowledge content once they have reached a point of idea saturation. We expect that the innovation effect of accessing diverse and well-codified content should become negative when an employee is experiencing overabundant knowledge content and thereby greater cognitive load. In sum, we posit that the effect of accessing diverse and wellcodified idea content on employee idea generation is nonlinear as follows:

Hypothesis (H2a): The amount of diverse content of product user ideas accessed by an employee has an inverted U-shaped relationship with employee idea generation.

Hypothesis (H2b): The amount of well-codified content of product user ideas accessed by an employee has an inverted U-shaped relationship with employee idea generation.

## Idea Implementation

To examine the innovation outcomes of employee participation versus those of product user participation, we focus on idea implementation. We suggest that participating employees overall possess different domain-specific knowledge than that of product users. Consequently, employees, compared to product users, may devote their attention to a different set of ideas, resulting in a different impact on idea implementation. Specifically, in OUICs, the domain-specific knowledge can be categorized into technological knowledge, use knowledge and company-specific knowledge [10, 48]. While employees and product users share certain technological and use knowledge [48], employees, in general, possess advantages over product users in terms of company-specific knowledge. This company-specific knowledge may relate to project processes, the needs of certain individuals or organizational units, the strategic goals of the host firm, among others. [10].

Due to this difference in knowledge and expertise, employees may attend to and promote ideas that are not only salient but would support a strong composite of feasibility and desirability. This includes whether the focal idea fits with the host firm’s resource capacities, is technologically feasible, and/or aligns with existing product portfolio and firm strategies [26]. Such ideas would be valuable for new product development and likely to be implemented. By contrast, there is evidence that ideas highly promoted by product users often have limited value to the host firm because of minority group biases [23] and product users’ inability to attend to valuable ideas when confronted with massive redundancy [84]. Additionally, product users often promote ideas based on “liking” rather than the novelty and usefulness of the ideas [23].

Moreover, employees, with company-specific knowledge in mind, may generate ideas that are more viable than those from product users, considering that product users contribute ideas mainly based on the knowledge derived from using the products/services without an understanding of the complexities involved in bringing the idea to fruition [10, 48]. Such ideas contributed by employees help reduce the strain on the innovation efforts of the host firm [22, 26]. In addition, as discussed earlier, absorbing the knowledge acquired from the ideas accessed provides individuals with an opportunity to combine their knowledge or ideas with those of others into ideas that are more valuable. Thus, if employees do attend to ideas that are overall more valuable than those attended by product users, employees should be more likely than product users to be inspired to generate valuable ideas. Taken together, we posit that:

Hypothesis (H3a): Ideas promoted by employees are overall more likely to be implemented than those promoted only by product users.

Hypothesis (H3b): Ideas contributed by employees are overall more likely to be implemented than those contributed by product users.

## Methodology

## Context: Salesforce.com’s IdeaExchange Community

We selected IdeaExchange, the OUIC of Salesforce.com, as our empirical setting. Salesforce.com launched IdeaExchange in 2007 and has established a successful, long-term relationship with its product users for value co-creation and product innovation. According to our interview with the community manager, IdeaExchange was launched as a forum to gather suggestions and ideas for new product development. Ideas can either relate to an existing product or a new product for an incremental change or a radical change. Product users are encouraged to share their own ideas and also to discuss and evaluate others’ ideas. Employees of Salesforce.com are also encouraged to participate in the community. Participating employees receive education from the community management team on how to communicate and interact with product users for better community results. Notably, while participation remains voluntary, Salesforce employees from different levels/ functions might possess different motives for participating in the community. Employees from Research & Development (R&D), for example, may decide to participate to gain new insights into existing products or support new product development. Marketing and sales employees may choose to participate to foster stronger customer relationships.

## Data Collection

We first collected longitudinal data including 4,472 product user ideas and 337 employee ideas created from July 2012 to June 2014 (a two-year period). We then analyzed all the ideas in the dataset to identify employees who participate in the community. Specifically, for each idea we accessed, we checked the ideator and commenters/voters’ community profiles to see if s/he is an employee or a product user. The entire process identified a total of 122 Salesforce.com employees in the community. Figure 1 in the Appendix provides an example of a community profile. In addition to some demographic information regarding the member, the profile records the contributions—for example, number of comments, number of votes, and number of ideas—that a member has given to the community. Table 2 in the Appendix summarizes the demographic characteristics of these employees as of June 2014.

Finally, we collected all the product user ideas that an employee had commented and/or voted on to identify ideas that were accessed by the employee (we assume an employee had at least accessed and read the idea content before commenting and/or voting). Specifically, for each employee, via his/her online profile, we collected all the product user ideas s/he had commented and/or voted on within the two-year period. For all the 122 employees, a total of 8,088 product user ideas were collected, ordered by employee.<sup>2</sup> The previously mentioned 4,472 ideas were all the ideas of product users created during the two-year period whereas the 8,088 ideas represent all ideas accessed by the 122 employees during the two-year period. We recorded these as two separate datasets although there was some overlap between them (i.e., ideas that were created by product users as well as accessed by the employees within the two-year period).

## Variable Measures

## Idea Content Codifiedness and Diversity

To measure the diversity and codifiedness of idea content accessed by the employees, we applied qualitative coding on the 8,088 ideas. Idea content codifiedness reflects how well individuals have converted their tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge as idea content. A higher degree of codifiedness implies that individuals have codified their ideas to a fuller extent, which may hinge on many factors. For example, whether an idea was well thought out may affect the codifiedness of the idea content. Or perhaps an idea was not well codified because the ideator proposing it lacked the knowledge necessary to articulate it properly. Or maybe the idea involved too much tacit knowledge to codify it well. Considering the aforementioned factors, we discussed and developed a coding scheme before coding the idea content. Table 2 details our coding scheme. During the process of content analysis of these 8,088 ideas, an idea would receive a score of 0, 1, 2, or 3 based on its level of codifiedness. Table 3 in the Appendix shows some product user ideas representing different levels of codifiedness according to our coding scheme.

Because the coding of idea content codifiedness is subjective, we followed an iterative sample coding process suggested by Lombard et al. [51] in order to evaluate the reliability of our coding scheme and results. Specifically, two authors independently coded a subset of 150 ideas. After categorizing the first 100 ideas, we achieved a Cohen’s kappa of 0.71 (a Cohen’s kappa score of [0.40, 0.75] is from fair to good [15]). We discussed the sources of disagreement and the reasons why we coded as we had and through this, reached a better understanding of the coding protocol. A Cohen’s kappa of 0.82 was achieved on the remaining 50 ideas, ensuring an acceptable level of reliability between the coders. Then, one of the authors continued with the remainder of the ideas and assigned a final score for each of the 8,088 ideas.<sup>3</sup>

Idea content diversity reflects how diverse the content of an idea is. To measure this variable, we utilized the idea tags assigned to each idea. As shown in Table 3 in the Appendix, idea tags are assigned to ideas about different product/service categories. Hence, idea tags objectively reflect an idea’s content diversity [6, 22]. There are 41 idea categories (or tags) listed by Salesfore.com (as of June 2014). To quantify this variable for each idea, we created an idea/category matrix: 8,088 rows (ordered by ideas accessed by each employee) x 41 columns. Each row represents an idea where we marked “1” under the corresponding column if the idea tag represented by that column was assigned. For example, an idea assigned three different tags would have three columns marked as “1.”

## Employee Idea Promotion

Idea promotion reflects the level of support provided by an employee to an idea based on the employee’s assessment of the idea. To measure employee idea promotion, we utilized employees’ commenting activities in the community. We focused on the commenting, because compared to voting, commenting overall requires more attention, time, and cognitive effort from an employee and thereby conveys a higher degree of assessment. By analyzing the content of comments, we are able to identify whether an employee chose to support an idea. We thus developed supportive tone and assigned the comments into three categories (i.e., positive argument, positive comment, and neutral comment) that represent the nature of the assessment. Table 3 categorizes, describes, and exemplifies the supportive tones. We then applied qualitative coding on all the employee comments associated with the ideas of product users in our datasets. A total of 5,402 distinct employee comments were coded and assigned a score of 0, 1, or 2 based on Table 3. A Cohen’s Kappa score of 0.77 was reached between two independent raters in assessing the reliability of the coding procedure. One rater conducted the entire coding work and the other coded a subsample of 1,200 ideas. Furthermore, a third rater randomly selected and coded 150 ideas and compared the results to those from the leading rater, resulting in a Cohen’s Kappa score of 0.72.

## Idea Implementation and Employee Idea Generation

In line with previous studies (e.g., [74]), we used number of new ideas (i.e., idea quantity) to measure idea generation. The number of new ideas generated by an employee was collected from the records in his/her online profile. Also, in line with previous studies (e.g., [6, 50]), idea implementation was measured as a binary variable. It was coded as “1” if the idea had a status tag of under pilot tests, partially implemented, or implemented, and “0” if not.

## Control Variables

On the ideator level, we included the community tenure because it may impact the likelihood of idea implementation. For both product users and employees, community tenure was measured in months by calculating the interval between the month of their first community activity and the end month of our data collection. On the idea level, we included the readability [50]. Readability was measured by calculating the Flesch-Kincaid Reading Ease score<sup>4</sup> of each idea. Also, while we did not use employees’ voting in measuring idea promotion, we included the number of employee votes an idea received as a control variable. Table 4 summarizes the variables discussed.

## Modeling Strategy and Estimation Approach

## Employee Idea Generation and Promotion

To test Hypotheses H1a through H2b, we constructed a panel dataset in order to take potential endogeneity into account. Endogeneity includes omitted-variable bias, measurement error, and/or reverse causality [31]. For example, one employee may generate and/or promote more ideas than another not only because of accessing diverse and well-codified idea content, but because of his/her personal characteristics and ability. Also, an employee may frequently access idea content simply because his/her personal characteristics make him/her interested in reading ideas or because his/her job function requires him/her to do so. Therefore, building a panel dataset allows us to include and control individual fixed-effects and avoid omitted variable bias [31]. In addition, an employee may access more user ideas than others simply because s/he is interested in generating ideas. We therefore measure idea content diversity and codifiedness prior to measuring employees’ idea generation. If accessing ideas of product users is to impact employee idea generation, the predetermined idea content diversity and codifiedness should demonstrate a significant effect. We also included employees’ community tenure to help eliminate time effects.

Given the aforementioned factors, we constructed a panel dataset containing monthly observations for each employee from July 2012 to June 2014, a total of 24 months. Because there are 16 employees who joined the community after July 2012, their community behavior data are not complete in the present study. We therefore created two panel datasets: one trimmed, balanced panel dataset including 2,544 observations (106 x 24) and one unbalanced panel dataset including all the 122 employees (2,784 observations).

For each unit of observation, idea content codifiedness is calculated by adding the content codifiedness score of all the ideas accessed by the employee in a particular month. For example, an employee would have a total codifiedness score of 6 in a particular month if s/he commented and/or voted on a total of 3 ideas with a codifiedness score of 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Idea content diversity is calculated by comparing and counting the number of distinct idea categories in the idea/ category matrix. For example, an employee would have a total diversity score of 6 if all the ideas s/he accessed in a particular month pertained to 6 different idea categories. Then, we observed how many ideas the employee generated in the subsequent month and used this number as the dependent variable of employee idea generation for each unit of observation. For the other dependent variable of employee idea promotion, we measured it over the same monthly time period considering that employees’ commenting activities usually occur right after the content access. For each monthly observation, employee idea promotion was calculated by adding the supportive tone score of all the positive arguments and positive comments contributed by the focal employee during that month. For example, an employee would receive an idea promotion score of 3 if s/he had one positive argument (score 2) and one positive comment (score 1) during that month.

The dependent variable of employee idea generation is a count variable, which fits with a Poisson panel model. The negative binomial (NB) panel model is a generalization of Poisson panel model in that the former allows the sample variance to be different from the sample mean when the data is over-dispersed. Our subsequent model tests show that the over-dispersion parameter α is larger than 0, indicating a NB panel model fits better with our data [31]. We chose a NB fixed-effects panel model over random effects to control individual fixed-effects and avoid potential omitted variable bias. The Hausman test statistic (shown in the results section) also indicates that a fixed-effects model is more appropriate than a random effects model. In addition, the dependent variable of employee idea generation suffers from excess zeros because some employees generated no or only a few ideas during the two-year time period. Thus, in many monthly observations, there were no ideas generated by the employees. To account for excess zeros, we added a zero-inflation part to estimate a full zero-inflated negative binomial fixed-effects (ZINB) panel model [31].

The ZINB model assumes that excess zeros in the dependent variable are generated by two distinct processes. In the case of our study, these two processes entail the presence of active participants vs. lurkers. Prior studies generally define lurkers as passive members who explore and benefit from the community content without contributing back to community activities [54]. Given the context of our study, we refer to lurkers as employees who have no intent of sharing even when they have new ideas. Put differently, of these excess zeros, some come from the active participants who happen to yield zero ideas; others come from the lurkers who might have new ideas but do not want to share them and therefore have “zero” ideas. The ZINB regression thereby entails two models: a count model—NB model—to model the count process, and a logit model to differentiate the two processes regarding the zero outcomes [31].

$$

## Additional Analyses

We conducted several additional analyses. For the logistic datasets, we ran the models without the ideas having a status of “pilot/beta” or “partially implemented” and compared the results with those of the original models. We conducted this extra analysis because according to our interview with the community manager, ideas under pilot test by Salesforce.com are highly likely to be implemented, but this may not apply to all the cases. The results are consistent as shown in Table 4 in the Appendix.

For the panel dataset, we first used the Shannon-Weiner Diversity Index (H)<sup>6</sup> as an alternative measure for idea content diversity to avoid a range metric. Specifically, for each employee during each month, we first calculated a parameter p for each different idea category the employee encountered by dividing the total number of idea tags of that category by the total number of idea tags of all categories. We then used the following formula to calculate the Shannon-Weiner Diversity Index for each employee during each month [52]:

$$
\mathrm{H} = - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (p \mathrm{i} * l n p \mathrm{i})\tag{7}
$$

where n is the total number of distinct idea categories.

We reran the model using the absolute value of H. Table 5 in the Appendix summarizes the results using the alternative measures. The results are consistent in terms of the sign and significance of the variables.

Furthermore, one major criticism of panel models is that the choice of period cutoffs (calendar months in this study) may be arbitrary [31]. Hence, instead of building monthly observations for each employee, we broke down the panel and restructured our data into employee-bi-week pairs. This gave us a total of 5,512 observations (52 x 106) in the balanced panel model and 6,025 observations in the unbalanced panel model. We then ran the panel procedures on both models; the results are consistent with those from the employee-month pairs. In addition, after breaking down the panel into biweekly observations, we observed that a majority of employees only generate one or no idea in most bi-weeks. We therefore modified our ZINB panel to fit a logit model estimated by unconditional fixed-effects. The results confirm the inverted U-shaped relationship between accessing diverse and well-codified idea content and employee idea generation. Tables 6 and 7 in the Appendix present the results of our additional analyses of the panel data.

## Discussion

Despite a large number of studies on how firms can leverage OUICs to enhance their innovation capability, theoretical explanations on the role of internal employees in OUICs have been scant. This gap in the literature motivated us to focus on employees who voluntarily participate in the communities. We explain and reveal how participating in OUICs affects employee innovativeness. Our findings have several important implications for research on OUICs as well as other online innovation communities, discussed next.

## Theoretical Implications

An important contribution to the extant OUIC literature lies in our differentiating employee participation in the OUIC from product user participation. First, our results suggest that failure to discern the community role of employees from that of product users may prevent host firms from harnessing the full potential of OUICs. Product users in general perceive their participation as an optional, extra-role activity [4, 41]. Whereas product users are largely driven by a state of autonomous motivation (e.g., intrinsic playful task, self-efficacy, and information seeking), enjoy revealing their knowledge and ideas to peers [40, 53], and have a high sense of selfdetermination [29, 73], employees may possess a controlled motivation (e.g., careerand reputation-related issues, organizational atmosphere, and arrangements) [4, 82]. Compared to product users, employees play a unique role in terms of community activity (see Table 1) and required innovation knowledge. Findings from the extant research reveal that numerous product user ideas as well as ideas promoted by product users often lack novelty and/or usefulness, limiting their value to the host firms [22, 23]. By contrast, our findings indicate that internal employees, when participating in OUICs, would be overall more likely than product users to distinguish good ideas from bad and to generate actionable ideas. Hence, firms that have recognized the important role of participating employees and the value of OUICs in facilitating employee innovativeness can be expected to be more innovative than firms that have not. And firms that are ambivalent about assigning employees to participate in OUICs may be lured into wasting resources for gaining access to distant knowledge.

The delineation of roles of product users and employees may also explain some inconsistent findings in prior studies. For example, a study by Di Gangi and Wasko [22] found that idea popularity (the number of votes an idea has received) has no impact on the likelihood of idea implementation. However, a recent study from Li et al. [50] shows that idea popularity is positively related to the likelihood of idea implementation. A closer look suggests that this inconsistency may relate to the different datasets gathered by the two studies from Dell’s IdeaStorm community. The former focused on ideas created from the inception of the community (February 2007) to June 2007 when there was limited participation from Dell’s employees, whereas the latter collected data over a 44-month period during which the participation from Dell’s employees had been maturing [6]. Our study suggests that the difference in the frequency and degree of employee participation and resulting variation in employee idea promotion (e.g., employee votes an idea received) may, in part, account for such inconsistent findings.

Second, we contribute to the literature on knowledge creation and sharing in a broader online innovation community context. Our study sheds light on the types of behaviors and knowledge-creation processes that encourage members of online innovation communities to innovate and the role of IT in enabling such knowledge creation, an emerging and important topic in the online innovation community literature (e.g., [24, 79, 80]). Our study shows that members are increasingly utilizing the features embedded in the community platform to innovate. Through figures/pictures, video/web page links, personal drawings and other media, ideators are able to convert their tacit knowledge and details related to personal experience into well-codified idea content. Such well-codified content constitutes the knowledge base of the community and is subsequently accessed and processed by others to become part of their new ideas. The result is that individuals are able to achieve “externalization” (creating explicit knowledge from tacit knowledge) and “combination” (reconfiguring existing explicit information into new knowledge) of knowledge for innovation without face-to-face interactions [60, 62]. Our study therefore indicates that, in an IT-enabled online innovation context, intellectual engagement with tacit-knowledge embedded IT artifacts (e.g., software codes and ideas) resides at the core of socialization aiming at new knowledge creation and innovation.

Third, our attention perspective on participation contributes to the research on knowledge sharing in online innovation communities. As our literature review reveals, a large body of extant research explains knowledge sharing in online innovation communities from a social network perspective. Studies in this research stream emphasize that the dynamics of knowledge sharing largely hinge on individuals’ personal connections and ties in the community (e.g., [12, 18, 42, 71]). However, more recent research shows that the social network variables are weak in explaining knowledge sharing and much knowledge sharing behavior occurs even when there are no connections between knowledge seekers and providers [33]. The attention view discussed in our study may provide an explanation on what drives knowledge sharing under these circumstances. When lacking personal connections, we suggest that the characteristics of interaction content such as diversity and codifiedness affect members’ attention allocation decision and subsequent knowledge sharing behavior. This attention perspective may also explain how social capital initially emerges from knowledge contribution in many different types of online communities (e.g., [13, 32, 78, 89]).

## Practical Implications

From the practical perspective, our study highlights the necessity of continued participation from both internal and external participants to build a thriving community. OUICs have been increasingly adopted and implemented across industries. Nevertheless, many empirical studies point out some hard challenges (e.g., moving ideas forward and picking the winners [69, 84]) faced by the host firms in managing ideas with the aim of producing innovations [14, 23, 84]. Our study suggests that continued participation and contribution from employees is as essential as finding and retaining product users for the sustainability of OUICs. On the one hand, by facilitating the implementation of product users’ ideas, participating employees can elicit more ideas and attract more product users to join the community for new product development. On the other hand, the ideas contributed by employees will augment the host firms’ innovative performance. We therefore suggest that host firms develop a strategic vision and action plan to increase the scope and level of employee participation.

Our investigation of Salesforce.com’s OUIC allows us to obtain valuable insights and knowledge to provide advice on when and how host firms should develop employee participation. Specifically, we suggest that host firms or the community management teams take multiple steps in order to build the level and scope of employee engagement. The first step is to target and enable internal champions, including executive sponsors and senior management. Not only does their initial participation help galvanize and legitimize the rest of the firm to join, but their subsequent community behavior will form community culture and impact incoming employees [17, 67]. Communicating the importance and value of building a thriving OUIC would help enable these internal champions. For example, community managers may use our results and the positive effects associated with employee innovativeness to encourage the kind of participation and activities they wish to promote. Once the participation of internal champions is achieved, engaging key middle managers and team leaders is the next step given that peer-to-peer evangelism is more effective than top-down [17]. To this end, host firms may need to emphasize different benefits to different participants. Over time, this extended team should include different functions and levels from the host firm to act as influencers within the community.

## Limitations and Conclusion

There are several limitations to this research that might affect its broad application. First, because employees may not leave a comment or vote every time they access an idea, identifying and coding the idea content based on employee comments/votes may not include all the idea content an employee has accessed. In addition, by only coding ideas accessed by an employee, we do not take into account all comments an employee may have accessed. Second, we did not include the employee ideas accessed by employees. We chose to focus on the content of product user ideas because employees may readily exchange comments and promote each other’s ideas internally, rendering it difficult to isolate the effects of employee promotion of employee ideas occurring via the platform. Third, there is room for improvement regarding the variable measures. For example, future research could measure idea generation in terms of both quantity and quality. By adding the quality dimension, studies might reach other conclusions. Similarly, future studies could add other dimensions, such as optimism and encouragement, into account when evaluating the “tone” of employee comments to confirm and complement our findings.

Notwithstanding these limitations, our study extends prior research on firm-hosted OUICs by focusing on internal employees and examining their participation and related outcomes. Our study offers preliminary evidence that interacting with product users in OUICs has significant impacts on employee innovativeness. We thus suggest that host firms develop and leverage the OUIC as a secondary context or structure wherein employees are engaged with nonroutine tasks and innovations.
