# Developing and Testing a Theoretical Path Model of Web Page Impression Formation and Its Consequence

Abstract. Impressions at first glance matter in the digital world in that they could lead to lasting impact on credibility perceptions, usage intention, and user satisfaction. Past studies have found that much of impression formation is affected by visual design-related features. Despite its importance, little is known of how different forms of visual aesthetics (i.e., classical vs. expressive aesthetics) influence web page impression formation. Drawing on impression formation literature and cue utilization theory, we formulate a theoretical model of web page impression formation with a temporal sequence of processing stages: automatic processing, initial perception confirmation, and impression formation, which in turn affects approach-avoidance tendency. We conduct two within-subject studies to collect both self-reported and eye movement data to test our predictions. Using real-world web pages as stimuli, the first study reveals that people engage in both automatic processing and attentive processing to form web page impressions, and arousal (measured by pupil size) shapes how people allocate attention (measured by peak duration and fixation count) to visual aesthetics. Using web pages with manipulated aesthetics in the second study, we illuminate the theoretical logic of web page impression formation by showing that people first engage in automatic processing and then engage in attentive processing. Both studies provide strong empirical support for the mediating role of arousal on the effects of visual aesthetics on attention allocation. These findings contribute to an initial theoretical understanding of visual aesthetics effects on impression formation in the digital world and provide important implications for website designers

## 1. Introduction

The question of how one’s impression of a new object or subject is formed has captured sustained interest in many disciplines because this cognitive process is ubiquitous and consequential in everyday life. Many studies have shown that a reliable impression can be formed very quickly, even within the first 39 milliseconds (ms), and has consistent influential effects over time (Bar et al. 2006). The powerful influence of impressions has been documented in various situations such as job interviews (Dougherty et al. 1994, Barrick et al. 2010), performance assessments (Greenlees et al. 2005), marketing and advertising (Brown 1970, Evans et al. 2000, Oxoby and Finnigan 2007), and others.

In the area of website design, information systems (IS) and human–computer interaction (HCI) researchers have also shown that users’ impressions of a new web page at first glance can significantly affect their subsequent experiences with the web page such as trust/credibility (Lowry et al. 2008, Robins and Holmes 2008, Lindgaard et al. 2011), perceived usability (Thielsch and Hirschfeld 2010, Lindgaard et al. 2011), and intention to use (Thielsch et al. 2013). These perceptions have been consistently confirmed as key factors that determine web users’ revisit and purchase actions (Pavlou and Fygenson 2006, Fang et al. 2014). Today, web page impressions are even more critical due to the increasingly intense competition in the online market. Woollaston (2013) reported that the number of websites has grown phenomenally, with more than 500 new websites being created every minute. Halile (2014) estimated that most visitors spent fewer than 15 seconds on a web page. Thus, for researchers and website designers, it is extremely important to understand how to garner favorable web page impressions from users in a fraction of time, so as to improve their willingness to explore the web pages further.

Given that impressions of objects or subjects are often formed by using visual appearance (Bar et al. 2006), several IS and HCI studies have identified classical and expressive aesthetics as two primary and general visual design features that may influence web page impression formation (Tractinsky et al. 2006, van Schaik and Ling 2009, Tuch et al. 2012a). Classical aesthetics emphasizes an orderly layout and clear design, whereas expressive aesthetics focuses on the creativity and inspiration of the design (Lavie and Tractinsky 2004). However, most of these studies have only identified the superficial association between visual aesthetics and impressions by using simple correlation analyses without examining the human cognitive processes that undergird web page impression formation. Our thorough review of the literature has also indicated inconclusive evidence of the relative importance of classical versus expressive aesthetics, and one plausible reason is that most of these studies are atheoretical. For example, Tractinsky et al. (2006) find that impressions highly correlate with both classical and expressive aesthetics, whereas van Schaik and Ling (2009) find that classically aesthetic pages are rated as more attractive than expressively aesthetic pages.

To unravel the cognitive processes of web page impression formation and its subsequent effect in a rigorous manner, we adopt the theory of impression formation (Fiske and Neuberg 1990, Leder et al. 2004) to build a continuum model of web page impression formation. In line with previous research, we propose the following process sequence. First, perceivers immediately form initial perceptions of the target web page based on its visual aesthetics (Bar et al. 2006, Lindgaard et al. 2006). Next, if perceivers have extra time, then they will adopt bottom-up processing by attending to aesthetic features to confirm or slightly modify the initial perceptions, and these perceptions will become web page impressions. Following cue utilization theory (Easterbrook 1959, Sharot and Phelps 2004), we posit that biological arousal and attentional responses will govern this confirmatory process. However, if perceivers do not have adequate time to engage in further processing, then initial perceptions will become web page impressions directly (Fiske and Neuberg 1990). Lastly, web page impressions will drive the subsequent approach-avoidance tendency toward the web page (i.e., staying and exploring it deeper or leaving it) when perceivers encounter with the web page again (Pieters and Wedel 2007, Teixeira et al. 2012).

We conduct two studies to test our predictions with a combination of self-reported and eye movement data. Visual information acquisition is fast and governed by nonretrospective processes during web page im pression formation (Sheng et al. 2013, Shi et al. 2013). As such, we complement the traditional self-reported method with the eye-tracking method, which is partic ularly suited to automatically and accurately capture human attention and arousal data. We measure fixations and duration to assess human attention (Wedel and Pieters 2008) and pupil size to assess arousal (Bradley et al. 2008). Through a rigorous theory-based assessment that uses both objective and perceptual data, our study develops an initial HCI theory of web page impression formation from a design aesthetics perspective and provides important implications for website designers.

## 2. Literature Review 2.1. Web Page Impression

When encountering someone for the first time, we are usually quick to judge whether we like that person or not (Bar et al. 2006). Such an impression at first glance is formed based on the characteristics of others from single, simple, and static samples of their appearance (Olivola and Todorov 2010). Similarly, users form impressions of a new web page via an instant assessment of visual aesthetics at first glance (Lindgaard et al. 2006). The impression of a web page is formed fast and effortlessly, and can help a user to decide whether to stay at this page or switch to other sites (Tuch et al. 2012a). Over the past decade, a growing body of research in IS and HCI has been conducted to understand the nature of web page impression and its antecedents. Our review identifies four main findings. First, users can recognize the visual aesthetical difference and form web page impressions very quickly, within short durations ranging from 39 to 50 ms (Lindgaard et al. 2006, Lindgaard et al. 2008). Second, web page impressions are consistent across various exposure time durations. Immediate evaluations of new web pages within different exposure time durations, such as 50 ms, 1s, 3s, 7 s, and 10 s, are highly correlated with each other (Lindgaard et al. 2006, Tractinsky et al. 2006, Lindgaard et al. 2008) and the stability of the impression increases with longer exposure time (Bar et al. 2006, Tuch et al. 2012a). Third, web page impressions formed within different exposure periods also correlate highly with the overal evaluation when there is no time limit, indicating that web page impressions are lasting (Tractinsky et al. 2006). Last, visual aesthetic features in the form of classical and expressive aesthetics play an important role in determining web page impressions (Tractinsky et al. 2006, Michailidou et al. 2008, van Schaik and Ling 2009).

Although prior studies have offered some important insights into web page impression formation, there are still some limitations. First, only simple and direct associations between design characteristics and web page impressions have been examined. The human cognitive and psychological processes underlying web page impression formation are still unknown. Second, studies on the relative importance of classical versus expressive aesthetics have yielded inconsistent results (Tractinsky et al. 2006, van Schaik and Ling 2009). In addition, some findings in prior studies may be superficial and incomplete, partly because of the deficiencies in research methods. For example, much of the research used one item (i.e., perceived attractiveness/ beauty) to measure web page impression, even though it has been defined as an opinion of a web page that involves perceived attractiveness as well as attitude (Lindgaard et al. 2006, van Schaik and Ling 2009). Also, several studies lacked rigor by using only ordinary correlation or regression analysis to analyze within-subject data (e.g., Lindgaard et al. 2006, Tuch et al. 2012b).

Our research departs from prior studies in two aspects. Theoretically, drawing on the impression formation literature, we propose a theoretical model to unveil the human cognitive states, including arousal and attention, during web page impression formation. The different cognitive paths from classical and expressive aesthetics to web page impression suggested by our model may reconcile the inconsistent findings in previous literature. Methodologically, consistent with prior literature, we adopt a within-subject experiment design because visual aesthetics is usually operationalized from an interactionist perspective that regards aesthetic assessment as a subjective experience. However, we employ the eye-tracking method to capture users’ cognitive states objectively and automatically. Using both multi-item subjective measurements and objective eye-tracking data, we are able to capture users’ impressions of new web pages comprehensively. We also go beyond simple correlational analyses by adopting multilevel structural equation modeling (SEM) and repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). All of these methods should increase the robustness and validity of our findings.

## 2.2. Impression Formation Literature

In order to theoretically examine how visual aesthetical designs affect web page impression and the subsequent influence of web page impression on exploration decision, we consult the impression formation literature to grasp the process by which various information about a subject or object stimulus that people encounter for the first time is integrated to form a global impression of that stimulus.

Social psychology theorists maintain that people would generally take two approaches to form impressions of other people: category- or stereotype-based and attribute- or element-based processes (Fiske and Pavelchak 1986, Kunda and Thagard 1996). From the categorical perspective, people make sense of other people primarily based on stereotypes of various social categories such as gender, age, race, and physical appearances that are usually easy to observe visually. The attribute perspective assumes that people make sense of other people by focusing on their particular individuating characteristics such as behavior and per sonality. Fiske and Neuberg (1990) synthesize these two approaches into a continuum model by proposing that people process stereotypes and individuating information serially.<sup>1</sup> People first engage in the stereotype-based process to generate an initial categorization of the target person automatically. They then engage in more attribute-based processes if they have adequate time and perceive that the target person is relevant. Specifically, they allocate their attention to additional information to confirm the initial categorization. If this is successful, then people’s impressions of the target person will be based on the initial categorization. However, if the additional information is inconsistent with the initial categorization, then recategorization occurs. People need to engage in deeper analysis and take account of all the attributes of the target person to form impressions.

In addition to human subjects, theorists (e.g., Leder et al. 2004, Jacobsen 2006) also investigate the impression formation process of art objects. The consecutive model of aesthetic appreciation by Leder et al. (2004) summarizes a variety of findings on how complexity, symmetry, style, familiarity, and expertise, among other factors, influence the aesthetic experience of art. The model comprises five main processing stages: perceptual analyses, memory integration, explicit classification, cognitive mastering, and final evaluation. The first three stages are intuitive and comprise basic cognitive processes by which perceivers analyze aesthetic features such as order, symmetry, prototypicality, style, and others. These processes are quite fast and unconscious, leading to an initial perception (Jacobsen and Hofel¨ 2002, Augustin et al. 2008). In the following stage of cognitive mastering, perceivers experience a deeper aesthetic evaluation in a bottom-up manner (Leder and Nadal 2014). With several cycles of feedback and feedforward devoted to perception–cognition–emotion interactions, perceivers form the final aesthetic judgment

Although the two models account for impression formation toward different targets (human and artwork), they share some key assumptions. Both models assume a serial process that individuals first engage in fast and automatic early processing, followed by more attribute-based (Fiske and Pavelchak 1986) or bottomup processing (Leder and Nadal 2014). Both believe that the serial flow of information processing is time sensitive. Fiske and Neuberg (1990) indicate that sufficient resources, such as time and cognitive resources, are the important conditions determining whether perceivers will pay attention to detailed attribute information. Leder and Nadal (2014) suggest that perceptual analysis can be quickly completed, whereas the aesthetic process combining artspecific and self-related interpretation needs a longer period. Both also recognize that after the fast early processing, perceivers will generate an initial perception or categorization of a stimulus, which has a significant lasting impact on the final impression. This initial perception is primarily based on various visual variables that can be easily observed with little effort (e.g., physical appearance, age, and gender of a human; order, color, and symmetry of an artwork).

Users usually form impressions of a new web page based on its visual design (Bar et al. 2006, Tuch et al. 2012a). Although the two models have been developed for social interactions (Fiske and Neuberg 1990) and art experiences (Leder et al. 2004), respectively, their shared key assumptions can provide important insights to understand the process of web page impression formation.

## 3. Theoretical Model and Hypotheses

Numerous studies have been devoted to examining the impact of visual aesthetics on web page impression formation (e.g., Tractinsky et al. 2006, van Schaik and Ling 2009). Synthesizing these findings with the two impression formation models just discussed (Fiske and Neuberg 1990, Leder et al. 2004), we formulate a theoretical model accounting for the effects of web page classical and expressive aesthetics on web page impression and the subsequent influence of web page impression, as shown in Figure 1. This model includes a temporal sequence of processing stages. First, we propose that when an individual encounters a new web page, he/she will generate an initial perception of the web page based on its classical and expressive aesthetics. This initial perception is formed immediately and automatically upon the onset of the target web page. Then, if the individual has adequate time, he/she will proceed to a confirmatory bottom-up visual processing stage. Specifically, the individual will allocate attention to specific web page design features to confirm and adjust the initial perception, which will, in turn, form the impression of the web page. However, if time is limited, then the initial perception will sustain and become the impression of the web page. We also propose that visual processing of the target web page during the confirmation process would follow the proposition from cue utilization theory, which suggests that arousing features can attract attention at the expense of a narrowing attention scope (Easterbrook 1959). Lastly, we propose that when the individual encounters the target web page again, the formed impression during the initial exposure carries over to affect his/her approach-avoidance tendency toward the web page, leading to the decision whether to stay with and explore the web page or leave it (Deng and Poole 2010). We illustrate the details of the research model below.

## 3.1. Direct Effects of Visual Aesthetics on Impression Formation

Lavie and Tractinsky (2004) identify classical aesthetics and expressive aesthetics as the major visual aesthetics that can influence impression formation. Classical aesthetics emphasizes the clear, clean, and orderly design of a web page (e.g., grouping similar content and logical layout) (Deng and Poole 2010), whereas expressive aesthetics reflects how inspiring the web page design is (e.g., interesting and unusual layout or artifacts that may excite users) (Lavie and Tractinsky 2004). These two dimensions of visual aesthetics have been heavily researched in the website design area and found to be the prominent visual features driving automatic emotional reactions (e.g., arousal and pleasure) and preferences (Tractinsky and Lowengart 2007, Deng and Poole 2010, Cai and Xu 2011). One key shared assumption of the Fiske and Neuberg (1990) and Leder et al. (2004) models is that people can quickly form an impression upon the appearance of a visual stimulus. Empirical studies have shown that the processing of visual aesthetics i fast and automatic. People can detect whether a stimulus is aesthetic appealing or not even within 50 ms (Lindgaard et al. 2006, Tuch et al. 2012a). Neuroscience research has also revealed that processing geometric patterns such as symmetry and complexity, associated with anterior front median brain activities, occurs around 300 ms after stimulus onset (Jacobsen and Hofel¨ 2002). In the context of web page evaluation, people can perceive the different levels of visual complexity even within a presentation time as short as 17 ms (Tuch et al. 2012a). Thus, people can develop impressions toward a new web page based on its classical and expressive aesthetics im mediately and perhaps even unconsciously (Bar et al 2006). Numerous studies have consistently shown that intuitive, orderly, and understandable web pages can increase perceived control and enjoyment on the one hand, and expressive and decorative design can enhance the perceived hedonic quality of a web page on the other (Deng and Poole 2010, Cai and Xu 2011, Zhang 2013). Therefore, we propose the following direct effects of web page aesthetics on web page impression.

## 3.2. Indirect Effects of Visual Aesthetics on Impression Formation Through Arousal and Attention

Both the Fiske and Neuberg (1990) and Leder et al. (2004) models assume that people first engage in automatic processing of a visual stimulus and then a confirmation process by which they adopt bottom-up processing to allocate attention to detailed features of the stimulus if time permits. Jacobsen and Hofel¨ (2002) find that after the initial processing occurring around 300 ms after the onset of a stimulus, a deeper aesthetic evaluation associated with the brain’s right hemisphere activities begins at around 600 ms. In the context of web page evaluation, Tuch et al. (2012a) find that visual complexity can significantly influence aesthetic perception even within 17 ms, whereas the effects of other design features (e.g., prototypicality) would become as influential as visual complexity when presentation time increases. Therefore, in our model, we propose that, after the automatic processing of classical and expressive aesthetics, people will allocate their attention to the detailed aesthetical features to confirm their initial perceptions formed during the earlier automatic processing if they have more time. However, both the Fiske and Neuberg (1990) and Leder et al. (2004) models do not reveal how attention is allocated. We adopt cue utilization theory to explore how bottom-up visual processing is performed and its consequences.

Cue utilization theory (Easterbrook 1959) posits that, during the processing of visual stimuli, arousal reduces the range of cues that an individual would process, resulting in a narrowing attention scope but a sharpening of attention on the arousing attributes. In other words, arousal can affect the two dimensions of attention allocation—scope and duration—differently. Drawing on this theory, some studies have offered significant insights regarding the importance of arousal and attention in human reactions to environmental stimuli for a variety of tasks, such as memory, performance, and product evaluation (e.g., Bacon 1974, Gruber and Block 2003). Recent advances in eye-tracking technology have provided more reliable records of eye movements and have led to a better understanding of how visual cues capture attention (Herendy 2009, Dimoka et al. 2012, Sheng et al. 2013). Specifically, pupil size is a key element in arousal and can thus serve as a measure to study arousal (Partala and Surakka 2003, Bradley et al. 2008). Fixation duration and fixation count (or the number of fixations) are regarded as two main operationalizations of attention (Cian et al. 2014). Relating these visual parameters to cue utilization theory, we can expect that an increased pupil size, which reflects heightened arousal, will be associated with a declined fixation count and/or prolonged dwell time on the arousing stimuli, which would in turn influence information processing (Easterbrook 1959, Sharot and Phelps 2004).

We assert that the two dimensions of visual aesthetics will affect arousal differently. The theory of curiosity and exploration posits that the complexity, novelty, uncertainty, and conflicting nature of a stimulus can potentially affect arousal (Berlyne 1960). A high level of classical aesthetics can reduce uncertainty and bring orderliness and clarity to the environment, leading people to comprehend the environment effortlessly (Kaplan and Kaplan 1982). Thus, a result of a high level of web page classical aesthetics is that web users can browse the website fluently with less activation of energy allocation and intensity. Therefore, classical aesthetics should lead to low arousal. Expressive aesthetics, in contrast, has been consistently related to arousal positively. A web page with a high level of expressive aesthetics has an inspiring and original design (Zeng et al. 2009). Such a design is unusual and interesting to the extent that users would spend relatively more energy and time to view and comprehend it. Prior studies have shown that an inspiring, novel, and unusual design may cause surprise and curiosity that would lead to higher arousal (Deng and Poole 2010, Cai and Xu 2011).

In addition, the cue utilization theory suggests that arousal can influence visual attention by increasing the fixation time on the arousing stimuli while narrowing the attention scope (Easterbrook 1959). When browsing a new web page, users will allocate more attention to the visual stimuli that surprise or inspire them (e.g., an interesting artifact or a colorful component). The heightened arousal will delay the disengagement of attention (Fox et al. 2001), resulting in a longer peak duration, which is defined as the longest fixation duration on certain parts of a web page.<sup>2</sup> In contrast, the fixation count, which refers to the total number of fixations on a certain web page, should be negatively associated with arousal. With a low level of arousal, one’s attention will be less constrained and more freely dispersed to any part of a web page, which will result in a higher number of fixations. It is worth noting that, according to the Fiske and Neuberg (1990) and Leder et al. (2004) models, one necessary condition for the effects of arousal on visual processing to emerge is that users must have adequate time to experience the attention allocation process. When the exposure time lengthens, users can adopt bottom-up processing to selectively allocate attention to detailed design features to confirm the initial perceptions induced by automatic and instant processing In contrast, with limited exposure time, the confirmation procedure with selective attention allocation would be hardly executed by users and initial perceptions generated during the automatic processing will sustain. Hence, we hypothesize the following

Hypothesis 2. With adequate exposure time, (a) the level of web page classical aesthetics negatively influences the users arousal and (b) the level of web page expressive aesthetics positively influences the users’ arousal.

Hypothesis 3. With adequate exposure time, the users arousal (a) positively influences the peak duration but (b) negatively influences the fixation count on a web page

## 3.3. The Effects of Attention on Web Page Impression

Attention is a form of human perception and cognition in response to the immediate physical environment and a critical predictor of the evaluation of external stimuli (Rahinel and Ahluwalia 2015). In the context of visual information processing, marketing literature defines engagement as the maintenance of one’s attention on an object, with more allocated attention indicating higher engagement with the object (e.g., Pieters and Wedel 2007, Cian et al. 2014). Indeed, attention or feeling of engagement has been shown to affect persuasion and attitude positively (Lee and Labroo 2004).

In this research, we propose that peak duration and fixation count both can positively influence web page impression. Longer peak duration and a greater number of fixations represent heightened engagement, which will in turn bolster positive web page impression. Longer peak duration suggests that observers are highly involved with certain fixation locations on which their gazes are focused (Teixeira et al. 2012). In picture and scene viewing, some studies have demonstrated that gaze is attracted by “informative areas” of a picture (e.g., Antes 1974). These areas are the interesting ones that impress observers most and attract longer fixations (Loftus and Mackworth 1978), which are found to be associated with better recall and recognition of the overall pictures (Tversky 1974). As an impression is formed very quickly, a minor difference of a part of the target could change the final impression (Howlett et al. 2013). We expect that the association between visual fixation duration and web page impression also exists when people are exposed to new web pages. When users browse a new web page, their visual attention will be attracted to the most interesting area and they will then fixate on it (Cyr et al. 2009, Sheng et al. 2013). Longer peak duration means that this web page contains an area that engages users more and prevents them from switching their attention. The heightened attentional engagement in a certain informative and interesting segment of a web page can induce more positive impressions of the web page.

Christianson et al. (1991) note that the memory of a scene is related to the number of fixations made on it, with higher fixation count representing a higher recognition score. Marketing literature also shows that a greater number of fixations means that an advertisement has more attention-grabbing details that engage consumers, which in turn predicts attitude and persuasion (Pieters and Wedel 2007, Teixeira et al. 2012, Cian et al. 2014). Similarly, in the context of web page impression formation, more fixations reflect that the web page has more attractions that can capture users’ attention and impress them more positively (Pieters and Wedel 2007). Hence, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 4. With adequate exposure time, (a) peak duration and (b) fixation count are positively associated with the users’ positive impressions of a web page.

## 3.4. The Effect of Web Page Impression on Approach-Avoidance Tendency

We also explore whether web page impression has a lasting effect on users’ subsequent intentions and behaviors. According to the notion of “feelings as information” (Schwarz 1990), people tend to approach the stimulus/environment that elicits positive and pleasant feelings but avoid places that induce negative and unpleasant feelings. To the extent that individuals prefer positive outcomes over negative ones, a feeling of positive impression suggests that the current situation is safe and credible, which will in turn induce approach actions promoting further interactions and explorations. Conversely, a feeling of negative impression induces avoidance behaviors, driving people to stay away from the stimuli. There has been consistent evidence that the impression of a web page is a significant predictor of perceived credibility and trustworthiness (Robins and Holmes 2008, Lindgaard et al 2011), which are the necessary conditions for users to approach the web page further. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 5. Users’ positive impressions elicited by a web page’s aesthetic design features positively influence their approach tendency toward the web page.

## 4. Overview of Studies

We conducted two studies with the eye-tracking method to test our hypotheses. Study 1 serves two purposes. First, it attempts to provide preliminary support for the propositions that visual aesthetics would influence web page impression directly as well as indirectly via arousal and attention under the condition of adequate exposure time. Second, it seek to determine the web page exposure time length under which users would experience the attention allocation process to confirm their initial perceptions. Study 2 seeks to replicate the findings of Study 1 using a set of controlled web page stimuli as well as provide evidence on the effect of web page impression on approach-avoidance tendency.

## 5. Study 1: Examining the Web Page Impression Formation Process Using Real-World Web Pages

Study 1 aims to provide preliminary support for the web page impression formation process in the adequate exposure time condition. We used real-world Chinese e-commerce web pages as the stimuli. We assessed classical aesthetics, expressive aesthetics, and web page impression with self-reported scales. Participants’ arousal and attention were captured by the eye-tracking method.

## 5.1. Stimuli

We had two criteria in selecting real-world web page stimuli. First, they should vary in terms of web page expressive and classical aesthetics. Second, they should appear as new to participants to ensure that only impressions at first glance were evoked. We conducted a pilot study to select the appropriate web page set as the experimental stimuli. As a result, 24 real-world web pages with different levels of classical and expressive aesthetics were selected. Online Appendix A describes the selection and validation process.

## 5.2. Procedure

We recruited students from a large university in China as participants in the main study. Participants who took part in the pilot study, students from designrelated majors, and students with self-declared visual impairments (e.g., color blindness) were excluded. All the participants have had at least three to four years of Internet experience and online shopping experience of three to five times per month. Sixty-five qualified participants attended our experiment in exchange for 25 yuan (RMB) for their time and effort. Because of equipment errors, six participants’ eye movement data were not completely captured. After removing the data for these six participants, we obtained 1,416 (24 × 59) valid observations from 59 participants.

Before participants entered the eye-tracking laboratory, we explained to them (1) the purpose of the study, which was to provide feedback to merchants on the quality of their websites; (2) the evaluation process; and (3) the working of the eye-tracking apparatus (i.e., EyeLink 1000 plus). After indicating their participation consent, participants were directed to the research laboratory. They first learned how to operate the equipment. We adjusted the eye-tracker equipment in order to calibrate their eye foci. The distance between the monitor and the participants’ eyes was about 70 centimeters. To reduce the effect of head motion on the accuracy of the eye-tracking system, we asked participants to put their chins on the chin rest, and we adjusted the chin rest to keep their eyes as high as the central 2/3 position of the screen. We only started when the participants fully understood the procedure and their eye foci were calibrated successfully. During the study, the 24 web page screenshots were consecutively shown to participants in a random sequence. Before exposing each web page stimulus, a guidance sentence was presented on the screen followed by a crosshair (“+”) at the center of the screen to ensure that all the participants viewed the screen at the same starting point. Following the practice of previous studies (Lindgaard et al. 2006, Kim and Fesenmaier 2008), we presented each web page to the participants for three seconds.<sup>3</sup> The participants were then asked to complete an online questionnaire measuring web page impression. This process iterated until each participant finished viewing and evaluating all of the 24 web pages. Participants then had some time (the time length depended on the subject’s need) to close their eyes to rest to avoid fatigue. After resting, the iteration process repeated, whereby after the presentation of each web page, the participants were asked to answer the intermixed questions measuring both aesthetics constructs. The whole study took about 40 minutes.

## 5.3. Measures

Visual aesthetics and impressions are subjective experiences (Lindgaard et al. 2006, Moshagen and Thielsch 2010). Thus, the self-report method was used to measure classical aesthetics, expressive aesthetics, and web page impression. The measures were adapted from existing valid and reliable instruments. Adapting from past studies (e.g., Lavie and Tractinsky 2004, Lindgaard et al. 2006, Tuch et al. 2012b), we created four items each for both the classical and expressive aesthetics constructs and three items for the valence of web page impression construct, as shown in Online Appendix B. The questionnaire was created in English and reviewed for content validity by a group of three faculty members. As our study was conducted in the Chinese context, we invited two graduate students to independently translate the original items into Chinese and then formed a consensus on the final version for the questionnaire. Then, the Chinese items were translated back into English by another professional translator to confirm translation equivalence. We conducted multilevel exploratory factor and confirmatory factor analyses to assess the validity and reliability of the measures of the valence of web page impression and classical and expressive aesthetics. Online Appendix C presents the correlation matrices of the indicators, factor loadings, and item reliability of our measurement instruments. The results indicated adequate reliability and validity.

As noted previously, users’ web page impressions are formed in a fraction of time. The eye-tracking method can automatically and accurately record their eye movements and thus is more suited to measure participants’ attention and arousal than the self-report method. Specifically, attention has two dimensions, including how often a web page draws the observer’s eyes (fixation count) and the time of the longest fixation on any part in the given web page (peak duration). The feeling of arousal was measured by the average pupil diameter (pupil size).

## 5.4. Results

Our study was a within-subject design in that every participant browsed all of the 24 web page stimuli. The nested nature of our data (i.e., observations nested within individuals) leads to the nonindependence of observations. Hence, traditional SEM is not suitable. Multilevel analysis, as implemented in Mplus version 7.0, was chosen instead. We used multilevel SEM (Qureshi and Fang 2011, Muthén and Muthén 2012, Ilies et al. 2017) with random coefficient models to test Hypotheses 1–4. To obtain a deeper understanding of the relationships between visual aesthetics, arousal, attention, and the valence of web page impression, we also used multilevel mediation model (Bauer et al. 2006) to test the mediation effects of arousal and attention. Before conducting these analyses, all the data were centered relative to each individual’s mean to remove the between-individual variance (i.e., groupmean centering; Enders and Tofighi 2007) such that the estimates represent pure within-individual relations. We proposed two important controls because of their potential confounding effects. First, humans may vary in search mode for different objects: sometimes they may tend to saccade or scan widely, or sometimes they may tend to observe only within specific ranges. Therefore, we examined the effect of average saccade amplitude, which is the average distance one’s eyes “travel” during the movement on the valence of web page impression. We found this effect positive and significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , indicating that those whose eyes habitually “traveled” a greater distance from one fixation to another had more positive web page impressions. Second, subjects could choose to move their eyes off the web page. Therefore, we also tested the effect of total duration time, which measured the total time one viewed the web page on the valence of web page impression. We found that the effect was positive but not significant (p = 0.52). More importantly, the inclusion or removal of these two controls relating to one’s distinct eye movement habits and total viewing time, respectively, would not alter our results in any substantial way. Thus, we only report the effects of our research variables thereafter.

## 5.5. Discussion

In Study 1, the data collected by eye-tracking and selfreport methods provided empirical support for our theoretical model. Specifically, we found that classical and expressive aesthetics influenced arousa in different directions—expressive aesthetics, which emphasizes design inspiration and originality, led to high arousal, whereas classical aesthetics, which emphasizes clarity and orderly design, resulted in low arousal. Consistent with cue utilization theory we found that arousal induced by visual aesthetics was positively related to peak duration but negatively related to fixation count. Finally, the results also showed that fixation count positively influenced people’s impressions of the web pages. However, the hypothesized positive link from peak duration to the valence of web page impression was not supported by the data. There are several possible explanations for this lack of empirical support. First, the web page stimuli were from the real world. The design elements associated with expressive aesthetics might be different; for instance, some web pages might use images, whereas some might use colors. This inconsistency might nullify the effect of peak duration. Second, real-world web pages differing in the number of web page elements might also weaken the effect of peak duration. These peculiarities of our research stimul might result in the insignificance of peak duration and thus suggest a further need for investigation. Therefore, in Study 2, we manipulated classical and expressive aesthetics while controlling for other possible confounding factors, such as number of design elements, product picture size, and text content. Besides fulfilling the objective to replicate Study 1, the direct manipulation of classical and expressive aesthetics in Study 2 would also allow us to explicate their effects on web page impression without the interferences caused by any uncontrolled web page design elements. Furthermore, the three-second time setting in Study 1 did not enable us to disentangle the initial perception formation through automatic and quick processing and the confirmation process involving visual attention allocation. Therefore, we varied exposure time in Study 2, aiming to demonstrate the stages that were involved in web page impression formation. Lastly, Study 2 would also reveal the effect of web page impression on approach tendency toward the web page.

## 6. Study 2: Manipulating Web Page Visual Aesthetics

Unlike in Study 1, which used real-world web pages, in Study 2 we conducted a factorial experiment by manipulating web page classical and expressive aesthetics. Study 2 seeks to examine the proposition that the confirmation process involving attention allocation would not occur when the exposure time is limited, as well as demonstrate the carryover effect of web page impression on approach tendency.

## 6.1. Design and Operation of Variables

A 2 (classical aesthetics: low vs. high) × 2 (expressive aesthetics: low vs. high) × 2 (exposure time: one second vs. three seconds) mixed experimental design was employed. The nature of the exposure time was manipulated as a between-subjects factor. As supported by Study 1, a period of three seconds was selected as the adequate exposure time condition, whereas a period of one second was selected as the limited exposure time condition. Previous studies have shown that within an extremely short exposure time (e.g., 50 ms or 500 ms), people can still form web page impressions (Lindgaard et al. 2006, Tuch et al. 2012a). However, Tuch et al. (2012a) suggest that impressions under an extremely short presentation time may primarily derive from the automatic processing of classical aesthetics, whereas expressive aesthetics can only become as influential as classical aesthetics when presentation time increases to one second. Therefore, to ensure that participants form web page impressions based on the processing of both classical and expressive aesthetics within a limited exposure time, we set one second as the experimental manipulation for the limited time condition.

Both classical and expressive aesthetics were withinsubject factors and manipulated based on previously validated operationalizations (Deng and Poole 2010, Cai and Xu 2011). Web page classical aesthetics was manipulated by varying the layout of web page elements. For a typical e-commerce web page, there are usually three important elements: heading, navigator, and product list. We designed a high level of classical aesthetics by using a logical organization of web page elements such that the heading was displayed at the top, the navigation pane was displayed on the left side, and six products were organized in an orderly manner beside the navigation pane. In contrast, a low level of classical aesthetics was designed by applying a free-form layout such that the navigation pane and six products cluttered on the screen without any sense of logical organization.

Grounded on past research, we manipulated web page expressive aesthetics by varying the background colors, table shading, and text font (Cai and Xu 2011, Kupor et al. 2018). Color is an intrinsic, universal, and context-independent aesthetic stimulus embodying meanings (Labrecque et al. 2013). Neuroscientists have speculated that human beings’ biological, emotional, and cognitive links to color are of synesthesia and present at birth (e.g., Collier 1996, Ward 2004).

In terms of aesthetical design, color has long been confirmed as the most significant and fundamental determinant of expressiveness and inspiration of objects and consequently could affect people’s perceptions and behaviors (Gorn et al. 2004, Elliot and Maier 2014). For example, many artists and designers have indicated that color combinations often motivate their creativities, and they use color to express their interesting thoughts (Roig 2006, Stewart 2008). Empirical studies on color psychology have demonstrated that color is a crucial component of expressive aesthetics to stimulate and attract humans in various design areas, including product packaging (Payne 1958, Hagtvedt and Brasel 2017), retail store atmospherics (Crowley 1993, Babin et al. 2003), brand logos (Bottomley and Doyle 2006), advertisements (Meyers-Levy and Peracchio 1995, Lohse and Rosen 2001), and consumer selfdesign (Deng et al. 2010). In the area of web page design, prior research has also supported that designs with appealing colors can significantly enhance the expressive aesthetics of a web page, as colors make a web page more visually stimulating and pleasing to viewers (Hall and Hanna 2004, Cai and Xu 2011). More importantly, a great body of empirical studies has also consistently shown that well-designed web page colors can positively influence affective and perceptual reactions toward the web page, including positive emotion, trust, satisfaction, and purchase intention (e.g., Gorn et al. 2004, Cyr et al. 2010, Cai and Xu 2011). A detailed literature review of the role of color in web page expressive aesthetics and its subsequent affective and perceptual influences is shown in Online Appendix D.

From the experimental methodology perspective, color is also a proper design feature to manipulate web page expressive aesthetics. First, manipulating the color, especially the web page background color, is relatively flexible and simple but has a powerful visual influence on users, as the color can occupy the whole computer screen (Stevenson et al. 2000). Second, compared with other potential expressive features (e.g., style, theme, and prototypicality) that may be affected by personal preferences and biases (Leder et al. 2004), effects of color are relatively consistent across different groups of people (Valdez and Mehrabian 1994, Elliot and Maier 2014). Third, the operationalization of other expressive features could potentially alter the classical aesthetical design. For instance, some creative designs may change the orderliness and balance of the web page and, consequently, undermine classical aesthetics. Fourth, varying color does not affect the amount of information on a web page, whereas manipulating other features (e.g., pictures and media) could influence the amount of information users would process and invite confounding variables irrelevant to our research and bias the results. Lastly, color is ubiquitous and important for web pages. For most web pages, such as news and e-commerce, whose design styles and layouts are often relatively stable, adopting different colors could be the primary and cost-effective way to alter web page expressivity. Thus, using color to enhance expressive aesthetics can be easily and widely implemented on most web pages in practice.

However, the color itself can affect human arousal levels. For example, green-yellow, blue-green, and green are the most arousing; purple-blue and yellowred are the least arousing; and blue, yellow, and purple are relatively neutral (Valdez and Mehrabian 1994). To control the effect of any single color, we built the web pages of high expressive aesthetics with a combination of colors, including high, neutral, and low arousing ones, each of which occupied the same amount of area on the web pages. In contrast, a nocolor background was adopted as the stimulus of low expressive aesthetics because it is less inspiring and appealing. Overall, different from web page classical aesthetics, which is related to a clear and orderly structure of a web page, web page expressive aesthetics conveys the inspiration and appeal of the web page. As such, our manipulation of web page expressive aesthetics by using a color-combination design, as shown in Figure 2, is deemed appropriate.

To control the confounding effect of product type and enhance generalizability, we adopted four different product categories (search products: laptop and USB drive; experiential products: clothes and shoes). For each product category, we created four versions of web pages based on our manipulations of classical and expressive aesthetics, and, in each version, the same six products were presented. These products were basic, ordinary items with no innovative design. Altogether, we had 16 web pages (4 aesthetics combinations × 4 product categories) as the experiment stimuli (examples of shoe web pages are shown in Figure 2).

## 6.2. Procedure and Measures

We recruited students from a large university in China as participants. Participants who took part in Study 1, students from design-related majors, and students with self-declared visual impairments (e.g., color blindness) were excluded. Seventy-six qualified participants attended our experiment and were compensated with 25 yuan (RMB) for their time and effort. Because of equipment errors, seven participants’ eye movement data were not completely captured. After removing the data for these seven participants, we obtained 1,104 (16 × 69) valid observations from 69 participants.

The eye-tracking equipment instruction and setting were identical to Study 1. After indicating their participation consent, participants viewed 16 web pages one by one with a presentation time of one second or three seconds and then reported their impressions for each web page. The 16 web pages were randomly presented to each participant. After viewing each web page, participants were asked to indicate the valence of their impressions of the web page quickly with three five-point items: Is it a nice web page? (1 = bad; 5 = good); Is this web page attractive? (1 = unattractive; 5 = attractive); Do you like this web page? (1 = not like; 5 = like). The rating scales were represented by five points on a line in ascending order from left to right. Upon completing all of the 16 web pages, participants could have some time, determined by themselves, to close their eyes and take a break. Then, they were presented with the 16 web pages one by one again. In this round, they had unlimited time to view each web page. In the questionnaire following each web page, participants answered four questions related to their approach tendency toward the web manipulation of classical and expressive aesthetics. Therefore, Hypothesis 5 was supported.

## 6.4. Endogeneity Test

We collected data for the attention and the valence of web page impression constructs by using different methods to mitigate potential endogeneity issues— that is, participants first formed web page impressions, and then the impressions guided their attention allocation. Real-time eye movement data were captured during the participants’ browsing process to measure attention, and the valence of web page impression was measured by self-reported data after the browsing process. This temporal sequence of collecting the attention and the impression data could address the endogeneity problem to some extent.

To further investigate the possible feedback loop between web page impression and attention, we conducted a series of multilevel SEM analyses with the attention parameters (i.e., fixation count and peak duration) as the dependent variables and the valence of web page impression as the independent variable. We did not observe any significant effects of the valence of web page impression on fixation count and peak duration in the one-second exposure condition (fixation count: $0 . 0 1 5 ; p = 0 . 1 9 ;$ ; peak duration: <sup>−</sup>0.013, p = 0.22), in the three-second exposure condition (fixation count: $0 . 0 2 1 , p = 0 . 3 8 ;$ ; peak duration: $0 . 0 4 , p = 0 . 1 2 )$ and when we combined the data of the two conditions (fixation count: $0 . 0 1 8 ; p = 0 . 1 5 ;$ peak duration: 0.008, $p = 0 . 5 5 6 )$ .<sup>6</sup> Thus, there was no statistical evidence to support the feedback loop between web page impression and attention.

Furthermore, according to our theoretical arguments, people form their impressions of a web page by both automatic and attentive processing (Fiske and Neuberg 1990). Specifically, they first use automatic processing and then engage in attention allocation activities. In Study 2, we included a three-second exposure time condition, by which users could have enough time to complete the attention allocation. If web page impressions are formed before any attention allocation activities and guide the attention allocation process, then they should predict the attention variables, including fixation count and peak duration. However, the results of our analyses rejected this possibility. Therefore, we believe that the reverse causality from web page impressions to attention is not a serious threat in this research.

## 6.5. Discussion

Study 2 extends Study 1 in two aspects: (1) we manipulated the web page classical and expressive aesthetics instead of using subjective measures and excluded the confounding effects of other design factors, such as the number of elements, pictures, and so on; (2) in addition to the exposure time of three seconds, we involved another exposure time condition of one second to demonstrate the necessary condition for observing the effects of arousal and attention during web page impression formation. Study 2 provides further evidence that users can automatically process web page classical and expressive aesthetics to form initial perceptions of a web page, and, with adequate exposure time, they engage in an attention allocation process to confirm their initial perceptions and then form their impressions of the web page. Study 2 also reveals the carryover effect of the impression of a web page by demonstrating that it mediates the effects of visual aesthetics on users’ final approach tendency toward the web page. Overall, Study 2 complements Study 1 by providing further support to our theoretical model of web page impression formation.

## 7. Conclusions

## 7.1. General Discussion

Drawing on the literature of impression formation, we propose a continuum model demonstrating how visual aesthetics may influence web page impression. The data collected by eye-tracking and self-report methods provided strong empirical support for our research model. The major findings of this research can be summarized as follows.

First, this research supported the hypotheses drawn from the impression formation literature that, immediately after the onset of a new environment stimulus, people would form an automatic initial perceptual response based on its visual appearance. We found that users could develop qualitatively distinct perceptions of web page classical and expressive aesthetics within a fraction of time (one second), both of which would positively affect their web page impressions.

Second, besides the automatic processing immediately after being exposed to a stimulus, users would engage in a bottom-up process by which they allocate their attention to confirm the initial perceptual responses if the exposure time is adequate (e.g., three seconds). Specifically, this confirmation process was consistent with the propositions suggested by cue utilization theory. Using one’s pupil size as the measurement of arousal and peak duration and fixation counts as the measurements of visual attention, we found that classical aesthetics could reduce arousal, whereas expressive aesthetics could enhance arousal, and the arousal induced by visual aesthetics was positively related to peak duration but negatively related to fixation count. In Study 2, by controlling for other possible confounding web page design features, we confirmed that both peak duration and fixation counts could positively influence web page impression.

Further, we also found the carryover effect of web page impression elicited by visual aesthetics on approach tendency toward the web page. A favorable web page impression is a positive feeling that informs users that the e-commerce environment is perceived as safe and secure (Schwarz 1990), and thus they are more willing to explore (approach) the target web page The supplementary analyses suggested a mediation effect of web page impression on the relationships between web page aesthetics and approach tendency.

Regarding the relative importance of classical versus expressive aesthetics to web page impression, our results suggest that classical aesthetics should be given priority. We found that with limited exposure time (one second), on a web page with a high level of classical aesthetics, the effect of expressive aesthetics became less significant This result indicated that, although both classical and expressive aesthetics could be processed automatically and fast, classical aesthetics might be processed first. This finding was consistent with the model of art appreciation, which suggests that organization features (e.g., order and symmetry) are processed at an earlier stage than expressive features (e.g., prototypicality and style). To some extent, this finding may echo previous arguments (Zhang and Von Dran 2000, Cai and Xu 2011) that classical aesthetics should be classified as a hygiene design factor that is usually considered as a necessity to ensure usefulness and ease of use, whereas expressive aesthetics may serve as a motivating factor with a value-adding function in the later stage. However, this concrete mechanism needs further investigation.

## 7.2. Limitation and Directions for Future Research

Some limitations should be noted when interpreting the results of our study. First, the participants that we recruited were college students, which may restrict the applicability of the results to other populations. However, according to the report from the China Internet Network Information Center (CNNIC 2014), students are the driving force of the development of e-commerce and online shopping in China. Because our experiment stimuli were either selected from or a simulation of real-world websites, the use of a student sample should not present a serious threat to the external validity of this study.

Second, our empirical experiments were a simulation of the online browsing environment. During the experiments, participants were not allowed to click on any links on the web page stimuli as they would do during real-world online shopping. This may weaken the influence of website aesthetic design features on their emotional responses. However, the focus of this study is on web page impression that is formed at first glance. We believe that, in such a short duration, visual stimuli should be the primary predictors of arousal and attention.

Although this research has investigated the effects of two general and high-level dimensions (i.e., classical and expressive aesthetics) of website visual design on web page impression formation, it should be of interest for future research to examine how classical and expressive aesthetics may influence web page impression at a later stage. For instance, researchers may investigate whether expressive aesthetics would show an incremental value over classical aesthetics when one spends a longer time on a website. In addition, future research is also needed to explore how other dimensions of aesthetics (e.g., complexity) would affect web page impressions (Moshagen and Thielsch 2010). In this study, we used color to manipulate expressive aesthetics, as color has been widely accepted as an important determinant of object expressivity. However, there are other design features (e.g., image and theme) that can elicit expressive aesthetics. Future research is thus encouraged to examine the effects of these features.

Lastly, this research has proposed that arousal and attention are two important cognitive processes that undergird web page impression formation, and we have used the eye-tracking method to measure them objectively. However, there might be other potential mediators that cannot be captured by this method. For example, Porat and Tractinsky (2012) note that emotional valence (e.g., pleasure) evoked by a web page’s aesthetic features can affect one’s attitude toward the web page. Although their locus of inquiry differs from ours of web page impression in a fraction of time, emotional valence could play a role in shaping one’s web page impression. Future research can employ neuroscience techniques (e.g., electroencephalogram and functional magnetic resonance imaging) to understand other cognitive processes in web page impression formation that cannot be observed by the eye-tracking method.

## 7.3. Implications for Theory and Practice

This research makes several important theoretical contributions. First, it contributes to the literature related to impression formation in the digital world by examining the cognitive processes underlying one’s web page impression formation. Although several studies have been conducted to examine various factors that can trigger web users to generate positive impressions of a new website (e.g., Jardina et al. 2012), very limited research has been conducted to investigate the underlying mechanisms. Our study advances this research area by explicitly proposing and testing a continuum model of web page impression formation. Study 1 showed that web page aesthetics influenced impressions both directly and indirectly through the mediation of arousal and attention when participants were exposed to the web pages for a relatively longer period of time (three seconds), and Study 2 decomposed the impression formation process through the design of varying participants’ exposure durations. It clearly demonstrated that the global perception of the gist of the web page occurred spontaneously and fast (observed in the one-second condition group), which was then followed by attentive visual processing (observed in the three-second condition group), which in turn affected web page impressions. Furthermore, this model predicts that users’ web page impressions can have a pronounced effect on their approach tendency toward the web page. More importantly, our results derive credibility and validity from the use of the eye-tracking method, which provides rich and objective eye movement data to measure attention and arousal, and the use of a multilevel SEM analysis method to assess within-subject effects.

The present research also offers a more nuanced understanding of the relationships between classical and expressive aesthetics and web page impression. Previous studies have produced inconsistent results regarding their relative importance, partly because much of the previous research has been atheoretical or used potentially problematic methods to analyze within-subject effects (e.g., Lindgaard et al. 2006, Tractinsky et al. 2006). Through a rigorous theoretical assessment, our results suggest that classical aesthetics should be given priority over expressive aesthetics. Although both classical and expressive aesthetics can be processed fast, classical aesthetics seems to be processed earlier than expressive aesthetics, especially when the presentation time is relatively short (i.e., one second in Study 2). Therefore, in terms of eliciting a more favorable web page impression, the design of classical aesthetics should be considered first.

Lastly, we extend existing theoretical models of impression formation developed for human objects (Fiske and Neuberg 1990) and modern arts (Leder et al. 2004) to the area of website design. We find that the web page impression formation process follows the basic assumptions of those models that people first rely on automatic processing and then use attentive processing of visual aesthetics to form web page impressions, which in turn have a carryover effect on the final approach behavior. Meanwhile, we involve cue utilization theory to understand the cognitive mechanisms of the attentive processing, which are missing from both impression formation and website design literature. We find that arousal shapes the attention allocation process such that arousal-enhancing website features (e.g., expressive aesthetics) increase one’s peak duration but decrease the number of fixations on a web page, whereas arousal-reducing website features (e.g., classical aesthetics) increase the number of fixations but decrease peak duration. Both peak duration and the number of fixations are positively related to positive web page impression. As such, this study enriches the theoretical basis of web design by integrating existing theoretical models of impression formation with cue utilization theory.

The results of this research will be of interest to web designers and managers. Our results suggest that the two dimensions of attention—peak duration and fixation count—are positively associated with web page impressions. Therefore, besides using traditional methods such as survey and clickstream analysis, web designers and managers can engage users in experiments before launching their web pages by using the eye-tracking method to capture their eye fixation data and learn about their impressions. The two sources of data can complement each other to better predict whether new web pages can impress users at first glance. Another important implication arising from our findings is related to arousal. Some studies have suggested that arousal may benefit web page evaluation to some degree (Deng and Poole 2010). Our results suggest that arousal may narrow one’s attention scope, which can negatively influence web page impression. Therefore, design elements that increase arousal should be used with caution. Lastly, some designers may still be struggling with whether they should emphasize classical aesthetics or expressive aesthetics on their new web pages. Our results tentatively suggest that classical aesthetics may offer a safer route to elicit better web page impressions, as users tend to process classical aesthetics ahead of expressive aesthetics at first glance. Specifically, before adding inspiring and expressive design elements, designers need to make sure that their web pages are designed with a clear and logical structure to allow users to browse fluently.

## 7.4. Concluding Comments

This research enhances our understanding of the cognitive processes that undergird web page impression formation. Combining the eye-tracking data and the perceptual data for analyses, we demonstrate the significant association between visual aesthetics and web page impression formation and underlying mechanisms associated with arousal and attention. This study offers three important insights. First, website designers and testers should recognize the importance of web page impression at first glance, as it can significantly influence the approach tendency toward a web page. Second, they should be cognizant that whether a web page can impress users positively depends on the arousal and attention induced by visual design elements under different time conditions. Third, they should attach importance to the balance between conservative and innovative designs to maximize their effects on web page impression formation.
