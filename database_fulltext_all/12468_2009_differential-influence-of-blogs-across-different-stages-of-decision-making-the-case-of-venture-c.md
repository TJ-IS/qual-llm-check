# DIFFERENTIAL INFLUENCE OF BLOGS ACROSS DIFFERENT STAGES OF DECISION MAKING: THE CASE OF VENTURE CAPITALISTS<sup>1</sup>

Rohit Aggarwal Operations and Information Systems, David Eccles School of Business, University of Utah, Salt Lake City, UT 84112 U.S.A. {Rohit.Aggarwal@business.utah.edu}

Harpreet Singh

Information Systems and Operations Management, Naveen Jindal School of Business, University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX 75080 U.S.A. {Harpreet@utdallas.edu}

In this paper, we study the differential influence of online user-generated content (UGC), specifically blogs, across the multiple stages of decision making of venture capitalists: screening stage, choice stage, and contract stage. We conjecture that, first, blogs are influential at the screening stage; second, after the screening stage, blogs are noninfluential since decision makers evaluate entities closely at later stages; third, blogs increase the interest from multiple decision makers which in turn increases the cost of the deal for a decision maker. This empirical investigation provides support for the hypotheses, which we tested for funding decisions by venture capitalists in information technology ventures. In particular, this study indicates that blogs can help managers in getting their products/services selected at the screening stage, but, beyond that, blogs do not help directly. However, since more decision makers screen products/services that receive blog coverage, the competition among decision makers helps managers in negotiating better contract terms. We advance the boundary of existing studies on the influence of UGC from single stage process to multiple stages.

Keywords: UGC, WOM, Blogs, IT ventures, VC funding, venture capital, econometric analysis, multistage decision making, screening stage, choice stage, contract stage

## Introduction

When a venture is covered by blogs, we [venture capitalists] presume there may be a pony in there somewhere, and consider the venture to be worthy of our time for a closer look. But whether my assessment of a venture gets influenced because some blogs write highly of it, I don’t think this happens (A venture capitalist on Forbes’ Midas Top 30 list for three consecutive years, 2009).

It was an early Tuesday morning in 2007. Three Dutch men wearing white business suits broke into a house where the resident was fast asleep. Hearing noises, he woke up, startled and confused. He soon learned that these three men were not burglars, but were instead super-enthusiastic entrepreneurs who wanted to pitch their venture to him. One would expect that the resident of the house was a rich private equity investor with millions of dollars at his disposal, but, in fact, he was simply a blogger (Vogelstein 2007). We relate this amusing story of the super-enthusiastic entrepreneurs to underscore the point that there is a widespread belief among entrepreneurs that online user-generated content (UGC), in particular blogs, can help startups acquire funding from private equity investors, specifically venture capitalists (VCs). During our interactions, VCs from many top firms (such as Sequoia Capital, Kleiner Perkins Caufield Byers, Accel Partners; see Appendix B for the list) confirm this viewpoint to varying degrees. In the quote opening this article, a wellreputed VC perceptively observes that blog coverage gravitates the attention of VCs toward a venture. However, the same VC also scoffs at any possible influence of blog coverage on outcome of his evaluation process after initial screening.

Out of a very large pool of ventures seeking financing, VCs first screen ventures based on heuristics and a subset of information. Ventures have cleared the first big hurdle toward receiving VC funding once VCs begin the evaluation process, referred to as the screening stage (Tyebjee and Bruno 1984). After the screening stage, VCs start their duediligence exercise, obtaining more information from entrepreneurs, possibly consulting experts, and finally forming their assessment about a venture. If VCs’ assessments about ventures are positive, they confirm their interest for financing. Receiving a confirmation from a VC is considered passing the choice stage (Tyebjee and Bruno 1984). Next, VCs discuss terms of financing with entrepreneurs, and finalize the termsheet of financing. This deal structuring stage is referred to as the contract stage (Tyebjee and Bruno 1984).

In this study, we investigate the differential effect of online UGC, specifically blogs, across multiple stages of decision making. We theoretically developed hypotheses using the well-established conceptual basis of the elaboration likelihood model (Cacioppo and Petty 1984). In particular, we investigate the following questions: Does blog coverage affect the probability that an entity will pass the screening stage? Once an entity passes the screening stage, does blog coverage affect its probability of passing the choice stage? Once an entity passes the choice stage, does blog coverage affect the negotiated terms at the contract stage?

This study primarily contributes to the growing body of literature on UGC by examining the differential influence of blog coverage on the multiple stages of decision making. Prior studies that investigated the influence of UGC on decision making implicitly assumed decision making to be a single stage process (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006; Dellarocas et al. 2008; Forman et al. 2008; Godes and Mayzlin 2004). The assumption that decision making is a single stage process may be problematic because ignoring the intermediate stages of decision making can lead to biased parameters (Andrews and Manrai 1998; Shocker et al. 1991). In addition to biased estimates, another problem mentioned in the literature is that the diagnostic information generated by the multistage model may contain managerial insights not present in the diagnostic information offered by single-stage models (Gensch 1987). Analysis based on single-stage decision making fails to distinguish the scenario where the UGC affects all stages of decision making uniformly from the scenario where the UGC affects only one of the multiple stages. Such a distinction is imperative to understand the underlying phenomenon of UGC and to recognize scenarios where UGC will be effective and, more importantly, where it will not. To the best of our knowledge, previous studies that investigated the effect of UGC considered decision making as a single stage process, and thus may have the aforementioned limitations.

We also contribute to the literature by theoretically developing and empirically validating that in the contract stage, UGC indirectly affects the contract terms. Interest from multiple decision makers is an important piece of information to consider at the contract stage because it influences the leverage enjoyed by entities in negotiating terms. Without accounting for the interests of multiple decision makers in an entity, one may wrongly construe that the UGC directly affects the terms at the contract stage.

The venture-funding context investigated in this study is of significant importance. Eleven percent of private sector jobs come from venture-backed companies, and venture-backed revenue accounts for 21 percent of U.S. gross domestic product (NVCA 2011b). The venture capital domain is a large industry (NVCA 2011a), roughly equal to the combined size of three widely researched industry domains—online books (BookStats 2011), box-office (MPAA 2011), and music (Friedlander 2011). The venture funding context investigated is also distinct from those investigated by prior studies such as the choice of TV shows and the sale of books and movies, thus subjecting the effect of UGC to more comprehensive testing. Also, the extant research investigating UGC’s influence on decision making typically has not controlled for media coverage. This is an important distinction because without controlling for media coverage, UGC may capture the effect of media coverage. Our results suggest that UGC, specifically blogs, matters even after controlling for media coverage.

Prior work investigating the venture capital industry has largely relied on online databases such as VentureXpert (Dimo and De Clercq 2006; Dimov and Shepherd 2005; Dushnitsky and Lenox 2006; Gompers and Lerner 2000). The limitations of such databases and of prior studies in general are that they do not observe ventures that initially requested funding from VCs but were turned down, nor do they observe ventures that were invited to VCs’ offices for presentations, nor do they report ventures that received term sheets. To the best of our knowledge, prior studies only observe ventures that finally received funding from VCs, and thereby extant studies cannot examine the process of VC decision making that leads to the final funding. Because of its fine granularlevel data, this study is in a unique position to examine the differential influence of blogs on multiple stages of decision making.

The key findings of our econometric analysis are that the volume of blog coverage significantly increases the probability that an entity may pass the screening stage. The volume of blog coverage does not affect the probability that an entity may pass the choice stage. Interestingly, we also find that blog coverage does not directly affect the contract stage; rather, it indirectly affects the contract stage. The volume of UGC increases the number of decision makers interested in an entity, and this interest from multiple decision makers increases the leverage of an entity in negotiations. Due to this increased negotiating power, an entity receives better contract terms. In other words, the number of interested decision makers completely mediates the relation between the volume of blog coverage and the contract stage. We use numerous approaches to control for VC and venture-specific unobserved effects, and compare the reliability of the results reported across various models. Specifically, we report various fixedeffects models, random-effects models, and instrumentvariable models. All models produce qualitatively similar results, which corroborates the robustness of the results.

To sum up, this study proposes a new paradigm for investigating the effect of blog coverage, and suggests that past studies can be enriched if seen through the lens of multistage decision making.

## Literature Review

This paper relates to several strands of literature. First, the paper builds on the growing stream of literature on blogs in particular, and user generated content (UGC) in general, and extends the literature in several ways. Second, this paper complements the extant work investigating the influence of UGC on decision making. The representative literature in the area of UGC is reviewed, and a discussion of how the current study adds to the existing literature is provided. Third, because multiple stages of decision making are a fundamental aspect of the current study, relevant literature in this strand of work is discussed. Last but not the least, the body of work in the domain of new venture funding is discussed with special focus on the role of conventional media in funding decisions. Characteristics that distinguish blogs from conventional media are also provided.

## Literature on Blogs

Initial studies on blogs explored the motives of individuals writing blogs. Nardi et al. (2004) conducted exploratory indepth interviews to understand the motives of people blogging, and found that users have an innate desire to document events happening in their lives, express their opinions, share their ideas with others, articulate through writing, and contribute to the community. Wattal, Racherla, and Mandviwalla (2010) examined the influence of network externalities on the use of computing technologies, particularly blogs, in an organization. Based on the theory of network externality, they argued and empirically verified that the blog usage of a given employee increases with the blog usage increase of other employees who are in the network of the given employee.

Using three cases of Fortune 100 companies, Culnan et al. (2010) illustrated the challenges faced by companies in implementing social media applications such as blogs to interact with customers, and suggested some sources of value due to such applications. They also identified three factors— mindful adoption, community building, and absorptive capacity— that are important in effective implementation of social media. Aggarwal et al. (2011) investigated employee blogs and found that with an increase in negative posts, readership increases exponentially up to a certain level of negative posts, and then stabilizes beyond that point. They draw on attribution theory from research in social psychology to present an intuitive explanation of why letting an employee write negative posts may result in increased credibility for that employee’s blog, and why that could translate to increased readership.

In a similar vein, a study by Singh et al. (2010) investigates the dynamics of the blog reading behavior of employees in company-hosted blogs. Using the theory of Markov process, Singh et al. modeled the transition between different dynamic states of reading blogs. Their empirical model showed that employees demonstrate a variety-seeking blog reading behavior that is induced by various textual and reader attributes. Huang et al. (2010) studied the complementary side of the study by Singh et al. Instead of focusing on the employee’s reading behavior, they formulated the decisions of an employee—when to write or read blog, what to write or read (work-related or leisure-related), and how much to write or read—as a dynamic completion game in a dynamic structural model. They found that employees find more utility from reading work-related posts, and that low-status employees find more utility from writing posts than those of high-status employees.

Wattal, Schuff, Mandviwalla, and Williams (2010) investigate another interesting domain that is very different from the domains examined in aforementioned studies. They contrasted the influence of Web 2.0 (blogs, Youtube, Myspace) and traditional mass media (TV, radio, newspapers )on the performance of U.S. presidential candidates in the 2008 election. Their empirical investigation found that only the influence of blogs was significant, while the influence of traditional mass media was not significant. They attributed this surprising result to the journalism bias that goes into the reporting of news, and the dogma of the prevailing wisdom.

## Literature on the Influence of UGC on Decision Making

Extant studies investigating the effect of UGC make an important contribution and established that UGC influences decision making across a variety of domains. However, to the best of our knowledge these studies fall short in explaining the process by which UGC affects decision making. With the exception of this study, no other study has collected such finegrained, multistage, deal flow data from VCs. Because of the novel dataset collected, this study is in a unique position to examine the differential influence of blogs on multiple stages leading to final decision making. Some representative work in UGC is reviewed next.

In one of the earliest and most highly cited works, Godes and Mayzlin (2004) used data from online discussion forums about new television shows during the 1999–2000 season to demonstrate that online conversations can be used to measure the UGC. Besides volume, researchers focused on dispersion of conversations happening in various communities of Usenet, and found that higher dispersion was associated with higher future ratings. They did not find any significant association between the volume and future show ratings, and they attributed this to the failure to control for the valence of discussions because the positive volume may have offset the negative volume.

Antweiler and Frank (2004) collected messages from online stock message boards—Yahoo! Finance, and Raging Bull— and found that the volume of messages can predict subsequent trading volume, subsequent stock returns, and stock volatility. Another study in the financial market domain investigates the association between eWOM and final financing of a venture using data primarily from VentureXpert (Aggarwal et al. 2012). Since VentureXpert does not provide the VC deal flow data, the scope of this research, like other studies, was confined to examining the influence of UGC on final decision making. In a related study, Bollen et al. (2011) investigate the association between the sentiments of people as reflected on Twitter with stock price movement on the stock market. Their results indicate that the closing accuracy of the Dow Jones Industrial Average prediction could be improved after accounting for the sentiments reflected on Twitter.

Another highly explored domain for the effect of UGC is online book purchases. Chevalier and Mayzlin (2006) examined the effect of online reviews on relative sales of books at the two biggest online retail stores: Amazon.com and Barne & Noble BN.com. They found that an increase in the volume of reviews of a given book on one site led to an increase in sales of this book relative to the other site. They also found that negative reviews have a greater impact on relative sales than positive ones. Forman et al. (2008) investigated the impact that disclosing a reviewer’s identity has on book sales, and found that an increase in reviews with disclosed reviewer identity increases both the perceived helpfulness of reviews and the product sales. Other findings of their study were that an increase in the volume of reviews leads to an increase in sales, and that the valence has no effect on the sales.

Liu (2006), using online movie reviews, found that the volume of UGC can strongly predict both the aggregate box office revenues and weekly box office revenues, and that the valence of UGC is not associated with box office revenues. Clemons et al. (2006) found that while high ratings are a good predictor of growth in sales, poor ratings are not a good predictor of poor sales. Dellarocas et al. (2008) also investigated the association between the box office revenues and UGC, specifically online movie reviews. They found that online movie reviews substantially increased the forecasting accuracy of a model for very early post-release motion picture revenues. Liu et al. (2010) also investigated the forecasting accuracy of UGC on box office sales; however, the distinguishing feature was that they investigated the predictive power of UGC across different phases of the movie life cycle. They found that the volume of UGC at different phases of a movie life cycle can significantly improve the prediction accuracy of box office sales in the first week and total box office sales, whereas valence does not. While previous studies focused on the improvement in prediction of box office sales because of UGC, Duan et al. (2008) distinguished the effect of UGC as both a precursor to and an outcome of box office sales. Their choice of box office domain to conduct such a study is good because this domain offers clear daily sales data and attracts enough daily UGC volume to conduct meaningful analysis. However, conducting such a study in other domains may not be possible because of the lack of the either of the two characteristics.

## Literature on the Multistage Decision Making

We first discuss a few representative contexts in which decision making has been considered as a multiple stage process, although none of the contexts investigates the influence of UGC. Before discussing contexts, however, an overview of the theoretical underpinnings of multistage decision making is presented.

Decision makers use multistage decision making so that they can better utilize limited available resources and can reduce the high cognitive cost associated with evaluating large numbers of entities under consideration. At the screening stage, cognitively less-effortful decision rules are used (Roberts and Lattin 1997). Because of information processing limitations (Andrews and Srinivasan 1998) and time constraints (Bettman et al. 1998), decision makers screen entities using subsets of available information (Bettman and Park 1980). After the screening stage, decision makers use a moreeffortful decision rule, and they use comprehensive information to make their decisions (Gensch 1987; Manrai 1995). By screening entities at the screening stage, decision makers manage to reduce the load to the level where they can actively seek comprehensive information about the entities and carefully evaluate all aspects of the screened entities.

The diagnostic information offered by multistage analysis supersedes the information offered by single-stage analysis. There is substantial empirical evidence that the influence of attributes varies across different stages of decision making (Manrai 1995; Roberts and Lattin 1997; Shocker et al. 1991).

Past work by Roberts and Lattin (1997) studied scanner (point of sales) data for packaged goods to evaluate differential influence of variables across the two stages of decision making. Roberts and Lattin (1991) investigated the influence of marketing mix variables in ready-to-eat cereals and found that the multistage decision-making analysis not only improves the prediction accuracy over the single-stage decisionmaking analysis, but also provides better diagnostic information. Andrews and Srinivasan (1995) examined two contexts, yogurt and ketchup sales, and reported similar findings. In atypical contexts of software purchases, Gu et al. (2011) inferred the consideration set from Amazon’s shopping assistance tool and found that most users do not consider alternatives at purchase time. However, their results are not exactly comparable to prior work or the current study, since they strictly focus on the purchase time, when due-diligence about alternatives is already over.

One concern about multistage studies reported in the literature is that, since most of these studies do not have data on the screened entities, there is a chance that the improvements reported due to multistage analysis might be a data artifact (Roberts and Lattin 1997). Since, in this study, screened entities data is explicitly collected, we are in a unique position to clearly attribute the diagnostic improvements observed to multiple stages in the decision-making process. Another important point of distinction between this study and prior work in multistage decision-making reported in the literature is that none of them studied the differential influence of UGC across the multiple stages.

## Literature on the Influence of Media on Venture Financing

Many studies in the venture literature have demonstrated that ventures need to build legitimacy to acquire financial resources (Pollock and Rindova 2003; Starr and MacMillan 1990; Suchman 1995; Zimmerman and Zeitz 2002; Zott and Huy 2007). Past research found that ventures can build legitimacy if they can get endorsements from secondary sources of information (Sanders and Boivie 2004), specifically endorsements from media such as newspapers (Pollock and Rindova 2003). Research shows that the volume of media coverage has a positive influence, whereas valence has no significant influence on financing received by ventures (Petkova 2006). However, UGC differs from traditional media across multiple dimensions and results from traditional media may not extend to UGC.

First and foremost, the difference is that traditional media outlets have limited space, whereas blogs offer infinite space to bloggers. Hence, the coverage of startups on blogs is vaster than that of traditional media. Another important difference is that UGC offers two-way communication between a source and a reader, and hence is more engaging, whereas traditional media is largely a one-way communication channel and it only broadcasts information. The third difference is that blogs empower people to publish their ideas unfiltered, whereas in traditional media there is a team of fact-checkers, copy editors, proof-readers, and editors who ensure that articles being printed are free of errors. Since blogs are written more candidly and are often syntactically imperfect, information on blogs may be considered as an unaltered opinion of writers, compared to the information of conventional media. Indeed, there is some work that suggests people find blogs more credible than traditional media (Thomas and Kaye 2004). To the best of our knowledge, new venture literature has not considered the influence of UGC on venture financing and has focused primarily on traditional media (such as newspapers and trade magazines) as secondary sources of information.

## Hypothesis Development

## Blogs Influence the Screening Stage

According to elaboration likelihood model (ELM),<sup>2</sup> individuals take one of two distinct routes—peripheral route and central route—to process any information, and make decisions (Petty et al. 1983). Using the peripheral route, individuals use heuristics and various simple cues associated with information to make an opinion. Consequently, the cognitive effort required by individuals to process information is drastically reduced in the peripheral route, whereas using the central route, individuals carefully attempt to evaluate the message and put considerable effort into analyzing information.

A fundamental aspect of ELM is the extent of elaboration put into processing the information, where elaboration refers to “the extent to which a person carefully thinks about issuerelevant information” (Petty and Cacioppo 1986). The concept of elaboration level is fundamental because it determines the route choice to process information (Petty and Cacioppo 1986). For high elaboration, individuals take the central route to evaluate information; while for low elaboration, individuals take the peripheral route to analyze information (Cacioppo et al. 1986). To sum up, the amount of elaboration given to any information determines the mode choice for processing the information, and eventually the consequences.

ELM suggests that the elaboration level put into thinking about any information depends on two factors: ability and motivation to process the information (Petty and Cacioppo 1981). The factors that affect the extent of elaboration can be classified broadly as relating to motivation or ability to process, although some factors relate to both motivation and ability. One motivational factor that has received by far the most attention from researchers is personal consequence/ relevance (Petty and Cacioppo 1986). If the personal consequences are dire, the motivation will be stronger to process it elaborately. As the personal importance of the decision increases, it is postulated that decision makers will become more motivated to allocate their cognitive resources to processing the information (Cacioppo and Petty 1984).

At the screening stage, the focus of decision makers is not to make a final decision about an entity, but to reduce the number of entities under consideration. Since at the screening stage the motivation is not to make the final choice, and decision makers always have the opportunity to evaluate the entities later, the benefit of accurately rejecting entities at the screening stage is small. Because the personal consequence of selecting an unworthy entity at the screening stage is low, motivation to reject entities is not very high at the screening stage, and because elaboration depends on motivation, elabo ration may not be high in the screening stage. Furthermore, at the screening stage, where the number of entities is large, limited cognitive capacity constrains the ability of decision makers to evaluate every entity carefully. Inadequate ability to investigate every entity carefully would decrease the extent of elaboration at the screening stage even more. ELM suggests that at low levels of elaboration, decision makers choose the peripheral route of information processing and use heuristics to simplify their cognitive efforts in evaluating information (Petty and Cacioppo 1981). Using the peripheral route, decision makers use simple heuristics that are cognitively less demanding, and use information cues related to objects under consideration to get to decisions quickly. Past work suggests that one such heuristic utilized by individuals is the number of arguments as an information cue to make decisions (Petty and Cacioppo 1984). If the amount of elaboration is low, then decision makers can be affected by simple cues, such as the total number of arguments presented to decision makers. This suggests that decision makers may use the volume of blog coverage as an information cue to make decisions at the screening stage. Findings from the interviews of many well-established VCs also corroborate that VCs may consider blog coverage as a screening criterion and may consider a venture with significant blog coverage as worthy of the decision maker’s time for a careful evaluation (see VCs interviewed in Appendix C).

$H _ { I } .$ The blog coverage increases the probability that an entity will pass the screening stage.

## Blogs Do Not Influence the Choice Stage

At the choice stage, the focus of decision makers is on the final decision about an entity. Since at the choice stage, motivation is to make the final decision about an entity, the potential loss from accepting a wrong entity may be very high. Because of high financial consequences associated with a wrong decision, motivation to be correct is high and thereby elaboration should be high at the choice stage. Furthermore, at the choice stage, the decision maker has to evaluate only screened entities. Because of a manageable numbers of entities, the cognitive capacity needed to examine them carefully is no longer a constraint. High ability should increase even further the level of elaboration put into the choice stage. ELM suggests that at high levels of elaboration, decision makers choose the central route to process information (Petty Cacioppo 1981). Using the central route, decision makers carefully evaluate every piece of information related to entities under consideration, and no longer rely on information cues to make decisions. Thus, the blog coverage may no longer be used as an information cue as in peripheral route; rather, it may be processed actively along with other available entity-relevant information using the central route (Petty and Wegener 1999).

When decision makers are highly motivated and able to follow the central route, they will appraise closely the extent to which blogs provide information beyond that gathered from other traditional and perhaps more reliable sources. To obtain more information, decision makers typically rely on representatives of screened entities (Moriarty and Spekman 1984; Ozanne and Churchill 1971; Webster 1970). Findings from previous research indicate that representatives can address the apprehensions raised by decision makers, provide details required by decision makers, and likely will be truthful to avoid future confrontations with decision makers (Webster 1970). Since providing customer service is not free, representatives are likely to spend limited time and resources to serve their potential customers, rather than to serve large numbers of random bloggers. Thus bloggers, being devoid of this basic information source, are likely to have lesser insights about screened entities in comparison to decision makers. In addition, decision makers are more motivated and committed to accurate analysis of the information collected about screened entities than bloggers. Because of the difference in motivation level, bloggers are unlikely to commit resources and time for researching entities to the extent that decision makers will. Due to the higher elaboration, decision makers may also seek the services of experts to assist in their analysis of screened entities. Therefore, it is unlikely that decision makers, who deploy more resources, who have better information access, and who may hire experts to assist in their analysis, would not come up with assessments of screened entities that are better than the assessment of bloggers. Because of the aforementioned factors, decision makers may not consider blog coverage to evaluate screened entities. Therefore, we hypothesize

$H _ { 2 } .$ The blog coverage does not affect the probability that an entity will pass the choice stage.

## Blogs Do Not Directly Influence the Contract Stage

In certain business contexts, there may exist another stage, the contract stage, where the focus of a decision maker is to negotiate a contract with a chosen entity. A decision maker conducts elaborate due-diligence, and chooses an entity at the choice stage only if it is advantageous to do so. Since contracting with the chosen entity is of personal relevance, ELM suggests that a decision maker would elaborately examine information relevant for a contract. Because all of the aforementioned arguments for the choice stage also apply at the contract stage, a natural conclusion based on ELM is that blogs should not matter at the contract stage either. However, an additional piece of key information at the contract stage is the interest of other decision makers in the chosen entity (Fisher and Ury 1991). ELM suggests that decision makers will be motivated to process the interests of other decision makers, since discarding this information will make them lowball the negotiating leverage of a chosen entity, and hence offer a lesser appealing contract.

A chosen entity for a given decision maker is likely to have initiated interactions with other competing decision makers in addition to the given decision maker. Blog coverage increases the probability of an entity passing the screening stage with not only a given decision maker, but also with other decision makers. Consequently, blog coverage increases the number of decision makers evaluating an entity at the choice stage. Even if blog coverage does not affect the probability at the choice stage, since blog coverage increases the number of decision makers evaluating an entity at the choice stage, one can say with mathematical verity that more decision makers are expected to select a chosen entity beyond the choice stage. To sum up, since more decision makers select an entity at the screening stage because of blog coverage, more decision makers will select this entity at the choice stage.

As the number of decision makers interested in an entity increases, the leverage in favor of an entity increases because an entity is less reliant on a given decision maker (Fisher et al. 1991). Fisher and Ury (1991) called this increased leverage due to alternative interests the best alternative to a negotiated agreement (BATNA). Reduced leverage in negotiations forces decision makers to offer better contract terms to the chosen entity (Fisher et al. 1991). Since blog coverage increases the interest for an entity, and interest from other decision makers favors an entity in negotiating contracts, we hypothesize

$H _ { 3 a } \mathrm { : }$ The blog coverage does not directly affect the contract stage.

$H _ { 3 b } \mathbf { . }$ The blog coverage indirectly affects the contract stage, where the interest from multiple decision makers mediates the influence of blog coverage on the contract stage.

## Empirical Analysis

## Data Collection

To collect data, we randomly selected 200 VCs investing in early stage IT ventures from the VentureXpert database. After repeated follow-ups, 33 VCs agreed to share their deal flow data with us for the year 2008. We compared a few characteristics between VCs providing data and VCs not providing data. Based on the comparisons, it does not seem that the two groups of VCs are statistically different. Next, we provide details about these comparisons.

We conducted the unpaired two-sample t-tests between the mean age of VCs who provided data and those who did not. We failed to reject the null hypothesis, which is that both groups have a similar mean age (p-value = 0.26, t-stat = 1.14). Similarly, we tested the mean fund size for both groups of VCs, and found that both groups have similar mean fund size (p-value = 0.24, t-stat = -1.18). These tests provide a good indication that the VCs in our sample represent the population of VCs investing in IT domains.

VCs provided data about the ventures that approached them to seek financing in 2008, the ventures they invited for presentations, the ventures they decided to fund, and the ventures they finally offered term sheets. Data provided by the 33 VCs indicated that they received 3,898 funding requests from 2,622 ventures. VCs selected 689 ventures for presentations and, out of these, 530 ventures were unique. VCs finally offered term sheets to 215 ventures and, out of these, 177 ventures were unique. The average fund size of VCs was \$122.02 million and the average valuation offered to ventures by VCs was \$14.67 million.

Dependent Variables: The screening stage decision is operationalized by a dummy variable, $y _ { i j } ^ { s c r e e i n i n g }$ , which takes the value 1 if a VC i gives an invitation to a venture $j ,$ and takes the value 0 otherwise. Similarly, the choice stage decision is operationalized by a dummy variable, $, y _ { i j } ^ { c h o i c e }$ , which takes the value 1 if a VC i confirms her intent to invest in a venture $j ,$ and takes the value 0 otherwise. Out of the different terms negotiated at the contract stage, the most widely discussed was the valuation of a venture. Entrepreneurs lose equity to VCs whenever they accept financing, and valuation is an important factor in determining the split of equity between VCs and entrepreneurs. Valuation is defined as “the product of the price paid per share in the financing round and the shares outstanding prior to the financing round” (Gompers and Lerner 2000, p. 288). The higher the valuation of a venture, the lesser the loss of equity to entrepreneurs, ceteris paribus. The contract stage decision is operationalized by the valuation, , offered in a term sheet by a VC i to a venture j.

Independent Variables: We measured the volume of blog coverage for a venture by the number of posts citing a venture, and collected posts data from one of the largest blog search engines. Since a VC may read blogs until the day a decision is made, and can potentially change her decision based on the information posted on blogs, we find it appropriate to consider blog coverage until the day a venture was given a decision. This decision can be a screening stage decision, a choice stage decision, or a contract stage decision. The time of the selection stage decision has been operationalized as the day of presentation before a VC. The time of the choice stage decision has been operationalized as the day when a VC communicates intent to invest in a venture. The time of the contract stage decision has been operationalized as the day when the term sheet is offered to a venture.

Controls: An exhaustive list of controls was added to avoid any possible confounding on the estimated influence of the volume of blog coverage on multiple stages of VC decision making due to other factors. Factors controlled for in this study are valence of blog coverage, trusted referrals, industry type, founders’ reputation, founders’ experience, age of ventures, location of ventures, amount raised, amount requested for financing, projected revenues, media coverage received by ventures, and press releases made by ventures.

Following the literature (Deephouse 1996; Pollock and Rindova 2003), the valence of blog coverage is calculated using the Janis-Fadner coefficient of imbalance (Janis and Fadner 1949). The measure is calculated using the formulae

$$
\begin{array}{l} V a l e n c e = \frac {P ^ {2} - P N}{R ^ {2}} i f P > N; \\ V a l e n c e = 0 i f P = N; \\ V a l e n c e = \frac {P N - P ^ {2}}{R ^ {2}} i f P <   N \end{array}
$$

where P(N) is the total number of positive (negative) blog posts citing a venture and R is the total number of positive, negative, and neutral blog posts citing a venture. The range of this variable is from -1 to 1, where -1 indicates that the entire coverage is negative and 1 indicates that the entire coverage is positive.

We followed the literature and used supervised methods for classifying posts into positive, negative, and neutral sentiment (Dave et al. 2003; Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011; Pang et al. 2002; Shanahan et al. 2006). First, we built three binary classifiers (positive/negative, negative/neutral, and positive/neutral) and then combined these three binary classifiers using a standard round-robin approach, also known as the pair-wise class binarization approach (Koppel and Schler 2006). We trained binary classifiers using a dynamic language model classifier with n-grams (n = 8) from the LingPipe toolkit. The overall accuracy of the classifier was 73.15 percent.

Since VCs receive an overwhelming number of plans every year, they use referrals from their trusted sources as one of the screening criteria, and are more likely to consider a venture with a trusted referral (Shane and Stuart 2002; Tyebjee and Bruno 1984). To control for the influence of a trusted referral on a VC’s decision making, a dummy variable, $d _ { i j } ^ { r e f e r r a l } ;$ , was created which took the value 1 if a VC i received a trusted referral for a venture j and takes the value 0 otherwise.

In this study, we used the Venture Economics Primary Industry Minor Group classification of industries to survey VCs investing in software and services, one of the nine categories of industries classified by the group. This category is further divided into two subcategories—software and IT services—under the Venture Economics Primary Industry Sub-Group 1 classification. Sub-Group 1 classification is widely used by Pricewaterhouse Coopers and the National Venture Capital Association for reporting statistics and trends in the venture capital industry. To control for the different industry classification, a dummy variable, $d _ { j } ^ { S o f t w a r e }$ , was created which takes the value 1 if the venture j belongs to the software subcategory and 0 if the venture belongs to the IT services subcategory.

Founders’ reputation is operationalized by the average number of successful exits (IPOs and acquisitions) led by the founders of a venture. Founders’ industry experience is measured by the average number of years of industry experience of founders. Information related to founders’ reputation and industry experience was provided by VCs. Age of a venture is calculated as the difference in months between the date when the venture was incorporated and the date when a financing request was made. Location is classified into three categories: east, west, and the rest. We followed the extant literature in defining the eastern and western regions (Gompers and Lerner 2000). Ventures are categorized as eastern state ventures if they are located in Connecticut, Delaware, the District of Columbia, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Vermont, and West Virginia. Ventures are classified as western state ventures if they are located in Alaska, Arizona, California, Colorado, Hawaii, Idaho, Montana, New Mexico, Nevada, Oregon, Utah, Washington, and Wyoming. To control for location-specific effects, we added two location dummies: eastern states, $d _ { j } ^ { E a s t e r n }$ , and western states, $d _ { j } ^ { W e s t e r n }$ . VCs also provided us data about the amount raised, amount requested, and projected revenues of ventures. Amount raised refers to the total money invested in the venture until the financing request was made. Amount requested for financing refers to the amount a venture is looking to raise from a VC. Projected revenue is operationalized as a venture’s assessment of its revenue in three years.

We also controlled for alternate conventional information sources such as newspapers, magazines, and press releases, and data for these conventional sources was collected from Lexis-Nexis. In addition, we gathered controls for the information that was generated only after VCs had interacted with entrepreneurs (i.e., after ventures had passed the screening stage). VC assessment of the founding team is an important decision criterion. We surveyed two founding team level assessments from VCs: technical capability and management capability. VCs were requested to report their assessments of technical capability and management capability of ventures over the scale of 1–10, where 10 refers to the highest technical expertise level and 1 refers to the lowest level.

After ventures passed the choice stage, VCs negotiated valuations and other contract terms with ventures in the contract stage. To control for the potential competition faced by VCs, we collected the number of VCs that were interested in a venture. VCs could provide this data only for the ventures that passed the screening stage because it takes effort and time to find out who is interested in a venture that a VC is considering for financing. Descriptive statistics for variables are provided in Table 1.

## Models and Results

Screening Stage and Choice Stage Models: To investigate the influence of blogs on the screening stage and the choice stage of a VC’s decision making, we use the logit model in conjunction with fixed-effects. Using the logit model, we investigated the probabilities of ventures that pass the screening stage and the choice stage as functions of blog coverage and control variables. It is plausible to say that a venture with unobserved high quality may receive higher blog coverage and may have a higher chance of getting selected at the screening stage and the choice stage. Because the impact of unobserved quality of a venture is in the same direction as that of blog coverage, the estimate of blog coverage may also capture the influence of unobserved quality. Therefore, it is important to account for such unobserved effects. We use a VC and venture fixed-effects model that accounts for VCspecific and venture-specific unobserved effects. ## Robustness Checks

We used the VC and ventures fixed-effects model as the main model. For fixed effects, entities with one observation do not contribute to estimating the coefficients. Further, in the fixedeffects selection models, groups with the same value of dependent variable also do not contribute to the likelihood function (Davidson and MacKinnon 1993). This reduces the data points used to estimate the coefficients. Specifically, data points used to estimate coefficients were 394 at the screening sage, 88 at the choice stage, and 74 at the contract stage. Out of these, there were 158 unique ventures at the screening stage, 40 unique ventures at the choice stage, and 36 unique ventures at the contract stage. To check the stability of our estimates we report the results from models that use all of the observations—VC fixed effects model, VC and venture random effects model, and instrumental variable (IV) model. All three models report parameters that are qualitatively similar, thus corroborating the stability of our estimates. The robustness check results for additional models for the screening stage, the choice stage, and the contract stage are reported in Tables 4, 5, and 6, respectively.

For the instrument variable model, we use two instruments, namely whether founders’ maintain a blog or not, and citations to founders’ blogs. To test that the instruments are not weakly identified, we check the F-statistics of equations where blog coverage is regressed on the instruments and controls (Stock et al. 2002). We find that the F-statistic is more than the critical value for all three decision stages— 207.03 for the screening stage, 32.38 for the choice stage, and 28.4 for contract stage—which suggests that the instruments are not weakly identified. To test that the instruments are orthogonal to the main equations, we use Sargan test for overidentification. We fail to reject the null hypothesis at all decision stages by a wide margin (p = 0.23 for selection stage; p = 0.29 for choice stage, and p = 0.27 for contract stage), which suggests that the instruments considered are not part of the main equation and are valid.

## Summary of Results

The findings suggest that the volume of blog coverage increases the probability with which an entity will pass the screening stage, ceteris paribus, which provides support for hypothesis 1.

The coefficient of volume of blog coverage at the screening stage is not only statistically significant, but it is also practically relevant. With the increase in volume of blog coverage by one standard deviation, the odds of passing the screening stage increase by 2.3 times. Since clearing the screening stage is a prerequisite for VC funding, anything that can increase the success chances of ventures at the screening stage is of significant value. Hypothesis 2 is also supported, which suggests that the volume of blog coverage does not influence the choice stage, ceteris paribus. We also find that the volume of blog coverage does not directly affect the contract stage; rather, the blog coverage indirectly affects the terms decided at the contract stage, ceteris paribus. The volume of blog coverage leads to an increase in the number of decision makers interested in entities, which in turn leads to better contract terms for entities. Thus, hypothesis 3a and 3b are also supported.

## Conclusion and Discussion

The primary objective of this article is to advance an understanding of the influence of UGC, particularly blog coverage, on decision making. Using the elaboration likelihood model, we conceptualize that the blog coverage significantly influences the screening stage decisions, but it does not significantly influence the later stage decisions. The analysis of VC funding decisions shows that blog coverage influences the screening stage decisions of VCs, but not their choice stage decisions. However, if one were to consider VC decision making as a single-stage process, then one may wrongly construe that the blog coverage influences choice stage as well. Based on the single-stage analysis, managers may interpret that UGC helps in every context, which may not be true. Our theoretical justifications and research findings indicate that there are exceptions to this interpreted blanket effect of UGC. Our findings suggest that if firms do not have a problem in passing the screening stage of their prospective partners, then blog coverage may not help them in any way, whereas managers whose firms face problems in passing the screening stage could benefit if their firms are covered by bloggers.

The findings also indicate that blog coverage indirectly influences decision making at the contract stage. Because of the positive influence at the screening stage, an entity with blog coverage is screened by multiple decision makers. Having more decision makers interested generates better alternatives to a negotiated agreement (BATNA), and thus an entity can leverage this to its benefit at the contract stage. An example of such a scenario is in industries where suppliers try to become qualified vendors for reputable buyers. Supplier competition is a typical trait in industrial purchase settings, with many suppliers vying to acquire business from an esteemed buyer (Perdue and Summers 1991). Such a setting could be furniture manufacturers trying to attract business from Ikea, or stationary suppliers competing to win a contract from Walmart, or call center solution providers looking to service calls for Citibank. In such situations, buyers have high bargaining power, putting suppliers at a disadvantage during negotiations. However, an increase in blog coverage in favor of certain suppliers may interest competitors (such as Ashley Furniture, Target, and American Express, corresponding to the above examples). More interested decision makers provides better leverage to suppliers for negotiations and in turn leads to better contract terms for suppliers (Fisher et al. 1991).

We investigate a research context in which wrong decisions lead to significant losses for decision makers. Consequently, motivation to gather and analyze information is very high at the choice stage of decision making. On the contrary, prior work investigates research contexts in which wrong decisions largely do not lead to significant losses for decision makers. Prior studies have typically investigated the effect of UGC, particularly online reviews, on products such as books, movie tickets, music, and DVDs that may not impose large disutility on decision makers even if they were to make wrong choices. Thus, decision makers in prior studies may not be as motivated as those in our research context at the choice stage. Because of this potential difference in the level of motivation, it is not clear whether decision makers in these previously researched contexts would make elaborate attempts to gather and analyze information beyond the information offered by UGC at the choice stage.

Our study is likely to be applicable to contexts where the motivation to avoid a wrong decision is high. For example, in car buying, decision makers are likely to screen the car options to a handful of models first, and for screening they are likely to consider UGC. Next, decision makers would likely make trips to local dealers to gather more information about the screened cars before making a final decision. At this stage decision makers may not rely solely on UGC to finalize the car choice. Other such contexts could be HD flat-panel TVs, SLR cameras, quality outdoor gear, and premier watches. It would be interesting either to test the reproducibility of results in a context that shares characteristics with our context, or to conduct analysis in a context sharing traits with those investigated by prior work. Due to the paucity of work investigating the UGC over multistage decision making, focusing on either direction would add to the growing literature on UGC.

A limitation of our work is that in our dataset we do not observe significant negative posts. The valence of blog coverage for ventures was positive, as most of the blog posts either explained ventures or highlighted their positive aspects. This could be why we observed that the valence of blog coverage was insignificant at every stage. Many other singlestage analysis-based studies also report similar findings for the valence of UGC (Forman et al. 2008; Godes and Mayzlin 2004; Liu 2006). This data idiosyncrasy pertains not only to our work, but also to prior studies investigating the effect of UGC. It would be interesting to see how the effect of valence may change in contexts where the valence of UGC is negative. A related future research question could be to investigate why users discuss products even when they do not have strong opinions about them. Particularly, what factors would encourage users to express their opinion and their experience with a product, rather than reiterating the features of a product. It will also be interesting to investigate the identity of people creating UGC, and to understand their motives in writing about a product or service.

This study proposes a new paradigm for investigating the effect of UGC and suggests that past studies can be enriched if seen through the lens of the multistage decision-making process. The multistage decision-making approach has a sound theoretical basis and has strong empirical support as it provides better predictive accuracy than the single-stage decision-making approach. Moreover, the estimates are unbiased, and the analysis has a greater diagnostic capability. Future studies investigating the effect of UGC can provide better insights if they consider the multistage decision-making approach.

## Appendix A

## Detail of the Sentiment Analysis

In this work, we followed the sentiment analysis literature, and used supervised learning methods, which take manually classified data (corpus) as input and automatically extract features (combination of words and parts of speech of words) for sentiment analysis (Dave et al. 2003; Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011; Pang et al. 2002; Shanahan et al. 2006). These supervised methods do not rely on manually or semi-manually constructed discriminant-word lexicons. Prior research has shown that supervised methods perform better than lexicon-based approaches for sentiment analysis (Chaovalit and Zhou 2005; Pang et al. 2002).

We used two dimensions across which we developed in total four classifiers. One dimension used is the venture category provided by VCs, and the two categories provided are software and IT services. The second dimension used is the media type, and the two media types are blog posts and media and press releases. Next, taking an example of classifier for blog posts under venture category software, we provide details about how we constructed a classifier. We randomly picked 600 blog posts for ventures under the category software. Three raters (graduate students) then manually classified posts into one of the following three categories: positive, negative, and neutral. Each post was categorized by two randomly assigned raters out of three raters. In the case of disagreement, the third rater’s judgment was considered as final. To check for inter-rater reliability, we calculated Cohen’s kappa between each two pairs (Cohen 1960). Cohen’s kappa is a statistical measure for examining the reliability of agreement between two raters when categorizing items. Cohen’s kappa came out to be 0.807, 0.812, and 0.818 which connotes almost perfect agreement among raters (Landis and Koch 1977).

The manually classified corpus was then used to construct a classifier using a language-based model, namely, the dynamic language model (Carpenter 2005), using LingPipe. Typically past studies use n-gram (specifically, n = 8) based character language model with a generalized form of Witten-Bell smoothing to create a classifier from the manually classified corpus (Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011). Using this approach, the accuracy of our classifier was 70.12 percent, which is quite good. However, in this study, we needed to classify posts into three categories, and previous research on sentiment analysis has shown that in such situations, pair-wise coupling with stacking gives better results than a single multivariate classifier (Koppel and Schler 2006). The basic idea of pair-wise coupling is to convert c-class problem into series of binary classification problems by learning one classifier for each pair. This is accomplished by using training data of the pair alone and ignoring rest of the data. The predictions from these classifiers are then combined via a framework referred to as stacking—mapping of each of 2<sup>c</sup> possible outcomes to a particular class c (Wolpert 1992). This approach requires us to built three binary classifiers— positive/negative, negative/neutral, and positive/neutral—and then combine possible outcomes into a single category (class) using optimal stack (Koppel and Schler 2006; Savicky and Fuernkranz 2003). The accuracy of this approach was 73.47 percent, which is better than the single multivariate classifier approach. We have reported model results using pair-wise coupling in the paper, and the results from the single multivariate classifier were also qualitatively similar.
