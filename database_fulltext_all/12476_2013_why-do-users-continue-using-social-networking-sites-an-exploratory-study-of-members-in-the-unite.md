# Why do users continue using social networking sites? An exploratory study of members in the United States and Taiwan

## A B S T R A C T

In this paper, we draw on an extended Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT) to explore factors that affect members’ continued use intention toward Social Networking Sites (SNSs). We also theorize about the intricate relationships among a variety of UGT constructs. Further, we conduct this research in a global context by comparing SNS use in the United States and Taiwan. Empirical survey data are collected to validate the research model, and several intriguing findings are observed. Our research results indicate that four determinants, i.e., gratifications, perceived critical mass, subjective norms, and privacy concerns, influence SNS users’ continuance intention and that regional differences moderate the effects of both gratifications and privacy concerns on continuance intention. Our study makes noticeable contributions to the literature on UGT and SNSs. The findings reported also inform service providers in developing better strategies for member retention.

Published by Elsevier B.V.

## 1. Introduction

Social networking sites (SNSs) such as Facebook, MySpace, and Google+ have flourished in recent years. SNSs offer a platform for online users to interact with one another and manage their friendships. Millions of Internet users are now members of one or more networking sites. As of October 2012, Facebook, for example, had attracted more than one billion active users who shared over 140.3 billion connections on Facebook [25]. The success of SNSs has tremendous implications, as SNSs open a brand new frontier for businesses. More than one-third of marketers consider Facebook important for their businesses. In all, 67% of B2C and 41% of B2B companies have successfully acquired new customers through Facebook [35]. Educators nationwide have also tapped into the potential impact of SNSs on education [21]. Government agencies across Federal, State, county, and municipal levels have likewise invested in SNSs to interact with the public. As of 2011 in the United States, all 15 of the cabinet departments of the federal government had a Facebook presence [20]. Through Facebook and its various applications, these departments publish content (e.g., statements and policies) and solicit comments from citizens to improve their services.

To date, the understanding of the primary antecedents of members’ intention to continue using SNSs is limited [60,61]. Continued use is imperative to the long-term sustainability of SNSs [15]. Despite the strategic importance of continued site use, recent studies have confirmed that a portion of online users have started reducing their SNS use and even abandoned SNSs [66]. Given this pressing reduction in SNS use, the current study attempts to uncover the key factors that affect members’ SNS continuance intention. New knowledge in this regard will help service providers develop strategies that successfully promote SNS use. Few studies have explored the antecedents of members’ continued use of SNSs [32,33,36,60]. To enrich our understanding of the research phenomenon, it is imperative that we investigate user behavior through alternative and pertinent theoretical underpinnings [7]. In this paper, we analyze users’ continued use intention toward SNSs through the theoretical lens of the Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT), which can be used to explain how SNSs serve users gratifications.

Based on UGT, previous studies have used the motivations for using SNSs to predict users’ specific behaviors concerning SNSs [4,53,63]. These studies focused on exploring the motivations for using SNSs or explaining the relationships among the motivations and user behaviors. The factors that influence members’ SNS continuance intention, however, have not been investigated. For example, Xu et al. [74] proposed a model consisting of user gratifications, loneliness, and social presence as predictors of members’ current SNS use but did not investigate the factors influencing SNS users’ continued use. To better reveal the relationships between users’ gratification sought and their continued use intention, this study also attempts to identify users’ primary gratifications sought from using SNSs. While prior studies, such as Xu et al. [74], have uncovered several interesting types of gratification (e.g., utilitarian gratification and hedonic gratification), new studies are needed to further identify other types of gratification that members may derive from SNS use. Therefore, one research question which drives the research motivation for this study is as follows:

RQ1: How does gratification motivate members to continue using SNSs?

On the other hand, the UGT literature recognizes that media use is a complex process and advocates for a continued emphasis on theory building. The UGT literature also calls for modernizing UGT theory within the new media ecology [58]. To better explain the media consumption of the public, the extant literature on UGT has developed several imperative theoretical extensions [57]. Hence, additional UGT-related factors should be included to predict SNS users’ continued use intention. Therefore, the second research question that drives the research motivation for this study is as follows:

RQ2: Other than the gratifications sought, what factors motivate members to continue using SNSs?

Drawing upon UGT, we develop an extended research model of members’ gratifications and continued use intention. In addition to gratifications, this study also considers privacy concerns, perceived critical mass, and subjective norms as supplementary key factors that jointly influence individuals’ decision to continue using SNSs. These principal constructs of the research model directly relate to continued use intention: gratifications and privacy concerns are cultivated by members’ initial use experience [10,53]; perceived critical mass signals the prospective, sustainable growth of SNS, which determines the future gains that current members may derive if they continue using a SNS [46]; and subjective norms help retain SNS users through the constant influence of reference groups, building a strong connection between members and SNSs [36].

Furthermore, we examine SNS members’ continued use intention within a global context. In addition to Facebook, other SNSs also have a worldwide reach among Internet users: Hi5 is popular in Latin American, South America, and Europe, and Bebo has users across the United Kingdom, New Zealand, and Australia [10]. However, the regional differences in members’ use patterns among various user groups are largely unknown [52]. A majority of the existing SNS literature has focused on uniform user groups, such as Americans [62] and Australians [5]. Hence, the third research question in this study is as follows:

## RQ3: Are the effects of antecedent variables on SNS users’ continuance intention different among different regions?

Before we conduct a worldwide survey to comprehensively understand the effects of regional differences on SNS usage behavior, this study explores regional differences in SNS use patterns between the United States and Taiwan. These two user groups are selected because they are examples of Western and Eastern societies and have large geographic, linguistic, and ethnic differences as well as dissimilar social principles, economic standards, and cultural values. An improved understanding of SNS members in a global context allows SNS providers to recognize and appreciate various niche markets and develop strategies that promote local members’ continued SNS use.

To answer these three research questions, this study proposes a research model to investigate the determinants of SNS users’ continued use intention and explores the moderating effects of region on our proposed model by comparing American SNS users with Taiwanese SNS users. The paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews the related literature and theoretical foundation. Section 3 presents the research framework and elaborates on research hypotheses. Section 4 discusses the research methodology. Section 5 presents the analysis results. Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper by discussing its limitations, theoretical contributions, and implications for practice.

## 2. Background and theoretical foundation

## 2.1. Literature on social networking sites

To date, a number of research streams exist in the domain of SNSs [10]. One stream of research focuses on the determinants of SNS adoption or SNS usage behavior. For example, Baek et al. [4] revealed six motivations for linking on Facebook, i.e., information sharing, convenience and entertainment, to pass time, interpersonal utility, control, and work promotion. Smock et al. [63] found that motivations for general Facebook use and motivations for the use of specific features of the site (such as status updates, comments, wall posts, private messages, chat, and groups) differ. Hu et al. [33] proposed an integrated research model that combined the perspectives of status quo bias theory with TAM to understanding the factors affecting nonadopters’ acceptance and use the technology. Another stream of research focuses on the factors that influence SNS users’ continuance intention. For example, Shi et al. [60] found that the positive disconfirmations of maintaining offline contacts, being entertained, and seeking information significantly affect users’ satisfaction with Facebook, which, in turn, significantly affects users’ continuance intention toward Facebook. Kim [36] incorporated interpersonal influence into the expectation-confirmation model to investigate SNS users’ continuance intention. Furthermore, some studies have aimed to understand self-disclosure behavior related to SNSs or the negative effects of privacy concerns on SNS use. For example, Chen [15] developed a research model to identify key stimuli and inhibitors of member self-disclosure and found that privacy values may attenuate the relationship between an individual’s attitude toward a SNS and self-disclosure behavior. By surveying 80 users, Lo [44] tested the role of risk and trust in members’ willingness to offer personal information on networking sites. Hence, while users’ gratifications obtained from using SNSs and interpersonal influences enhance SNS usage behavior, privacy concerns may attenuate SNS use intention.

However, although the abovementioned studies have demon strated either the antecedents of continuance intention toward SNSs or the gratifications sought from using SNSs, to our best knowledge, no previous study has investigated SNS users’ continuance intention from the perspective of users’ gratifications sought from using SNSs. Therefore, we are the first to propose an integrated research model based on UGT that incorporates positive interpersonal influences and negative personal concerns to predict SNS users’ continuance intention. More importantly, while prior UGT studies have included the additional constructs in explaining individuals’ media consumption, no study has contrasted or established the likely relationships among them [58,74]. Hence, this study also investigates the relationships between gratifications and interpersonal influences related to SNS use. Furthermore, the impact of regional differences on SNS usage behavior is an important research issue. Since the social networks formed in SNSs may cross regions, several existing studies have also explored SNS users’ behavior from a global perspective. For example, Posey et al. [52] found that British SNS users have a higher level of self-disclosure than French users and that the formation mechanisms of selfdisclosure are the same for the two groups. Tsoi and Chen [70] found that users from Hong Kong and France are different in their perceptions of SNSs, such as their privacy concerns,trust in SNSs, and overall attitudes toward SNSs. Therefore, this study also explores the moderating role of regional differences in our research model.

## 2.2. Theoretical foundation

UGT is an influential sociological theory that explains why individuals actively select specific media outlets to fulfill gratification needs. UGT considers media gratification to result from socially distributed life change events and individual responses to social situations [8]. Unlike earlier communication theories that advocate for a passive role of the audience in media consumption, UGT considers the audience to be a point of departure and suggests that individuals actively search for, identify with, and employ media to fulfill specific gratification needs [9]. That is, users are goal oriented in their media use, and they seek out a media source that best meets their needs. UGT posits that individuals have free will in determining their interactions with media as well as the consequences that follow. UGT has been fruitfully applied in the context of traditional media, such as newspapers, radio, and telephones. Recent studies have applied UGT in studying the gratifications related to computermediated communication (CMC) technologies. People choose to use a particular CMC technology, instead of other media choices, to communicate with others in order to fulfill their particular gratification needs [38].

UGT has significant implications for the current study. First, SNSs are a new application of CMC technology, and therefore, UGT is appropriate for the current study. Second, UGT suggests that existing members will continue to use SNSs if their gratification needs can be fulfilled by SNS use. This study defines perceived gratifications as the personal needs that a user perceives SNS use to satisfy. In addition, we identify privacy issues as a major hazard that SNSs bring to site members, perceived critical mass as an indicator of expected long-term gains from SNSs, and subjective norms as a key source of social influence, which jointly affect members’ decisions to continue using SNSs. These constructs have been highlighted by the existing literature on SNSs as important factors that pertain to online users’ behavioral choices [16,23,62].

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

## 3.1. Research model

Through the application of UGT, we contend that gratifications largely explain members’ intention to continue using a SNS. To incorporate interpersonal influences and negative effects into the model, we postulate that privacy concerns, perceived critical mass, and subjective norms also affect members’ continuance intention. Based on pertinent literature, we contend that perceived critical mass and subjective norms may directly relate to perceived gratifications. In addition, we argue that privacy concerns have an indirect, moderating effect on perceived gratifications. We are also interested in exploring whether gratifications mediate the impacts of perceived critical mass and subjective norms on members’ intention to continue using a SNS. Through these efforts, we have interconnected the set of theoretical constructs in the expanded

UGT framework and elucidated the mechanisms through which new constructs evolve around the classic UGT tenet— perceived gratification. As in Fig. 1, we present the research model.

## 3.2. Hypothesis development

Unlike alternative theoretical frameworks (e.g., Diffusion Theory) that concern the initial adoption of an innovation, UGT explains the imperative motivations related to ones’ continued use of an information system. That is, UGT is appropriate for explaining the continued use of an innovation from which users have an initial use experience [64]. Within other research contexts, the existing literature has confirmed that gratifications obtained from a CMC technology are positively associated with the time and frequency of an individual’s technology use [40]. Raacke and Bonds-Raacke [54] reported that gratifications obtained from Facebook and MySpace include keeping in touch with old friends, making new friends, and feeling connected. Furthermore, Smock et al. [63] suggested that certain motivations may predict how an individual uses Facebook (e.g., for status updates or comments). In line with UGT, we contend that members purposefully utilize SNSs to meet their needs and may continue using SNSs when they perceive their gratifications obtained to be satisfactory. That is, the greater a user’s gratifications obtained from using a SNS, the more likely he or she is to continue using a SNS. Therefore, we expect the following hypothesis to hold:

H1. Perceived gratifications are positively associated with SNS users’ continuance intention.

As increasingly more users disseminate personal information onto SNSs, the debate regarding Internet privacy has become increasingly prevalent in public discourse. For example, Facebook’s News Feed feature presents users’ information in a new way by making all new updates available to all ‘‘friends’’ in an optout manner. The News Feed has spurred criticism from many users because the default mode is to make all information public, causing new privacy concerns [31]. Privacy concerns have also arisen in e-commerce, because websites establish relationships with their customers and provide personalized services based on personal information that customers share [2,49]. As an important predictor of privacy-related behavior, privacy concerns have been widely used as a predict variable in information systems research [24,75]. Privacy concerns are defined as ‘‘concerns about possible loss of privacy as a result of information disclosure to an online business (p. 4)’’ [75]. Privacy is not a concern in closed information systems that are used within an organization, but it has become a growing issue for online consumers, negatively affecting sales revenues for online retailers. Previous studies have confirmed that SNS users are concerned about their privacy [27]. Hence, we argue that privacy is a demotivating factor in SNS use, as people may be concerned about privacy related to SNS use and become reluctant to continue using SNSs. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2a. Privacy concerns are negatively associated with SNS users continuance intention.

Regarding existing members, we expect privacy concerns to positively moderate the association between perceived gratifications and continuance intention. Social Exchange Theory (SET) suggests that individuals are goal oriented and that their choice of social interaction results from a subjective analysis of the costs and benefits of social interactions. Within the current research context, SET suggests that members seek to maximize their benefits and minimize their costs during their interactions on a SNS [15]. When members have high privacy concerns, their continued use intention is largely dependent upon their perceived gratifications, which serve as a key benefit to justify the likely costs of SNS use (i.e., the loss of private data). With low privacy concerns, however, gratifications may become less pertinent to the decision to continue using a site; hence, the predictive power of gratifications concerning continuance intention is likely lower. As a result, privacy concerns strengthen the relationship between gratifications and continuance intention. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2b. Privacy concerns positively moderate the association between gratifications and continuance intention.

Members’ choice of media also depends on their assessment of the media’s expected long-term viability. Perceived critical mass is based on the notion of critical mass, which refers to the point at which a sufficient number of users have used a technology to allow self-sustaining continued use and growth [47,72]. Since it is often difficult for users to measure the actual critical mass threshold, an individual usually relies on his or her perception of whether the system has reached this threshold, which is termed perceived critical mass. In this study, perceived critical mass is defined as the degree to which a SNS user believes that many people he/she communicates with are using the same SNS and that they will continue their use in future. When members perceive that a networking site has reached critical mass, they will expect the people that they communicate with using the SNS to continue to use the SNS in the future and thus will have a higher intention to use the SNS. Hence, we expect the following hypothesis to hold:

H3a. Perceived critical mass is positively associated with a SNS user’s continuance intention.

In addition, perceived critical mass may affect perceived gratifications, as perceived critical mass may indicate gratifications sought from using a SNS in the future. Existing members of a SNS may perceive their continued use of the same networking site in the future to be in their own interest, considering the vast amount of past investments in terms of time, effort, and emotions that they have spent on that website, which would be otherwise lost [1]. As a result, members inevitably consider the future prospects of their service providers when determining their perceived gratification. Unless one is convinced that a networking site is going to continue to prosper, he or she will not consider that networking service to be an appropriate solution to meet his or her demands concerning information sharing, entertainment, and relationship maintenance. This is similar to the ‘‘shadow of the future,’’ where considerations of the future may shape current cognitions and behavior [3]. Therefore, we expect the following hypothesis to hold:

H3b. Perceived critical mass is positively associated with a user’s perceived gratifications.

Subjective norms are a type of subjective social pressure derived from a cohesive social reference group (e.g., family members, friends, or peers). From a social psychology perspective, subjective norms encourage members of certain groups to conform to the group norms as a means to be a part of an in-group. A person’s beliefs concerning subjective norms refer to ‘‘the person’s perception that most people who are important to him or her think he should or should not perform the behavior in question (p. 302)’’ [26]. The use of SNSs has become a new social norm in the Web 2.0 age. When an individual’s own reference group strongly endorses the use of SNSs, he or she may choose to value and comply with the group’s opinion and continue using SNSs. Previous studies have found preliminary evidence that subjective norms act as a relevant predictor of users’ behavioral intention toward SNSs [5,16]. Therefore, we posit that members continue using SNSs in part because of the pressure of subjective norms. Hence, we expect the following hypothesis to hold:

H4a. Perceived subjective norms are positively associated with a SNS user’s continuance intention.

The gratification literature suggests that an individual’s perceived gratifications are subject to the influence of his or her immediate surrounding social environment [57]. A reference group’s strong approval of the use of a SNS indicates that the high value that the group places on the specific networking service. Therefore, members’ perceived gratifications obtained from a SNS are likely affected by their own reference group through the mechanisms of subjective norms. Prior studies have proposed a possible connection between subjective norms and obtained gratifications (e.g., level of task performance support) [56,73]. Thus, we expect the following hypothesis to hold:

H4b. Perceived subjective norms are positively associated with a user’s perceived gratifications.

To understand the effects of SNS users’ characteristics pertaining to their region on continuance intention, this study investigates the moderating effect of regional differences on American and Taiwanese users’ continuance intention. Previous studies have found preliminary evidence suggesting that regional differences affect SNS usage behavior. For example, Jackson and Wang [34] found that gratifications obtained and SNS use (e.g., time spent on SNS on an average day) differ between American SNS users and Chinese SNS users. Kim et al. [37] found that the primary gratifications from SNS use are similar between American and Korean users but that the weights placed on these gratifications differ. Furthermore, Chen et al. [14] suggested that people from collectivistic cultures (e.g., Taiwan) have a higher tolerance for sharing their personal information than people from individualistic cultures (e.g., U.S.). Thus, privacy concerns may differentially affect the perceptions and behavior of different user groups. Hence, we propose the following hypotheses:

H5a. Region moderates the association between perceived gratifications and SNS continuance intention.

H5b. Region moderates the association between privacy concerns and SNS continuance intention.

H5c. Region moderates the moderating effect of privacy concerns on the association between gratifications and SNS continuance intention.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Measurement development

Measurement items for all the principal constructs except gratifications were borrowed from existing measures to ensure validity. The detailed measurements of these constructs and their sources in the literature are shown in Table 1. All items were measured on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly agree (7) to strongly disagree (1). These measurement instruments were pretested with 50 users of a SNS to check the psychometric properties of the measurement scales. Following these pretests, the measurement instruments were shortened by removing items that loaded poorly on their postulated constructs. Moreover, the wording of the measurement instruments was refined to increase the clarity of the items and to remove any potential ambiguities.

Since SNSs are an innovative information technology, the previous findings on SNS gratifications are inconsistent. To further improve our understanding of SNS gratification, we employed a two-step approach to identify member’s sought gratifications. First, we interviewed ten experienced users (5+ years of SNS experience) through an in-depth interview method to understand that gratifications that they sought through SNS use. The in-depth interviews employed an open-ended, discovery-oriented data collection approach to obtain a deep level of information from relatively few respondents. Five Taiwanese volunteers were interviewed independently at a lab located in a university in Taiwan. The other five volunteers were interviewed independently at a meeting room located in a university in the United States. Each interviewee spoke about the gratifications sought from using SNSs. Then, the two interviewers summarized the results independently and compared the results with each other. If the results concerning an item were inconsistent, one of the authors listened to the voice recordings to resolve the inconsistency. Based on the interview data, we uncovered a total of 25 different gratifications. Second, we consulted the relevant literature to capture other likely gratifications. Because SNS represents a CMC technology, we expected typical gratifications obtained from CMC technology to apply to the SNS context. Therefore, we considered the gratifications obtained from major CMC technologies (e.g., Instant messaging, Email, and Blog) as additional components of gratifications sought from using SNSs [45,69]. The two-step approach led to a total of 31 measurement items related to SNS users’ gratifications.

## 4.2. Survey administration

The research model was tested using survey data. We collected survey responses from undergraduate college students. College students are a significant segment of SNS users. A 2010 Pew survey showed that college students presented the largest portion of Facebook users [39]. The student sample is therefore appropriate for the current research. To examine the impact of regional differences on SNS use intention, we developed the survey in two languages: an English version and a Chinese version. Separate web surveys were conducted in the U.S. and Taiwan to collect empirical data. A total of 114 respondents from an American university answered the U.S. version survey, while a total of 136 respondents from a Taiwanese university participated in the Taiwanese version survey in 2010. After removing incomplete questionnaires and non-SNS users, 103 respondents from the U.S. and 122 respondents from Taiwan were included in the study. The valid response rates are 90.35% (U.S. sample) and 89.71% (Taiwanese sample). Respondents were requested to answer the survey based on their experiences with their favorite SNS. ## 5. Data analyses and results

The analysis procedure for this study includes two stages. In the first stage, a factor analysis was conducted to ascertain the gratifications of SNS users. In the second stage, a partial least squares (PLS) test was conducted to validate the research model and hypotheses.

## 5.1. Gratifications in SNS use

As Table 3 shows, all 225 respondents were included to conduct a principal component analysis with varimax rotation to extract the gratifications related to SNS use. Among the entire set of 31 proposed gratification items, empirical results confirmed that 19 items strongly pertained to gratifications sought from using SNSs, and these items were categorized into 5 distinctive groups. The first factor was represented by the variables related to searching for information and obtaining valuable information. Therefore, the first factor included 5 items and was named ‘‘Information’’. The eigenvalue of the first factor is 3.600, and Cronbach’s a is 0.891. The second factor was represented by the items ‘‘because it is pleasant,’’ ‘‘to have fun,’’ ‘‘because it is entertaining to me,’’ and ‘‘because it relaxes me’’. Hence, we included these items in a factor named ‘‘Entertainment’’. The eigenvalue and Cronbach’s a are 3.160 and 0.901, respectively. The third factor was named ‘‘Fashion,’’ because four items in the questionnaire were related to fashion and style. The eigenvalue and Cronbach’s a are 3.144 and 0.903, respectively. The fourth factor was represented by meeting strangers; thus, this factor was named ‘‘Sociability.’’ The eigenvalue of this factor is 2.365, and Cronbach’s a is 0.783. The final factor was named ‘‘Relationship Maintenance,’’ because this factor expresses SNS users’ motivation for keeping in contact with their family and friends. The eigenvalue of this factor is 2.128, and Cronbach’s a is higher than 0.75.

## 5.2. Determinants of SNS users’ continuance intention

We tested the research model using structural equation modeling. We employed PLS, which uses a component-based approach to estimation and place minimal restrictions on the sample size and residual distributions. PLS also supports model testing where both reflective constructs and formative constructs are present [18]. PLS is therefore appropriate. Table 4 reports the correlation matrix, AVEs, and descriptive statistics of the reflective constructs. Measurement reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Nunnally [48] suggested that Cronbach’s alpha of .70 or greater is considered acceptable. As in Table 4, the internal consistencies of all variables are acceptable, indicating that the reliability of the variables is satisfactory. Convergent and discriminant validity are inferred when (1) the square root of each construct is larger than its correlations with the other constructs (i.e., the AVE shared between the construct and its indicators is larger than the AVE shared between the construct and the other items); (2) all AVEs are greater than .50; and (3) the PLS indicators load much higher on their hypothesized construct than on other constructs (i.e., own loadings are higher than cross loadings) [18]. As shown in Table 4, the square roots of the AVE are all greater than 0.8 and greater than all other cross correlations, indicating that the variance explained by each construct is much larger than the measurement error variance. Furthermore, all items of the principal reflective constructs load high on their own constructs. These tests validate the measurement properties of the principal constructs.

While the other constructs are modeled in this study as reflective constructs, gratifications are considered a second-order formative construct because of its multi-faceted nature. Formative measurements are different from reflective measurements in that the direction of causality is the opposite. A reflective construct causes variance in its reflective indicators, whereas formative indicators cause variance in the relevant formative construct [12]. To evaluate the formative measurement model of gratifications, the guidelines proposed by Cenfetelli and Bassellier [12] were followed: (1) examine multicollinearity, (2) determine the number of indicators, (3) assess the possible co-occurrence of negative and positive indicator weights, (4) determine the absolute versus relative contributions made by formative indicators. The measurement quality of SNS gratifications is satisfactory. In addition, the factor weights of the five underlying first-order constructs are all significant at the level of 0.001.

Since H4b is supported, we performed supplementary tests to verify whether gratification mediates the effects of subjective norms on continuance intention. By following the approach of Baron and Kenny [6], we found that the four conditions of a mediation effect were met: (1) a significant relationship exists between the independent variable and dependent variable; that is, the path coefficient from subjective norms to continuance intention is significant (b = .476, p < .001); (2) a significant relationship exists between the independent variable and mediating variable; that is, the path coefficient from subjective norms to gratifications is significant $( b = . 4 5 7 , \ p < . 0 0 1 ) ; \ ( 3 )$ a significant relationship exists between the mediating variable and dependent variable; that is, the path coefficient from gratifications to continuance intention is significant $( b = . 4 0 5 , p < . 0 0 1 )$ ; and (4) a relationship exists between the mediating variable and the dependent variable when controlling for the independent variable. When gratification was not controlled for in the model, subjective norms significantly affected continuance intention. With gratification as a mediator included in the model, however, the strength of the above relationship between subjective norms and continuance intention decreased (i.e., the path coefficient was reduced from 0.476 to 0.395), suggesting that gratification mediates, though weakly, the relationship between subjective norms and continuance intention.

## 5.3. Moderating effects of regional differences

To answer RQ3, we statistically compared the independent variables in the study to examine whether any differences exist between American and Taiwanese SNS users. To do so, we conducted a split-group test on the survey respondents. Since there are two sample groups, it is necessary to inspect whether the constructs can be used equivalently and compared across groups before we conduct a group comparison. Hence, we tested the configural, metric, and scalar invariance using AMOS. The analysis results showed that the configural invariance model is acceptable (with CFI, TLI, and IFI above 0.9 and RMSEA below 0.08) [71]; in addition, the DCFI (baseline – metric) is 0.011, indicating that the metric invariance is satisfactory [50]. Further, the results show that DCFI (metric – scalar) is 0.067, which is slightly over the 0.05 threshold [55]. Since the results of the invariance tests are acceptable, we conducted a group comparison between the American and Taiwanese SNS users.

To fully understand the gratifications sought by American and Taiwanese SNS users, we compared the mean difference for each gratification factor between the two samples. We ranked the gratifications separately for the two groups and compared the means using a paired t-test. The order of primary gratifications sought by American users in using SNSs is relationship maintenance, entertainment, information, sociability, and fashion, while the order for Taiwanese users is entertainment and relationship maintenance, information, sociability, and fashion. We found that relationship maintenance was the primary gratification sought by both American and Taiwanese users. However, entertainment was also a primary gratification sought by Taiwanese users, as indicated by the insignificant mean difference between relationship maintenance and entertainment for Taiwanese users. This result is consistent with prior reports; for example, approximately 80% of Taiwanese users consider games to be their primary reason for using Facebook, and in 2009, the introduction of free online games such as ‘‘Happy Farm’’ led to a 3000% growth in the user base [30]. Finally, the results show that for both groups, fashion is the least important gratification sought from using SNSs.

In addition to comparing the mean differences in gratifications within each group, we conducted an independent t-test to compare the mean differences in gratifications between the American and Taiwanese groups. The results show that Taiwanese SNS users are significantly more motivated by entertainment, information, sociability and fashion gratifications in using SNSs compared with Americans, while there is no significant mean difference in relationship maintenance between the two groups. These results indicate that SNS user experiences differ between America and Taiwan.

## 6. Discussions and conclusions

For a SNS to sustain success, the continued use of its members is important. Nevertheless, SNS members’ use may wane as members constantly weigh the potential benefits and costs associated with SNS use. Recent studies have confirmed that some SNS members have started reducing their SNS use or have even abandoned SNSs [66]. From the theoretical lens of UGT, we developed an extended research model with three antecedents, i.e., privacy concerns, critical mass, and subjective norms, to identify the key predictors of SNS members’ continuance intention. We validated the research model using empirical data from SNS users in the U.S. and Taiwan.

## 6.1. Limitations and future directions

Our study also has several limitations that warrant additional research attention. First, the current study uses college students as the research sample. Although college students present the largest user group of leading SNSs, such as Facebook, other users, such as teenagers and working adults over 22 year old, are all actively engaged in SNSs. Thus, the results must be treated with caution in terms of their generalizability to other user populations of SNSs. Future studies should validate the research model among these populations. Second, the current study employs a cross-sectional design, which does not allow for a longitudinal analysis of the observed effects. Hence, the results should be treated with caution in that causality cannot be inferred from cross-sectional data. While a longitudinal analysis is preferred, valid cross-sectional models must first be established before future research can examine their viability over time. Third, the current study utilizes self-reported survey data to assess online use behavior on a networking site. Future research may employ objective measurements to assess the actual behavior of SNS users. Fourth, future studies may further examine regional differences in SNS perceptions and use. To this end, future studies that use a more diversified, international sample of SNS users will further elucidate the effect of regional differences on the uses and gratifications related to SNS use. Theories regarding cultural differences, for example, may be used to explain the observed differences between users of different geographical locations.

Moreover, since the research data were collected from a single survey, common method bias may be an issue in this study. We checked for the extent of common method bias. First, Harman’s one-factor test was performed by including all the variables in a principal component factor analysis [51]. Common method bias exists when one single factor emerges or when one factor accounts for the majority of the covariance among the variables. The results showed that none of the emergent factors explained the majority of the covariance. Second, the correlation matrix was examined for highly correlated factors. Common method bias exists when there are extremely high correlations between factors $( r > . 9 ) .$ . Table 4 shows that no evidence of common method bias. Therefore, common method bias is not a serious problem in this study. Nevertheless, taking measures to prevent common method bias before data collection, e.g., using separate data collection methods, would be a better approach for future research.

## 6.2. Implications for researchers and practitioners

This study contributes to the literature in a number of ways. First, we applied UGT theory in a study on SNSs. Prior studies on SNSs have largely ignored the role of gratifications in explaining members’ continuance intention toward SNSs. In this study, we employed an in-depth interview method and reviewed previous gratification studies to identify a detailed and broader set of SNS gratifications that members value. Our identified SNS gratifications were therefore informed by both reported user gratifications and prior literature. Although prior studies have suggested a number of potential SNS gratifications [54,60], our results extend the existing repository of SNS gratifications by deriving five major categories of gratifications.

Second, this research is the first effort to compare and report the different impacts of determinants of continuance intention toward SNS use within a global context. Using data collected from the U.S. and Taiwan, we found that gratifications related to members needs for information, entertainment, fashion, and sociability differed significantly between the two groups. Compared with US users, Taiwanese users sought gratification from their SNS use to a great extent. Despite the differences in gratifications sought between the two groups, we found that the assessments of the relative important of the various SNS gratifications were largely similar between the groups. In general, the most important gratification sought by SNS users was relationship maintenance, followed by entertainment, information, sociability, and fashion needs. Taiwanese users, however, considered entertainment to be equally important as relationship maintenance.

Moreover, we found that the association between gratifications and continued use intention and the association between privacy concerns and continued use intention differed between American and Taiwanese SNS users. Compared with Taiwanese users, privacy concerns had a greater effect on continuance intention toward SNS use for US users. Furthermore, regional differences moderate the moderating effect of privacy concerns on the association between gratifications and SNS continuance intention. These observations are consistent with prior findings that privacy concerns have different impacts on Internet users from different cultures [52]. Unlike Western societies, Eastern societies, such as Taiwan, are less concerned about user privacy, as indicated by the lack of awareness of privacy issues and under-developed privacy regulations in Eastern societies [22]. Taiwan, for example, passed its first privacy-related legislation (e.g., the Computer-Processed Personal Data Protection Law) in 1995, whereas the U.S. led the world in privacy protection, with regulations such as the Freedom of Information Act of 1966, Privacy Act of 1974, and Privacy Protection Act of 1980. As a result, privacy has not been recognized as a salient issue in Taiwan. For example, studies have shown that most Taiwanese websites fail to respect customer privacy and do not meet the requirements of the fair information practice principles concerning user privacy [76]. Given these differences in conceptions of privacy, compared with American users, Taiwanese users are less aware of privacy issues when they are using SNSs. Privacy concerns, therefore, are not an important factor that influences Taiwanese users’ continued SNS use.

We found that the association between critical mass and continued use intention and the association between subiective norms and continued use intention did not differ between the two user groups, suggesting that critical mass and subjective norms are consistent, robust predictors of continued use intention regardless of the region from which the user originates. While UGT has recently been introduced into SNS use studies with additional predictors [74], we are the first to test a model based on UGT within a global context and report the impacts of four predictors, i.e., gratifications, privacy concerns, perceived critical mass, and subjective norms, on SNS users’ continuance intention.

Third, our study extends UGT by incorporating additional predictors related to interpersonal influences and the negative effects of privacy concerns. In this paper, we enrich the theoretical tenets of UGT by adding several important theoretical variables that have been suggested by the extended UGT literature, including the adverse influence of media on individuals’ personal lives, long-term expectations, and social environment and influence. More important, we are the first (to the best of our knowledge) to theorize about the intricate relationships among these four related constructs. The results show that subjective norms directly affect gratifications, while privacy concerns indirectly affect gratifications by positively moderating the impacts of gratifications on continuance intention. A follow-up analysis of the mediating role of gratifications showed that gratifications weakly mediate the effect of subjective norms on continuance intention. The findings of this study demonstrate the connections and departures of these theoretical extensions from UGT and enrich the theoretical model of UGT for future studies. As a result of the theoretical extensions of UGT, our research model had great power in predicting members’ continuance intention.

Our paper also has strong practical implications. Regarding gratifications, the findings of this study highlight the importance of satisfy members’ need for information, entertainment, fashion, sociability, and relationship maintenance. SNS providers are therefore advised to improve their services to offer site features that enhance users experiences related to these dimensions. For example, a SNS provider could provide a wide selection of free online games to increase the entertainment value that members derive from the SNS. For instance, the Facebook App ‘‘Candy Crush Saga’’ was the most popular game on Facebook in March 2013, with over 25 million average monthly users. The success of this game increased members continued use Facebook. Furthermore, SNS providers may enhance the search function on their sites to help members easily obtain information or the communication interface on their sites to improve members’ ability to socialize and maintain their relationships.

Moreover, the results show that privacy concerns decrease SNS use among SNS members. The literature suggests that SNS providers can offset the negative impact of privacy concerns on member behavior by taking measures to instill trust in members [43]. Alternatively, privacy protection policies and privacy-aware system architecture and software features may assure members of their privacy and thus mitigate the impact of privacy concerns on SNS use [43,65]. Further, SNS providers should increase users’ perceived critical mass concerning their SNSs. A number of approaches are available to attract new members and expand SNS user bases, including site features (e.g., Friendster’s enhanced feature that allows users to view profiles from other users), intensive promotions, potential user discovery, and user education [42]. Meanwhile, SNS providers may increase members’ continued use intention by reinforcing subjective norms regarding SNS use. To this end, public awareness campaigns may be used. Awareness programs help remove cognitive biases pertaining to SNSs and cultivate supportive perceptions of SNSs among online users. Through word of mouth, more individuals are likely to be recommended to use SNSs.

Finally, our empirical data reveal many differences between the two user groups. Taiwanese SNS users valued SNS for fulfilling their entertainment needs, and therefore, SNS providers may incorporate more online games into their sites as one strategy to increase the continued use intention of Taiwanese members. In addition, we found that gratification alone is insufficient in promoting the continued use intention of American SNS members. SNS providers therefore should not only take innovative measures to enhance users’ gratifications obtained from SNS use but also prioritize their investment in quelling privacy concerns, enhancing perceived critical mass and promoting subjective norms when targeting American users. Our findings indicate that SNS providers’ strategy for promoting SNS members’ continued SNS use should differ depending on which specific region or culture they are targeting. Hence, while SNSs are a global platform with users from many regions over the world, understanding the regional differences among users is necessary to attract new users from around the world. For example, SNS providers may allow users to customize their privacy settings based on their desired level of privacy to overcome the negative impacts of privacy concerns on SNS use.
