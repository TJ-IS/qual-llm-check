ABSTRACT: Based on the theory of effective use and adaptive structuration theory, we propose that employees’ system exploration behavior can be affected by factors related to three major components: task, system, and organizational environment. Specifically, we examine how task characteristics (job autonomy and task variety), system complexity, and innovation climate jointly affect employees’ exploration, which, in turn, leads to extended use of enterprise systems. A field survey of enterprise resource planning (ERP) users yields several interesting findings. First, job autonomy and task variety directly enhance system exploration. Second, system complexity plays a moderating role by strengthening the relationship between job autonomy and exploration and weakening the relationship between task variety and exploration. Third, innovation climate, also acting as a moderator, strengthens both the impact of job autonomy on exploration and the impact of system exploration on extended use. This research contributes to information systems (IS) research by theoretically articulating that system exploration is subject to the simultaneous influences of task, system, and organizational environment factors and empirically testing these factors’ main effects and interactions to shed new light on system exploration research. It also contributes to IS practice by suggesting that organizations could enhance employees’ system exploration and facilitate the transition from exploration to extended use by increasing job autonomy and task variety, designing personalized training programs to reduce system complexity, and developing organizational climates that foster innovations.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: autonomy, ERP, innovation climate, IS jobs, system exploration, task complexity, task variety.

Modern information systems supporting business strategies and operations such as enterprise systems are usually highly complex, comprising many functional modules and requiring coordinated use by multiple users [95]. Although firms are eager to maximize the return on information technology (IT) investments by requiring employees to assimilate system use into their job routines, these systems are often underutilized [59]. To fully realize the benefits of a system, employees are expected to use more functions of the system to accomplish their job tasks, that is, to conduct extended use [49]. Before they can actually extend their system use, employees need to explore the system by experimenting with it and finding more possible ways of using the system [54, 56, 58]. System exploration enables employees to better integrate technology into their work to enhance productivity by leveraging previously undiscovered features [64], thus helping firms to fully materialize the potential of enterprise systems [5, 67]. The importance of system exploration has long been noted by researchers. When analyzing why IT users have disappointing performance, Zuboff explained that their “errors were sins of omission—an underutilization of the data interface resulting from their refusal to notice, to think, to explore, to experiment, or to improve” [118, p.413]. Burton-Jones and Grange [20] emphasize that system exploration can drive effective use of the system and finally lead to improved performance. Hence, the existing evidence suggests that system exploration facilitates extended use, and extended use leads to employee productivity and firm performance. Therefore, it is imperative to thoroughly understand employees’ system exploration behavior and its effect on extended use.

Although researchers have made impressive progress in understanding system exploration during the past decade [5, 48, 51, 67, 72], there are still some gaps in the literature. First, whereas system usage is intertwined with job tasks [31], we have limited knowledge on the effects of task characteristics on system exploration [6, 33]. Scholars examined one job characteristic, job autonomy, as an antecedent of system exploration and showed that it is a meaningful path to generate new knowledge [5]. Following this path, more research is needed to further examine the role of job autonomy and other job characteristics in influencing system exploration. Second, the impact of system per se on users’ exploration behavior has been largely neglected in prior research. Despite the consensus among researchers that system characteristics can influence system use behaviors [20, 34, 76], to our knowledge little research has investigated how system characteristics affect system exploration behavior. Third, few scholars have investigated how the work environment can facilitate system exploration until recently [64, 65, 67]. Given that system exploration is innovative in nature, innovation climate is one of the most relevant environmental factors that can nurture system exploration and facilitate the transition from system exploration to extended use [94, 113]. Yet, no attempt has been made to understand the impact of innovation climate on system exploration.

The objective of this paper is to understand which antecedents influence employees’ system exploration and how. Based on the rationales of theory of effective use [20] and adaptive structuration theory [34], we propose that system exploration is influenced by the interactions of three fundamental components: task, system, and organizational environment. Although a number of constructs associated with each component could influence system exploration, in this research we focus on those that have a high level of theoretical and practical relevance. First, current employees’ work is often featured as dynamic [93], and full of uncertainty [85]. On the one hand, employees are confronted with a variety of job tasks [85]; on the other hand, employees need autonomy to cope with dynamism and uncertainty at work [5]. Assuming that system usage is intertwined with job tasks [31], we attempt to investigate how job autonomy and task variety influence system exploration. Second, with the aim to automate the entire organization, an enterprise system is usually quite complex, comprising many modules that can support and coordinate between different business functions [31]. Whereas system complexity generally inhibits individuals’ adoption of innovations [88], the rich features may also create room for exploration. Thus, it is worth taking a deeper look at how system complexity affects system exploration. Third, as already mentioned, because system exploration by nature is innovative [5, 49], we intend to investigate how the organizational environment affects system exploration, and innovation climate is an environmental factor that will naturally and logically affect system exploration. Accordingly, we develop a research model to explain how job autonomy, task variety, system complexity, and innovation climate directly and interactively influence system exploration. In addition to their theoretical relevance, another important consideration behind our selection of these four constructs is that all of them can be proactively adjusted by the organization to enhance system exploration and use. For example, autonomy and variety can be changed by modifying employees’ job design, system complexity can be altered by upgrading or customizing the system (altering physical complexity) or providing training to employees (altering perceptual complexity), and innovation climate can be fostered by appropriate leadership and management measures. Therefore, these constructs have great practical relevance because they can be incorporated into organizational interventions to improve system exploration and use.

This study makes important contributions to the information systems (IS) literature. It is probably the first study that integrates task, system, and organizational environment factors into a single model and precisely delineates how they interact with each other to affect system exploration. By doing so, the study opens a new avenue for future research to investigate system exploration and use. First, although it is well acknowledged that employees’ tasks are interlinked with systems’ technical functions [31], this study further shows that task characteristics (i.e., task variety and autonomy) are strong antecedents of employees’ system exploration behavior. Second, it reveals the mechanism through which system complexity affects system exploration. Rather than exerting a direct influence, system complexity plays an interesting moderator role—the effect of job autonomy is stronger, whereas the effect of task variety on exploration is reversed from positive to negative when the system is more complex. Third, it integrates an organizational-level factor, innovation climate, into the research model, and illustrates how it can interact with individual-level antecedents to influence system exploration and use. Specifically, innovation climate can strengthen the impact of job autonomy on system exploration and facilitate the transition from exploration to system use. Although we focus on the effects of several specific constructs on system exploration and use in this paper, the theoretical implications go beyond these findings and suggest that other constructs from the domains of task, system, and environment could also have significant influences on system exploration and use, and this study is intended to inspire future research to discover more unknown interesting effects.

## Theoretical Development

## Theoretical Framework

We employ the theory of effective use (TEU) [20] and adaptive structuration theory (AST) [34] as the underlying theoretical framework for this study. We do not intend to strictly follow these theories to develop our research model and only rely on their logics and rationales to justify the key dimensions and constructs we selected. According to

TEU, the essence of system use is effective use, defined as “using a system in a way that helps attain the goal for using the system” [20, p. 633], and the purpose/goal for using an information system is “to faithfully represent a real world domain” [20, p. 636]. For enterprise systems, the goal would be to represent the state of business processes. TEU identifies learning and adaptation as two drivers of effective use in the sense that users can learn new features and experiment with creative ways of using the system to more effectively accomplish their job, that is, to make the system more faithfully represent the task domain that it is intended to represent. In this study, we define system exploration as the extent to which a user seeks and experiments with new features and explores creative ways of using an information system [5, 54]. Based on this definition, system exploration involves both learning and adaptation.

Following TEU, we assume that the ultimate goal of system exploration is to help achieve effective use. To effectively use a system, users need to have transparent interaction with the system’s physical surface, achieve representational fidelity by accessing the representation embedded in the system to faithfully represent business domains, and leverage the representations by performing informed actions [20]. Consistent with prior research, TEU posits that system use involves user, system, and task [21]. Hence, we submit that system exploration represents activities with which a user tries to access faithful representations in the system through transparent interactions with the system’s interface in order to undertake informed actions in accomplishing specific tasks.

Although TEU posits that system use is socially constructed, it does not consider the influence of organizational contextual factors. AST provides a complementary perspective, suggesting that “the major sources of structure for groups as they interact with an advanced information technology are: the technology itself, the tasks, and the organizational environment” [34, p. 128]. Despite different theoretical nuances, AST and TEU have similar aims to explain variation in impacts of IT, and both of them suggest that the impacts of IT are determined not only by IT itself but also by how IT is used by people. The interaction between IT and people is inevitably a social interaction influenced by various structures in the organizational environment [34]. While TEU focuses on users’ access of representations embedded in IT, AST emphasizes the importance of social structures on IT usage. Therefore, AST complements TEU nicely with the consideration of organizational environment. Based on TEU and AST, we contend that the influences on employees’ system exploration behavior stem mainly from three areas: task characteristics, system characteristics, and organizational environment. Specifically, we identify job autonomy and task variety as two task characteristics, system complexity as a system characteristic, and innovation climate as an organizational environmental factor. Next, we discuss system exploration and explain in detail why these influencing factors are selected.

## System Exploration

According to TEU, system exploration can drive effective system use because it involves both learning and adaptation [20]. Contemporary enterprise information systems are characterized by complexity and feature-richness, which have expanded the latitude of tasks in organizations and allowed users to move beyond routine use to value-adding, creative use [51, 54, 67]. Employees at first need to learn and understand the system features before they can effectively use the system to improve performance [20]. Moreover, TEU suggests that the representation embedded in enterprise systems is socially constructed and fallible; thus a system can be adapted by users to achieve better representation fidelity [20]. Other scholars have proposed similar notions such as Orlikowski’s [76] technology duality and DeSantis and Poole’s [34] adaptive structuration of technologies. Hence, employees are able to adapt the system in various ways to better accomplish their tasks. For example, Boudreau and Robey [16] found that employees reinvented some features of an enterprise resource planning (ERP) system by using them in ways different from their original specifications. As technology and work become increasingly inseparable in modern organizations [77], effective use of specific features of the system have critical implications for employee performance [21, 48]. By exploring the introduced system, employees can discover and re-create meaningful applications to enhance their productivity [64] or optimize organizational processes [5].

Previous studies have identified many antecedents for system exploration. For instance, job autonomy is found to enhance system exploration directly [5] or indirectly [56]. Team learning and empowerment climates are found to be able to drive users to explore the introduced system [64, 67]. Perceived usefulness, attitude, and intrinsic motivation are found to increase system exploration [54, 57]. Although these studies have greatly improved our understanding of system exploration, for the most part, they treat the three components (i.e., task, technology, and environment) separately. Prior research has suggested that the investigation of the interaction between technology and its surrounding context (including task and environment) can offer a more comprehensive understanding of technology appropriation [76]. Responding to this call, we attempt to integrate task, technology, and environment into a single research frame to generate new knowledge of system exploration.

## Task Characteristics

Different from routine use that is usually mandated [61, 68, 116], system exploration is purely voluntary and not under the coercive pressure of organizational policies. Since it is an extra-role and innovative behavior [5, 51, 54], employees are not likely to engage in system exploration unless they are sufficiently motivated. We propose that task characteristics play a critical role in motivating and enabling employees to explore the system because the ultimate goal of system exploration is to effectively accomplish tasks at work. Although various job/task characteristics have been identified in the management literature [43, 71, 75], in this paper we focus on job autonomy and task variety because of their fundamental connections with system exploration, as we explain next.

Job autonomy refers to the degree to which individuals perceive that they can determine the method, pace, and effort to accomplish tasks in the workplace [44, 100]. It relates to system exploration at two levels. First, since system exploration requires spending extra time and effort to find new ways of using the system, high job autonomy motivates employees and allows them to have self-discretion in undertaking such activities outside their job requirements. Second, employees with job autonomy have authority to determine the task domain that the system should represent, and if they find problems with the representation they can adapt the system to improve its representational fidelity [20]. With low job autonomy, employees are neither motivated to creatively use the system, nor entitled to decide whether and how the system should be adapted.

Task variety is defined as the extent to which an individual engages in a wide range of tasks at work [71]. An obvious linkage between task variety and system exploration is that when employees work on more tasks, they are more likely to be exposed to new system features that they have to learn before they can use them. A hidden linkage between the two is that a high level of task variety requires employees to possess business knowledge in various task domains, and the intermingling of this different knowledge enables the employees to keenly notice inappropriate system design and take initiatives to adapt the system to faithfully represent the task domains, especially when the system function involves several interdependent tasks. As Burton-Jones and Grange put it, “a user must learn the domain and how faithfully the IS represents it to know what to access, and how to adapt and leverage it” [20, p. 650]. Whereas task variety normally enhances system exploration, it also consumes individuals’ cognitive capacities. If a large portion of individuals’ cognitive resources have to be allocated to understanding the technology, it can be difficult to perform system exploration when task variety is high because task variety will compete for the limited remaining cognitive resources [78, 97, 101, 102].

## System Complexity

Among various system characteristics, we focus on complexity because it is arguably the most salient characteristic for modern enterprise systems [95]. Following Rogers [88], system complexity is defined as the extent to which an enterprise system is difficult to understand and use. A system is complex when it is “made up of a large number of parts that interact in a non-simple way” [98, p.195]. An enterprise system is usually composed of many software modules arranged in a deliberately intricate way to support and coordinate different types of business functions [31]. Hence, such systems are considered to be typically complex [74, 92]. It should be noted that system complexity is not a constant and can be changed by upgrade, reconfiguration, customization, or adaptation. Even if the system is physically unchanged, users’ perception of its complexity can be changed by training and learning.

Complexity is an important antecedent of individuals’ adoption of innovations [88]. Many IS studies have investigated system complexity and its effect on IT adoption and use [10, 37, 52, 83, 87, 103, 105]. Some researchers consider system complexity as the opposite side of perceived ease of use [3, 17, 32]. A consistent finding of these studies is that complexity in general can inhibit users’ adoption and use of information systems.

Previous research on IT adoption and use suggests that system complexity is likely to create a barrier for system exploration, too. When a system is complex, users need to spend more cognitive efforts and could have difficulty in judging how effectively it can be used [20]. Consequently, they might feel uncertain about whether it is necessary to explore the system to find new ways of using it. Despite its negative main effect, system complexity could increase the potential for exploratory use. A complex system has a rich set of features that could be explored to provide more possible ways to attain business goals [51]. However, this potential cannot be materialized unless users are capable of conquering the complexity. Thus, the impact of system complexity on system exploration will be more complicated than just the main effect, and it is possible that system complexity interacts with other factors to influence system exploration. This will be discussed more fully in the hypothesis development section.

## Innovation Climate

There exist different types of organizational climates such as safety, service, innovation, learning, and empowerment climates [22, 67]. In this study, we focus on innovation climate because it is presumably the most influential contextual factor for system exploration that has an innovative nature [5, 113]. Based on prior research [15, 111], innovation climate is defined as the extent to which employees believe that their organizational unit (e.g., department or work team) encourages innovative behavior and has high tolerance for the accompanying risks. Social influence theory suggests that individuals’ immediate social environment is the most important information source that they employ to interpret and construct reality [60, 89]. Hence, we propose that innovation climate will inevitably influence employees’ system exploration.

The past few decades have witnessed increasing interest in the impact of organizational climate on employee behaviors [62]. Generally, climate represents the signals concerning organizational expectations for behavior and potential outcomes of the behavior, which individuals receive from their surrounding environment [94]. In the workplace, employees rely on cues or signals from the organizational context to interpret events, form attitudes, and understand expectations with regard to their behavior and its consequences [89], which in turn can influence their own behavior [22]. Thus, climate can influence how members of an organization perceive or evaluate organizational policies, procedures, and practices [91], and direct their attention and activities toward desired outcomes [6, 53]. This logic is consistent with AST, which suggests that the organizational environment can be an important source of structure that influences employees’ interaction with technologies [34].

Innovation climate can foster innovative behaviors such as system exploration in multiple ways. First, innovation climate reflects organizational values and norms with regard to employees’ innovative behavior [6, 53, 94, 117]. It conveys cues of an organization’s “expectancies” and “instrumentalities” [50], which can direct employees to regulate their own behavior to be more innovative [94]. More specifically, innovation climate makes employees believe that innovative behavior is expected, desired, supported, and rewarded by the organization, which provides a strong motivational force in directing and sustaining employee efforts to engage in innovative behaviors [9, 15, 111]. Second, innovation climate can facilitate innovative behavior by legitimating risk taking [112, 117]. In general, innovative behavior involves experimenting with new ideas and the risk of failure [6, 75]. Innovation climate encourages risk taking by offering psychological safety, a shared belief that it is safe to take reasoned risks without fear of negative consequences to self-image, status, or career [35]. Anderson and West [9] also suggest that a team climate that provides mutual trust and approval for attempts to introduce new and improved ways of doing things at work will greatly drive innovative behaviors. Furthermore, innovation climate encourages employees to voice and communicate novel ideas in the workplace [7]. Continuous exposure to diverse ideas can facilitate employees’ learning, and consequently has a positive impact on their creative thinking [35, 80].

To examine the interactions among task, system, and environment, we suggest that system complexity and innovation climate play a moderating role.

System complexity interacts with both job autonomy and task variety so that their influences on system exploration are strengthened and weakened, respectively. Innovation climate strengthens both the impact of job autonomy on system exploration and the impact of system exploration on extended system use. Next, we discuss the theoretical rationales behind each hypothesis in the model in detail.

Based on the logic suggested by prior research, we propose that job autonomy can increase system exploration. First, job autonomy motivates creativity. Autonomy makes employees feel self-determined and free from external pressures and constraints [33]. It can also stimulate new and useful combinations among multiple dimensions of a task [75]. Highly autonomous employees are more likely to engage in risk taking, alternative thinking, and problem solving, all of which tend to foster creativity [6, 75, 104]. It has long been known that individuals can produce more innovative work when they have more control over methods and scheduling of their work [8]. Second, job autonomy stimulates increased work efforts. Job autonomy can induce a feeling of personal responsibility for performance outcomes and a pride in the ownership of the job [107], which in turn can motivate employees to develop skills and capabilities necessary for success at work [110]. Employees with strong job autonomy tend to perceive that they have a high level of control, freedom, and accountability in the specific position [55, 79] and in the organizational environment [99]. This perception of control over work tends to enhance employees’ work motivation and effort expenditure [12]. As an innovative and extra-role behavior, system exploration demands creativeness and additional efforts from employees. Therefore, with a high level of job autonomy, employees tend to be more creative and more willing to spend extra time and effort on learning unfamiliar features of the system and experimenting with new ways of using the system. Prior IS research has also shown that job autonomy is positively associated with employees’ attempts to innovate with IT [5] and indirectly increases exploratory system use by heightening intrinsic motivation [56]. ## Hypothesis 1: Job autonomy is positively associated with system exploration.

Employees with a high level of task variety need to apply a set of different skills to effectively perform their tasks, and they tend to find their job enriching and motivating [43, 71]. Task variety requires employees to focus simultaneously on multiple dimensions of their work, and it makes employees believe that their jobs are valuable and enjoyable, thus improving their enthusiasm for work [25, 33]. Consequently, employees are willing to make more effort at work [26], and are interested in finding new ways to solve problems or achieve goals by taking creative approaches [75]. This suggests that as task variety increases, employees will be more likely to explore the enterprise system. In addition, as business processes are deeply embedded in the enterprise system [31], many job tasks cannot be accomplished without the system. As contemporary organizations increasingly rely on information technologies to operate, technology and work are inseparably fused together [77]. Thus, task variety requires using a variety of features and modules of the system, and more system use can help employees better understand how the system works and inspire them to explore the system to find new ways to creatively solve problems and accomplish tasks. When performing a variety of tasks, an employee tends to encounter new problems that cannot be solved by his or her existing system skills and has to explore the system to learn new skills. That is, task variety can create opportunities that impel employees to perform system exploration. When task variety is low, employees are likely to find the job boring [43, 71], and a narrow set of system features will satisfy their job needs. They have no motivation to expand the scope of their system use, and repeated uses of basic system features cannot stimulate their imaginations in creatively using the system. As a result, they are not likely to explore the system. Thus:

## Hypothesis 2: Task variety is positively associated with system exploration.

Based on extant IS research, we contend that system complexity can impede employees’ exploration of the system that is introduced by: (1) creating a knowledge barrier, and (2) making it difficult to judge the outcome of system exploration. First, complex systems often create knowledge barriers that require users to develop new skills to overcome [10, 37]. To effectively use an enterprise system, users are impelled to acquire not only technical skills but also business knowledge [20, 52]. As a system’s complexity level increases, an exploration of the system demands even more advanced technical skills and a deeper understanding of how the modules interact with each other [95]. To develop these skills and knowledge would consume a substantial amount of an individual’s cognitive capacity. Since one’s cognitive capacity is limited [84], employees may not be able or willing to expend these resources for exploring the system. Second, based on TEU [20], system complexity makes it difficult for users to foresee how effectively it could be used. When users explore a complex system, they may not be able to ascertain that the new way of system use they strive to find is more effective than the existing way. This feeling of uncertainty can make users hesitate and wonder whether it is worth the effort to explore the system. Thus, we propose that system complexity will negatively affect system exploration.

## Hypothesis 3: System complexity is negatively associated with system exploration.

Based on prior research positing that task characteristics and technology characteristics interact with each other to influence individuals’ technology use behavior [42], we further propose that system complexity plays a significant moderating role. First, the relationship between job autonomy and system exploration is moderated by system complexity. Whereas it is cognitively demanding to explore a complex system, it is also intellectually challenging. “When a task requires a person to engage in activities that challenge or stretch his skills and abilities, that task almost invariably is experienced as meaningful by the individual” [44, p. 257]. Prior research shows that employees are more creative when they work on challenging jobs [75, 90]. The challenge of a task can interact with a person’s autonomy to enhance his or her exploration behavior. In the case of a highly complex system, users are likely to come across many new challenging system features. Although users with low autonomy do not have the leeway to look further into these features, users with high autonomy are likely to be excited by the challenge because their autonomy motivates them to be more adventurous. Thus, there will be a strong relationship between autonomy and exploration. In contrast, in the case of a fairly simple system, users will face few new features. Regardless of their autonomy level, users are unlikely to explore the system because not much can be explored. That is, there will be a weak relationship between autonomy and exploration. Overall, the above logic suggests that complexity strengthens the relationship between job autonomy and system exploration.

Hypothesis 3a: System complexity moderates the relationship between job autonomy and system exploration so that the relationship is stronger when the system is more complex.

Furthermore, we propose that the effect of task variety on system exploration is negatively moderated by system complexity. Although task variety can motivate system exploration behavior, it demands more skills, knowledge, and time from employees, consumes employees’ cognitive capacity, and increases their workload [73]. Given that individuals’ cognitive capacities are limited [78, 97, 101, 102], when employees work within their cognitive capacity limit, they demonstrate effective learning and performance; however, when their cognitive capacity limit is exceeded, their learning effectiveness and performance will decline [78, 101]. In our research context, when task variety increases, employees’ likelihood of system exploration will grow and so will their cognitive load. If a simple system is used, a small amount of cognitive resources are needed for exploring the system and the overall cognitive load caused by task variety and system complexity is still within the employees’ cognitive capacity limit. Thus, the positive effect of task variety on system exploration is unlikely to be affected seriously. On the contrary, if a complex system is used, the cognitive resources needed for exploring it will be large and the overall cognitive load caused by task variety and system complexity will likely exceed employees’ cognitive capacity limit, resulting in cognitive overload. The cognitive overload will be more serious as task variety increases. Without enough cognitive resources to process information related to inrole tasks, employees are unlikely to engage in extra-role system exploration. In addition, Ahuja and Thatcher [5] suggest that cognitive load can reduce system exploration, and Narayanan, Balasubramanian, and Swaminathan [73] find that software engineers’ learning and performance declined when too much task variety increased their cognitive load. Therefore, task variety will have a weaker positive or even negative effect on system exploration when system complexity is high.

Hypothesis 3b: System complexity moderates the relationship between task variety and system exploration so that the relationship is weaker when the system is more complex.

In this study, we conceptually differentiate between system exploration and system usage. System exploration is the process of searching for new way of using the system (e.g., learning, experimenting, and adapting) but does not directly complete job tasks, whereas system usage refers to using the system to directly complete job tasks. This conceptualization is consistent with TEU, which identifies learning and adaptation as two drivers of effective system use [20]. After finding new ways of using the system, employees are likely to apply these new ways in actual system usage to better perform job tasks [87]. Consequently, the scope of their system usage will be extended. Extended system usage is defined as using more of the technol-$\mathrm { \Delta _ { 0 g y } \mathrm { ^ { * } s } }$ features to support an individual’s existing and new job tasks [49]. This construct is conceptually similar to deep structure use as defined by Burton-Jones and Straub [23]. The more exploration employees undertake, the more new ways of system usage will likely be found and applied. If an employee is content with the basic system features that he or she is familiar with and rarely explores new features, chances are that this person will keep exploiting the same set of system features and the system usage scope is unlikely to be extended.

Hypothesis 4: System exploration is positively associated with extended system use.

Given that innovation climate can stimulate innovation in the workplace [1, 9, 94] and system exploration by nature is innovative [5, 54], innovation climate is likely to enhance system exploration. Prior research provides strong support for this hypothesis. First, innovation climate signals employees that their pursuit of creative ideas and new ways of accomplishing tasks via system exploration is valuable to the organization [9]. Innovation climate has been found to be positively related to the user perception that the use of innovative technologies can enhance job performance [60]. Second, innovation climate suggests that system exploration as a creative behavior will be rewarded [1, 7], and the reward expectation will spur system exploration. If organizations base their evaluation and reward systems on the completion of routine tasks, employees will be more likely to conform to standardized procedures and initiate less innovative thinking [2]. Third, innovation climate suggests that system exploration is a legitimate behavior supported by the organization [9, 94]. With organizational resources (e.g., training and dedicated IT support staff) allocated for facilitating employees to experiment with the technology, employees are empowered to delve into system exploration [67]. Fourth, innovation climate assures employees that their risk taking with system exploration will be encouraged and that even if they fail there will be no negative consequences imposed upon them [35, 75, 111]. With reduced fear of failure, employees are more willing to seek new ways of doing their jobs by creatively exploring the system. Finally, innovation climate encourages the exchange of ideas and gives rise to open information flow regarding system use [15, 115]. Due to its complexity, an enterprise system requires users to possess knowledge across business domains in order to explore its potential. When relevant knowledge and information on system usage smoothly flow across different units of an organization, employees can learn from each other about the interaction between system features and business processes and they are better prepared to explore the system. Thus,

Hypothesis 5: Innovation climate is positively associated with system exploration.

Organization researchers have consistently shown that climate plays a significant moderating role in changing employee behaviors and firm performance [11, 47]. Following their logic, we propose that the relationship between job autonomy and system exploration can be strengthened by innovation climate. First, the fit between job autonomy and innovation climate can create a positive reinforcement effect that accelerates innovative behaviors. Whereas job autonomy can stimulate employees to engage in risk taking and creative thinking [6, 75, 104], innovation climate can provide employees with a matching context that tolerates risks and encourages creative ideas [94, 111]. As a result, when innovation climate is high, employees with high job autonomy will be more motivated to explore the introduced system because their innovative initiatives are legitimized and reinforced by the organizational context [94, 112, 117]. Conversely, when innovation climate is low, employees are likely to perceive that their creative use of the enterprise system is not appreciated by others and is even devalued [50]. In this situation, employees with high job autonomy tend to feel discouraged and uncomfortable about conducting system exploration because of the conflict between the tendency to innovate associated with their job design and the norm to avoid risks or mistakes associated with their work context [94].

Second, innovation climate facilitates the innovative behavior of autonomous employees by providing supplementary resources such as practical support and supply of novel ideas [7, 9]. Although autonomous employees are motivated to perform system exploration, their capability of doing this is heavily influenced by the organizational context. When innovation climate is high, colleagues are encouraged to support each other and share their skills and knowledge with regard to system use [15, 60, 115]. Open information flow and healthy debates regarding system adaptations expose employees to diverse ideas and help them to examine the system features from new angles that they had not thought about before, thus inspiring them to creatively explore the system. Based on the arguments above, we propose:

Hypothesis 5a: Innovation climate moderates the relationship between job autonomy and system exploration so that the relationship is stronger when innovation climate is high.

Like other innovative activities, system exploration is a trial-and-error process that might not always lead to finding a viable new way of using the system. In an environment where innovation climate is strong, employees will receive practical support from the organization [9, 94] and learn creative ideas through open information exchange with coworkers [15, 115]. Compared with employees under weak innovation climate, they are more likely to find new ways to use the system meaningfully. That is, their system exploration tends to be more efficient. The relationship between system exploration and extended system use is, therefore, likely to be stronger when innovation climate is strong. Moreover, although system exploration contributes to extended system use, finding a new way of using the system does not guarantee that employees will use the new method to perform their tasks. The employees might be concerned that the new way might fail, that there is no incentive for applying innovative system use, or that using the system differently from the “standard” might be against the norm. Innovation climate can alleviate these concerns because it allows employees to fail [35, 75, 111], rewards innovations [1, 7], and approves creative initiatives [9, 94]. As a result, innovation climate will likely enhance the transition from system exploration to extended system use. Thus,

Hypothesis 5b: Innovation climate moderates the relationship between system exploration and extended system use so that the relationship is stronger when innovation climate is high.

## Methodology

## Data Collection

To test the research model, a field survey was conducted in six Chinese firms that have implemented ERP systems. To achieve sufficient variance in the constructs in our model, we carefully selected the firms to enlarge their diversity in industry type, ownership, ERP vendor, and amount of time since implementation. These firms are in six industries, have three ownership types, employ four ERP vendors, and have implemented ERP for an average of 3.67 years (see Appendix, Table A1). A total of 240 employees were selected from six different functional areas (accounting, human resources, marketing, manufacturing, purchasing, and research and development) of these companies as our survey respondents. The inclusion criteria are: (1) the respondents have used ERP for at least a year, and (2) they depend on ERP to complete job tasks. With the facilitation of the top managers, 240 paper-based questionnaires were distributed to the target respondents either by department heads or human resource offices. The respondents were informed that the survey was anonymous and that they should send the completed questionnaire to the researchers directly, ensuring that their supervisors and peers could not see their responses. A week later, three researchers (including one of the authors and two Ph. D. students) collected the questionnaires in person, site by site.

A total of 221 respondents returned the questionnaire, with a response rate of 92.1 percent. Eight questionnaires were dropped due to excessive missing data, leaving 213 respondents for data analysis. Among the 213 subjects, 94 (44.1 percent) are male and 119 (55.9 percent) are female. About one-fifth (20.2 percent) have a managerial position. A majority of the respondents are between twenty and thirtynine years old (93 percent), have at least an undergraduate degree (95.3 percent), and have served the current firm for more than a year (89.2 percent). The demographics of the respondents are shown in Appendix, Table A2.

## Measurement Development

The survey instruments are adapted from previously validated measures, and all of the items are reflective. The instruments can be found in Appendix, Table A3. Specifically, the three items for system exploration are adapted from Ahuja and Thatcher [5] and Karahanna and Agarwal [55]. Following Hsieh and Wang [49], extended system use is measured with three items. The three items for system complexity are adapted from Trait and Vessey [106] and Xia and Lee [114]. Job autonomy is measured by following Ahuja and Thatcher [5] and task variety is measured by following Morgeson and Humphrey [71], each with four items. Innovation climate is measured by three items adapted from Bock, Zumd, and Kim [15].

To rule out rival hypotheses, we control for seven variables that may potentially affect system exploration and extended use: perceived usefulness, self-efficacy, age, gender, education, personal innovativeness, and years of ERP use. The first five are included as control variables for both system exploration and extended use, and the last two are included only on system exploration. Perceived usefulness refers to a user’s belief that the introduced system can enhance his/her personal effectiveness in accomplishing tasks [32]. Its measures are adapted from Venkatesh and Bala [109]. Personal innovativeness refers to a user’s willingness to try out a new technology, and it implies users’ risk-taking propensity and tolerance of uncertainty regarding technology usage [4]. Its measures are derived from Agarwal and Prasad [4]. System self-efficacy describes the degree to which an employee believes that she or he is capable of performing a specific task/job using the introduced enterprise system [29]. It is measured by a scale adapted from Venkatesh and Bala [109]. Following prior research on system exploration and use [56, 58, 65, 67], we also control for the effects of gender, age, and education.

The original measures for the constructs were written in English. Since the respondents in this study were native Chinese speakers, a committee was employed to translate the questionnaire [108]. Specifically, three Ph.D. students, who are majoring in management are native Chinese speakers and also fluent in English, independently translated the English questionnaire into Chinese, and then discussed the items with one another to achieve agreement on the Chinese items. Then, another two bilingual Ph.D. students were asked to back-translate the Chinese items into English. The backtranslated English items were compared with the original English items, and we ensured that the two versions had the same semantic content so that construct equivalence was achieved during translation [36]. In addition, to ensure face validity of the measures, three steps were taken to pretest the questionnaire. First, the questionnaire was checked and revised by two scholars who have expertise in IT use and organizational behavior. Second, one author interviewed several employees in one of the target companies to check whether the items were understandable to them. Finally, to further validate the measures, the questionnaire was distributed to ten professionals (including five business users and five IT staff) for more suggestions and comments. These procedures resulted in minor wording changes on some items.

## Results

The partial least squares (PLS) technique is employed to test the research model based on the following considerations. First, as a second-generation component-based structural equation modeling technique, PLS can assess the measurement model and structural model simultaneously, and is particularly suited for explaining complex relationships due to its advantages of avoiding two critical issues—inadmissible solutions and factor indeterminacy [38]. Second, different from covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM), PLS is robust with fewer statistical identification issues [46]. Furthermore, prior research has found that PLS and CB-SEM can provide very similar parameter estimations and PLS has more power in maximizing variance explained [40, 46]. This study intends to explain variance in employees’ system exploration behavior. We thus believe PLS is appropriate for analyzing data in this study. Specifically, SmartPLS 2.0 [86] is employed in this study.

## Measurement Model

The measurement model is evaluated by examining each construct’s reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. First, as Table 1shows, all of the values for composite reliability and Cronbach’s alpha are greater than the threshold of 0.70, indicating good construct reliability [45]. Second, as shown in the Appendix, Table A4, except one item for innovation climate (0.668, which is also greater than the acceptable threshold of 0.60 [13, 24]), all of the items have a loading above the threshold of 0.70, suggesting satisfactory convergent validity [30]. Furthermore, we employ two methods to assess discriminant validity: (1) checking whether the item loadings on their own construct were greater than the loadings on other constructs, and (2) checking whether a construct’s square root of average variance extracted (AVE) is greater than its correlations with other constructs [39]. The results in Table 1 and the Appendix, Table A4, indicate acceptable discriminant validity.

## Common Method Bias

Our data were collected from single respondents using the same survey instrument, exposing the observed relationships to the threat of common method variance (CMV) [82]. We conducted three tests to assess the potential effect of CMV. First, Harman’s one-factor test [81] generated eight principal constructs, and the unrotated solution shows that the first construct explains only 30.8 percent of the variance, indicating that our data do not suffer from high CMV. Second, we performed a partial correlation test using a marker variable to partial out the influence of CMV. Following Lindell and Whitney [63], we used the second smallest positive correlation among measurement items (0.01) as a proxy for CMV to adjust the correlations between the principal constructs. The adjusted correlations were only slightly lower than the unadjusted correlations and their significance levels did not change, suggesting that CMV did not spuriously inflate the construct relationships [63]. This is consistent with Malhotra, Kim, and Patil [66], who find that CMV does not significantly affect IS studies involving survey data collected from single sources. Finally, we conducted the latent common method factor analysis proposed by Podsakoff et al. [82] and tailored to PLS by Liang et al. [59]. The results indicate that for each item the variance explained by the latent method factor is much smaller than the variance explained by its theoretical construct (see the Appendix, Table A5), suggesting the insignificant effect of CMV.

## Discussion

## Implications for Research

This study has several implications for research. First, although job autonomy has been found to be able to enhance system exploration [5, 56], our study confirms and extends prior research by showing that both job autonomy and task variety are critical antecedents to system exploration. Because system exploration is aimed toward better task performance [20] and system features are tightly intertwined with tasks [31, 77], task characteristics are inherently related to system exploration. Researchers have paid limited attention to task characteristics, although they are a key component of system use [21]. Our findings contribute to this underresearched area and calls for more efforts to understand the interaction between task and exploratory system use. Our findings also suggest that system exploration and system use have different natures. Whereas system use is part of job requirements, system exploration is more of an extra-role behavior. This explains why perceived usefulness, a strong traditional predictor of system use, has no effect on system exploration. Thus, it would be inappropriate to assume that antecedents of system use will also predict system exploration.

Second, the moderating role of system complexity suggests that focusing on IT artifacts can bring valuable insights into IS research. In contrast to the findings of prior IT acceptance research, system complexity has no direct effect on system exploration. Instead, it serves as a moderating factor: although it can strengthen the positive relationship between job autonomy and system exploration, the positive effect of task variety on system exploration would be weakened and finally reversed to become negative as system complexity increases from low to high. Although the idea that task characteristics and technology characteristics interact with each other to influence technology usage and individual performance is not new [14, 42], the concept of interaction is usually directly operationalized into a construct rather than examined as a moderation effect between two constructs. For example, Goodhue and Thompson [42] measure task–technology fit using eight factors, and Barki, Titah, and Boffo [14] model task–technology adaptation as a formative construct. No empirical evidence has been provided to show how task and technology interact with each other. Our study extends the literature by showing the interesting interaction effect between task characteristics and technology characteristics, and thus makes a unique contribution. In addition, this study offers a more nuanced understanding that goes beyond previous knowledge. For example, prior research suggests that when the system is complex, users might have difficulty in effectively using it [19]. Our findings suggest that this may be contingent on job autonomy: when employees have high job autonomy, they tend to take exploration of a complex system as a challenge and actually increase their level of system exploration, which helps to achieve extended use. Thus, our investigation of the interaction between task and system characteristics provides new insights into system exploration and use.

Third, considering the innovative nature of system exploration and use [5, 49] and drawing on relevant literature on innovative behavior [6, 75], we theoretically justify innovation climate as a valid antecedent of system exploration and use. Surprisingly, innovation climate is not found to have a direct effect on system exploration. Rather, it exerts its influence by strengthening the impact of job autonomy on system exploration. This sheds light on the mechanism through which innovation climate functions in the unique context of system exploration. In addition, prior research separately investigates either system exploration [5, 54] or explorative system use [49, 56], but does not examine them together. This is possibly because previously there was no clear theoretical foundation to distinguish between exploration and use.

## Implications for Practice

This study has several practical implications. First, based on the finding that job autonomy and task variety are significant antecedents of system exploration, organizations are advised to use job/task redesign as a management intervening measure to motivate employees to explore the system. To increase job autonomy, management can delegate appropriate rights to employees so that they can decide how they use enterprise systems to accomplish job tasks. Supervisors can also minimize, to an acceptable extent, monitoring of employees’ system usage to enhance their feelings of control at work [110]. To increase task variety, management can reconstruct jobs either by adding more task activities to employees’ current job [69, 70], or by requiring coordination among employees and work groups so as to broaden employees’ duties and activities in the workplace [41].

Second, enterprise systems are usually complex, thus making technical training essential to achieving implementation success [95]. The two opposite moderating effects of system complexity we find on the relationships between task properties (autonomy vs. variety) and system exploration suggest that managers could take different approaches to providing technical training to employees based on their task characteristics. Employees with high autonomy tend to have a strong sense of responsibility and ownership of the job, and they will proactively develop new skills through various means so that they are capable of exploring the system. In contrast, employees with high task variety have a heavy cognitive load and are unlikely to proactively seek new ways to explore the system. Therefore, training sessions can be “pushed” to employees with high task variety to reduce their burden in seeking new information, and “pulled” by employees with high autonomy so that they can selectively choose to learn new skills that they have not developed themselves. This tailored approach should be able to more effectively serve employees’ need of knowledge for system exploration.

Third, the moderating role of innovation climate suggests that organizations can build strong innovation climates to reinforce their job redesign initiatives and to facilitate employees to turn system exploration into extended use. In practice, several managerial instrumentalities can be employed to form innovation climates. For instance, the management can encourage employees to experiment with new system features by offering oral commends and relevant resource provision [94], and reward those who find new ways of using the system to more effectively achieve task goals [1]. Supervisors should take the lead in fostering an atmosphere of psychological safety to ensure that employees will not incur negative consequences even if their experimentation ends in failure [112]. Moreover, because system exploration requires knowledge in both the business and technology domains, it is critical for management to encourage and facilitate communications among work group members [7] and also interaction between supervisors and subordinates [104]. For example, formal and informal meetings can be held to discuss new ideas, and supervisors should implement an open-door policy to actively communicate with subordinates.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations, and addressing them can provide directions for future research. First, our respondents are from companies in China and their cultural background could limit the generalizability of our findings. Although we controlled for personal innovativeness that could mediate the effects of some cultural factors such as uncertainty avoidance, other cultural factors might influence our findings in unknown ways. Future research should explicitly take cultural factors into account to examine how they affect system exploration. Second, our cross-sectional data cannot provide direct evidence for causal relationships between constructs. Future research should employ a longitudinal design to extend our findings by investigating how the antecedents and moderators influence system exploration over time. Third, we select specific factors in the three domains (task, system, and environment) based on their relevance to system exploration. Although our findings demonstrate that system exploration is indeed influenced by the factors in these three domains, it does not exclude the possibility that there are other task, system, and environment factors that could influence system exploration. For example, factors such as task creativity, system flexibility, and leadership style could have effects on system exploration. For manageability concerns, we could not include too many factors in our research model. Future research can integrate a greater number of relevant factors into the research frame so that a more comprehensive understanding of system exploration and use can be achieved. Fourth, our study involves a specific enterprise system, ERP. This may limit the generalizability of our findings because ERP systems are designed to support backoffice operations and management may give employees only limited leeway in creatively using the system [61, 116]. Future studies could augment the generalizability of our research by examining other types of technologies that are designed to favor creative use (e.g., big data analytics and enterprise intelligence systems). Finally, we collected data from six firms that implemented four different ERP systems. The different technological contexts such as implementation scope and customization level might have nuanced implications in regard to the relationships among the major constructs in our model. Researchers should keep this in mind when they try to generalize our findings.

## Conclusion

This paper intends to understand employees’ exploration and extended use of complex enterprise systems. Through a field survey of 213 ERP users, we find (1) direct effects of task characteristics (job autonomy and task variety) on employees’ system exploration, (2) moderation effects of system complexity on the relationships between task characteristics and system exploration, and (3) a moderating role of innovation climate in shaping system exploration and extended use. Overall, this study contributes to the IS literature by showing that task characteristics, technology characteristics, and organizational environment are three major sources of influences on employees’ system exploration behavior, and they interact with each other in a complicated way to affect system exploration.
