# TECHNOLOGY-CENTRIC CONTESTATION OVER SYMBOLIC AND SOCIAL BOUNDARIES: THE SOCIAL JUSTICE IMPLICATIONS OF COVID-19 CONTACT TRACING TECHNOLOGIES<sup>1</sup>

Semi Min Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A. {minxx146@umn.edu}

Natalia Levina New York University, Stern School of Business, New York, NY, U.S.A. {nl28@stern.nyu.edu}

Hila Lifshitz University Of Warwick, Warwick Business School, Coventry, U.K. {hdiginnovation@gmail.com}

Information systems (IS) can amplify or reduce social justice by limiting or expanding access to material and nonmaterial resources. Using the lens of symbolic and social boundaries, we focus on the role of cultural discourse in demarcating and contesting boundaries associated with IS design and adoption. We examine news media-based journalistic discourse surrounding contact tracing systems implemented in South Korea and Singapore during the COVID-19 pandemic to develop a process model of technologycentric contestation. We found that public contestation over competing societal values, including public health, individual rights, and social justice, influenced how contact tracing systems were adopted and redesigned. Through the (re)design and adoption actions, symbolic and social boundaries were demarcated and contested. Our study illustrates the critical role of news media in counteracting the consequences of IS design and adoption decisions that limit access to resources for marginalized social groups.

Keywords: Social justice, societal values, symbolic boundaries, social boundaries, contact tracing, news media, societal discourse, technology adoption

## Introduction

The concept of social justice emerged during the Industrial Revolution, largely in reference to economic justice (Baudot, 2006). Social justice has since been developed as a plural concept, with a variety of facets related to economic, social, and cultural issues, as well as alternative definitions focused on the distributive, procedural, and interactional aspects of justice (Cropanzano et al., 2007; Sabbagh & Schmitt, 2016). Using the notion of rights and distributive justice, the United

Nations has broadly defined social justice as a state of fairness in the distribution of rights, opportunities, and privileges in society—encompassing fairness in the distribution of both material (e.g., money) and nonmaterial (e.g., freedom of movement) resources (Baudot, 2006).

The concept of social justice has inspired social movements and political activism, from the international worker’s rights movement at the turn of the 20th century to civil rights movements for racial and sexual minorities in the U.S. in the

1960s and 1970s (Tyler & Smith, 1995). Contemporary writing on social justice, however, acknowledges that the equal distribution of resources is an ideal to strive for rather than an achievable reality (Sen, 2008).

Moreover, social justice is a society-specific concept whereby each society, within its cultural context, holds its own views on the importance of equal resource distribution among diverse groups (Azour, 2014; Gewirtz & Cribb, 2002). These views differ because the value of attaining social justice (e.g., equal civil rights for all) competes with other societal values, such as public safety and security (e.g., limited civil rights for prisoners). Thus, social justice, like other societal values, becomes an aspiration—a broadly shared desire to attain lofty goals that are difficult to attain—rather than an achievable reality (Finnemore & Jurkovich, 2020). During policymaking, governments must prioritize conflicting value aspirations, which, in turn, shape social justice. Societal debates thus arise not only about how to prioritize conflicting values but also about whether the current state of affairs adequately reflects these values in practice.

Historically, technology has played an important role in limiting or expanding access to material and nonmaterial resources (henceforth, resources) among societal groups. For example, bicycles as a mode of transportation aided the suffragettes in bringing women to voting places (Strange, 2002). Nowadays, digital information systems (IS) are deployed by governments and individuals to attain diverse societal value aspirations, including amplifying or reducing social justice. For example, social justice can be increased by providing marginalized groups with access to information through technology-enabled educational opportunities (Rumble, 2007) and access to credit through microlending platforms (Flannery, 2007). Technology can also reinforce and amplify social injustice, such as loan processing and credit reporting technologies, creating higher loan interest rates for racial minorities (O’Neil, 2017). Similarly, predictive policing systems used in the U.S. have disproportionately targeted neighborhoods populated by people of color, leading to stricter surveillance (Benjamin, 2019).

Societal awareness of the potential for IS to amplify or diminish social justice opens up a space of contestation around a resource (re)distribution that IS design and adoption may produce. The diverse social justice consequences of IS are often debated in journalistic discourse, which reflects the views of diverse stakeholders, including government officials, social justice activists, and regular citizens (Monahan, 2010). Such public debate often surrounds which societal value aspirations should be prioritized in designing public technology (e.g., social justice vs. national security). Technology designers may be influenced by the public contestation process to revise their design choices. In the U.S.A., for example, awareness of the amplification of social injustice produced by algorithmic loan processing and predictive policing has given rise to the field of algorithmic fairness (Kleinberg et al., 2018), which has led to fairness-aware systems that enable more equitable outcomes for minority groups above and beyond the status quo (Schmitt et al., 2022).

In this paper, we seek to better understand the question of how social justice is shaped by public contestation surrounding IS. Drawing on the United Nation’s definition of social justice (Baudot, 2006), we explore this question in the context of the design and adoption of contact tracing systems in South Korea and Singapore during the COVID-19 pandemic. Contact tracing systems were designed to reduce the spread of the virus and protect public health by differentially restricting access to resources for some societal groups (i.e., those potentially exposed to the disease). However, the design and adoption of contact tracing systems opened up a space for contestation, bringing to the surface tensions between societal aspirations, such as public health vs. social justice (Budd et al., 2020; Parker et al., 2020). We conduct an archival study of news articles to understand how public contestation surrounding contact tracing systems shaped social justice in South Korea and Singapore in the early days of the pandemic.

To aid our theorizing, we draw on sociology literature on symbolic and social boundaries (Lamont & Molnár, 2002). This literature focuses on how societal stratification (e.g., gender discrimination) is the result of the demarcation of symbolic and social boundaries signifying differential access to resources. These boundaries are drawn and contested through cultural discourse. We theorize how IS design choices draw symbolic boundaries by categorizing specific user groups (e.g., quarantined people) and differentiating their access to resources (e.g., limiting freedom of movement). IS adoption may then produce social boundaries where specific user groups experience different levels of access to resources. Yet if the IS is not faithfully adopted or not adopted at all, symbolic and social boundaries may become misaligned. We show how the proposed symbolic boundaries and the enacted social boundaries are evaluated in the societal discourse in light of various societal value aspirations, including achieving social justice. We show how societal discourse plays a critical role in counteracting or reinforcing the consequences of IS design and adoption choices for social justice.

## Theoretical Background

## Dynamics of Symbolic and Social Boundaries

The concept of boundaries is often used in the IS literature to understand tensions among different social groups involved in IS development and use. The concept of boundaries can be used to understand knowledge integration, collaboration, and innovation processes, where different perspectives can enable or inhibit synergistic recombinative solutions (Abbott et al., 2013; Barrett et al., 2012; Carlile, 2002; Cummings et al., 2009; Langley et al., 2019; Leonardi et al., 2019; Levina & Vaast, 2008). These differences can play out in the form of power relations in IS development (e.g., Levina & Vaast 2008) as well as in IS adoption (e.g., Levina & Vaast 2005; Leonardi 2019; Barrett et al. 2012).

Boundaries can be formed around IT artifacts, which then serve as boundary objects and become central (e.g., serve as boundary objects) in defining differences and commonality across user groups (Kaplan et al., 2017; Levina & Vaast, 2005, 2008). The role of the IT artifact vis-à-vis various salient boundaries can change over time, reshaping the boundary relations involved (Barrett et al., 2012; Barrett & Oborn, 2010; Leonardi et al., 2019). Moreover, artifact design choices can reshape boundaries, such as creating or not creating functions enabling users to switch across platforms (Eaton et al., 2015; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013). Similarly, user behavior can reshape boundaries among user groups, such as online community members’ decisions to cross-list their content for multiple communities (Butler & Wang, 2012).

We draw on Lamont & Molnár’s (2002) work on symbolic and social boundaries, which extended the seminal work of Pierre Bourdieu’s Distinction (Bourdieu, 2018). The theory of symbolic and social boundaries unpacks how conceptual distinctions that individuals make in their everyday lives can lead to durable and institutionalized social differences (Lamont & Molnár, 2002). Scholars have explored boundaries based on race, gender, class, territorial inequality, and people living with HIV/AIDS (Clair et al., 2016; Lamont, 2018). As such, the theory of symbolic and social boundaries is particularly wellsuited to understanding the issues of social justice (distribution of resources) within a given cultural context and has helped social scientists theorize how social processes lead to the production and reproduction of inequality.

Lamont & Molnár (2002) define symbolic boundaries as “conceptual distinctions made by social actors to categorize objects, people, practices, and even time and space” (p. 168). Symbolic boundaries, including Black/White, citizen/foreigner, and qualified/unqualified, are an important means through which individuals gain status and monopolize resources and therefore play a critical role in the production of inequality and power dynamics (Lamont et al., 2015; Tilly, 1998). Symbolic boundaries are defined and contested through cultural discourse

—a space where individuals and groups strive to agree on definitions of reality because they perceive and make sense of their environment through a system of classification (Lamont & Molnár 2002). For example, in the 1990s, White workers in the U.S.A. drew symbolic boundaries between the Black and White race, stigmatizing being Black (e.g., stigmatizing Black workers as lacking a work ethic and being poor) (Lamont, 2001). Such a distinction shaped the expectations for African American workers and defined their roles within organizations. Similarly, gender boundaries can shape expectations around gender roles creating, for example, the notion of “gendered occupations,” like nursing and engineering (Ridgeway, 2011). The collective identity within a given category defines the central collective characteristic— rules for inclusion and exclusion (Lamont & Molnár 2002)— and is at stake during contestation over symbolic boundaries (Khaire & Wadhwani, 2010).

Lamont & Molnár (2002) define social boundaries as “objectified forms of social differences manifested in unequal access to and unequal distribution of resources (material and nonmaterial) and social opportunities” (p. 168). The concept of objectification is contrasted with the concept of embodiment and describes a particular mode of the reproduction of practices (Bourdieu, 2020). Objectification enables the reproduction of social relations among agents beyond a given context by symbolically representing— naming and categorizing—these relations through objects/labels (Lamont & Molnár, 2002). For example, an organizational policy may objectify the relations between the IT department and business users by mandating in writing who can initiate requests for IT work (Levina & Vaast, 2006).

Symbolic boundaries can become social boundaries, translating conceptual distinction into identifiable patterns of social inclusion and exclusion (e.g., racial categorization leads to a pattern of segregation in housing) (Lamont & Molnár, 2002). The process of meaning-making produces conceptual distinctions, which serve as a basis for symbolic boundaries (Lamont, 2018). The drawing of symbolic boundaries, however, is a contested process in which agents create and challenge emergent categorization through cultural discourse (Lamont and Molnár 2002). Symbolic boundaries may not necessarily translate into social boundaries, especially if the symbolic boundaries are contested in discourse. Thus, reaching an agreement on symbolic boundaries is an important condition for the objectification of symbolic boundaries into social boundaries (Lamont & Molnár, 2002). Symbolic and social boundaries are formed as part of a historically situated process that ties new boundaries to the existing ones—often around minority groups, reinforcing and worsening their already limited access to resources (Lamont, 2018). For example, in the 1990s, the stigmatized meaning of the African American category in the U.S.A. resulted in unequal access to resources, such as income inequality and lower eligibility for mortgage loans (Lamont, 2018). The taken-for-granted distinction between males and females is utilized by dominant groups to differentially limit access to resources (Lamont & Molnár, 2002) through opportunity hoarding and exploitation (Tilly, 1998).

## IS Shaping Social Justice through the Lens of Symbolic & Social Boundaries

Designing technologies involves categorizing users and work (Bowker & Star, 2000), thereby drawing symbolic boundaries: defining groups of users (“roles”) and determining differential access to resources (“permissions”) and different prescribed behaviors (“workflows”). Such systems are often developed to enhance centralized control over informational resources and to control users’ discretionary decisions (Doolin, 2004; Orlikowski, 1992; Orlikowski & Robey, 1991). For example, in enterprise resource planning projects, headquarters tend to gain control over informational resources and purchasing decision rights, while business units tend to lose them (Wagner & Newell, 2006).

Developing IS while drawing symbolic boundaries often becomes a political process due to the conflicting interests of diverse stakeholders (Jasperson et al., 2002), who contest emergent technology designs to advance their interests (Bloomfield et al., 1992; Callon, 1984; Nancy et al., 2016; Pinch & Bijker, 1984; Silva, 2007). For example, developing databases like the Nursing Interventions Classifications database became a political process, as nurses recognized that such categorization of their work might result in either enhancing or limiting their professional status (a resource) (Bowker & Star, 2000). The nurses fought to be involved in defining the categories used to describe their work practices (e.g., providing emotional support for the patients) to gain recognition for the aspects of their work that might be overlooked. Designing national digital identity systems also involves a complex negotiation among stakeholders over the meaning and scope of data categories that are collected and shared (Medaglia et al., 2022).

Symbolic boundary contestation often extends into IS implementation, as it is through IS adoption that symbolic distinctions can become social boundaries. Users may resist IS implementation efforts and the demarcation of social boundaries they imply and thus misalign symbolic and social boundaries. Users often resist IS implementation when they see their interests becoming marginalized (Avgerou & McGrath, 2007; Brown et al., 2002; Silva & Backhouse, 2003; Smith et al., 2010) and recognize that adopting technology may limit their access to resources (Doolin, 2004; Markus, 1981; Orlikowski, 1992). While still being shaped by technology designs, users may choose whether to adopt the technology, or they may deliberately use it in ways that are inconsistent with the designers’ intentions (Orlikowski, 1996, 2000). For example, users may choose not to adopt computer-aided system engineering tools when they see that it would result in managerial surveillance and control (Orlikowski, 1992). The misalignment between symbolic and social boundaries may also emerge due to users’ inadvertent actions, where users do not adopt technology as prescribed (Orlikowski, 2000). In an attempt to align symbolic and social boundaries, various enforcement modalities can be put in place to achieve compliance with prescribed uses (Smith et al., 2010). These modalities may include managerial authority, disciplining noncompliance, system training, and socializing compliance norms (Akrich, 1992; Bloomfield et al., 1992; De Vaujany et al., 2018; Silva & Backhouse, 2003).

Tying this IS literature to our understanding of social justice, we propose that demarcating symbolic and social boundaries through IS design and adoption is critical for understanding social justice. Based on Lamont and Molnár (2002), we turn our attention to discourse, where societal agreement or disagreement on symbolic and social boundaries takes shape. The focus on discourse is not new to IS research, as scholars have examined it as a way to understand the meaning-making process around new technologies (e.g., Barrett et al., 2013; Berente et al., 2011; Dolata & Schwabe, 2023) and as a way to explore hidden power relations through critical analysis (e.g., Beath & Orlikowski, 1994; Cukier et al., 2009). We add to this tradition by focusing on discourse as a place for open contestation over symbolic and social boundaries in light of societal value aspirations.

## Study Context

Contact tracing is a process used to slow down the spread of infectious diseases (CDC, 2019). The process involves collecting data to identify people who have come into contact with an infected person, notifying them, following up to confirm whether they have contracted the disease, and quarantining them (WTO, 2020). Unlike prior pandemics, where contact tracing was a manual process, throughout the COVID-19 pandemic, digital IS was developed, promising efficiencies and scalability in contact tracing (Anglemyer et al., 2020; Marabelli et al., 2021).

We chose contact tracing systems as a context to investigate social justice issues for the following reasons. First, contact tracing systems triggered public contestation (Rowe, 2020), as they were designed to protect public health by differentially restricting access to resources (e.g., individual freedom) for various social groups (e.g., those potentially exposed to the disease). To identify the contacts of infected individuals, for example, contact tracing systems utilized location tracking or Bluetooth-based proximity tracking, which restricted individuals’ access to resources (e.g., privacy rights).

Second, studying contact tracing technologies is relevant to our research because the contestation surrounding IS occurred publicly in the news media. For example, the media brought to light public concerns about the systems’ potential to restrict users’ privacy and freedom (Rowe, 2020; Rowe et al., 2020). The media also highlighted users’ refusal to download contact tracing applications (apps for short) due to their low usability (e.g., draining battery life, receiving frequent updates, etc.) (Redmiles, 2020; Trang et al., 2020). Moreover, the media criticized governments’ deployment of IS, resulting in the discriminatory treatment of certain social groups and thus producing social injustice (Zheng & Walsham, 2021). In response, governments often adjusted their IS design choices and publicly shared their rationale behind such changes (Braithwaite et al., 2020; Munzert et al., 2021; White & Van Basshuysen, 2021).

Third, the contact tracing systems showcased how societal contexts shape resource (re)distribution produced through IS adoptions (Riemer et al., 2020; Whitley, 2020). Different countries and regions adopted different contact tracing systems, making different compromises on the issue of public health and individual freedom (Riemer et al., 2020). For example, India mandated the use of GPS tracking, whereas Germany opted for the voluntary use of Bluetooth-based technology to protect individual freedom (Riemer et al., 2020). Additionally, political beliefs among individuals in the U.S.A. (Republican vs. Democrat) shaped the adoption of contact tracing systems (Ghose et al. 2020).

## Method and Data

## Method

We adopted a constructivist grounded theory approach to develop theoretical insights (Charmaz, 2006; Urquhart et al., 2010). Specifically, we followed the approach proposed by Charmaz (2005), who outlines how grounded theorizing can advance social justice inquiry as well as incorporate the researcher’s own onto-epistemological stance towards the phenomenon of study. In her later work, Charmaz further advocates for “studying both macro and micro discourses and how, when, and why people invoke, ignore, or challenge them” (Charmaz, 2020, p. 171).

To develop our theory, we conducted a comparative case study of how two societies, South Korea and Singapore, each designed and implemented contact tracing technologies during the COVID-19 pandemic. This comparative approach is well-established within the grounded theory literature (Eisenhardt, 1989). Charmaz (2006) argues that multiple cases can be used to saturate the categories emerging from varying contexts and compare them to identify variations in the investigated phenomena. IS scholars have also demonstrated that comparing two in-depth cases can deepen theorizing (Barley, 1990; Klecun et al., 2019; Levina & Vaast, 2005), as comparative analysis helps scholars practice “radical doubt” (Bourdieu & Wacquant, 1992, p. 235) and better understand whether emergent theory is idiosyncratic to a given case.

## Case Selection

As the COVID-19 pandemic took hold in early 2020, we started exploring the design and adoption of various digital contact tracing policies in different societies covered in the global media. By July 2020, we narrowed our focus to countries that were seen by public health experts as effectively controlling the pandemic, in part through their digital contact tracing policies (Han et al., 2020). In the case selection process, European countries were a poor choice due to fully or partially open physical borders, making it challenging to disentangle countrylevel versus regional phenomena. Additionally, some Asian countries were excluded due to our lack of language skills necessary to form an in-depth understanding of news media discourses (e.g., China, Japan, Thailand, Taiwan, and Vietnam). Three countries met the inclusion criteria: New Zealand, South Korea, and Singapore. We chose South Korea (henceforth Korea) and Singapore because we decided that the geographical isolation of New Zealand would make comparisons difficult. In addition, Korea and Singapore exhibited contextual similarities relevant to our study.

Korea and Singapore had controlled borders but were not completely isolated from their neighbors (Kim et al., 2021), were highly urbanized, and had high-level smartphone penetration (above 80%), which paved the way for digital contact tracing (Low, 2016). Korea and Singapore were wellpositioned for a COVID-19 pandemic response, given the public health system changes implemented based on lessons learned from prior respiratory disease epidemics (Kim et al., 2021). Country differences were not part of the case selection criteria, though some differences emerged as important, as our analysis in the discussion section demonstrates.

## Data Collection

News media, which is defined as mass media focused on reporting information to the general public and includes news agencies, newspapers, news magazines, and news channels, is an important source of archival data. It provides accounts of current events and tensions by detailing opinions expressed by various stakeholders. As such, these sources enable the examination of changing perceptions and attitudes toward phenomena as they unfold (Lofland et al., 2022). News publications are generally a result of journalism, which is defined in its purest form as witnessing an event and recording them for others to see and read (Klose, 2009). Though subject to institutional pressures, news publications are expected to adhere to the journalistic code of conduct, including verifying sources and protecting anonymity, and are thus less likely to contain “fake news,” which is often propagated on social media (Shirish et al., 2021). While in the internet age, citizens are increasingly contributing news and opinion pieces to traditional news publications, unlike in social media, these contributions are still subject to the editorial control of professional journalists (Mythen, 2010). We thus refer to discourse published in news media as journalistic discourse. Such discourse can shape perceptions of the meaning and adoption behaviors of new technologies (Hsu & Grodal, 2021) and, during the COVID-19 pandemic, was a major source of information and influence in shaping public opinions of public health measures (Geber & Ho, 2023; Jiang et al., 2022). Thus, while cultural discourse can be observed in both social and news media, we chose to analyze journalistic discourse rather than social media.

We collected archival data from the Factiva database, which archives 33,000 sources from 200 countries in 32 languages, searching for articles in news publications on relevant actors, technologies, actions, and discourse to understand the contestation involved in the design and adoption of digital contact tracing systems in Korea and Singapore. We collected archival data using keywords to search for articles published between January and September 2020 that discussed contact tracing technologies. We collected Korean news articles containing the terms “corona” and “contact tracing” or “epistemological investigation” at least three times. For the Singapore case, we collected English news articles that included the terms, “Singapore,” “COVID,” and contact tracing at least three times. Next, we identified a list of contact tracing technologies appearing in news media articles, including, for Korea, the Self-Quarantine Safety Protection app, SafetyBand, the information disclosure system, and electronic bracelets and, for Singapore, TraceTogether, the TraceTogether token, SafeEntry, SGWorkPass, FWMOMCare, and wristbands. We used this list of contact tracing technologies to find articles with detailed descriptions of these technologies. We also included government documents related to contact tracing technologies as a secondary source to understand how the government entered public discourse around contact tracing to justify and explain its design choices and implementation policies (Rowe et al., 2020).

When our analysis of the Factiva articles surfaced a particular tension in the design or implementation of a contract tracing technology in a given country, we looked for whether and how it was covered in other articles in our dataset. Upon encountering consistent depictions of the tension across diverse news sources and finding subsequent articles addressing the issue in a similar manner, we believed that we had achieved theoretical saturation. Conversely, when differences in the viewpoints presented across articles concerning government actions, citizens’ rights or behaviors, and aspirational values, we sought to investigate these differences further through theoretical sampling. We looked for more articles in our dataset and conducted additional searches in Factiva using more specific search terms related to the tension at hand.

While the difference between foreign and domestic press coverage was not part of our initial focus, the analysis revealed that the domestic press coverage of some tensions was different from foreign press coverage. We thus coded these differences, noting the article dates and whether the news sources were domestic or foreign. This was the case, for example, in Korea, where the foreign press criticized the government’s raid of religious group headquarters—an action initially largely supported by the domestic press. Later, the domestic Korean press also started criticizing the government’s actions, referencing the foreign articles. In Singapore, the foreign press criticized the government’s handling of migrant workers, while the domestic press acknowledged this social issue but did not directly criticize the government’s actions. Upon seeing such differences in reporting, we wondered if the freedom of the press in each country could help us explain these differences.

According to the 2020 World Press Freedom Index, Singapore ranked 158th out of 180 countries, while Korea ranked 42nd (Reporters without Borders, 2021). This ranking is based on documented cases of the Singaporean government demanding corrections to articles that it deemed wrong and suing journalists with critical views, forcing them to be unemployable, or pressuring them to leave the country (Reporters without Borders, 2021). Moreover, two business groups control all Singaporean public print and broadcast media, allowing the government a significant degree of control over the press (Reporters without Borders, 2021). Relevant to our study, a global association of journalists expressed concern about Singapore’s censoring of views critical of the government’s COVID-19 response using the law entitled “The Protection from Online Falsehoods and Manipulation Act” (Thum, 2020).

This analysis led us to pay attention to the role of news media discourse in our theoretical model, differentiating whether a particular issue was contested in both domestic and foreign sources (widely covered) or only in the foreign press (narrowly covered). The final analysis included 620 articles on Korea and 613 articles on Singapore.

## Data Analysis

In developing our constructivist grounded theory (Bryant & Charmaz, 2007; Charmaz, 2006), we first conducted an emic analysis for each country and then wrote memos to identify conceptual categories. We analyzed the phenomenon of the design and adoption of contact tracing systems in two societies without a specific theory in mind. The tensions around social justice emerged from our data analysis. Overall, our approach to theorizing was phenomenon-driven (Urquhart et al., 2010); we did not adopt critical theorizing as our epistemic stance.

Through open (or initial) coding of the data, we identified a set of recurring codes related to IS design choices, different types of actors, their actions and reactions, and key issues surrounding contact tracing systems. We then used axial (or focused) coding to identify the relationships between open codes commonly appearing in both cases to see if they fit into broader conceptual categories (Charmaz, 2006). At this stage, we began to focus on core tensions around IS limiting access to resources for certain social groups, thus triggering the contestation process. By focusing on these tensions, we identified who did what before and during the contestation process surrounding IS. After we started observing stable empirical patterns, we engaged in theoretical coding by looking for external theories focusing on how discourse shaped the production of social justice. The key purpose of theoretical coding is to “create and refine theoretical links” (Charmaz, 2006, p. 115). After applying multiple lenses to explain and enrich our findings, we found that the literature on symbolic and social boundaries was most insightful. In this stage, we related the literature on boundaries to the analysis of data based on memoing and the coding of each country’s contestation process. The resultant theory is a combination of observed patterns and several layers of theoretical abstraction and recoding based on extant theory (e.g., demarcating symbolic and social boundaries).

We also conducted a comparative analysis of the two cases, focusing on the differences and commonalities of the contestation processes. Specifically, when performing axial and theoretical coding, our unit of analysis was an episode of contestation. Through iterative cycles of constant comparison, we identified recurrent key constructs and relationships involved in the contestation process in both cases (e.g., demarcating symbolic boundaries, designing IS, and demarcating social boundaries). We then conducted a comparison focusing on the relationship between the boundary demarcation processes through IS and societal outcomes discussed in our data sources in light of the social value aspirations. This comparative analysis helped us identify factors that cause variations in the contestation process. Through this process, we learned that the two countries had differences salient to our inquiry. Besides the differences in the Freedom of the Press Index, Singapore had a higher percentage of immigrants (38%) than Korea (2%) and, therefore, higher cultural diversity (The Culture Factor, n.d.). Foreign workers comprise 38% of Singapore’s total labor force (Geddie et al., 2020), whereas Korea is a racially homogeneous society, with foreigners making up less than 2% of the total population. This helped us better understand why the contestation process unfolded differently in each country in the episodes of contestations that received different coverage in the foreign vs. domestic press.

## Findings

We present our analysis of the different stages of the contestation process around COVID-19 contact tracing systems in Korea and then in Singapore. Symbolic boundaries were drawn by the governments through IS design choices, categorizing people based on their risk to public health (e.g., the risk of spreading the virus) and determining the extent to which each group’s access to resources (e.g., limited privacy rights) would be limited through IS (e.g., GPS-based location tracking for high-risk individuals). Social boundaries were then produced through users’ (including government users’) actions, which produced differential access to resources in practice (e.g., limiting individual rights for quarantine violators).

## Korea

## Starting Point: An Initial Contact Tracing System (January–Mid-February 2020)

With the COVID-19 pandemic, Korea initially focused on a test, trace, and isolate strategy (Korean Government, 2020a). The authorities collected more information from people who tested positive for COVID-19 by tracking their movements and exposed contacts, who were identified through qualitative phone and in-person interviews, credit card transaction records, mobile phone data, and footage from security surveillance cameras (Korean Government, 2020a). In doing so, the government drew symbolic boundaries around COVID-19-positive individuals and their close contacts, limiting their access to privacy rights. When individual contact tracing was complete, the government published the person’s movement history on a central public website and sent text notifications to people in the locality. The information helped citizens avoid COVID-19 hotspots, and people experiencing mild symptoms could check the published movement history to determine whether they had come into contact with a confirmed case.

Stage 1: Minority Religious Group COVID-19 Outbreak: Contestation over Public Information Disclosure (mid-February–March 2020)

On February 18, 2020, Patient 31 tested positive, and COVID-19 cases increased rapidly, most of which were traced to Patient 31 (Baek & Lee, 2020). The government publicly disclosed a detailed movement path of Patient 31, and the patient was identified to have visited the Shincheonji Church. Domestic media strongly blamed Shincheonji Church members —already considered by many as being part of a cult (Cho, 2020). Some local authorities blamed the church members for the increase in COVID-19 cases and described them as “evil” and “murderers” (Huang, 2020). Negative sentiment was amplified by the domestic media, highlighting worshipers packed together and not wearing face masks. An online petition to disband the church and consider criminal prosecution was filed with the presidential office, and 75,000 signatures were collected. The Seoul city government even sued the religious group’s founder for murder and causing harm. The detailed personal information disclosed on the government website (e.g., individuals’ addresses) enabled individuals to be easily identified, and their names were rapidly circulated on social media with blaming accusations (Yoon, 2020).

Concern around blaming the Shincheonji Church members was raised primarily by foreign journalists, who highlighted that church members were experiencing significant negative consequences from the violation of their privacy rights, including forced outings of church members as well as social (e.g., ostracizing) and economic (e.g., losing jobs) consequences (Kim, 2020). Over time, the Shincheonji Church members refused to participate in contact tracing by not disclosing COVID-19 exposures (Cho, 2020). The symbolic boundary (people at risk of spreading the disease should be subjected to contact tracing) was misaligned with the social boundary (members of the Shincheonji Church who were at high risk of spreading the disease were not participating in contact tracing). This misalignment was brought to bear in the domestic media, where social boundaries were contested as ineffective in achieving the societal value aspiration of protecting public health.

The government updated the contact tracing system design by focusing on the religious group. In response to the public blame of the religious group, the government redrew a symbolic boundary around Shincheonji Church members and further limited the group’s privacy rights and freedom of movement. The government ordered a raid of the religious group’s headquarters to obtain a complete list of members (Korean Government, 2020a). The government also passed legislation to punish individuals suspected of being infected who deliberately avoided testing and refused hospitalization after positive tests. The government announced: “We have secured a list of about 9,300 members of Shincheonji groups and are testing them and preemptively placing them into quarantine without waiting for the results of contact with confirmed patients” (Korean Government, 2020b). This resulted in a social boundary being drawn around the whole religious group, with church members having fewer individual privacy rights than others in society.

The religious group contested the measures taken against them. The “forced outings” of church group members continued on social media, with the government publishing members’ addresses online. Continued public condemnation and stigmatization led to several church members tragically committing suicide (Kim, 2020). During an interview with foreign media, a church member said: “Please do not spread my personal information ... I am so sorry for my family and friends who would get hurt, and it’s too hard for me psychologically, more than physical pain” (Kim, 2020). As the stigma intensified, a spokesperson of the religious group appealed to Korean society’s value aspiration for social justice in an interview with CNN, saying: “It’s unfair that all people rebuke Shincheonji … It’s like a 19th-century witchhunt” (McGee, 2020). This interview was picked up and circulated by the domestic press (Kwon, 2020).

The religious group’s contestation gained broader support. As the epidemic spread and more Koreans were subjected to public personal information disclosure through the government’s contact tracing system, the Shincheonji Church members’ concerns gained wider support. Domestic journalists began to report concerns associated with the impact of excessive information sharing (Jang, 2020). They argued that people feared “criticisms and further damage” (e.g., being blamed for their COVID-19 positive status through disclosure by the government on a public website) more than being infected (Jang, 2020). The National Human Rights Commission argued that while disclosing patient movement history and contacts was necessary to prevent the spread of the virus, the information that was being disclosed was “unnecessarily specific,” causing psychological injury and discouraging self-reporting (Jeong, 2020). Subsequently, the domestic media began to claim that achieving contact tracing goals was unlikely without addressing the privacy concerns, as people were hiding their symptoms and test results.

The government updated the contact tracing system design to protect individual rights. Responding to the raised concerns, the government created guidelines for data collection and disclosure, saying: “Only information essential for protecting public health should be disclosed in light of legal restrictions and privacy protections of confirmed patients” (Korean Government, 2020c). The guidelines suggested that “personally identifiable information,” including work and home addresses, should be excluded from public disclosure. Once released from quarantine, the religious group members would be protected by the same privacy guidelines. Thus, the government reinstated the originally intended “symbolic boundary” around individuals exposed to COVID-19. Changing the data-sharing policies restored some privacy rights to this social group, even at the potential cost of increasing the public health risk.

## Stage 2: Dealing with Quarantine Violators: Contestation over Quarantine Monitoring Technologies (April 2020)

In April 2020, in response to the increasing number of COVID-19 cases among foreign travelers, the government drew a new symbolic boundary around all incoming travelers and mandated their testing and a 14-day quarantine regardless of test results (Hwang, 2020). Incoming travelers were also required to use the Self-Quarantine Safety Protection app while keeping app use voluntary for domestic quarantine cases. The app was developed to monitor quarantined individuals at the start of the spread of COVID-19 and had two key functions: (1) regular self-diagnosis and submission of the results to assigned municipal clerks and (2) GPS-based location tracking to deter quarantine violations. When quarantine rules were violated, the relevant municipal clerks were notified and took necessary actions. When introducing this system, the authorities claimed that limiting privacy and freedom of movement was inevitable to protect public health (Kwak, 2020).

Unfaithful system adoption: To avoid GPS tracking, foreign travelers under quarantine often misused the app by leaving their phones where they were being quarantined (Kim, 2020). This misuse was highlighted by the domestic media and sparked public outrage over the government’s failure to protect public health. The domestic media thus publicized the misalignment of symbolic boundary (inbound travelers are at a high risk of spreading the virus) was misaligned with the social boundary (a subset of individuals at high risk of spreading are not complying with public health rules).

The government updated the contact tracing system design to protect public health: To mitigate “Self-Quarantine Safety Protection” app misuse, the government redesigned the technology by pairing it with a Bluetoothenabled electronic bracelet to determine whether an individual was with their phone (Chung, 2020). Electronic bracelets generated an alarm at 20 meters from the registered phone and sent a report to the local authorities if the distance was larger. Since the technology was designed to be broadly effective for quarantine enforcement, the government also proposed extending the use of the app and bracelet to all individuals required to quarantine (Park, 2020). The symbolic boundary was therefore redrawn around all individuals exposed to COVID-19 and needing to be quarantined, not just around incoming travelers.

The newly proposed policy led to domestic journalists arguing that an electronic bracelet represented an unreasonable violation of the individual rights of the quarantined individuals (Han, 2020). Domestic media interviewed the Korean Bar Association, arguing that, unlike international cases, there was no legal basis for mandating electronic bracelets in domestic cases (Han, 2020). Domestic media indicated that members of the public perceived the electronic bracelets as closely resembling police criminal surveillance technology (e.g., electronic anklets). The government’s use of such bracelets on quarantined citizens was therefore perceived as treating them as criminals, violating their rights (e.g., freedom from arbitrary detention). The Korean National Human Rights Commission argued that this measure should be applied to the minimum extent, based on strict legal grounds (Park, 2020). In an interview with domestic media, a physician argued:

We successfully curb the virus through voluntary participation, cooperation, and solidarity, not control and surveillance ... Using electronic bracelets is a measure that makes individuals potential threats that need control and not the patient in need of protection … The government is too inspired by past success. (Ban, 2020)

The domestic media, therefore, contested the newly drawn symbolic boundary and the associated technology design choices. The contestation centered on the claim that people who obey quarantine rules should not be treated like criminals, appealing to the societal aspiration to uphold the right of individuals to be considered innocent until proven guilty.

The government updated the contact tracing system design to protect individual rights: In response, the government limited the use of electronic bracelets to quarantine violators and only used them with the app user’s consent, adjusting the symbolic boundary (Korean Government, 2020d). The government also began referring to the bracelet as “SafetyBand,” stating: “The name was chosen because it is worn for the safety of the public as well as those under quarantine … To protect public health and limit individual rights violation, only those violating quarantine rules are required to wear it” (Korean Government, 2020d).

Stage 3: Outbreak in an LGBTQ+ Club: Contestation over Public Information Disclosure & QR Code Check-in (May–Early September 2020)

After a sustained period of less than 20 COVID-19 cases reported per day, a new outbreak was linked to a single individual who visited at least five nightclubs on May 7, 2020 (Ahn, 2020). The government initiated the contact tracing process, and to aid government efforts, one nightclub posted on social media that the person in question had visited their establishment. The domestic media quickly identified all five clubs as being LGBTQ+ and started blaming the LGBTQ+ community for spreading COVID-19 (Ahn, 2020). Moreover, domestic journalists reported video footage on social media of one of the nightclubs, accompanied by a call for the government “to help put a stop to these disgusting goings-on” (Ahn, 2020).

Unfaithful system adoption: Both domestic and foreign media reported that the fear that testing positive for COVID-19 meant that one would be labeled “gay” resulted in a widespread refusal to get tested (Choi & Park, 2020). This stigmatization created a misalignment between the symbolic boundary (people exposed to COVID-19 should get tested, and their personal data should be available for contract tracing) and the social boundary (only people not afraid of being labeled “gay” were undergoing testing and sharing personal data).

The government updated the contact tracing system to protect public health: The government had difficulty ensuring voluntary compliance with the call for COVID-19 testing in light of the new, erroneous association between COVID-19 and being gay. The government requested that telecommunication companies share information on individuals who accessed the stations around the clubs for two weeks prior to May 7, regardless of whether these individuals were exposed to the infected person (Korean Government, 2020e). This was a personal data request on an unprecedented scale, invoking discussion about privacy concerns in the domestic media (Lee, 2020).

Contestation by LGBTQ+ rights advocates: The LGBTQ+ community and their advocates appealed to societal aspirations for social justice, including avoiding the stigmatization of minorities in domestic and foreign media (Choi & Park, 2020). LGBTQ+ rights organizations came together to issue a statement, reported in the press, criticizing the government’s publication of sensitive information, stating: “Sexual orientation is not relevant for virusprevention efforts, and such disclosure could violate rights to privacy. If information like that is disclosed, why would anyone come forward and admit that they visited those places?” (YTN, 2020b).

LGBTQ+ contestation gained broader support: Domestic news media supported the claim of LGBTQ+ rights organizations, arguing that a high degree of information disclosure was unhelpful. Foreign journalists stated that “[Korea is] a country that ranks low among developed economies for LGBTQ acceptance, and where many choose to keep their sexual orientations private to avoid discrimination at their workplaces or from their families” (Borowiec, 2020). The domestic media picked up on this article and publicized the misalignment between symbolic and social boundaries, amplifying the LGBTQ+ rights organizations’ statement that stigmatizing could lead people to avoid testing, thereby jeopardizing public health (Ahn, 2020).

The government updated the design of the contact tracing system to protect individual rights: In response, local authorities offered anonymous testing that only collected phone numbers and no other personal information (Korean Government, 2020g). Moreover, the government created new guidelines to reduce information-sharing with the public— authorities disclosed the names of businesses visited by the first patient only but did not disclose visits by subsequent patients visiting the same business (Kwon & Ock, 2020). The guidelines also prescribed the deletion of individual movement history from the government website, COVID-19- related apps, and news articles two weeks after a person tested positive. With these guidelines, the government stressed: “Stop blaming and stigmatizing confirmed patients. These acts put our society at risk by encouraging patients to go into hiding ... the authorities will thoroughly protect personal information during the contact tracing process” (Korean Government, 2020f). The new policy resulted in an eight-fold increase in people undergoing voluntary testing, aligning the symbolic and social boundaries (Kim, 2020). These changes helped achieve a greater alignment between the symbolic boundary drawn by the government (individuals exposed to COVID-19) and the social boundary (individuals undergoing voluntary testing and participating in contact tracing).

## Singapore

## Starting Point: Initial Contact Tracing System (January–February 2020)

In January 2020, a multi-ministry task force was formed to implement a contact tracing protocol (Vaswani, 2020). When individuals tested positive for COVID-19, they were instructed to self-quarantine and were required by law to assist officials by providing their recent movement history. Contact tracers then used data from interviews, a network of security cameras, digital signatures of activities at ATMs, and credit card transactions to identify close contacts of the infected individual and request they get tested (Khalik, 2020). Thus, initially, the Singaporean government drew a symbolic boundary around individuals infected with COVID-19 and their close contacts, restricting the privacy rights and freedom of movement of this group.

## Stage 1: Onset of Contestation over the TraceTogether App (March 2020)

As the pandemic escalated, the government focused on improving the effectiveness of its contact tracing efforts, which were initially imprecise and reached only 40% to 60% of contacts (Baharudin & Wong, 2020). The government developed an app called TraceTogether to be used by all Singaporeans, “to detect further cases and prevent further possible spread of the virus,” therefore drawing a symbolic boundary around all inhabitants of the country because they were potentially at risk of spreading the virus (Gov.sg, 2020a).

The TraceTogether app used Bluetooth to detect nearby phones. When the phones detected each other, they exchanged the encrypted data on individuals who had been within two meters for at least 30 minutes. To avoid potential privacy concerns, the government argued that the app would store the fully encrypted data locally on the person’s phone, and data older than 21 days would be deleted for noninfected people (Gov.sg, 2020). The data from the app was planned to be shared with central authorities only after an individual tested positive. The government envisioned that the population would use the app voluntarily, and once 75% of the population adopted it, efficient contact tracing would be achieved (Chong, 2020).

Contestation over the TraceTogehter app: As soon as individuals started adopting the app, an issue arose with one of the design choices: The continuous use of Bluetooth technology for scanning for other app users quickly drained phone batteries (Baharudin & Wong, 2020). Domestic media highlighted that users could hardly use their phones (their private economic resource) for other purposes. A user reported: “I would not be able to text or use my social media apps if I had to keep it [TraceTogether] running all the time” (Baharudin & Wong, 2020). TraceTogether was also rated 2.5 out of 5.0 in the Apple app store.

The foreign press reported that the slow adoption of TraceTogether had to do with privacy concerns, citing interviews with citizens who did not download the app because they weren’t “entirely persuaded by the government’s assurance that the data was secure” (Lin & Ping, 2020). The foreign media also reported that this concern “reflects the widespread suspicion that increasingly accompanies technological innovations” (Lin & Ping, 2020).

In response, the government issued an announcement arguing that the app did not track people’s whereabouts and that it had been designed with privacy-preserving safeguards (GovTech, 2020). The announcement also emphasized that the mobile number “is the only personal data that Ministry of Health will hold about you, and it is stored in a highly secured server together with a random anonymized User ID that is linked to your mobile number” (GovTech, 2020).

Unfaithful system adoption: Despite the government’s efforts to drive app adoption, as of April 10th, 2020, the TraceTogether app adoption rate reported in the domestic press was only approximately 12%—far below the government’s goal of 75% (Chong, 2020). This made individuals who had not yet adopted the app even less likely to adopt it: “I know it’s a social responsibility to download the app, but that won’t work unless a significant proportion of the population are using it. I’m just not sure if it’s a tradeoff I’m willing to take right now” (Sim & Lim, 2020). As a result, the social boundary (low adoption rate of the app) and the symbolic boundary (all inhabitants should use the app) were misaligned.

## Stage 2: COVID-19 Outbreak among Migrant Workers: Creating a Separate System for Migrant Workers (mid-April–June 2020)

In mid-April, there was a new COVID-19 outbreak among migrant workers (Sim & Kok, 2020). The infection risk within migrant worker dormitories was exacerbated by poor nutrition, limited access to healthcare and personal protective equipment, and overcrowded living conditions (12 to 20 people per room in housing complexes accommodating up to 24,000 migrants) (Ratcliffe, 2020). The outbreak resulted in a fivefold increase in the number of cases in Singapore within two weeks (Sim & Kok, 2020). The authorities imposed a “circuit breaker” (full lockdown) for all Singaporean inhabitants until the end of May and later extended it until the end of June.

The implementation of a full lockdown raised economic concerns (Sukhwal & Kankanhalli, 2022). According to one news article, a government minister lamented that “if you don’t die of the virus, you’ll die of economic stagnation” (Yong, 2020). Some Singaporeans started blaming migrants for the hardship caused by the lockdown, arguing that migrants come from “backward countries with poor hygiene habits like eating with their hands” (Mahtani, 2020).

The government updated contact tracing systems to protect public health in preparation for reopening from the lockdown. At this point, migrant workers were isolated in dorms under lock and key (Mahtani, 2020; Xinghui, 2020). Migrant workers were restricted to their rooms except when using the restroom or showering. Amnesty International argued: “The fact that thousands are now under quarantine in extremely close proximity could be a recipe for disaster unless their basic rights are respected” (Rathborn, 2020).

In preparation for the partial reopening of society, the government invested in new contact tracing systems, specifically targeting migrant workers to allow for some of them to return to work after reopening. Special technologies were developed for this purpose, designated specifically for the migrant workers, that restricted their access to resources (privacy and freedom of movement) “in order for them to return to work safely” (Smart Nation, 2020d). The government developed the SGWorkPass app (a check-in app for entering and leaving dormitories) (Wei, 2020), whereby a worker who received approval from both the government and their employee to resume work and leave the dormitory would see a green AccessCode on their app (Today Online, 2020). Based on the design of the application rules and accompanying procedures, a worker (even if fully healthy) would not be allowed to leave the dorms for any recreational (e.g., exercise) or personal (e.g., grocery shopping) reasons. The government also developed an FWMOMCare app specifically for migrant workers in dorms, which required twice-daily symptom checking and continuous location tracking, with all information from this app shared and stored by authorities and employers (Wei, 2020). As soon as the apps were ready for deployment, the government required migrant workers living in dorms to use both apps. Moreover, the TraceTogether app was mandated for migrant workers because they were living in risky settings, while still being voluntary for Singaporeans: “Migrant workers who stay or work in higher risk settings [dorms] will be required to download, activate, and maintain the latest version of the TraceTogether” (Ministry of Manpower, 2020). The use of these systems was fully enforced by the government, aligning the symbolic and social boundaries around migrant workers.

Eventually, when the government ended the lockdown for Singaporeans, only 20,000 out of 323,000 migrant workers living in dorms were allowed to leave the dorms (Xinghui, 2020), even though the vast majority of them were not at high risk of COVID-19, as many of them had already cleared the virus and were healthy (Geddie, 2020).

Limited contestation around the contact tracing systems for migrant workers: Criticism of the new system for migrant workers was primarily reported in foreign media. It was the foreign press that was putting the spotlight on the fact that the apps were only used to allow migrant workers to leave dorms for work, requiring them to return immediately and prohibiting them from socializing outside of the dorms, even after getting their exit codes (Aravindan, 2020). In an interview with a foreign newspaper, a Singaporean activist noted: “When they are not working, they should be able to relax and socialise freely, as locals expect to do” (Xinghui, 2020). Another activist, in an interview with foreign media, pointed out the exacerbation of the dependency that the apps were creating between the workers and their employees who needed to authorize the exit code: “[This policy] made them prisoners of their employers. It is not a provisional regulation but a permanent law since it is not included in the temporary measures to fight the crisis” (Galache, 2020). Foreign media also argued that using apps to prepare for reopening was a case of restrictive treatment:

Employers will need to track the health status of workers and ensure they comply with the rules. [Migrant workers] must download the TraceTogether contact tracing app and go for regular coronavirus testing…They are so stringent and restrictive that they are bound to be resented by many workers. (Xinghui, 2020)

Privacy International also raised concerns, saying enforcing the use of FWMOMCare on migrant workers was an example of privacy violation (Privacy International, 2020). Foreign media further expressed worries about the possibility of using mandatory wristbands for workers:

Singapore recently began pilot-testing more intrusive virus-tracking technology—asking a group of migrant workers to wear Bluetooth-enabled wristbands that can detect their locations and proximity to one another … Singapore was considering making the tracking wristbands mandatory for hundreds of thousands of its migrant workers. They can mandate it for sure. (Singer, 2020)

Domestic press accounts acknowledged the poor living conditions of migrant workers and reported the rising number of virus cases among migrant workers living in dorms (Today Online, 2020). Moreover, the domestic press pointed out that the COVID-19 crisis had exacerbated the social issues surrounding migrant workers and punctuated the need to address these issues going forward (Sin, 2020). It praised volunteer efforts to help migrant workers in dorms during the crisis, such as NGOs delivering meals, translating, and providing virtual social support for the migrant workers living in dorms (Tai, 2020). At the same time, domestic media largely praised the government’s efforts focused on migrant workers. Specific government officials were praised for speaking up against xenophobia, and the government, in general, was praised for quickly reacting to the spread of the virus in the dorms (e.g., setting up mobile testing centers) (Hui, 2020). Moreover, the domestic press stated that new apps specific to migrant dorms were helpful “health and safety measures before they [migrants] can start working” (Wei, 2020). When the domestic press accounts referred to the statements issued by migrant workers’ advocacy groups, such as Transit Workers Count Too (TWC2) and the Humanitarian Organization for Migration Economics, these accounts acknowledged the poor living conditions of the migrant workers in dorms but then praised the government’s efforts to provide mental health and other support to the workers (Gene, 2020). They did not report the same advocacy groups’ statements criticizing the government for causing these mental health challenges by implementing restrictive systems in the first place (Ratcliffe, 2020). In a foreign outlet, Alex Au, vice president of the TWC2, stated:

Despite the magnitude of the crisis … the situation of the foreign workers has not triggered public outrage … If we had a free press and freedom of information in Singapore, there would be more capacity to press the government for answers and accountability. (Galache, 2020)

As the statement from the Singaporean migrant worker rights activist suggests, the freedom of the press in Singapore may help explain the difference in coverage of the issue between domestic and foreign news sources.

The government updated contact tracing systems to protect public health: In preparation for opening up the society, the government designed new contact tracing systems for all Singaporean inhabitants, striving for a better solution than the voluntary adoption of the TraceTogether app. Key among these technologies was the SafeEntry system, which required visitors to public venues and taxi and ride-sharing passengers to scan a QR code (through the SafeEntry app, SingPass mobile app, or personal ID) (Yi, 2020). The QR scan enabled the authorities to collect personal data and location information. Singapore mandated the installation of SafeEntry in 200,000 venues (e.g., office buildings and schools) and in relevant vehicles. Through the SafeEntry system design, the government drew a new symbolic boundary around all citizens going into public venues, further restricting citizens’ privacy and freedom of movement rights. This system was well adopted, aligning the social boundary (all citizens used SafeEntry when entering public places) with the symbolic boundary.

The government remained concerned, however, with the low rate of adoption of the TraceTogether app (as of April 2020, only 20% of the population used it). While SafeEntry drew a symbolic boundary around people visiting public places and riding in taxis and ride-sharing services, the TraceTogether app was still seen as the key technology with which the government drew a symbolic boundary around all inhabitants going about their daily lives.

“To ensure better coverage for everyone in Singapore for effective contact tracing,” the government designed a selfpowered device called a TraceTogether token that performed the same function as the TraceTogether app but did not drain phone batteries (Smart Nation, 2020b). The token was a rectangular electronic device, that people could put in their pocket or carry on a string around their neck. When the government introduced the token, it argued for the shared value aspirations for individuals to be able to return to work (after the reopening):

The Token has an estimated battery life of 6 to 9 months and does not require any charging. Effective contact tracing is critical as Singapore take steps to open up…This will enable transmissions to be wellcontained, so that we do not have to go back into circuit breaker mode again due to an uncontrolled outbreak. (Smart Nation, 2020b)

The contestation around the TraceTogether token: The design of the TraceTogether token sparked additional contestation, as it restricted privacy and individual freedoms. Both domestic and foreign media reported that an online petition headlined “Singapore says ‘No’ to Wearable Devices for COVID-19 Contact Tracing” got more than 50,000 signatures (Asher, 2020).

The token triggered a fear of being constantly surveilled when wearing it. Foreign media reported that the petition stated:

All that is stopping the Singapore government from becoming a surveillance state is the advent and mandating the compulsory usage of such a wearable device … What comes next would be laws that state these devices must not be turned off [and] remain on a person at all times” (Asher, 2020). The domestic media also stated the government should enact legislation to limit data use to contact tracing purposes only, not for policing. (Tham, 2020)

The government updated the contact tracing system by designating a TraceTogether token for the elderly. To address public concerns and reduce public resistance, the authorities distributed the TraceTogether tokens to senior citizens considered at high risk of infection and having limited access to technology: “Tokens were given out to these seniors who are not digitally connected and are physically frail, living alone or have poor family support” (Smart Nation, 2020c). This action drew a new symbolic boundary around a minority group. As TraceTogether app use remained voluntary for nonmigrant workers, the social boundary (people who used the app or token) and the symbolic boundary (all citizens) remained misaligned.

Stage 3: The Government Bundled the SafeEntry and TraceTogether System and Mandated Their Use (July–September 2020)

By July to September 2020, COVID-19 infection numbers in Singapore were only increasing in migrant worker communities. Until September, migrant workers’ dormitories remained under quarantine and residents were not allowed to enjoy any leisure time outside (Xinghui & Sim, 2020). The confinement of migrant workers led to several suicides, raising concerns about their mental health (Geddie, 2020). Meanwhile, the government was focused on curbing the spread of the virus by updating its contact tracing systems for different risk groups and settings.

The government updated the contract tracing system for quarantining: The government enhanced contact tracing systems for incoming travelers, creating two different systems for incoming travelers, including migrant workers, and others. Incoming migrant workers had to use a smartwatch device that tracked and reported their GPS location and heart rate using biosensors (as a general health indicator) (Zhuo, 2020b). Other incoming travelers had to wear a wristband that calculated the distance between a Bluetooth “gateway” device plugged into the wall to indicate compliance with quarantine rules (Zhuo, 2020a). Each system designated a different symbolic boundary: incoming travelers were all viewed as a high-risk group for spreading COVID-19, but migrant workers were designated as posing the highest risk and had less privacy, with technology specifically targeting their group. The government enforced the mandatory use of both technologies without contestation, which aligned symbolic and social boundaries.

The government updated the contract tracing system, further restricting privacy rights: The government redesigned the TraceTogether app by changing how Bluetooth technology was used to significantly reduce battery drainage (Smart Nation, 2020a). The app update, however, also included collecting additional personal information (e.g., national identification and passport numbers), unlike the initial design, which only collected mobile numbers (Tham & Yee, 2020). The data collection change was justified by the government as: “[TraceTogether] facilitates quick and accurate contact tracing” (Smart Nation, 2020a).

Contestation continued over the TraceTogether app: The foreign press reported that citizens questioned why they should use the TraceTogether app with or without the tokens when SafeEntry already worked well, and the number of COVID-19 cases had decreased (Sim & Lim, 2020). The government admitted that it was too early to determine whether the TraceTogether app was helpful for contact tracing and argued that the voluntary adoption rate was too low to judge its efficacy (Hwa, 2020). Still, the adoption rate remained low.

The government updated the contact tracing system design by bundling SafeEntry with TraceTogether, effectively mandating the adoption of both: In September 2020, the government integrated SafeEntry with the TraceTogether system for more convenient check-in. This system mandated that all inhabitants check in with the TraceTogether app or token at venues where SafeEntry was installed. The app users had to scan QR codes with their mobile phones, and token users had to get venue staff to check them in. To boost the adoption of the new system, the government announced that bans on social gatherings and other restrictions would be lifted only if the “TraceTogether program ha[d] a participation rate of about 70 percent” (Gov.sg, 2020b). This announcement helped achieve a greater alignment between the symbolic boundary drawn by the government (all citizens should use the system) and the social boundary (the number of citizens using the system). The adoption rate increased for the TraceTogether system, surpassing its target (75%) (Meah, 2020). The adoption of the TraceTogether app was hailed by the foreign and domestic press as a key mechanism for curbing the spread of COVID-19 (Bloomberg, 2020; Cheng, 2020). However, in an interview with foreign media, one citizen explained the implication of the new policy: “It will hardly be a choice— buying food or perhaps even going to the doctor will require TraceTogether” (Stevens, 2020).

## Discussion

## Conceptualizing the Technology-Centric Contestation Process

Our analysis revealed that Korean and Singaporean societies experienced multiple episodes of contestation over contact tracing system design choices and adoption outcomes in light of often conflicting societal value aspirations, including social justice. Societal value aspirations, as important but competing ideals that bind each society together, underlie government policy, journalistic discourse, and people’s behavior. These value aspirations are not the same across different societies and for different societal groups, even within the same society. For example, the value aspirations for social justice for citizens versus migrants in a given society may be quite different (Taran, 2000). Journalistic discourse serves as a key mechanism for debating priorities among conflicting value aspirations while reporting on the government’s actions and on people’s behavior. Building on this observation, we propose a theoretical model of this technology-centric contestation process, which shows how journalistic discourse plays a part in shaping and reshaping the symbolic and social boundaries demarcated by the design and adoption of IS. During the COVID-19 pandemic, governments drew symbolic boundaries and designed IS to redistribute access to resources according to these boundaries. In both of our cases, government-designed IS (a specific contact tracing system) restricted access to resources (e.g., privacy rights) in a way that prioritized the societal value aspiration to protect public health over the aspiration to protect individual freedom. Such prioritization is consistent with our understanding of Eastern cultures, where the collective good is often prioritized over private interests and freedoms (Hofstede, 2011).

Through journalistic discourse, diverse stakeholders argued for or against the symbolic boundaries and IS design choices proposed by their government. Often, tensions over societal values were widely voiced in both domestic and foreign journalistic discourse. These tensions reflected the contestation between different stakeholders (e.g., civil rights activists opposed the government-imposed symbolic boundaries and IS design choices, while public health proponents and government officials justified their choices). Stakeholders debated conflicting societal value aspirations, arguing that contact tracing systems fell short of some of these values.

We observed four types of tensions where journalistic discourse discussed competing societal value aspirations by criticizing the government for (1) inappropriately drawing symbolic boundaries around the wrong group (e.g., in Korea, quarantining members of a religious group rather than people specifically exposed to COVID-19); (2) drawing symbolic boundaries that were too broad (e.g., in Korea, the use of electronic bracelets to all quarantining individuals instead of those who violated quarantine); (3) excessively limiting resource access (e.g., wearable devices were seen as too invasive in both Korea and Singapore); (4) not limiting access to resources enough (e.g., in Korea, not locking up religious group members).

In several episodes, tensions over societal values were not widely voiced. In some instances, the press, especially the domestic press, was fully supportive of the government’s contract tracing systems. For example, in Korea, most domestic journalists were supportive of the government’s actions vis-à- vis the religious group, even though some foreign journalists were voicing concerns about the violations of individual rights. Similarly, in Singapore, foreign journalists’ and domestic activists’ criticisms of the government’s actions vis-à-vis migrant workers were not echoed in the domestic press. While domestic news sources supported the government’s actions in these instances, foreign journalists, appealing to value aspirations upheld in other societies (e.g., Western values), criticized Korean or Singaporean governments, for example, for prioritizing public health over individual freedoms.

Our data suggests that the journalistic discourse shapes how the government redraws symbolic boundaries and (re)designs IS. This was often the case when tensions were widely voiced in journalistic discourse. For example, when the Singaporean news media criticized the government’s proposal to use tokens for inhabitants, it pushed the government to restrict token use to senior citizens and children. In other cases, despite the tensions being widely voiced, the government stayed the course and engaged primarily in rhetorical actions, entering the journalistic discourse by giving interviews and issuing press releases that justified their actions. For example, the Singaporean government continued to promote TraceTogether without addressing the battery drain issue in the first two phases.

Journalistic discourse also shapes IS adoption and the resulting alignment or misalignment of social and symbolic boundaries based on the faithful or unfaithful adoption of IS by diverse stakeholders. Social boundaries are produced when different stakeholders adopt IS. Social and symbolic boundaries are aligned when stakeholders faithfully adopt IS (according to designers’ intentions). When the journalistic discourse supports the government’s prioritization among competing societal value aspirations without voicing tensions, users may be influenced by such discourse and faithfully adopt the proposed technologies. This faithful adoption results in the (re)distribution of resources among social groups as the government has envisioned. However, even when tensions are widely voiced in journalistic discourse, faithful adoption may emerge, for example, when governmental enforcement is strong. This was observed when Singaporeans adopted TraceTogether bundled with SafeEntry during the third phase despite the privacy concerns voiced by the press.

Social and symbolic boundaries become misaligned when users unfaithfully adopt IS (e.g., LGBTQ+ members and, later, other members of Korean society refusing COVID-19 testing). This process may be exacerbated by the prominent voicing of the tensions involved in faithful adoption by the domestic press. In some cases, however, symbolic and social boundaries become misaligned even if tensions are not widely voiced in the journalistic discourse, as users reject or misuse IS for a variety of reasons, including personal priorities among conflicting societal value aspirations (e.g., some incoming travelers to Korea did not faithfully adopt the quarantine monitoring app because they prioritized their freedom of movement above public health).

Social boundaries produce certain societal outcomes, including those around social (in)justice, public health, and individual freedoms, which are continuously evaluated through journalistic discourse. The journalistic discourse debated ways in which these outcomes met or fell short of societal value aspirations. For example, when the Korean government, attempting to address public health concerns, raided the religious group headquarters, first foreign journalists and later domestic journalists argued that such governmental actions resulted in social injustice.

To summarize, the technology-centric contestation process model illustrates the dynamics through which societal outcomes (including social justice) associated with the design and adoption of IS (hence, technology-centric) are produced and evaluated by journalistic discourse. The government prioritizes conflicting societal values when drawing symbolic boundaries as part of its IS design choices, which triggers potential contestation in journalistic discourse. Tensions widely voiced in the press may influence the government’s IS design choices (redrawing the symbolic boundaries and associated resource restrictions) and the ways in which users adopt IS (aligning or misaligning symbolic and social boundaries). This process produces societal outcomes that are evaluated in the journalistic discourse in light of conflicting societal value aspirations. This, in turn, gives rise to a new wave of technology-centric contestation.

## Digitized Social Justice: Critical Dilemma in IS Design Choices

The technology-centric contestation process gives rise to some critical dilemmas about IS design choices when they produce a social distinction by distributing resources disproportionately across social groups. First, contact tracing systems pose a dilemma about information visibility. On the one hand, sharing private information with the public and the government may help protect inhabitants from health threats such as the COVID-19 virus. On the other hand, information collection and sharing may be weaponized against specific social groups (e.g., blaming minority groups in Korea) and used against individuals’ interests (e.g., use of private information for policing). Second, societies face a dilemma about the use of private technology for collective purposes. Individual self-interest and collective interests come into conflict when the government asks individuals to use their personal digital devices for public purposes (e.g., the battery drain issue in Singapore arose when residents felt taxed by the government regarding the use of their private phones). Third, societies face a dilemma around the use of intrusive wearable devices. On the one hand, governments may try to improve public health outcomes (e.g., reduce quarantine violations) by introducing wearable digital devices such as electronic bracelets and tokens. In our study, wearable digital devices were seen as a convenient alternative to taxing private resources (e.g., mobile phones). On the other hand, a device worn on the body creates a physical burden (a sense of intrusion) and a symbolic distinction with negative connotations (e.g., prisoners wear anklets). Digital technologies open up a new set of possibilities for handling the tensions between collective and private interests, as compared to the pre-digital options. Old-fashioned ways of enforcing compliance with the government’s public safety policies either involved a full lockdown of all people enforced by the police (as was the case in China in the early days of the pandemic) or locking up individuals that could pose a risk to public safety (e.g., a guarded hospital ward). Both options incur significant budgetary expenditures and severe restrictions on individual freedoms. The alternative is to give up on policy enforcement altogether and accept a significant decrease in public safety (as was the case in the U.S. and European countries, where many succumbed to the virus during the pandemic). Although digital technologies create new dilemmas, they nonetheless offer some protection for public health while reducing the costs of policy enforcement and allowing for lighter restrictions on personal freedoms.

## The Role of Journalistic Discourse in Striving for Social Justice

Our theoretical framework highlights the role of journalistic discourse in voicing social injustice. While journalistic discourse is only one of many factors (legal and political regimes being others) involved in fighting social injustice, it plays a key role nonetheless. Journalistic discourse reflects and influences value aspirations within each society (Kopytowska, 2015) and often shapes the government’s actions and people’s behaviors (Wagemans et al., 2019).

In both of our cases, the domestic news media reflected that the societal value aspiration to protect public health was more important than preserving individual freedoms. Many Eastern societies prioritize the collective good over individual benefits (Hofstede, 2011), and these priorities were clearly reflected in the domestic journalistic discourse. This was not the case during the COVID-19 pandemic in many Western societies, where journalistic discourse emphasized individual freedoms over public health (Rowe, 2020). Yet despite a general consensus to prioritize public health, in both Singapore and Korea, journalistic discourse also reflected each society’s aspirations to preserve individual freedom, at least for their citizens, and avoid social injustice when it is possible to do so without sacrificing public health. Thus, the journalistic discourse in both countries gave voice to tensions around conflicting societal values, and the governments of both countries strove to respond to the tensions widely voiced in the domestic press.

Despite these similarities, our comparison between Korea and Singapore suggests the need to further investigate how different levels of freedom of the press may shape the public contestation process. The freedom of the press has long been associated with struggles for social justice (Jansen et al., 2011). In our study, we saw that the domestic Singaporean press raised tensions pertaining to social injustice towards migrant workers, while the government was not criticized for its technology design choices and related policies. More research is needed to investigate if there is a direct link between the freedom of the press in Singapore (Reporters without Borders, 2021) and the domestic press’s reluctance to criticize the government.

Alternatively, rather than freedom of the press, the explanation for the limited coverage of the tensions pertaining to migrant workers in the domestic press may have to do with a difference in Singaporean society’s value aspirations to achieve social justice for all versus just for citizens. Because social justice tensions raised in the Korean domestic press pertained to citizens and because the Korean population is more homogeneous, consisting of only 2% noncitizens, we do not know whether the Korean press would have been as vocal in voicing social injustices experienced by noncitizens, nor do we know if the Korean government would have been willing to change technology design and policy if the tensions had pertained to noncitizens. Thus, the relationship between journalistic discourse and societal value aspirations for noncitizen groups is also worthy of further research.

## Contributions

Our research contributes to IS literature on social justice by developing a technology-centric social justice process model that highlights the importance of discourse in shaping social justice outcomes. The symbolic and social boundaries lens helped us understand how IS design involves drawing symbolic boundaries and how IS adoptions involve producing social boundaries. We then theorized how the technologycentric contestation process unfolds as stakeholders argue for and against symbolic and social boundaries drawn through the design and adoption of IT artifacts. Our model shows that the design and adoption of IT artifacts are central to the production of social distinctions and the skewed redistribution of resources in society. Our work complements prior literature on the role of IS in social justice, which considers IS either as a medium where social injustice is propagated (e.g., Chan & Wang, 2018) or challenged (e.g., Miranda et al., 2016; Selander & Jarvenpaa, 2016) or as a resource in its own right that is able to elevate (e.g., Diaz Andrade & Doolin, 2016) or denigrate marginalized social groups (e.g., Hsieh et al., 2008). We show how IS design and adoption serve to (re)distribute access to material and nonmaterial resources among social groups through IS use, sometimes exacerbating existing social inequality.

Our research further contributes to the IS literature on social justice by revealing the complexity of striving for social justice when incorporating digital technologies into public policy while also trying to live up to other societal value aspirations. The case of the COVID-19 contact tracing systems allowed us to focus on how technology design and adoption become a battleground when striving for social justice comes into conflict with the collective efforts to protect public health. Technologycentric contestation can become quite arduous as it involves debating multiple IS design options and the appropriateness of the symbolic boundaries being drawn. It is the malleability of IS design that opens up a larger space for contestation, but it is also this malleability that presents an opportunity to address the everlasting societal tensions in innovative ways. During the COVID-19 pandemic, many Western societies gave up instead of facing the challenges presented by this arduous process headon. They prioritized individual freedom and lowered their aspirations to protect public health, losing many lives in the process (Han et al., 2020).

We further contribute to the IS literature on social justice by unpacking critical dilemmas about IS design choices in relation to social justice outcomes. The arduous process of designing technology to improve public safety while respecting individual freedom gave rise to three dilemmas: the dilemma of information visibility, the dilemma of the use of private technology for collective purposes, and the dilemma of the use of digital wearable devices. Indeed, digital options allow for a more nuanced set of trade-offs than previously available—i.e., we don’t have to physically lock up everybody posing a threat to public safety. Yet the new dilemmas may trigger new contestation processes, where individuals try to protect their interests and avoid stigma by, for example, refusing to adopt technologies as prescribed. If the choices made by governments become widely contested, governments may respond with their own counteractions. For example, governments might direct technology designers to redesign IT artifacts to reduce the stigma and inconvenience associated with their use (Caldeira et al., 2022; Chandra et al., 2020), or they may redraw boundaries around IS use to increase individual freedom (e.g., mandate bracelets only for quarantine violators). Further research is needed to investigate how different public planners address the dilemma we have identified and what other dilemmas may arise, especially around information sharing with public and private stakeholders.

We also contribute to the IS literature on boundaries by proposing how the lens of symbolic and social boundaries can be used to understand (1) the production of social inequalities and (2) how the technology-centric contestation process unfolds. IS literature on boundaries has focused on knowledge processes and the power relations that these processes entail (Leonardi et al., 2019; Levina & Vaast, 2008; Orlikowski, 2002). This literature argues that knowledge, organizational, and professional boundaries shape the IS design processes (Levina & Vaast 2005) and that IS uses, in turn, shape boundaries (Levina & Vaast 2005; Leonardi 2019; Barrett et al. 2012). We add to this body of work by showing how boundaries are drawn through the IS design process and enacted in IS adoption in a way that may create or exacerbate social inequalities. Moreover, by using Lamont and Molnár’s (2002) distinction between symbolic and social boundaries, we add an important nuance to the IS literature on boundaries, which has tended to focus on enacted (social) boundaries rather than on envisioned symbolic ones.

We also contribute to IS literature by highlighting the role of discourse in shaping IS design choices and adoption processes. While much has been written about how users resist IS implementation through their actions (Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009), the IS literature is less focused on how users and others advocating on their behalf may engage in discourse that pushes back on IS design choices by appealing to shared cultural value aspirations. Moreover, while the unfaithful adoption of IS as a form of resistance is well-documented in the literature (e.g., Avgerou & McGrath, 2007; Brown et al., 2002; Smith et al., 2010), the lens of symbolic and social boundaries helps us better understand the role of discourse in linking tensions among value aspirations during design and adoption. We add to the IS literature that treats discourse as a medium of communication (e.g., around social movement) (e.g., Miranda et al., 2015; Vaast et al., 2017), a source of data about a phenomenon (Sukhwal & Kankanhalli, 2022), or a way to shape IT diffusion (Barrett et al., 2013) by showing that discourse is the place where social justice outcomes related to IS design and use are contested.

Finally, building on this insight, our analysis foregrounds journalistic discourse as a central space in which IS design and adoption take shape when the government is designing technologies for public use. Our analysis suggests that cultural context plays a key role in shaping journalistic discourse around IS design and adoption, as societal value aspirations debated in journalistic discourse are deeply embedded in the culture. This technology-centric contestation and, eventually, its societal outcomes are shaped not only by similarities in culture (e.g., Eastern cultural values of collective harmony) but also by differences in national contexts (Medaglia et al., 2022; Rowe et al., 2020). Our work suggests that more research is needed both on the role of journalistic discourse in IS design and adoption and on the role of cultural context in shaping discourse on technology design and adoption (Meier & Dopson, 2019). Moreover, more work is needed to better understand how narrow vs. wide coverage in foreign vs. domestic media shapes discourses on technology.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study focused on journalistic media sources to theorize the technology-centric contestation process and did not use social media such as Twitter and Facebook, which currently also serve as a barometer of public opinion (Miranda et al., 2016; Vaast et al., 2017). While we chose this focus to limit fake news stories, which are more prominent on social media (Shirish et al. 2020), future research on the technology-centric contestation processes should compare journalistic discourse with social media discourse. Moreover, even within our focus on journalistic discourse, we have inferred the government’s response as well as the user’s adoption decisions from the journalistic accounts. Future research should address this limitation by collecting primary data from the actors involved in the design of public technologies as well as from the users making sense of and employing these technologies.

We speculated that freedom of the press may be an important factor in the contestation process around the design and adoption of new public technologies. However, our study focused on only two countries, so future research is needed to investigate how freedom of the press may shape the contestation processes surrounding public technologies in a broader sample of countries and with different technologies. Moreover, future research should delve deeper into whether restrictions on the freedom of the press shape social justicerelated outcomes more heavily than other types of outcomes (e.g., public health or individual freedoms).

Finally, our study suggests that the contestation process may unfold differently when social injustice concerns noncitizens vs. citizens. Noncitizens often have less of a voice in society than citizens. Indeed, not only Singaporean migrant workers but also Korean migrant workers experience a wide variety of social injustices (Amnesty, 2006). Hence, future research is warranted to explore more broadly the differences in the technology-centric contestation process when it comes to citizens vs. noncitizens. For example, it is important to learn more about whether the societal value aspirations for social justice differ for noncitizens and what role they play in the dilemmas surrounding technology design choices.

## Conclusion

Technologies are often seen as solutions to social problems and potential enablers of social justice. Indeed, technologies provide access to information and other valuable resources for people who may not otherwise have such access. Yet technologies that are designed to address social problems can, in practice, reproduce or reinforce social inequalities because societal value aspirations for social justice may come into conflict with other societal value aspirations, such as public health, economic stability, and national security. Designers and policymakers develop and implement technologies to support specific societal values and are often unable or unwilling to account for the impact of these technologies on social justice. This leads to technology becoming central to cultural discourse, where conflicting societal values are debated in relation to technology design and adoption. Journalism, and especially independent journalism, plays a key role in voicing tensions between the conflicting values at stake. This contestation process shapes and reshapes technology designers’ choices and users’ adoption decisions. Through this complex technology-centric contestation process, each society must confront the question of how much social justice matters to its members and whether they are willing to commit to this value, even if it comes at a cost to other important value aspirations.

## About the Authors

Semi Min received her Ph.D. from New York University Stern School of Business and is currently an assistant professor at the Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota. Her main research interests are in understanding the adoption of emerging technologies in society and how discourse plays a role in these processes. Currently, her research explores theories of smart contracts and the adoption of blockchain and contact tracing technologies using both qualitative and quantitative methodological approaches.

Natalia Levina received her Ph.D. in information technology from MIT’s Sloan School of Management and is a Paganelli-Bull Professor at New York University Stern School of Business. She is also a part-time research environment professor at the Warwick Business School. Her main research interest centers on how people span organizational, professional, cultural, and other boundaries while developing and using new technologies. Currently, her research explores the adoption of AI in organizations, open innovation practices, platform governance and blockchain technologies, and the epistemic underpinnings of innovation practices. She has served in a number of editorial positions at Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, Organization Science, and Information & Organization. She co-founded and has chaired the AIS Special Interest Group (SIG) on the Grounded Theory Method.

Hila Lifshitz is a professor of management at Warwick Business School and a visiting faculty at Harvard University, at the Lab for Innovation Science (LISH). She is heading the Artificial Intelligence Innovation Network at WBS. Her research focuses on developing an in-depth empirical and theoretical understanding of the microfoundations of scientific and technological innovation and knowledge creation processes in the digital age. She explores how the ability to innovate is being transformed, as well as the challenges and opportunities the transformation means for organizations, professionals, and their work. She received her doctorate from Harvard Business School, based on an in-depth three-year longitudinal field study of NASA’s experimentation with open innovation online platforms and communities, resulting in a scientific breakthrough. Her recent research focuses on the future of knowledge work—in particular, the use of AI for innovation as well as critical decision.
