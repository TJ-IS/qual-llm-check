## Abstract

As firms increasingly engage in interorganizational information systems (IS) projects, including IS offshoring projects, the challenge for client firms of effectively exercising control across organizational boundaries becomes critical. Although the importance of informal controls (clan and self-control) in this context has been recognized, prior research has focused primarily on formal controls. Consequently, our understanding of the use of clan and self-control in interorganizational IS projects is scarce, and partly inconsistent or contradictory. This study focuses the client’s role in promoting informal controls in interorganizational projects involving distant client–vendor relationships as well as the effectiveness of such controls. We use matched-pair survey data from 86 IS offshoring projects involving client and vendor relationship managers. Our results show an interesting pattern: while clan control is considerably more difficult to promote than self-control in client–vendor project relationships, only clan control has a direct positive impact on project performance. Moreover, formal control modes, national cultural values, and project context factors moderate both the promotion and the effectiveness of informal controls. Our study’s main contributions lie in establishing the relevance of the distinction between informal control given and received, and the importance of the client’s role in promoting informal controls. ## Introduction

Organizations engage in interorganizational information systems (IS) projects, including IS offshoring projects, to gain productivity as well as competitiveness improvements. Such improvements are related to bene<sup>fi</sup>ting from economies of scale and scope on the part of the vendor, substantially lower labour costs in the vendor country, but also accessing and harnessing vendor expertise in addressing challenging IS tasks (Dibbern et al, 2008; Tiwana & Keil, 2009). A necessary prerequisite for reaping the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of projects involving vendors is that the client organization is able to effectively exercise control over these projects (e.g., Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Gopal & Gosain, 2010). Control consists of activities intended to motivate individuals to act in ways consistent with project objectives and is exercised through formal and informal control mechanisms (Ouchi, 1980; Kirsch et al, 2002). Formal controls involve the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and evaluation of desired behaviours and outcomes (Ouchi, 1979), whereas informal controls rely on social or people-based strategies (Kirsch et al, 2002; Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003). Although formal mechanisms often dominate the control portfolio, there is strong consensus on the importance of informal control mechanisms (Kirsch, 2004; Kohli & Kettinger, 2004; Jaworski, 1988; Chua et al, 2012). For instance, Kirsch (2004) shows that the addition of informal controls to the control portfolio can help mitigate cultural and priority differences between organizational units in the context of globally distributed IS projects. At the same time, these as well as other typical characteristics of IS offshoring projects (e.g., geographic and time zone differences) increase project complexity, making effective control of interorganizational IS offshore projects a highly challenging task (Mahnke et al, 2008; Tiwana & Keil, 2009).

Despite the importance of informal control in interorganizational IS projects, many prior studies on outsourcing and offshoring projects exclusively focus on formal control modes (e.g., Rustagi et al, 2008; Tiwana, 2008). In contrast, research on the use of informal control, that is, clan and self-control, in client–vendor relationships remains sparse and has produced mixed results. Two gaps in prior research are particularly noteworthy. First, little is known about the client’s challenge of promoting informal control in interorganizational project settings. Previous studies primarily rely on data reported by the vendor/controllee (e.g., Tiwana & Keil, 2009; Gopal & Gosain, 2010; Srivastava & Teo, 2012), and thus may not accurately capture the client’s promotion of informal controls in the client–vendor relationship. Consequently, we know little about how and to what extent informal controls promoted by the client are actually perceived by the vendor (cf. Ouchi, 1978). Second, in addition to the fact that many studies investigating the effectiveness of controls in IS offshoring project contexts exclude informal controls, even studies that address informal controls exclude self-control from their enquiry (e.g., Gopal & Gosain, 2010; Tiwana, 2010; Srivastava & Teo, 2012). Hence, the link between self-control and offshore project performance remains understudied (Srivastava & Teo, 2012). This is an important gap since ‘there are contexts where these controls have a strong role to play in determining project performance’ (Gopal & Gosain, 2010, p. 19). Furthermore, studies are inconclusive as to whether clan control has a positive (Gopal & Gosain, 2010), a negative (Tiwana, 2010), or no effect (Tiwana & Keil, 2009) on performance in (offshore) outsourced IS projects.

We address the two research gaps outlined above by conducting a quantitative study using matched-pair data from 86 IS offshoring projects. By collecting data from both the client and vendor side of the control dyad, we aim to contribute to a more nuanced understanding regarding the promotion and effectiveness of informal controls in IS offshore project relationships. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our study focuses on the important control relationship between the client (controller) and vendor (controllee) representative managing the interface between the two organizations. Key responsibilities of these managers include boundary spanning, gatekeeping, and mediating activities (Mahnke et al, 2008). They therefore play ‘a critical role in the overall management and ultimate success of offshore IS projects’ (Rai et al, 2009, p. 635).

To study the client manager’s role in promoting informal controls as well as to explain prior inconclusive results regarding the effectiveness of informal controls in client– vendor control relationships, we draw on Ouchi’s (1978) notion of control transmission, that is, the distinction between control given and control received. Here, it is important to note that the nature of control transmission for informal controls is quite different compared with control transmission for formal controls (Ouchi, 1978). In the case of formal controls, it is the controller (client manager) who speci<sup>fi</sup>es, monitors, and evaluates desired behaviours and outcomes, and thus exercises control over the controllee (vendor manager). In contrast, in the case of informal controls, the controller can only promote such controls, for instance, by establishing an environment conducive to the exercise of clan and self-control. However, it is actually the controllee who exercises clan and self-control, respectively.

In the next section, we review and discuss prior literature on the promotion and effectiveness of informal control in client–vendor relationships and relate it to the concept of control transmission. We then develop the research model and hypotheses. Subsequently, we describe the research methodology and present our data analysis results. We conclude by discussing the article’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings and implications.

## Theoretical background

## Informal control promotion

Control theory typically differentiates between two types or modes of informal control: clan control and self-control (e.g., Kirsch, 1996, 1997). Kirsch (1997) de<sup>fi</sup>nes a clan ‘as a group of individuals who are dependent on one another and who share a set of common goals’ (p. 217). Clan control operates when behaviours are motivated by shared beliefs, norms, and values and a common vision, as well as when individuals display a great deal of solidarity and regularity in their relationships with others by behaving in a manner that is consistent with agreed-upon behaviours (Ouchi, 1979; Kirsch et al, 2010). Examples of clan control mechanisms include the use of socialization strategies (e.g., dinner meetings and other social events) as well as regular joint project meetings and trainings (Kirsch, 1996; Kirsch et al, 2002). Corresponding mechanisms aim to promulgate common norms and values (Kirsch, 1997), or to identify and enforce acceptable behaviours through shared experiences and rituals (Ouchi, 1980).

Some scholars argue that clan control is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to promote in outsourced IS projects (e.g., Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Tiwana & Keil, 2009). The argument is that individuals from independent organizations are unlikely to embrace shared norms and values that are necessary to enact clan control (Tiwana & Keil, 2009). In our study, however, we conceptualize the controller and controllee as the client and vendor manager operating at the client–vendor interface of an IS offshoring project. In such settings, the client and vendor manager need to be in frequent and continuous interaction, for instance, to bridge expertise and knowledge domain asymmetries between the involved organizations (Levina, 2005; Gopal & Gosain, 2010). Adding to this, interface managers on both the client and vendor side usually possess considerable IT expertise. This shared professional frame of reference, together with frequent interaction, increases the client manager’s ability to understand the vendor counterpart’s goals, norms, and values, and thus the ability to promote clan control (Kirsch et al, 2002). In this study, we therefore view clan control as the mechanisms the client manager uses to cultivate a work environment with the vendor contact conducive to aligning behaviours. In such an environment, shared norms and values supportive to the project are retained and reinforced, whereas norms and values that impede the achievement of project goals are inhibited (Chua et al, 2012). For example, client managers can actively participate in project meetings or promote mutual site visits in order to cultivate shared goals, norms, and values.

Unlike clan control, self-control is a function of intrinsic motivation (Manz et al, 1987) as well as individual standards and objectives ( Jaworski, 1988). In self-control, the controllee de<sup>fi</sup>nes both the goals and the actions required to achieve these goals (Henderson & Lee, 1992; Kirsch, 1996). Thus, control mechanisms supporting self-control are primarily implemented by the individual controllee (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003). Consequently, prior studies on IS (offshore) outsourcing projects often exclude self-control, arguing that self-control mechanisms are initiated internally by the vendor (e.g., Tiwana & Keil, 2009; Gopal & Gosain, 2010; Tiwana, 2010). However, prior literature also suggests that the controller can promote, that is, enable, encourage, or request the controllee to exercise self-control by appropriately structuring the work environment (Kirsch, 1996; Kirsch et al, 2002; Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003). This perspective takes into account Kirsch’s (1996) observation that controllers may need to intervene to ensure that controllees engage in self-control. For example, clients may review and select vendor project personnel, give recommendations for software testing procedures, and demand for greater involvement by the vendor’s senior management, thereby promoting the vendor’s employment of self-control (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003).

To better understand the client’s role in promoting informal control in interorganizational IS projects, we use Ouchi’s (1978) concept of control transmission as a theoretical lens. This concept builds on the distinction between control given and control received. In our study, control given is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree of informal control promotion as perceived by the client manager (controller), whereas control received is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree of informal control promotion as perceived by the vendor manager (controllee). Here, it is important to note that both client and vendor perceptions relate to the same set of informal control mechanisms, namely, those promoted by the client manager. By drawing on Ouchi’s control transmission concept, we are able to measure the degree to which informal control mechanisms transmit accurately between client and vendor manager, that is, the consistency with which informal controls given (i.e., promoted) by the client manager are also received by the vendor counterpart.

## Informal control effectiveness

Although the importance of informal control is widely recognized, still relatively little is known about the effectiveness of clan and self-controls in IS (offshore) outsourcing projects. First, many studies solely focus on the performance effects of formal controls (e.g., Tiwana & Keil, 2007; Tiwana, 2008). Second, those studies that look at informal control mostly focus on clan control (e.g., Gopal & Gosain, 2010; Tiwana, 2010; Srivastava & Teo, 2012; Wiener et al, 2012). In fact, only few studies include both informal clan and self-control in their enquiry (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Tiwana & Keil, 2009; Keil et al, 2013). Third, prior studies looking at the effectiveness of clan control in interorganizational IS projects show inconclusive and even contradictory results. For example, while Srivastava & Teo (2012) report that clan control positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences both project ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (in terms of costs) and project quality, Gopal & Gosain (2010) <sup>fi</sup>nd only a positive link between clan control and project quality but a negative relationship between clan control and project ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in offshore outsourced contexts. Furthermore, Tiwana (2010) <sup>fi</sup>nds that clan control decreases project performance (measured as a combination of goal alignment and project adaptiveness) in IS outsourcing. In contrast, Tiwana & Keil (2009) <sup>fi</sup>nd no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between clan control and IS outsourcing project performance although controllers seem to make greater use of clan control in outsourced settings as compared with internal settings.

To explain differences in control effectiveness between internal and outsourced IS projects, Tiwana & Keil (2009) introduce the conceptual distinction between attempted and realized control. This distinction is not only closely related to Ouchi’s (1978) conceptualization of control transmission (i.e., the distinction between control given and received); it may also offer an explanation for some of the inconsistent results outlined above. Attempted control refers to control mechanisms given by the controller, ‘independent of whether or how they are exercised’ (Tiwana & Keil, 2009, p. 13). Realized control refers to the extent to which given controls are successfully exercised, that is, received and implemented by the controllee. In other words, Tiwana & Keil (2009) argue that only those control mechanisms, which are perceived and put into action by the vendor manager, translate into improved project performance.

However, empirical evidence for the link between the successful transmission or promotion of informal control and superior performance remains absent. In the following, we therefore examine the effectiveness of informal control by speci<sup>fi</sup>cally testing for the link between clan and self-control received and IS offshoring project performance. Project performance is typically characterized in terms of efficiency and quality (e.g., Barki et al, 2001; Wallace et al, 2004; Gopal & Gosain, 2010). The ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency dimension addresses whether a project is on budget and time, that is, process performance (Nidumolu & Subramani, 2003), whereas the quality dimension covers the degree to which a project meets client requirements and adheres to IS standards (Kirsch, 1996), which is also referred to as product performance (Henderson & Lee, 1992). To account for potentially inverse effects of informal control on performance dimensions (Gopal & Gosain, 2010), we will test for the effects of informal control received on both project efficiency and quality.

## Hypotheses development

## Informal control promotion in IS offshoring projects

Previous research has found that deployment of clan control fosters social cohesion and norm-based alignment of behaviours in (IS) project teams, facilitating project team success (Kirsch 1996, 1997; Ouchi, 1980; Chua et al, 2012). Recent studies show that clan control can be successfully promoted not only in internal project contexts (Kirsch et al, 2010) but also in distant offshore settings (Persson et al, 2012). When successfully promoted, clan control may reduce the need for formal (behaviour) control, which is often viewed as a costly and inef<sup>fi</sup>cient form of control in offshore outsourced projects (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Gopal & Gosain, 2010).

Prior studies have also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several challenges related to the use of clan control in offshore contexts. For example, the client and vendor manager may come from different occupational and cultural backgrounds, work from geographically dispersed locations (Dibbern et al, 2008), and differ in their professional training (Levina & Vaast, 2008). These different types of ‘distances’ may hamper the promotion of clan control in offshore IS projects. However, given that the focus of our study is on the primary control relationship between the client and vendor representatives who are responsible for managing the interorganizational interface of an IS offshoring project, we argue that the client manager is in a position to initiate or facilitate clan controls (Towry, 2003) and may also establish a clan-like pattern of interaction with the vendor counterpart (Persson et al, 2012). This is because these interface managers need to be in frequent interaction in order to uphold the client–vendor relationship, to communicate and verify project requirements, and in general so that the client can exercise control over project tasks (e.g., Tiwana, 2010). The client–vendor interface also serves as a key channel for bridging knowledge domains (Levina, 2005), especially client business domain knowledge and vendor IT domain knowledge (Kirsch et al, 2002; Gopal & Gosain, 2010).

In addition, the client and vendor counterparts, while having different roles and contributing different types of expertise, typically consist of individuals with IT expertise on both the client and the vendor side. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the usual setup for vendor management in outsourcing/ offshoring relationships is that the client <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT department is responsible for controlling the vendor <sup>fi</sup>rm (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003). This means that the client and vendor manager working at the interorganizational interface have a common professional frame of reference that facilitates their interaction and their understanding of each other (cf. Mahnke et al, 2008). This shared professional frame of reference, together with frequent interaction, is likely to facilitate the establishment of shared understanding, shared norms and values, and mutual trust between the client and vendor manager (Fischer et al, 2011; Gregory et al, 2013). Client managers can leverage this common ground of interaction to build a clan-like af<sup>fi</sup>nity with the vendor manager, stimulate cooperative and collegial vendor behaviours, and thus successfully promote clan control (Kirsch et al, 2002; Kirsch et al, 2010).

Turning to the topic of self-control, the promotion of this informal control mode can also be facilitated by the frequent interaction and shared frame of reference between the managers bridging the client–vendor interface. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, frequent interaction is likely to provide the client manager with opportunities to foster self-control exercised by the vendor manager, as well as insights into how best to tailor the use of self-control to <sup>fi</sup>t the controllee’s pro<sup>fi</sup>le. Through this tailoring process, the client manager increases the likelihood that attempts to foster self-control (i.e., self-control given) is accurately received and accepted by the vendor manager. In addition, the IS knowledge that the client manager possesses also enables the promotion of self-control (Kirsch, 1996) since it allows the controller to understand the work processes and actual use of self-control on the vendor side.

Furthermore, the fact that the vendor manager is highly likely to have considerable IS knowledge makes this manager more conducive to respond positively to controller promotion of self-control mechanisms (cf. Kirsch & Cummings, 1996; Kirsch, 1997). Moreover, role expectations on the part of the vendor also make the vendor manager receptive to requests for self-control by the client (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003). This is because, although client managers may be knowledgeable about IS, vendor managers are expected to possess greater knowledge about the speci<sup>fi</sup>c work tasks than their client counterparts (Gopal et al, 2003), and therefore expect the client manager to grant them some extent of autonomy in managing the assigned project tasks (Tiwana & Keil, 2009).

In summary, we argue that frequent interactions between the client and vendor manager, a shared frame of reference and IS knowledge, along with clear role expectations on both sides of the relationship foster a shared understanding of applied informal controls (cf. Poppo & Zenger, 2002; Goo et al, 2009), and thus the successful promotion of both clan control and self-control. We therefore suggest that clan control and self-control can be successfully promoted by the client manager in IS offshore project relationships and propose the following hypotheses:

Clan control given by the client manager positively influences clan control received by the vendor manager.

Self-control given by the client manager positively influences self-control received by the vendor manager.

Informal control effectiveness in IS offshoring projects In IS projects, which are characterized by complexity and uncertainty, and where consequently, appropriate behaviours are hard to specify and monitor (Ouchi, 1979), clan control can help improve performance (Chua et al, 2012). These common IS project attributes are enhanced in the context of IS offshoring projects, which require cooperation between two distant organizations, and where complexity and uncertainty on both sides of the relationship are channelled through the client–vendor interface. Here, clan control can be particularly useful, partly because it facilitates cooperation and partly because clan control improves control adaptiveness, thus positively affecting project performance (Kirsch et al, 2002; Kirsch, 2004; Kohli & Kettinger, 2004; Chua et al, 2012).

First, successfully promoted clan control between the client and vendor managers fosters continuous communication and bilateral knowledge transfer (Kirsch et al, 2010). Such a collaborative culture – an important manifestation of clan control – ensures that the client and vendor managers can leverage their speci<sup>fi</sup>c competencies for relevant project tasks and also encourages them to openly share and discuss ideas, issues, and questions (Gopal & Gosain, 2010). With clan control operating, the vendor manager will also be more open to expose work outcomes to scrutiny by the client manager. As a result, communication quality and speed is improved, which in turn leads to fewer errors (Gopal et al, 2003; Gopal & Gosain, 2010). Such a collaborative culture may be particularly important in application development projects where signi<sup>fi</sup>cant task interdependencies exist. With client and vendor manager having a shared understanding of how to approach the accomplishment of interdependent tasks, development efforts are more likely to meet client demands; that is, lead to improved project quality. This argument is in line with earlier studies in IS offshore contexts that show a positive effect of clan control and a collaborative culture on project quality (Gopal & Gosain, 2010; Srivastava & Teo, 2012).

Second, market dynamics and changing user needs can introduce new requirements in the midst of an IS offshore project (Tiwana & Keil, 2009; Tiwana, 2010). Particularly in complex projects, such as custom application development projects, this can result in technical or managerial issues that may adversely affect the ability to complete the project on time and within budgeted costs.

Clan control can mitigate this inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency by fostering shared interpretations and a broad understanding of how project tasks should be conducted as opposed to strict reliance on highly speci<sup>fi</sup>ed task descriptions (Turner & Makhija, 2006). In other words, clan control enables controllees ‘to extrapolate in new situations and act quickly, because even if the situation is new, the values are clear’ (Lebas & Weigenstein, 1986, p. 265). Furthermore, clan control results in social cohesion, which is likely to facilitate quick resolution of disputes that would otherwise slowdown project progress (Chua et al 2012). Therefore, clan control also increases project ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency by leading to higher levels of control <sup>fl</sup>exibility and adaptability (Turner & Makhija, 2006). On the basis of these arguments, we suggest:

## Clan control received by the vendor manager positively affects IS offshore project performance in terms of both (a) project efficiency and (b) project quality.

Like clan control, self-control has the potential to increase IS project performance: prior empirical studies <sup>fi</sup>nd a performance-enhancing effect of self-control in internal project settings (Henderson & Lee, 1992; Tiwana & Keil, 2009). However, the literature also provides opposing arguments and inconclusive results concerning the effectiveness of self-controls in IS projects in general and (offshore) outsourced projects in particular. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, some scholars argue that reliance on self-control may prompt controllees working on interdependent project tasks to adopt their own approaches, resulting in incompatible work processes and outcomes, which adversely affect project performance (Maruping et al, 2009). In addition, in outsourced contexts, vendors are likely to have superior technical expertise and knowledge (Gopal et al, 2003; Tiwana & Keil, 2009), while sometimes lacking adequate knowledge of the client business domain (Levina & Vaast, 2008). Problems resulting from lack of business domain knowledge may remain unnoticed if the vendor manages the project process with minimal involvement by the client (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003).

In contrast, other scholars consider complex IS project tasks such as application development, which are inherently knowledge-intensive and demand creativity, as being well suited for the promotion and use of self-control (Henderson & Lee, 1992). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the successful promotion of self-controls provides the controllee with autonomy and discretion, which may bene<sup>fi</sup>t IS project performance in two ways. First, a certain degree of autonomy allows the controllee to effectively plan and prioritize assigned project tasks as well as to apply established work practices (Kirsch et al, 2002), fostering the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of controllee work processes. Second, controllee autonomy can also improve the quality of task outcomes by enabling controllees to leverage their (superior) IS knowledge in accomplishing project tasks (Kirsch et al, 2002). For example, Henderson & Lee (1992) <sup>fi</sup>nd that team member selfcontrol in IS projects is positively related to the quality of project outcomes.

Both of the above-stated, performance-related bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of self-control operating are likely to apply to the client– vendor control dyad managing the interorganizational interface of an IS offshoring project. This is because highly experienced and knowledgeable vendor managers with proven skills usually serve as contact for the client manager. Hence, providing the vendor manager with some autonomy in managing the assigned project tasks (i.e., creating an environment where the controllee can effectively exercise self-control) is expected to be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for achieving both project ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and quality targets. This is in line with prior studies, which <sup>fi</sup>nd that promoting and exercising self-controls and assisting the controllee in the implementation of such controls (e.g., through training) signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhance individual and project performance (Druskat & Wheeler, 2003; Tiwana & Keil 2009). We thus suggest:

Self-control received by the vendor manager positively affects IS offshore project performance in terms of both (a) project efficiency and (b) project quality.

## Methodology

## Data collection

As part of a multi-year research project on the transmission of control in interorganizational IS projects, we collected matched-pair data from 172 respondents representing 86 IS offshoring projects. To do so, we contacted senior IS executives from a wide range of industries using the ‘known sponsor approach’ (Patton, 1990). If senior IS executives expressed interest in the research project, they were asked to identify appropriate projects and respondents (Kirsch et al, 2002; Keil et al, 2013). In order to be selected, three criteria had to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled. First, projects either had to be completed during the last 12 months or had been underway for at least 3 months and completed at least one major project milestone. Second, projects had to allow access to the primary control interface, namely, the control dyad consisting of the client representative and the vendor counterpart together being responsible for the day-to-day management of the project. Third, client and vendor interface managers must have had operated in their roles for at least 2 months. Thus, we only studied IS projects and control relationships that had progressed to a reasonable maturity. Out of 210 invited client and vendor representatives, 189 completed the questionnaire, resulting in a response rate of 90%. Non-paired data records were dropped from the analysis, resulting in a <sup>fi</sup>nal sample size of 86 matched pairs from 86 offshore projects. t-tests on all main constructs comparing the early (<sup>fi</sup>rst 30) and late (last 30) respondents showed no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences, providing assurance against non-response bias (Armstrong & Overton, 1977).

Client representatives in our data sample had more than 1 year of IS experience (97%) and offshore experience (92%), respectively, with 69% (36%) having more than 5 years of experience in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of IS (offshoring). On the part of the vendor, 97% of the respondents indicated that they had more than 1 year of experience with IS offshoring projects, and 72% reported having more than 5 years of offshore experience. The client and vendor representatives were typically project managers but also held similar titles such as IT Manager and Development Manager. All client <sup>fi</sup>rms in our sample operated from German-speaking countries and represented multiple industries, including IT (4 <sup>fi</sup>rms), manufacturing (4), aerospace (3), energy (2), engineering (2), and health care (2). Vendor <sup>fi</sup>rms came from India (70 projects), Vietnam (9), Belarus (5), Mexico (1), and Slovakia (1). As shown in Table 1, our sample also varies in project focus, project size, and vendor type.

## Reliability and validity

Partial least squares (PLS) path-weighting scheme was used to assess the reliability and validity of the measurement model. One clan control received item, one clan control given item, and one self-control received item were removed from the model because of low loadings. All remaining items loaded above Nunnally’s (1978) recommended threshold of 0.6 (see Table A2 in the Appendix). The average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct was greater than 0.5, indicating convergent validity for all scales (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Composite reliability measures were all above 0.7, indicating adequate construct reliability (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Each construct also shared more variance with its assigned items than with any other construct (see off-diagonal cells in Table 2) and cross-loadings were lower than within-construct loadings, establishing discriminant validity (Hulland, 1999).

## Control variables

We included four control variables in our model to test for rival explanations. To control for alternative explanations for clan and self-control received, we included the client’s offshore experience and client industry based on the following arguments: experience with offshoring may increase the client’s ability to communicate control mechanisms to the vendor. Projects involving clients from highly regulated industries (e.g., health care) are likely to show a preference for formal mechanisms (Boss et al, 2009), thereby potentially impacting the reception of informal controls by the vendor. We also included the size of the project and prior interactions. Project size is considered an important control variable for IS project performance (Pressman, 2001). Similarly, prior client–vendor interactions are expected to facilitate project collaboration, and hence positively impact project performance (Ethiraj et al, 2005). Both variables were measured by asking client executives to assess the total project effort in person months (proxy for project size) and the client <sup>fi</sup>rm’s experience with the offshore vendor (proxy for prior interactions).

## Data analysis and results

We transformed our research model into a structural equation model using the software SmartPLS 2.0 (Ringle et al, 2005). PLS path modelling is an appropriate choice in settings where the focus is on predicting key target constructs (Hair et al, 2011). In addition, the PLS estimation method is ‘remarkably stable even at low sample sizes’ (Gefen et al, 2011, p. A3). As recommended by Hair et al (2011), we assessed our model with a bootstrap size of 5,000 samples. We here report on the statistical test of our research model as well as our post hoc analysis, which aims to provide deeper insight on the test results.

## Test of the research model

H1 and H2 refer to the promotion of clan and self-control, respectively. While clan control given has no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on clan control received (β = 0.073, ns), selfcontrol given is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and positively related to self-control received (β = 0.247, P<0.01). Thus, only H2 is supported. H3 and H4 refer to the effectiveness of clan and self-control, respectively. Clan control received signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly increases both project ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (β = 0.238, P<0.05) and quality (β = 0.267, P<0.01), supporting H3. In contrast, self-control received neither affects project ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (β = 0.110, ns) nor project quality (β = 0.140, ns). Owing to the non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant paths between self-control received and project ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency/quality the null hypothesis cannot be rejected for H4. The control variables do not show any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects. The data analysis results are presented in Table 3.

The analysis results provide support for our hypotheses on the promotion of self-control, that is, the accurate transmission of self-control between the client and vendor manager (H2), and the effectiveness of clan control (H3) in IS offshore project relationships. In contrast, the results do not support the two other hypotheses. In the following, we therefore conduct additional post hoc analyses to better understand under which conditions, if any, clan control can be successfully promoted, that is, accurately transmitted (H1), and whether and when self-control can have a project performance effect (H4). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we test for the moderating effect of (1) formal control (behaviour and outcome control), (2) national culture (power distance, individualism, and uncertainty avoidance), and (3) project context characteristics (vendor type and project focus) on the promotion of clan control and the effectiveness of self-control, respectively.

## Post hoc analysis

The <sup>fi</sup>rst part of our post hoc analysis focuses on the two modes of formal control, behaviour, and outcome control (Kirsch, 1996). While outcome control refers to the prespeci<sup>fi</sup>cation of desired outputs (both interim and <sup>fi</sup>nal) by the client manager (controller), behaviour control refers to the explicit prescription of procedures and rules the vendor manager (controllee) has to follow. In practice, controllers combine formal with informal controls into a portfolio of controls (Kirsch, 1997; Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003). Here, recent research indicates that the promotion and effectiveness of one control mode are likely to be moderated by the simultaneous use of other modes (e.g., Tiwana, 2010; Persson et al, 2012; Srivastava & Teo, 2012). Thus, the use of formal controls may impact the degree to which the vendor receives clan controls given by the client and impede the effectiveness of selfcontrol.

The second part of the post hoc analysis addresses national culture. Prior studies argue that national culture may play an important role for the functioning of informal control in globally distributed projects (Kirsch, 2004; Tiwana, 2010). This is because important indicators of informal control, such as shared beliefs, norms, and values also constitute fundamental aspects of culture. We thus include three key cultural values, namely, power distance, individualism, and uncertainty avoidance (Hofstede, 1980), in our post hoc analysis since these are considered the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential, reliable, and frequently used among Hofstede’s cultural dimensions (Triandis, 2004; Kirkman et al, 2006). Power distance is the extent to which status inequalities are recognized and accepted. Individualism refers to the degree to which an individual’s social behaviour is driven by personal rather than collective goals. Uncertainty avoidance refers to an individual’s degree of tolerance for ambiguity and uncertainty. In line with other studies in the IS offshoring context (e.g., Rai et al, 2009), we focus on the vendor’s espoused culture. This concept recognizes that individuals of the same national origin may vary in terms of the degree to which they embrace the values associated with their national culture (e.g., Srite & Karahanna, 2006).

## Limitations

Before turning to the discussion of our results and the contributions of our study, it is important to acknowledge that the study results should be interpreted in the light of four limitations. First, similar to earlier studies on IS project control, the focus of our study was on one speci<sup>fi</sup>c control dyad, namely, the control relationship between the client and vendor ‘middlemen’ (Mahnke et $a l ,$ 2008). Since IS project control arrangements normally involve several control dyads (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Soh et al, 2011), we suggest that future studies devote more attention to promotion and enactment of (informal) control across several dyads. This may also provide interesting insights into how control modes and mechanisms are used by different stakeholder groups (Keil et al, 2013).

Second, our study’s focus on project performance only in terms of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and quality may account for the nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of self-control on performance. In spite of these limitations, our study, based on a solid dyadic data set that captures both sides of the client– vendor interface, offers important and novel insights into the promotion and the effectiveness of informal control in the offshored IS project setting.

Our study makes several contributions with considerable implications for research and practice. The <sup>fi</sup>rst contribution lies in highlighting the distinction between informal control given and received, which enables a more nuanced understanding of the promotion and effectiveness of informal control in client–vendor relationships. Second, our study provides new insights into the interplay between formal and informal control. In the following, we discuss these contributions as well as their theoretical and practical implications.

## Informal control promotion and effectiveness

Our <sup>fi</sup>rst contribution comes from applying a control transmission lens (Ouchi, 1978) to the question of how informal control is promoted in interorganizational IS projects. We <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence that self-control can be successfully promoted in IS offshore project relationships. This is because of the frequent interactions between the client and vendor project manager operating at the interface between the two organizations. Such frequent interaction, as well as a common frame of reference based on professional background and pro-<sup>fi</sup>le similarity (cf. Kirsch et al, 2002) enable the client manager to tailor and successfully promote the use of self-control mechanisms by the vendor manager, thereby achieving accurate transmission of self-controls. Furthermore, the promotion of self-control is facilitated

In contrast, clan control seems to be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to promote in interorganizational control relationships. That is, high levels of clan control given by the client manager do not necessarily translate into high levels of clan control received by the vendor manager. This is contrary to what we have suggested in our hypothesis. Apparently, the frequent interaction and the shared professional frame of reference between the client and vendor contact are not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to facilitate the propagation of social cohesion and norm-based alignment of controllee activities. Rather, organizational boundaries (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003), distance challenges (Dibbern et al, 2008), as well as the intangible nature of clan controls (Kirsch, 1997) seem to act as natural barriers that inhibit the promotion of clan control between client and vendor manager. Thus, the successful promotion of clan control seems to require considerable efforts on the client side (e.g., mutual site visits), which are particularly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to execute in offshore project settings. Here, it is particularly interesting to compare our results with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings by Chua et al (2012) from the onsite interaction between client managers and a project team consisting of both client and vendor representatives: Chua et al (2012) show that leveraging the clan requires constant efforts to reinforce positive shared beliefs, norms, and values, as well as inhibiting norms and values that impede the achievement of project goals. Comparing our results with those of Chua et al (2012) suggests that clan control can be promoted across organizational boundaries in the case of co-location but not necessarily in the (remote) offshore context. Nevertheless, our results also show that if the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of promoting clan control over distance can be overcome, doing so is likely to pay off: Once successfully promoted (i.e., received by the vendor manager), clan control is positively and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with IS offshore project performance in terms of both ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and quality.

In contrast to the positive effects of clan control on the performance of IS offshoring projects, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that even though frequent interaction, a common frame of reference, and clear mutual role expectations help promote self-control, this does not automatically translate into higher performance; in fact, self-control as such cannot be proven to have a positive project performance impact in the offshore context. This <sup>fi</sup>nding adds to prior work, which questions the effectiveness of self-control in (offshore) outsourced contexts (Tiwana & Keil, 2009; Gopal & Gosain, 2010). Moreover, while Tiwana & Keil (2009) argue that social requirements for realizing self-control are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to meet in outsourced projects, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the fault may lie not in the promotion of selfcontrol, but rather in whether self-control practiced by the vendors matters. Our post hoc analysis (discussed further below) suggests that self-control does matter under speci<sup>fi</sup>c conditions and that we therefore should ask more ‘how’ and ‘when’ questions rather than ‘if’ questions in future studies covering self-control.

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings also have implications for future studies on control of offshored IS projects. In particular, since informal control does not necessarily transmit accurately from the client to the vendor manager – that is, differences between control given and control received are likely to arise (cf. Ouchi, 1978) – control given by the controller cannot safely be used as a proxy for control received by the controllee, and vice versa. Thus, future studies on IS project control should pay particular attention to report explicitly from what side of the control dyad data have been collected. In addition, more studies that collect and use data from both sides of the control dyad would be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial in order to shed further light on the challenge of control transmission in general and informal control promotion in particular.

## Interplay between informal and formal control

Another contribution is that our study adds to our understanding of the interplay between formal and informal controls. Previous studies on outsourced IS projects are inconclusive as to whether formal and informal controls act as complements (Beck & Schott, 2012; Persson et al, 2012), substitutes (Srivastava & Teo, 2012), or simultaneously as both (Tiwana, 2010). Our results show that the promotion of informal clan control is suppressed by high levels of formal behaviour control. This is in line with Rijsdijk & van den Ende (2011) who note that ‘process [behaviour] controls tend to con<sup>fl</sup>ict with clan control’ (p. 877). A possible explanation for this observation is that (explicit) formal controls are likely to dominate control portfolios (Kirsch, 1997; Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Chua et al, 2012), which in turn may impede the reception of (implicit) clan controls by the controllee. In other words, vendor managers receiving high levels of formal control may tend to perceive rather low levels of clan control.

As prior studies have not included the interplay between behaviour and self-control, our study offers an important contribution in <sup>fi</sup>nding that these two control modes can complement each other. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the reception of self-control increases project ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, but not project quality, when vendor managers simultaneously receive high levels of behaviour control. Hence, in IS offshore relationships behaviour control seems to strengthen the effects of self-control on project ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency rather than enhance project performance per se. A possible interpretation of this <sup>fi</sup>nding is that a clear upfront speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of expected vendor behaviours and work processes decreases behavioural uncertainty on the part of the vendor manager when exercising self-control, thereby enabling this manager to work more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. In contrast to prior studies, which either excluded self-control from their scope (Tiwana & Keil, 2007; Srivastava & Teo, 2012) or found a negative impact of self-control on project performance on an aggregated level (Tiwana & Keil, 2009), our <sup>fi</sup>nding provides more nuanced insights into how self-control operates in offshore outsourced contexts. These insights also point to a need for future research encompassing both clan and self-control, and devoting attention to the effects of different control mode combinations. For instance, longitudinal case studies can help provide a deeper understanding of how and why contextual factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence the interaction between formal and informal control modes as well as the dynamics of control mode interaction.

## Moderating effects of national culture and project context

Finally, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two additional factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the promotion of clan control: power distance and uncertainty avoidance; and three additional factors that positively moderate the relationship between self-control and project performance: individualism, vendor type, and project focus (see Figure 4 above).

In particular, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that offshore vendor managers with high power distance seem to have dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in accurately receiving clan controls given by the client. In general, high power distance vendors are more hesitant to build up a relationship characterized by frequent and open interaction with the client and less receptive towards participative (clan) environments that are based on mutual understanding of norms and values (Kirkman & Shapiro, 2001). In such environments, a collaborative team culture – an important manifestation of clan control – supports members to freely share resources and ideas with others (Gopal & Gosain, 2010). Individuals with high power distance, however, do not feel comfortable working within a collaborative context and avoid communication characterized by a high frequency in question–feedback– reassurance loops (Keil et al, 2007), partly because seeking such communication loops might be interpreted as indirect criticism of the superior (De Luque & Sommer, 2000). Thus, vendor managers with high power distance are typically less willing to absorb clan controls given by the client, leading to poor promotion of such controls.

Similarly, promoting clan control seems to be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult in project settings where vendor contacts with high uncertainty avoidance are involved. This is because individuals with high uncertainty avoidance prefer structured environments and formal rules rather than informal ways of working (Hofstede, 1980). Often, such controllees are dependent on experts and/or superiors for answers and expect de<sup>fi</sup>ned behaviours (Narayanaswamy & Henry, 2005). They thus feel more comfortable with formal (behaviour) controls and are less receptive towards clan controls, which rely on propagating common beliefs, norms, and values as well as a collaborative team culture (Gopal & Gosain, 2010).

Against our general <sup>fi</sup>nding that self-control does not by itself affect IS offshore project performance, there are scenarios where self-control is effective. By identifying moderating effects of contextual factors that strengthen the link between self-control and performance, our study helps close a frequently observed gap in the literature (e.g., Srivastava & Teo, 2012). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our results suggest that self-control enhances project quality if the project focus is application development. This is in line with Henderson & Lee (1992) who argue that creative and knowledge-intensive project tasks, such as application development, are well suited for promoting self-controls. They also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the use of self-controls is positively related to the quality of application development projects. A possible interpretation of this is that particularly complex tasks bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the use of self-controls. It is possible to theorize that complex tasks, which make, for example, behaviour control more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, also necessitate judgement-based balancing of sometimes ambiguous and/or con<sup>fl</sup>icting demands and requirements. Following the argument of Henderson & Lee (1992), it is highly likely that higher use of informal self-control will allow for a more nuanced adaptation of practices in this context. Similarly, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that in non-captive vendor settings, selfcontrol has a positive effect on project quality. One reason for this might be that non-captive vendors undergo a careful, competitive screening process upfront, which might not always be the case in captive setups. Thus, it can be expected that there is good <sup>fi</sup>t between the expertise of a non-captive vendor and the speci<sup>fi</sup>c project task, which in turn is expected to positively impact the quality of task outcomes. Another reason could be that the autonomy of non-captive vendors increases the vendor’s propensity to employ self-controls and to do this in a way that ensures project quality, thus improving the chances for repeat business and longer relationships.

In addition, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that vendor manager’s individualism positively moderates the relationship between selfcontrol and project ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. We believe that this is because, in individualistic cultures, people expect personal reward and recognition from keeping their own and organizational interests and goals in line (Trompenaars, 1994). Thus, providing individualistic vendor liaisons with some degree of autonomy not only drives their motivation but also their ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, for instance, by allowing and encouraging them to monitor their own progress towards achieving common project goals. While prior studies have found that the controller’s promotion of self-control represents an important prerequisite for the controllee to feel motivated (Kirsch, 1996; Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003), we add to this knowledge by <sup>fi</sup>nding that promotion of self-control towards individualistic controllees can also increase project ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

Together, these additional <sup>fi</sup>ndings point to two important areas for future research, namely, the impact of culture on control effects and the role of various context factors in moderating the relationship between control and performance. Culture is known to be important in the context of IS offshore projects (Dibbern et al, 2008; Rai et al, 2009). Our study suggests that more work is needed to understand how control portfolios can be adapted to leverage the positive features of speci<sup>fi</sup>c cultural values as well as to avoid cultural clashes with control practices. Furthermore, the fact that not only culture but also context factors and control interaction effects (discussed above) clearly have an impact on control effectiveness and project outcomes suggests that studies that allow for more context factors to be taken into account are needed. Looking at the bigger picture, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings also suggest a need for more rich case studies on control (e.g., Gregory et al, 2013), which can capture the nuances of control practices and client– vendor interaction and thus hopefully further develop our current theoretical understanding.

## Implications for practice

A key managerial implication from our study is the need for client IS offshore managers to understand the importance and challenges of promoting informal controls. Interestingly, client managers face opposing challenges for self-control and clan control: while the former is easier to promote, it is less likely to be effective; while the latter is highly challenging to promote, it pays off if you succeed. It is possible that offshore distance challenges are a key culprit in this pattern, and thus managers should pay particular attention to overcoming distance challenges and <sup>fi</sup>nding ways to build social ties. In other words, in the offshore context, it is important that client managers use the rare opportunities to make site visits and to otherwise meet in person not only to exercise formal controls but also to engage in social interaction that improves the basis for promoting informal controls and making them effective (Chua et al, 2012).

Concerning each individual control mode, our study results concur with prior studies in suggesting that managers should use caution in investing in the promotion of self-control (e.g., Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003) because of its limited and condition-dependent impact on project performance. At the same time, our results show that client managers who are able to con<sup>fi</sup>gure the control portfolio properly can still use self-controls to increase project ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency if combined with behaviour controls; and that in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of application development as well as in a non-captive setting, self-controls may improve project quality. In other words, in the offshore context, managers need to pay careful attention to whether the speci<sup>fi</sup>c project context and conditions make self-controls useful as well as to combining self-control and behaviour control to gain a positive impact of the former.

Our study can also be seen as presenting offshore client managers with an imperative to improve their skills and tactics for promoting clan control because of the dormant potential in promoting this control mode. Earlier studies have shown that clan control can be exercised in a colocated interorganizational context (Chua et al, 2012). If client managers are able to replicate successful conditions and tactics for promoting clan control from the co-located to the offshored context, this will likely be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial. In other words, the importance of an of<sup>fi</sup>ce party or a barbeque should not be underestimated: all work and no play not only makes Jack a dull boy, it also impedes project control. Another practical implication related to clan control is for clients to let the potential for development of clan-like interaction between parties in<sup>fl</sup>uence vendor and team member selection.

Furthermore, our study joins many prior studies that call for sensitivity in adapting controls to cultural contexts: considering the fact that informal control makes up an important part of the control portfolio in globally distributed IS projects (Kirsch, 2004), client managers could, for example, request involvement in the screening and interviewing process for selecting the vendor manager and explicitly focus on cultural aspects in this process. Naturally, appointing client relationship managers with understanding of the vendor counterpart’s culture should also be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial, as should cultural training of both parties. As for cultural management training, this should not only cover culture in general and how culture affects management and business practices, but also explicitly address how various control modes, mechanisms, and control combinations are likely to be perceived and reacted to in relevant cultural contexts.
