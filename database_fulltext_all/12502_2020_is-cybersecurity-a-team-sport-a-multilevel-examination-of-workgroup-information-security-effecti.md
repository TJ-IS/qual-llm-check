# IS CYBERSECURITY A TEAM SPORT? A MULTILEVEL EXAMINATION OF WORKGROUP INFORMATION SECURITY EFFECTIVENESS<sup>1</sup>

Chul Woo Yoo

Department of Information Technology and Operations Management, College of Business, Florida Atlantic University, Boca Raton, FL 33431 U.S.A. {yooc@fau.edu}

Department of Information Technology and Operations Management, College of Business, Florida Atlantic University, Boca Raton, FL 33431 U.S.A. {jgoo@fau.edu}

H. Raghav Rao Department of Information Systems and Cyber Security, College of Business, University of Texas at San Antonio, San Antonio, TX 78249 U.S.A. {hr.rao@utsa.edu}

Organizations maintain their desired level of cybersecurity by evaluating the information security (ISec) effectiveness of their organizational units. Numerous ISec studies have delved into examining individual compliance, yet studies that develop an understanding about achieving the desired effect of the workgroup ISec have been scarce in the literature. Against this backdrop, this research note draws on the input–process– output framework to take a multilevel approach and examines how an individual security factor (“individual self-efficacy”) and two core workgroup mechanisms (“workgroup collective efficacy” and “security knowledge coordination”) can together affect workgroup information security effectiveness (WISE). Using a survey, data for the independent variables of the study was collected from 68 branch offices (with 536 employees in total) of a law enforcement agency in South Korea. Separately, for WISE, we obtained the branch offices’ annual security assessment scores from the organization. The results reveal the cross-level nature of WISE, suggesting that group mechanisms significantly mediate the relationship between individual self-efficacy and WISE. The results support the view that group mechanisms are important in ISec management and need to be considered alongside the individual-level analysis in the pursuit of workgroup ISec performance. The findings provide insightful implications for both theory and practice

Keywords: Workgroup information security effectiveness, collective efficacy, security knowledge coordina tion, multilevel structural equation model, social cognitive theory

## Introduction

The increasing use of workgroups/teams as the basic organizing unit for effective management of organizational tasks has changed the nature of work. With shared knowledge among workgroup members, as well as the coordinative and synergistic dynamics of their interactions, workgroups often attain given group goals more effectively than employees working individually (Kozlowski and Bell 2013). Similarly, organizations attempt to maintain their desired level of cybersecurity by measuring the security effectiveness of the workgroup (Johnston et al. 2019), along with the workgroup’s exercise group processes by which individual cybersecurity efforts manifest at the workgroup level. While research in the management and psychology disciplines has been devoted to understanding how group performance is derived from group processes of individual inputs, past literature in information security (ISec) has paid less attention to the role of group processes by which individuals influence ISec performance at the workgroup level, despite its practical importance (Johnston et al. 2019; NIST 2008; Rao and An 1995). Recent practitioner reports, for instance, have pointed out that because security breaches often lead to a negative impact on the business performance of the workgroups, workgroup information security effectiveness (WISE) has become increasingly important and is thus evaluated in practice (Burgress 2017).

To address WISE, we draw inspiration from the example of a soccer team. As illustrated by Bandura (2000, p. 76), the accomplishments of a soccer team are the product of players working collaboratively, as a team, toward the shared goal of winning, as opposed to simply pursuing the goals or records of individual players. This indicates the importance of having workgroup-oriented goals and exercising collaborative workgroup mechanisms for group-level performance. Applied to ISec, the role of group-oriented ISec mechanisms for nurturing collective responsibility and performance can be naturally emphasized. Although the main objective of this study is to understand the group-level ISec phenomena in terms of the role of workgroup mechanisms and effectiveness, we believe that ISec at the workgroup level cannot be easily detached from the individual level because individual security is a necessary condition for workgroup ISec processes to occur, and ISec is only as good as the weakest link.

Thus, we argue that WISE is the coordinated performance at the group level, which demands cross-level efforts to be exercised at both workgroup and individual levels; this suggests adopting a multilevel, rather than a single level, approach to studying WISE, and shifting management attention from directing individual policy compliance to evaluating workgroup security performance. Hsu et al. (2015) also suggest that management should seek workgroup mechanisms that help bring group members together for integrated, concerted, and collective actions that promote the ISec effectiveness of workgroups. However, the extant ISec literature, for the most part, has overlooked such multilevel phenomena, while leaning largely toward understanding individual ISec in terms of individual compliance or ethical behaviors typically adopted as the dependent variables (Cram et al. 2019; Moody et al. 2018; Willison et al. 2018).

This research addresses this gap in the literature by bringing attention to the ISec performance of workgroups and examines how WISE can be attained through two key workgroup mechanisms of group performance—emergent states and group processes conceptualized as workgroup collective efficacy (WCE) and security knowledge coordination (SKC), respectively (Srivastava et al. 2006, p. 1239)— alongside a principal individual security factor, known as individual selfefficacy (ISE). Specifically drawing on social cognitive theory and the knowledge coordination literature, we view that even with the same organizational security policies and technologies in place, WISE may vary, depending on possible individual and intergroup variations. Such variations may occur, not just because of possible differential degrees of members’ ISE, but also because of WCE and SKC across workgroups. That is, while uniform ISec policies and standard guidelines usually remain generic to serve varying situations, the differential degrees of WCE and SKC across workgroups may mediate individual endeavors and result in differential levels of WISE when the security programs are deployed in the field.

Therefore, the current study seeks to address two research questions: (1) How do workgroup collective efficacy and security knowledge coordination mediate between individual self-efficacy and workgroup information security effectiveness? (2) At the group-level, how does security knowledge coordination relate to workgroup collective efficacy? To answer these questions, we conducted a multilevel study in a law enforcement agency in South Korea. Data for the independent variables and the dependent variable were collected from two separate sources: (1) A survey across an organization with 68 branch offices and 536 employees was administered to capture perceptual data about independent variables, and (2) separately, objective data of each branch office’s annual security assessment score was obtained from the organizational annual security assessment report for the dependent variable, WISE. The data was then analyzed using multilevel structural equation modeling (MSEM) to examine cross-level relationships between the group dependent variable (i.e., WISE) and the predictor variables at both group (i.e., WCE and SKC) and individual levels (i.e., ISE).

This research note makes a twofold contribution to the ISec literature. First, building on the input–process–output (IPO) framework used in prior group performance literature, this research sheds light on the multilevel nature of WISE, amid relatively scarce research with respect to the ISec effectiveness of workgroups. The results show clear evidence that ISE does not directly predict WISE. Instead, the relationship between ISE and WISE is fully mediated by workgroup processes of WCE and SKC. This study empirically shows the need for cross-level efforts and processes in attaining desired security performance at a workgroup level. We believe that the use of MSEM, the tool that we employed to test the multi level IPO framework, is a first in ISec literature. Second, beyond the dominant interest of directing individual compliance behaviors in extant ISec literature, this study takes an approach to evaluating ISec effectiveness at the group level, with an emphasis on the impact of group mechanisms on WISE. The empirical results clearly show that two core group mechanisms (i.e., WCE and SKC) are significantly related to the objective measure of WISE. While the literature has identified a variety of factors that shape individual compliance, when it comes to the evaluation of ISec effectiveness in workgroups, our analysis suggests that the grouplevel processes of SKC and WCE should be leveraged in pursuit of desired group ISec goals.

## Theory Development

## Workgroup Information Security Effectiveness (WISE)

ISec effectiveness refers to the extent to which a given organizational unit effectively accomplishes its ISec goals. The given units may include organizations or their subsets (i.e., groups; for example, teams, workgroups, and branch offices) or individuals (i.e., employees). Each unit pursues its security goals by exercising a relevant level of control over its security practices (Anderson and Moore 2006). ISec effectiveness, therefore, hinges on the pursuit of specified security attainment at the corresponding levels of the organizational unit, and the intentional exercise of a course of actions for established ISec goals. Drawing on a variety of theories, including deterrence theory (D’Arcy and Herath 2011; D’Arcy et al. 2009; Herath and Rao 2009a), control theory (Boss et al. 2009; Khansa et al. 2017), theory of planned behavior (Bulgurcu et al. 2010; Dinev and Hu 2007), institutional theory (Hsu et al. 2012; Hu et al. 2007), protection motivation theory (Boss et al. 2015; Vance et al. 2012), communication theory (Johnston and Warkentin 2010), learning theories (Puhakainen and Siponen 2010), psychological ownership theory (Yoo et al. 2018), accountability theory (Vance et al. 2013), and criminology theories (Hu et al. 2011; Siponen and Vance 2010; Wang et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2019; Willison and Backhouse 2006), prior behavioral studies of ISec effectiveness have delved into the behavioral aspects of individual human agents in pursuit of individual ISec policy compliance (Ng et al. 2009). The security goals at the individual level have been focused on directing individual behaviors to comply with an organization’s ISec policies and procedures to address the notion of the weakest link in the ISec chain (Moody et al. 2018). As such, the prior literature demonstrates that the individual security goal is closely related to individual-level interests (Liang et al. 2013).

In contrast, workgroup information security effectiveness (WISE) involves group-oriented ISec goals and performance; thus, it requires a shift of focus from individual compliance to group functioning, such as group-level efforts of monitoring and working together to produce desired effects for given ISec performance goals and assessment, where the individuals’ combined beliefs and collective effort in pursuit of certain attainment play an important role. As such, the workgroup is viewed as a collective agency in an organization that turns organizational guidelines into predictable and situationspecific courses of actions within a group (Zohar and Luria 2005). Distinguished from individual ISec effectiveness, even in the same organizational environment, WISE can vary with the differential exercise of group mechanisms across workgroups. Developing and leveraging adequate group mechanisms becomes a key to managing members’ capability and motivation, and to producing desired group-level performance (Srivastava et al. 2006). Therefore, it is critical to understand the role of group-level factors in examining the effectiveness of ISec management at a workgroup level. Traditionally, while a lack of attention has been paid to workgroup-level ISec issues (Johnston et al. 2019), a few studies have suggested the importance of addressing ISec performance at the group-level. Line and Moe (2015), for example, have suggested that work coordination and collaborative decisionmaking in teams are important aspects of the incident response process in the face of security breaches. They hint at the usefulness of applying group process research to the study of ISec effectiveness.

In the ISec context, our application of group process both resembles and differs from earlier research regarding applications of group process to group performance. Drawing on earlier group studies, we conceptualize WISE to be the result of multiple group processes. As in any other contexts of group-oriented activities, we expect that the group processes of SKC and WCE would help people adhere to group goals and thus play a critical role in enhancing WISE. On the other hand, our application of group process in the context of ISec departs from earlier models by adopting a cross-level stance toward WISE. We believe that group-level ISec phenomena cannot be considered separately from the individual level because WCE inherently emanates from ISE, and SKC fundamentally requires individual inputs (i.e., ISE). It could be argued that no amount of WCE would help if one member of a workgroup is lax with ISec. In other words, in the context of ISec, the group process is of critical importance and. at the same time, needs to be considered alongside the individual level of analysis.

This suggests adoption of a multilevel perspective to the study of WISE. First, multilevel theory posits that a phenomenon can be studied at two levels: individual and workgroup (Klein and Kozlowski 2000; Lapointe and Rivard 2005). By adopting a multilevel perspective, we first conceptualize WISE as a workgroup-level phenomenon. Thus, we chose to focus on workgroup processes. A majority of ISec studies at the individual level have long been interested in directing the individual’s security compliance without discussion of WISE. We go one step further and acknowledge a possibility that the resulting model will be cross-level, recognizing that individual constructs as well as workgroup-level constructs may influence WISE. Multilevel research calls for studying a phenomenon at the unit level by examining the processes by which lower-level properties manifest at a higher level (Klein and Kozlowski 2000). Advancing this precept to the specific context of ISec, we seek to understand if group processes of SKC and WCE would play a unique mediating role between ISE and WISE.

To this end, this paper employs the input–process–output (IPO) framework to develop and empirically test a cross-level model that links constructs from different levels—ISE at the individual level and SKC and WCE at the workgroup level— in predicting WISE.

## Input–Process–Output Framework

The seminal work of McGrath (1984) depicted groups as systems for structuring social interaction and task performance. From this perspective, the workgroup performance literature has adopted the input–proces–output (IPO) model as a major framework for conceptualizing workgroups, specifically as to how they structure themselves, interact interpersonally, develop consensus, solve problems, and perform (Ilgen et al. 2005). The literature has illustrated that group success is not only a function of individual members’ own capabilities, but also the processes individual members use to interact with each other to accomplish the work (Barrick et al. 2007). Mathieu et al. (2008) also argued that group process is the focal point of all of inputs, and that all the effects or consequences also flow from it.

First, in the IPO framework, input (I) refers to the conditions that exist prior to group action and represents various resources available to the workgroup as individual member characteristics (e.g., expertise). In this study, we focus on the ISE of employees as an individual-level input. Prior literature has illustrated that ISE is the individuals’ clear manifestation toward the goal of ISec (Bulgurcu et al. 2010), and is a critical individual input for the ISec outcome (Boss et al. 2015).

Second, process (P) involves mechanisms that enable members to interact with other members and their task environment. Particularly, focusing on group mechanisms, Marks et al. (2001) differentiated the role of emergent states as distinct from group processes in promoting group effectiveness, and both factors have become the core mechanisms in group performance literature (Srivastava et al. 2006). While emergent states describe the cognitive, motivational, and affective states of groups, group processes involve mobilizing members interdependent working relationships toward a better utilization of various resources for desired outputs. Following Marks et al., we posit that workgroups that have relevant emergent states and strong group processes will have a high degree of ISec success. Although there are a fairly large set of emergent states and group processes examined in the literature (Kozlowski and Bell 2013), Srivastava et al. (2006) have argued that in the case of teams performing uncertain and complex tasks, collective efficacy and knowledge sharing in teams can increase team effectiveness, as well as improve decision-making and coordination. This claim is applicable to our study context, in that collective ISec is often challenging and requires both confidence and knowledge. Therefore, we conceptualize WCE as an emergent state and SKC as a group process that engender the collective ISec goals, WISE.

Third, output (O) represents the criteria to assess the effectiveness of workgroup actions. Workgroup effectiveness is generally conceptualized as multifaceted to address both internal (e.g., member satisfaction) and external (i.e., productivity and performance) criteria (Huang and Cummings 2011). Bommer et al.’s (1995) meta analysis has addressed performance as the most widely studied criterion variable in the literature, due to the nature of workgroups that exist to perform group tasks. In line with this view, we adopt WISE as a performance output of ISec in workgroups. Further theoretical development on these constructs from different levels is provided in the following sections.

## Self-Efficacy Versus Collective Efficacy

The basic premise of social cognitive theory (SCT) involves the ways that individuals exercise some level of control over their own lives (Bandura 1997). According to SCT, the controls that individuals exercise are influenced by the strength of their efficacy beliefs, and these beliefs may have consequences in organizing the courses of actions they choose to pursue and the effort they exert in pursuit of those goals. In line with this premise, prior literature on ISec has addressed individuals’ self-efficacy as a principal antecedent of individual ISec effectiveness (Bulgurcu et al. 2010; Herath and Rao 2009b; Ifinedo 2014; Siponen et al. 2014). As SCT acknowledges that such individual controls operate within the collective’s influence, the theory has been extended to the exercise of collective agency: people’s combined beliefs that they can work together to produce desired effects (Goddard 2001; Tasa et al. 2011). Collective agency (e.g., workgroups) supports a collective’s goals and is actively engaged in analyzing, responding to, and controlling their behaviors, internal states, and environments, especially when there exists an interactive and coordinative group structure. Given such conditions, collective efficacy is defined as a group’s shared understanding of its own collective ability to organize and conduct a series of actions required to attain the group’s goals (Bandura 1997).

Bandura (1999) argued that collective efficacy differs from self-efficacy in that the referent of the efficacy perceptions is the group, as opposed to the individual or self. Collective efficacy is also related to group goal and coordination (Tasa et al. 2007). We can revisit the example of the soccer team. In the team, for strikers, confidence of scoring (i.e., individual goals) forms self-efficacy. On the other hand, collective efficacy is constructed based upon the combined interactions with other members, who work together in pursuit of the desired group goal (i.e., winning a game). If the coordination in the team is poor, strikers will think their team is not efficacious (i.e., high chance of losing the game), although they may feel themselves efficacious individually. That is, a simple aggregation of the individual self-efficacy in a group may not equate to collective efficacy (Preacher et al. 2010) because the individual referent of self-efficacy does not reflect the intentional exercise of collective actions, such as the coordination between players, for the team’s goal of winning a game. Therefore, it is argued that workgroup effectiveness is not directly derived from the sum of the individual members’ selfefficacy (Salanova et al. 2003). Rather, it is the product of interactive and coordinated dynamics of its members that produce desired effects in pursuit of workgroup goals (Bandura 1999).

When applied to workgroup ISec, following SCT, we posit that the security practices performed as a workgroup are directly influenced by the sense of collective efficacy. Specifically, the higher the collective sense of security efficacy, the more likely it is that workgroup members overcome vulnerabilities and become more resilient as a group. Such resiliency would tend to foster effective ISec standards within the workgroup (Jarvenpaa and Majchrzak 2008). Thus, the exercise of collective security actions through shared beliefs would result in better ISec performance of the workgroup.

## Security Knowledge Coordination

Having sufficient knowledge is critical for mitigating ISec risks and managing overall organizational ISec effectiveness (Dang-Pham et al. 2017). Individual employees are typically considered as the principal agents who are expected to possess security knowledge and skills, so appropriate ISec measures should be exercised corresponding to specific security incidents and threats (Herath and Rao 2009a). However, because the workgroup in an organization is viewed as a collective agency, proper group processes, such as the coordination of tasks or skills in workgroups, become critical in enhancing the effectiveness (Marks et al. 2001). For example, security knowledge, such as the use of the systems with accompanying rules and procedures to avoid unauthorized data access among internal personnel, must be coordinated within a workgroup as collective agency. Recent ISec research has also suggested that ISec effectiveness can be enhanced when employees help each other to better perform their ISec related activities, rather than performing their own duties myopically (Hsu et al. 2015). This implies that ISec coordination efforts in workgroup may enhance the ISec effectiveness (Da Veiga and Eloff 2010). Organizational research is also consistent with this view, and highlights the importance of workgroup coordination for group performance (Faraj and Sproull 2000; Lorinkova et al. 2013).

For this study, security knowledge coordination is defined as an unfolding process of linked security knowledge and interrelated actions to realize a collective security performance. A basic premise of knowledge coordination is that knowledge must be coordinated among group members to maximize workgroup performance, as opposed to just having the knowledge reside in the workgroup (Kane and Borgatti 2011; Kozlowski and Bell 2013). Because knowledge resides within individuals, knowledge coordination involves the workgroup’s awareness of knowledge location dispersed across the workgroup members with implicit/explicit interactions and the orchestration of knowledge processing (Gabelica et al. 2016). For example, a workgroup can benefit from the differentiated knowledge of its members when they elaborate on their shared understanding, as well as who knows what in the workgroup, and synchronize this knowledge collaboratively to complete tasks efficiently.

Knowledge coordination is particularly desirable when considering circumstances wherein the security threats are increasingly complex and members in workgroups are asked to coordinate their efforts to integrate necessary ISec knowledge and skills (Cruz 2018). For example, workgroups tend to look for coordination mechanisms through which they can integrate the knowledge of their members for a positive impact on group performance (Kanawattanachai and Yoo 2007). Similarly, helping colleagues to deal with their security issues—such as voluntary security orientation for new members, or security knowledge sharing and interemployee encouragement (Hsu et al. 2015)—can be examples of security knowledge coordination. Thus, such coordination engenders the efficient use of security knowledge by preventing redundant deployment of security practices or reinvention of the security wheel. It is also instrumental in stimulating the collective efficacy and motivation of individual members to invest in the pursuit of the workgroup’s security goals (Safa and Von Solms 2016; Wollack and Fremer 2013).

We argue that ISE would influence WISE, theorizing group processes as mediators. In particular, as Figure 1 illustrates our two mediators form a multiple-step multiple-mediator relationship (Hayes 2009). Thus, in the following section we propose hypotheses to test the mediating role of group process variables between individual input (i.e., ISE) and workgroup output (i.e., WISE), as well as a relationship of constructs within the group processes.

## Mediating Role of Workgroup Collective Efficacy (WCE)

The multilevel IPO framework of WISE argues that in workgroups, group processes mediate the effect of individual input on ISec performance. In other words, WCE may be formed through collective ISE, by which WISE emerges from ISE of workgroup members. Independently, these conceptual linkages (i.e., self-efficacy ÷ collective efficacy; collective efficacy ÷ workgroup effectiveness) have been supported in the literature, although not in the context of ISec. For example, Tasa et al. (2007) illustrated that collective efficacy, not selfefficacy, influences team performance. It is consistent with previous group-level studies that identify collective efficacy as the antecedent to group-level output. For example, Goddard et al. (2004) argued that low collective efficacy may lead workgroups to believe that tasks are harder than they actually are, so that workgroups tend to avoid such tasks and are less likely to persist in their efforts as a result. Low collective efficacy also often results in poor task planning and increases stress (Walumbwa et al. 2004). In contrast, high collective efficacy supports a workgroup’s goals and leads the workgroup members to greater efforts in analyzing and responding to both internal states and external environments (Carroll et al. 2005). Extending this view to the ISec context, we argue that WCE results in workgroups staying alert (Lu et al. 2005), and helps to establish a strong security climate (Goo et al.

2014) in ever changing and challenging ISec environments. In turn, this helps develop the atmosphere of collective responsibility in the workplace and motivate workgroup members to collaborate in the attainment of specific workgroup security goals or security assessment (Da Veiga and Eloff 2010; Xu et al. 2005).

In regards to the relationship between self-efficacy and collective efficacy, Gibson (1999) argued that collective efficacy is formed through the individual members’ self-efficacy, as the members exchange information and experiences within the workgroup. Wang and Lin (2007) also found that groups with members possessing high self-efficacy have higher collective efficacy than groups that do not, due to their cognitive skills and collaborative learning during the group decision and action. It is to be noted that the self-efficacy of a single employee would not be sufficient to severely affect the overall performance of a unit, because in most circumstances, individual compliance is driven by perceptions of individual benefit of compliance and cost of noncompliance (Bulgurcu et al. 2010). Based on the discussion, while acknowledging ISE as one of core drivers of individual ISec behaviors (Boss et al. 2015; Moody et al. 2018), we propose that ISE must permeate throughout group members to ramp up the group-level beliefs and states, particularly because WCE plays a key mediating role between ISE and WISE. Therefore, we hypothesize

H1: Workgroup collective efficacy plays a mediating role between individual self-efficacy and workgroup information security effectiveness.

## Mediating Role of Security Knowledge Coordination (SKC)

A basic premise of the IPO framework argues that individual members in a group should be equipped with relevant knowledge and capability with respect to tasks, and that the group should process inputs to coordinate the goal-directed undertakings for group performance (Marks et al. 2001). Consistent with this view, we argue that ISE needs an elevation to the workgroup context, so as to adequately address the organization unit goals, even though ISE could effectively lead to positive security behaviors of individual members. Workgroups, in turn, deploy proper processes that direct individuals’ security knowledge and skills in a way that security tasks are dealt with in a timely and cost-efficient manner. These conceptual linkages (i.e., self-efficacy ÷ knowledge coordination; knowledge coordination ÷ workgroup effectiveness) have been supported independently in the literature, though not in the ISec context.

Prior literature has provided evidence that knowledge coordination has a positive effect on varying contexts of group performance, such as software development teams (Faraj and Sproull 2000), virtual teams (Kanawattanachai and Yoo 2007) and audit teams in the U.S. government (Gupta et al. 1994). For example, Rico et al. (2008) argued that given the heterogeneity of skills, knowledge, and experiences among members in workgroups, knowledge coordination is a key to workgroup performance. For the relationship between ISE and SKC, ISE of individual workgroup members would impact the ability to coordinate disparate levels of security knowledge, interests and experiences within a workgroup (Safa and Von Solms 2016). For example, Yoo and Sanders (2013) have argued that workgroup ISec process could be particularly enhanced when high caliber security experts are present within the workgroup. Kane and Borgatti (2011) have also hinted at the importance of individuals’ information technology proficiency in realizing the full potential of group work.

Along these lines, we argue that ISec is a context wherein knowledge coordination can result in a positive impact on WISE, and the ISE of workgroup members may help enact coordination when orchestrating workgroup members’ disparate security knowledge. SKC is particularly desired in a setting of branch offices where the sensitive information, such as internal transaction records and reports as well as consumer financial data, is transmitted through a network infrastructure. In such a situation, an individual member may have difficulty conducting tasks involving group-level security practices such as complying with layered access rights among workgroup members. The individual member can benefit from other member’s ISE through the coordination mechanism (Hsu et al. 2015), resulting in enhanced WISE. Therefore, we argue that SKC has influence on WISE and that ISE’s impact on group-level security goals is mediated by SKC. Thus, we hypothesize

H2: Security knowledge coordination plays a mediating role between individual self-efficacy and workgroup information security effectiveness.

## The Multiple-step multiple-mediator Relationship of SKC and WCE

Building on the multilevel IPO model, we hypothesized the mediating role of SKC and WCE in conveying the effect of individual input in favor of ISec performance. Specifically, H2 argues that ISE of individual workgroup members would impact the ability to coordinate disparate levels of security knowledge, interests, and experiences within a workgroup (Safa and Von Solms 2016). Separately, H1 argues that WCE formed through collective ISE positively relates to WISE by developing the atmosphere of collective responsibility in the workplace and motivates workgroup members to attain specific workgroup security goals or security assessment results (Da Veiga and Eloff 2010; Xu et al. 2005). Given these two paths (i.e., ISE ÷ SKC and WCE ÷ WISE) justified above, we propose a multiple-step mediation (i.e., ISE ÷ SKC ÷ WCE ÷ WISE) as explained below.

Prior literature on group performance has hinted at an intricate relationship between SKC and WCE. For example, Gibson and Early (2007) have pointed out that coordination among members is the core mechanism for developing collective efficacy in a workgroup as it allows for group members to interact with one another. Specifically, Lester et al. (2002) found that coordination processes during the early stages of workgroup interaction were significantly related to changes in collective beliefs about a group’s capability to perform the tasks as intergroup knowledge sharing and cooperation increased the group’s certainty in accomplishing the group goals. As group members learn about each other’s skills and capabilities during the practice of knowledge coordination, they shape the collective beliefs of the group so that it can be effective. Further, as models of workgroup effectiveness suggest (Campion et al. 1993; Gladstein 1984), the coordination (and facilitation) of knowledge flows serves as a group enhancement function, in that it fosters openness and satisfies interpersonal relationships. Such an enhancement mechanism in turn helps workgroup members to become aware of togetherness when performing group tasks, which engenders the perception of collective efficacy in workgroup (Stout et al. 1999).

Consistent with the prior literature, we argue that workgroups that execute a high degree of SKC lead their members toward better understanding of ISec tasks and group-related factors, such as task/operational procedures, member roles/responsibilities, interdependencies, equipment functioning/limitations, and environmental constraints in workgroup, which in turn leads to members being more likely to express confidence in their group’s capability to successfully execute their ISec duties. Based on the argument above and prior hypotheses (i.e., H1 and H2), we posit that there exists a multiple-step multiple-mediator relationship between ISE and WISE (Hayes 2009). In other words, a multiple-step mediation of WCE enhanced through SKC helps WISE to emerge from ISE of workgroup members for its total effect (i.e., ISE ÷ SKC ÷ WCE ÷ WISE). Therefore, we hypothesize

H3: Security knowledge coordination and workgroup collective efficacy play the role of multiplestep multiple-mediator between individual selfefficacy and workgroup information security effectiveness.

## Methods

## Data Collection

The data was collected from a law enforcement agency in the Southwest region of South Korea whose main organizational tasks are enforcing law, protecting property, and preserving order. This agency is known as the J Organization. The J

Organization is composed of branch offices that are geographically dispersed and each managed by independent managers. We considered the branch offices as J Organization’s respective workgroups. The J Organization views ISec as central to its mission, and thus the security belief and commitment among employees in geographically dispersed workgroups are considered important in maintaining security. Per the chief information security officer (CISO) of J Organization, any security breach in a geographically dispersed branch office can become a serious threat to the entire organization. In an effort to carry out governance regarding countering possible security breaches, the office of ISec employs branch-based metrics, which measure branch office ISec effectiveness as well as other security metrics annually. The security behaviors of employees in branch offices are an integral part of branch-based security metrics, which is why the office of ISec is dedicated to imposing comprehensive ISec policies on the branch offices along with security training. Therefore, the branch office is appropriate to represent the workgroup for this study.

We collected data from two separate sources. First, we conducted a cross-sectional study across 68 branch offices of J Organization (with 597 employees participating) using a survey questionnaire pertaining to the predictor variables in the research model. Second, the data for the criterion variable was obtained from the office of ISec, which was based on the results of the given year’s branch-based performance assessment as it became available. As such, the survey was administered before the annual assessments were done in order to avoid a possible response bias in the predictor variables. Moreover, special care was taken to avoid possible social desirability bias by explaining to the survey participants that this survey was not related to respondents’ work evaluation. We also pledged that individual responses would be completely confidential, and data collected from the survey questionnaires would be summarized and reported only in aggregate form.

## Operationalization of Constructs

The dependent variable, workgroup information security effectiveness (WISE), was obtained from the J Organization’s annual branch-based performance assessment, which included the ISec effectiveness score of each branch office on a 100- point scale. As part of the annual assessment, the organization keeps track of ten key security indicators at the branch office level, including (1) physical security, (2) network systems security, (3) employees’ PC security, (4) monitoring and auditing, (5) human security, (6) password control, (7) access control, (8) operations management security, (9) business continuity management, and (10) employees security training. Each indicator is composed of multiple corresponding measures that track the performance of each branch office against set goals or targets; there were a total of more than 150 measures employed for tracking 10 key indicators (please see Appendix A for more details). The evaluation scores of all ten indicators were then aggregated to compute final WISE scores, which were then reported to the CISO for his approval. As the official records of the organization, the security assessment reports are used internally for human resources and externally for jurisdiction and audit.

All predictor variables in the survey were measured using multi-item scales with 5-point Likert rating systems, ranging from 1 being “strongly disagree” to 5 being “strongly agree.” After items were developed in English they were translated into a Korean version of the survey for use at a data collection site in South Korea. Since there are language-oriented differences such as nuance and interpretation, close attention was given to the translation by employing the back-translation technique. Further, three bilingual speakers assisted in the process to confirm there was no threat of semantic discrepancies.

We operationalized individual self-efficacy (ISE) as an individual-referent construct in the survey to measure the individual nature of the input in the IPO framework. However, for workgroup collective efficacy (WCE) and security knowledge coordination (SKC), we employed the referentshift design to ensure that the constructs properly capture group-level phenomena using group-referent items (Gully et al. 2002; Van Mierlo et al. 2009). In other words, the referent-shifted items were intended to capture the respondents’ perceptions of the group’s traits or abilities, as opposed to the respondents’ perceptions of their own. Therefore, it is possible that a respondent may perceive a strong WCE even though her ISE might be weak. Specifically, following the suggestion by Hofmann (2004) and Kudaravalli et al. (2017), we wrote the verbiage of the items that constitute these two latent constructs as a collective, “we as a group” or “our branch office” instead of “I,” to help properly shift the referent of construct from an individual focus to a group focus (please see Appendix B for the items).

A conscientious effort was made to adapt existing measures from prior studies for the latent constructs in this research. In regard to ISE, three items were adapted from Bulgurcu et al.’s (2010) work to measure respondents’ security skill, knowledge and confidence. For WCE, we adapted three items from extant studies (Paskevich et al. 1999; Salanova et al. 2003) to measure the extent of workgroup members’ consensual judgement about their branch office’s ISec capability. For SKC, we adapted five items from Faraj and Sproull (2000)’s work to measure the extent of workgroup member’s collective perception on security knowledge location, security knowledge needs and willingness to share security knowledge in their workgroups.

After assessing the content validity of these items with a panel of ISec experts, the refinement of the items in the questionnaire was made through a pilot test with 100 employees of other organizations with a similar structure of branch offices. As a result, a final set of 11 items representing 3 latent constructs were presented to the respondents in the J Organization.

## Analysis and Results

According to Preacher et al. (2010), traditional multilevel analysis techniques (MLM) are unable to model effects involving upper level dependent variables, whereas multilevel structural equation model (MSEM) can be used to accommodate mediation models containing mediators and outcome variables assessed at Level 2 (e.g., bottom-up effects). In their paper, Preacher et al. demonstrate that MSEM can separate variables and effects into within and between-group components to yield a more thorough and less misleading understanding of indirect effects in hierarchical data, as well as how MSEM can be deployed to accommodate mediation models, including mediators and outcome variables measured at multilevel. Carefully following such valid applications of MSEM, the current study is modeled to statistically capture the bottom-up effects (i.e., using an individual-level construct in predicting the higher-level constructs).

The analysis was performed in three steps. First, using 536 individual responses, the measurement model was assessed in terms of internal consistency, convergent validity and discriminant validity. Second, we checked within-group agreement and intra-class correlation for the validity of grouplevel constructs because the data was collected with the referent-shifted items. Third, we tested the hypothesized relationships among research variables with MSEM using Mplus software.

Many studies have analyzed the multilevel model by either aggregating the individual-level predictors to the group-level or disaggregating the group-level outcome variables to the individual-level to fit in with a single level regression analysis at the appropriate level (e.g., Hofmann 2004; Kanawattanachai and Yoo 2007). These methods, however, produce several statistical concerns, such as not presenting the heterogeneity within groups, decreased power of the statistical tests, inefficient estimates, biased standard errors, and overly liberal inferences for the model parameters (Krull and MacKinnon 1999; MacKinnon 2008). Thus, for the current study we chose MSEM along with our referent-shift design to carefully address the known statistical issues. Because our hypothesis testing involves the dependent variable measured at the group level and the independent variables captured through the referent-shifted perception of individual respondents with respect to their workgroup, we believe that the MSEM employed for this study is appropriate for the model because of its cross-level nature.

## Assessment of the Measurement Model

The results from composite reliability (> 0.8), Cronbach’s alpha (> 0.8), and average variance extracted (> 0.5) provide support for both convergent validity and unidimensionality of the measures on their associated factors. Table 2 lists the correlation matrix at the individual level and the workgroup level. The square root of AVE on the diagonal shows strong evidence of discriminant validity. We also conducted exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with varimax rotation and confirmatory factor (CFA) analysis. The results from both EFA and CFA support the unidimensionality of the measurement model by clearly providing three factors and good model fit (please see Appendix C for details).

## Assessment of Group-Level Constructs

We examined both inter-rater agreement and intra-class correlation (ICC) to assess the homogeneity of members’ perception for the group-referent variables. First, we calculated James’ index $( \mathrm { r } _ { \mathrm { w g ( j ) } } )$ to assess the inter-rater agreement for the multiple item scale (please see Appendix D for details). Generally, it is considered appropriate if the rwg(j) of the scale is greater than 0.7 (George 1990). Our test results show that for each group, rwg(j) values of all constructs exceed 0.7. Second, ICC was checked. Average values of ICC(1) and ICC(2) for each construct are WCE (0.22, 0.69) and SKC (0.21, 0.67). James (1982) reported a median ICC(1) value of 0.12 in the organizational literature, and Glick (1985) recommended an ICC(2) cutoff of 0.60. The results of rwg(j), ICC(1) and ICC(2) indicate that sample groups have enough within-group agreement and reliability, and between-group variability.

## Results

Before testing the hypotheses, we checked multicollinearity and minimum sample size. First, the variance inflation factors (VIF) values (ISE: 1.13, WCE:1.20, and SKC: 1.11) of independent variables were much lower than the recommended threshold of 5, indicating no threat of multicollinearity in the estimates (Neter et al. 1996). Second, our cluster sample size of 68 workgroups exceeded the minimum of 40 (Meuleman and Billiet 2009), which is adequate for model testing. We checked the model fit with several important indices (Hooper et al. 2008). All indices, except chi square value (178.35, $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and standardized root mean squared residual (SRMR) between (0.104), indicated a good fit of the model. Root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) (0.062) is lower than 0.08. Comparative fit index (CFI)/Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) (0.931/0.910) is larger than 0.9. SRMR within (0.069) is lower than 0.08. Overall, our model showed satisfactory model fit.

Based on the analytical procedure of MSEM, the results of the model were assessed. Following Preacher et al. (2010, p. 221), Figure 2 presents the summary results of the between model and the within model analyses with unstandardized values. All hypotheses in the between model are supported with expected signs. For H1, H2 and H3, indirect, direct and total effect were calculated by using MSEM in Mplus. First, Figure 2 shows that both WCE (7.355, p = 0.02) and SKC (20.821, p < 0.001) were significantly and directly related to WISE, respectively. As expected, the relationship between ISE and WCE was significant (0.723, p = 0.01), and the relationship between ISE and SKC was also significant (0.399, p = 0.03).

Next, we checked the indirect effects on three different relationships, all of which turned out to be significant (see Appendix E for further details of mediation analyses). Specifically, all indirect relationships between ISE and WISE mediated through WCE (H1, 5.315, p = 0.04), through SKC (H2, 8.299, p = 0.04), and through both SKC and WCE (H3, 1.298, p = 0.04) were significant, supporting H1, H2, and H3. Although the direct relationship between ISE and WISE was insignificant (-0.271, p = 0.48), the total effect (i.e., direct effect plus sum of all indirect effects) between ISE and WISE turned out to be significant (14.641, p = 0.04). In addition, we constructed the confidence intervals of each path to test the mediation effects at the 95% significance level using Mplus. As shown in Appendix E, all CIs of three indirect effects do not include zero, whereas the direct effect CIs include zero. The results in combination strongly supported our hypothesized relationship of both H1, H2, and H3 as full mediation. This mediation test clearly indicates that the variances at the group level of WCE provide more concrete explanation than what is provided by the individual level of ISE, as far as WISE is concerned. The results also illustrate that the ISE of each individual employee is important but should be coordinated to produce a desirable effect on the security performance at the workgroup level. In addition, the significant role of SKC in shaping WCE suggests that collective efficacy can be further strengthened when workgroups have security coordination practices. The findings of the analysis provide unique and important implications that bridge the understanding between individual-level and grouplevel security phenomena. Last, although not hypothesized, the within model results confirmed that SKC has a significant impact (0.485, p < 0.001) on WCE, and ISE significantly influences both WCE (0.343, p < 0.001) and SKC (0.114, p < 0.001) at the individual level.

## Discussion and Implications

Apart from the conventional approach related to directing individuals’ security compliance, the current study was initiated to understand the effective management of ISec performance at the workgroup level by examining the role of key group performance mechanisms, such as workgroup collective efficacy (WCE) and security knowledge coordination (SKC), alongside individual self-efficacy (ISE). The study of 68 workgroups in a law enforcement agency in South Korea supports our view that cross-level efforts, with the exercise of group management mechanisms, play a critical role of mediating ISE in the attainment of workgroup information security effectiveness (WISE), all of which were assessed with the organization’s security metrics as part of their annual branch-based performance evaluation. By framing the multilevel theory in terms of the IPO framework and testing the research model using the multilevel method of MSEM, our analysis has clearly articulated the multilevel nature of WISE and has suggested group mechanisms by which ISE could influence WISE. This study’s findings provided insightful implications for theory and practice, as follows.

First, our results empirically suggest that ISE is an integral part of WISE, and its effect is fully mediated through the group mechanisms of both WCE and SKC. This means that the individual security agents equipped with their own efficacy need coordination to attain workgroup ISec goals. While the results reaffirm the important role of ISE, this paper extends those insights beyond what we would obtain at the individual level, in applying ISE. While the significance of ISE has long been validated in the ISec literature, no study has thus far attempted to bridge an individual-level factor to group-level ISec performance. Our research note contributes to the extant ISec literature in this regard, and it is the first attempt to empirically provide an explanation as to the way ISE is significant in the context of WISE. While the behavioral control mechanisms for individual compliance identified in the extant literature (e.g., Cram et al. 2019; Moody et al. 2018) are anticipated to continue to serve as basic ingredients for overall security management, the results of the study clearly showed how group mechanisms and the individual factor play across different levels for achieving the ISec performance of the workgroup.

Second, the results show how the positive group dynamics inside the workplace bring an increased sense of belongingness to individual employees and thus help individuals in a workgroup invest themselves in the pursuit of workgroups ISec goals (Goo et al. 2014). This research showed that WCE enhanced WISE, which supported the view that a group or team-based approach for ISec management should not be neglected (Da Veiga and Eloff 2010). Our analysis indicates that WCE shapes workgroup members’ collective security actions. In other words, a sense of collective competence and motivation in achieving collective security goals may lead to an intentional course of actions in pursuit of the workgroup’s security performance. This finding empirically supports the notion of the exercise of collective agency in social cognitive theory (Bandura 1997). Although scarce attention has been paid to collective agency in the ISec context, our results suggest that escalating ISec management from the individual level to the workgroup level could be an effective way to enhance the collective agency that leads to the desired organizational ISec effectiveness. This result is particularly in line with the view that the group/organization-level approach to ISec management is desirable when considering the interdependent nature of ISec risks at work (Huang et al. 2014).

This finding also suggests that WCE can play a corrective role to counter possible situations of employees’ opportunistic security behaviors, which may emanate from his or her high self-efficacy. As argued by D’Arcy and Hovav (2009), for example, an increase in self-efficacy may not be linearly related to security compliance, and the organization’s efforts with security education may not be as effective as planned. Another issue is that security risks can emanate from employees’ intentional security avoidance, in the pursuit of individual productivity goals (Anderson and Moore 2006; Goo et al. 2014). However, the collective efficacy beliefs of workgroup members may work to counter such individual opportunism. For instance, individual employees could develop the perception of collective efficacy through a variety of interlocking security practices executed in workgroups and through interactions with one another when dealing with various security incidents. Such practices of the workgroup may work as cues to discourage individuals’ opportunistic security behaviors. This would reshape the perception and value of ISec and ultimately lead to increased intention to work together for the attainment of workgroup ISec performance goals.

Third, the resulting effect of SKC on WISE is consistent with recent ISec research, which argues in support of the positive impact of employees’ extra-role behaviors on ISec effectiveness (Hsu et al. 2015). Our results suggest that SKC would be particularly desirable for improving the effectiveness of workgroup ISec because the available ISec knowledge and skills across members can be integrated to deal with the circumstances of increasingly complex security threats (Yun et al. 2005). In addition, SKC’s constructive role in shaping WCE also supports the view in strategic management literature that recommends integrating the specialist knowledge of members, instead of creating new knowledge in the workgroup (Grant 1996).

## Limitations and Future Directions

As with all research, this study has limitations that must be recognized. First, the WISE measure is one of the strengths of the paper as it is a real-world artifact that exists in the field independent of the researchers. We do note, however, that specific details of branch-based security evaluation had been customized (but were not divulged to us) based on the needs and environments of J Organization to reflect the actual security practices of the branch offices. This may raise concerns with the generalizability of WISE. According to the CISO, however, their measures were developed based on Korean ISec standards (NIS 2010). That means our dependent variable has a certain level of generalizability in terms of managing organizational ISec. Future studies can benefit by examining different types of ISec performance measurements.

Second, WCE and SKC were not measured in terms of the objective skills or capabilities of the workgroup per se, but instead, were measured through self-reported individual responses about the workgroup’s capabilities to organize and execute the courses of action required to attain the security goals. That is, respondents’ perceptions of WCE and SKC may be colored by their own individual perceptions about their group’s encounters with the ISec practices. However, we believe that the referent-shift design (Hofmann 2004; Kudaravalli et al. 2017) along with the appropriate statistical assessment of group referent constructs has ensured that our focal constructs properly dealt with group-level phenomena using group-referent items.

Another potential limitation concerns the nature of the sample utilized in this study, and thus, the external validity of our findings. The survey in this study was aimed at workgroups that exist within the distributed structure of branch offices. Although such a sampling frame has been widely used in information systems research, no claim of external validity for this study’s findings can be made beyond the sampling frame. In addition, the sample was composed of branch offices in a Korean domestic organization. We anticipate that there could be systematic differences across countries with differential cultural factors. The workgroups described in this research note belong to a single organization (J Organization), and members only belong to non-overlapping groups. However, in other organizations, employees can belong to multiple workgroups, and workgroups can even span organizations. Besides being tested in a single Korean organization, the empirical test is also limited to an organization in the law and order sector, where the ISec environment may differ from other sectors. We believe that considering these complexities in the study design can provide good opportunities for future research.

Similarly, while not the focus of the current study, understanding the role of group mechanisms in enhancing WISE may not be culturally neutral because of the high collectivism and power distance nature of Korean culture (Hovav and D’Arcy 2012). For example, as found in Hovav and D’Arcy (2012), different types of deterrent measures have been seen among Korean and U.S. cultures. It is also possible that workgroup members are mandated to take certain actions by the workgroup senior in the Korean culture, and thus collective efficacy of the workgroup can be related to the efficacy of the senior person in the workgroup. Therefore, incorporating the role of a senior and examining the leadership in the workgroup, as well as the influence of the cultural factors, would be a natural extension of the current study.

## Conclusions

Notwithstanding the above limitations, this research makes two significant contributions. First, by adopting a multilevel approach, this study opens up a new stream of research that evaluates and predicts ISec effectiveness at the workgroup level. In addition, advancing multilevel theory, our study is among the first to introduce the IPO framework along with the multilevel method of MSEM for testing cross-level models in the ISec literature. With two core group mechanisms drawn from group performance literature, this study has provided empirical evidence of their significant and positive role in enhancing WISE. Second, by empirically examining the ISE and the WCE in a single model, we have clearly distinguished the impact of WCE from ISE, in pursuit of the WISE, where the group process of SKC plays a central role in effectively deploying the individual’s security knowledge for the benefit of WISE. With it, this study has made a unique and important contribution to the ISec literature by linking the ISec phenomena occurring at individual and group levels.

## Appendix A

## Workgroup Information Security Effectiveness

As explained in the body of the paper, information regarding the dependent variable, WISE, is provided in detail in this appendix. It is important to note that full details were unavailable from J Organization. This was because of the necessity to uphold ISec regulations and the confidentiality of information as enforced by the organization’s ISec policy. However, generic information regarding WISE and its contextualization in the ISec context was shared with us by J Organization and is detailed in this Appendix A.

Before we provide details on ten indicators composing the WISE measure, we begin by describing both tasks and the ISec environment in the branch offices of J Organization. Branch officers are mainly in charge of local public order, case investigation, and other civil affairs. Although they deal with cybercrime reports and are given cybersecurity awareness training, they are not considered IT experts. As law-enforcers they are trained to use relevant information, as compared to maintaining and securing information assets. For example, for case investigation or civil affairs they access criminal record systems as they are supported by the organization’s private and dedicated infrastructure. Although Internet access is available and allowed in the branch offices, dedicated network infrastructures through network segregation are used for the main law enforcement services. Proper security procedures for accessing confidential information based on the level of clearance are thus essential to retrieving information and submitting reports with the dedicated systems. While the information regarding cases or people is not to be shared, the information regarding ISec procedures put in place need to be shared for common understanding by employees in the branch offices, so that it helps avoid possible employees’ misconduct or system exploitation. Therefore, employees need to be equipped with knowledge regarding how to use the systems and accompanying procedures to avoid invalid data access and information leakage among the internal personnel, and to protect information assets from outsiders. These practices require coordination efforts within workgroups. In addition, while the branch offices are given feedback from the ISec office for future corrective actions, individual employees in branch offices are not informed of the results of the annual ISec evaluation, such as their actual workgroup ISec performance rating. Therefore, we believe that, while plausible, the concern of acquiescence bias in responses from the branch officers is not a serious threat in the data.

As addressed in the paper, we adopted WISE as the dependent variable in this paper. WISE is a critical measure of ISec effectiveness at J Organization, encompassing ten broad areas of ISec. These ten sub-dimensions reflect different aspects of security effectiveness. However, they are tightly linked by the main theme, ISec. And they are under the control of the branch. Therefore, we believe our broader construct (i.e., ISE, WCE and SKC) and their survey items are well linked theoretically and empirically to WISE because our focal constructs measure and target ISec-related perceptions of employees in the branch offices in the sample organization.WISE in this study is not a typical measurement of a construct but an actual artifact that exists in the organization, independent of the researchers. We believe that very few security studies have used broader survey items to measure predictors of dependent variables using real-world data, due mainly to the difficulty of accessing such information. We consider this is a strength of the study because it shows how the perception of WCE and SKC influence a real-world outcome—one that measures the actual security practices of the branch offices in terms of the branch office’s annual security assessment scores, as assessed by the head office at J Organization. Table A1 illustrates ten indicators of WISE and their generic descriptions

Although the information regarding actual measures used for each indicator was not disclosed due to its confidential nature, J Organization graciously shared with us important characteristics of the ten indicators. First, regarding the exact formula or weights used in aggregating ten indicators to produce the branch office’s annual security assessment scores, J Organization treated all ten indicators as equally important.

Second, with varying degree, all ten indicators track individual employee’s actual actions with its corresponding measures for ISec assessment of the branch offices. Take the indicator of access control as an example; basic access control is the responsibility of a security administrator, which is thus not included in the measurement of branch offices. However, the branch office as well as the employees who handle the tasks are required to closely cooperate with the administrator for prompting access re-certifications in maintaining an up-to-date list. Otherwise, i would result in the issue of “access (or privilege) creep,” leading to increased insider threat. Thus, these actions are included in the evaluation of branch offices.

Third, all ten indicators for evaluating the branch office fall under the branch office’s control and responsibility, meaning the operations under the branch office’s control of the ISec are measured for each of the ten indicators, whereas managing network systems or developing contingent plans, for example, are not counted in their evaluation because they are the responsibility of the IT department. Take the network systems security indicator as an example—compliance with network segregation policy is evaluated because network segregation compliance requires multifactor authentication by the employees in the branch office for gaining access to various systems in the organization. For the operations management security indicator, manual tracking and securing the inventory of registered IT devices in branch offices is evaluated, whereas purchasing and installing network devices is the responsibility of the IT department.

Last, due to the nature of the law enforcement organization, every branch office was strictly required to stay on the same page in terms of ISec policies and implementation. However, because the ISec evaluation through ten indicators was largely based on actual actions/behaviors of employees in the branch office who were often not ISec experts, the ISec performance differed across branch offices. Therefore, a branch office as a whole was responsible for exercising ISec procedures in a coordinated manner for its ISec evaluation.

In sum, given this information, we believe that the ten indicators sufficiently capture WISE in the branch offices, allowing for an examination of important group-level dynamics that emerge in an ISec context. This convinced us that the influence of workgroup mechanisms, (i.e., WCE and SKC) as well as individual factors (i.e., ISE) were well captured through the ten indicators.

## Assessment of Group-Level Constructs

In the case of shared constructs, construct definitions rest on assumptions regarding within-group agreement and between-group variability (Bliese 2000). The construct and measures would be flawed if these assumptions were wrong. We therefore checked both issues: (1) within group agreement, which examines whether the construct is shared within groups with James Index (rwg(j)), and (2) between group reliability (variability), which examines whether groups differ significantly with intra-class correlation (ICC). ICC(1), also known as ICC(1, 1), represents a form of proportional consistency. James (1982) explained the ICC(1) as the degree of reliability in regards to a single assessment of the group mean. To estimate the ICC(1), the ratio of between-group variance to total variance is computed. One the other hand, ICC(2), also known as ICC(1, k) [k is the number of data values per group], provides an estimate of the reliability of the group means (Shrout and Fleiss 1979). ICC(1) and ICC(2) are related to each other as a function of group size (Klein and Kozlowski 2000).

The results of our assessment showed that values of rwg(j) (see Table D1), ICC(1) and ICC(2) [i.e., WCE (0.22, 0.69) and SKC (0.21, 0.67)] were well above their respective thresholds, which are 0.7 for James Index (rwg(j)) (George 1990; James 1988), 0.12 for a median value of ICC(1) (James 1982), and 0.6 for ICC(2) (Glick 1985). This indicates that sample groups hold significant within-group agreement and reliability and between-group variability. Therefore, we confirmed that the referent-shifted items that were used to measure the constructs were valid for the two group-level constructs.

## Mediation Analyses

This study involves a multiple-step multiple-mediator model to test the hypotheses (Hayes 2009). As Figure E1 illustrates, the relationship between individual self-efficacy (ISE) and workgroup information security effectiveness (WISE) is mediated by multiple mediators: workgroup collective efficacy (WCE) and security knowledge coordination (SKC). Moreover, we hypothesized that a multiple-step mediating relationship exists between mediators at the same time. So, special care was taken to test mediation in the model using Mplus. We selected MODEL CONSTRAINT command to create functions of model parameters because simple MODEL INDIRECT command did not work for the multilevel model in Mplus. (Please refer to the reference of Mplus code examples (Preacher 2019, p.9) for details of Mplus command information used in the study.)

Figure E1. Multiple-Step Multiple-Mediator Model

To examine the mediation effects, three types of paths are assessed (Vance et al. 2015): (1) the paths from the independent variables to the mediating variables (i.e., path $\mathbf { A } _ { 1 } , \mathbf { A } _ { 2 }$ ,and $\mathbf { A } _ { 3 }$ in Figure E1), (2) the paths from the mediating variables to the dependent variable (i.e., path $\mathrm { B } _ { \mathrm { l } }$ and $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { B } _ { 2 } ) , } \end{array}$ and (3) the path from the independent variable to the dependent variable (i.e., path C’, the direct effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable when assessed simultaneously with paths $\mathbf { A } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ and $\mathbf { B } _ { \mathrm { i } } , \mathbf { i } = 1 , 2$ , and $3 ; \mathrm { j } = 1$ and 2). With these paths and for our multiplestep multiple-mediating model, Mplus computed the direct effect (i $. \mathrm { e } . , \mathrm { C } ^ { \setminus } )$ , the indirect effects $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \mathrm { A } , \mathrm { B } _ { 1 }$ for H1 ${ \bf A } _ { 2 } { \bf B } _ { 2 }$ for H2, and $\mathbf { A } _ { 2 } \mathbf { A } _ { 3 } \mathbf { B } _ { 1 }$ for H3), and the total effect between ISE and WISE (labeled as $\mathrm { C } ) ,$ which is equal to the direct effect of ISE on WISE, plus the sum of the indirect effect through WCE, the indirect effect through SKC, and the indirect effect through the multiple-step of SKC and WCE (i.e., ${ \bf C } = { \bf C } ^ { 3 } + { \bf A } _ { 1 } { \bf B }$ $+ \mathbf { A } _ { 2 } \mathbf { B } _ { 2 } + \mathbf { A } _ { 2 } \mathbf { A } _ { 3 } \mathbf { B } _ { 1 } )$ , as shown in Figure E1.

Next, among three popular methods for constructing confidence intervals (CIs) of the mediation effects—delta method, bootstrap methods, and the distribution of the product of the coefficients method (MacKinnon et al. 2007; MacKinnon et al. 2002; Tofighi and Thoemmes 2014)— Mplus constructed CIs using delta method (Preacher et al. 2010). The delta method is a general method that derives the variance of a function of asymptotically normal random variables with known variance (Gold et al. 2018), and computes an asymptotic normal theory confidence intervals that are based on the asymptotic properties of the maximum likelihood (ML) estimator (Sobel 1982). The asymptotic normal theory confidence interval utilizes the estimate of the indirect effect, its standard error, and standard normal distribution critical values to generate a symmetric confidence interval (Tofighi and Thoemmes 2014). Although bootstrap (resampling) methods are often suggested for constructing CIs in the single level analysis due to possible asymmetric distribution of the mediation effects (Vance et al. 2015), Mplus adopts delta method because bootstrap (resampling) methods are not yet available to test an indirect effect for a multilevel mediation model (Preacher et al. 2010; Tofighi and Thoemmes 2014). Instead, Mplus MSEM uses the maximum likelihood robust (MLR) estimator, which is the ML estimator with standard errors that are robust to non-normality

In addition, although the distribution of the product of the coefficients method (MacKinnon et al. 2007; MacKinnon et al. 2002) has been shown to be an alternative for calculating CIs in the multilevel model, compared with the asymptotic normal theory and bootstrap methods (MacKinnon et al. 2007; MacKinnon et al. 2002), the distribution of the product of the coefficients method is not yet available to test a multiplestep multiple-mediator model for the study (Tofighi and Thoemmes 2014). Therefore, given the unavailability of two other methods for calculating CIs of mediation effect in our multiple-step multiple-mediator model, tests of the mediation effects using CIs constructed with delta method in Mplus deemed appropriate for our multiple-step multiple-mediator model (Preacher et al. 2010).

The results are provided in Table E1. For the CIs of estimates calculated by Mplus, we state with 95% confidence that the effect exists if zero is not between the lower and upper bound of the CIs (MacKinnon 2008). Furthermore, full or partial mediation of the focal relationships can also be determined by evaluating the CIs for the direct effect (i.e., C’). Specifically , full mediation occurs when (1) zero is not between the lower and upper CIs of indirect effect $( \mathrm { i . e . , A _ { 1 } B _ { 1 , } A _ { 2 } B _ { 2 , } o r A _ { 2 } A _ { 3 } B _ { 1 } } )$ and (2) zero is between the lower and upper CIs of the direct effect (i.e., C’). Partial mediation occurs when (1) zero is not between the lower and upper CIs of indirect effect $( \mathrm { i . e . , A _ { 1 } B _ { 1 , } A _ { 2 } B _ { 2 , } o r A _ { 2 } A _ { 3 } B _ { 1 } } )$ and (2) zero is not between the lower and upper CIs of the direct effect (Shrout and Bolger 2002). As shown in Table E1, CIs of the direct effect (C’, b = -0.271) do include zero, which means the direct effect is insignificant. However, CIs of all indirect effects, $\mathrm { A } _ { 1 } \mathrm { B } _ { 1 } ( \mathrm { b } = 5 . 3 1 5 , p = 0 . 0 4 ) , \mathrm { A } _ { 2 } \mathrm { B } _ { 1 }$ $( \mathbf { b } = 8 . 2 9 9 , p = 0 . 0 4 )$ and A A B (b = 1.298, p = 0.04), do not include zero, which means the indirect effects are significant, supporting H1, H2, and H3 respectively. And the total effect between ISE and WISE $( \mathrm { C } , \mathrm { b } = 1 4 . 6 4 1 , p = 0 . 0 4 )$ is also significant. The results clearly indicate that there exists the full mediation of all three indirect relationships in the model.
