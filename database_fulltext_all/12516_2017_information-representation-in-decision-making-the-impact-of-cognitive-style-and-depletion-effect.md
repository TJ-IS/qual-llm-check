## A B S T R A C T

Although the literature on information representation in decision support has argued for a long time that the way in which information is presented to decision makers should fit both task characteristics and the cognitive style of decision makers, the latter aspect has received much less attention in empirical research. Most studies that took into account cognitive style used rather general instruments to measure it, which do not focus on the specifics of managerial decision making. In this paper, we describe an experiment that uses an instrument specifically developed for a managerial context to study the relationship between cog nitive style and decision performance when using tabular or graphical representations. We also take into account that having to deal with a misfitting information representation depletes cognitive resources, and thus might not only impede the solution of the current problem, but also impact subsequent problems. Our results confirm that a mismatch between information representation and cognitive style indeed has effects that last beyond the solution of the current decision problem.

## 1. Introduction

The effects that different forms of presenting information might have on decision makers have been an important topic of the DSS literature since its early beginnings (e.g., [1,2]). Presentation of information in information systems is still an important topic. Information visualization, which deals with different methods of presenting data, has developed into a large subfield of computer science [3]. The very early literature on information presentation already emphasized that the form in which information is provided to decision makers has to fit both the characteristics of the problem and of the decision maker, in particular their cognitive style. However, over time, the fit of problem representations to decision maker characteristics received considerably less attention in literature than the fit to task characteristics [4]. This lack of consideration of decision maker characteristics is still evident in recent literature on information visualization. Evaluation of different visualization forms is an important topic in that literature, and various methods are used for this purpose [5]. Although literature sometimes mentions that individual characteristics might play a role in the effectiveness of visual representations [6], empirical studies that actually refer to user characteristics are quite rare. According to a survey by Isenberg et al. [7], <5% of the evaluation studies surveyed involved some forms of user performance experiments at all, and that survey does not even mention experiments considering individual user characteristics or decision making styles.

This prolonged disinterest of researchers in the relationship between individual characteristics of decision makers and the suitability of information presentation formats is surprising. It is a well established fact in research on managerial decision making [8] that individuals approach decision problems in different ways, and many instruments for measuring decision making styles have been developed (e.g., [9,10]; for a comparison of different instruments, see [11]). The argument raised in early literature against considering decision making styles [12], that the concept lacks conceptual clarity and empirical validity, is thus no longer valid. In our opinion, it is therefore necessary to reconsider the relationship between decision making styles and information presentation. Since computer based information is often used for decision making in a business context, we specifically focus on an instrument that was developed to measure cognitive style of managers in an organizational setting, the Cognitive Style Index CSI developed by Allinson and Hayes [9], and analyze whether different forms of information representation are suitable for decision makers having different decision making styles according to that classification. To the best of our knowledge, this instrument was not used in information system research before, but to us it seems to be particularly useful to classify potential users of DSS in a managerial context.

Regardless of how it is measured, the concept of a decision making style refers to a stable personal trait. However, the way in which individuals make decisions is not necessarily constant over time. Decision making requires cognitive effort, and human beings have only limited cognitive resources [13,14], which are depleted by tasks such as decision making. Depletion of cognitive resources negatively influences performance in following tasks that again require cognitive resources. Cognitive resources can be replenished over time by resting or consumption of glucose, but in the short run, depletion leads to a degradation of cognitive processes and has a negative effect on decision quality [15,16]. Having to deal with an inadequate problem representation, which does not fit the decision maker’s cognitive style, increases the cognitive load [17,18]. However, given a suficient stock of cognitive resources, it is possible that this depletion does not yet affect solution quality in the current problem, but will only impede performance in subsequent problems. Therefore, we study the effect of fit or misfit between problem representation and cognitive style not only with respect to one single decision problem, but in the context of a sequence of decision problems to identify such delayed effects.

The emphasis of this study is on information presentation. From the decision maker’s perspective, we focus on the acquisition of information needed for decision making, rather than on processing. We therefore use a task in which subjects have to rank a number of alternatives, rather than selecting only one alternative. A ranking task forces decision makers to evaluate every alternative, while in a choice task, decision makers who follow a simple aspiration based strategy could stop their search after finding one satisfactory alternative. We thus ensure that all subjects have to obtain and process the same significant amount of information. At the same time, we kept the necessary calculations at a low level by using very simple lotteries as decision alternatives.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 provides the theoretical basis of our research by giving a literature review on cognitive fit of presentation formats and decision making. Section 3 formulates our specific hypotheses. Section 4 presents the experiment we have conducted. Section 5 reviews the results and Section 6 concludes the paper with a discussion and an outlook to future research.

## 2. Literature review and motivation

Information representation constitutes a major research topic for information systems [19]. Multiple studies in the literature show the importance of information representation in different settings, e.g., multi-criteria decision problems [20], electronically supported negotiations [21], process models [22,23], auctions [24], simulation [25], design of supply chain networks [26] and representation of uncertainty in data [27]. It is known that the way decision makers are provided with information influences their decisions from task involvement to potential biases [28], but the mechanics of these effects are not yet completely understood [29]. Kelton et al. [29] provide an exhaustive literature review about the effects of static information representation on individual decision making. Interactive information representation, in which the decision maker can select which information is presented in which form, is reviewed by Dilla et al. [30].

Early literature on information representation, as surveyed e.g. by DeSanctis [2] and Jarvenpaa and Dickson [31] focused on the comparison of direct effects of different presentation forms. Studies which only compared presentation forms, but did not include other factors such as psychometric characteristics of the decision maker, found only mixed evidence concerning performance (e.g. [4,32]). Attempts to resolve these inconclusive results motivated the development of the cognitive fit model [33] as a general framework integrating various factors that influence problem solving performance.

According to this model, problem solving operates on a mental representation of the problem in working memory. This mental representation results from the task definition and the external problem representation provided to the decision maker. If processing these elements is within the cognitive capabilities of the person, there is a match between the information and the problem-solving elements, resulting in an effective solution process. In case of a mismatch, creating the mental representation requires additional processing steps. Hence, an inconsistency between external representation and preferred internal model leads to ineficient problem solving [33]. Later works extended the cognitive fit model by emphasizing aspects of individual cognition and perception [34–36]. Kelton et al. [29] extend the cognitive fit model and argue that this model should serve as a general framework for information representation studies. In particular, Kelton et al. [29], in their extended version of the cognitive fit model, refer to cognitive style. One important property of cognitive style according to Kelton et al. [29, p. 81] is that “. . . certain characteristics of the decision maker such as . . . cognitive style are unlikely to be affected by a single use of a presentation format or task.”, and therefore cognitive style can be considered as a factor that influences decision making performance in a consistent way across several tasks.

Although the extended cognitive fit model indicates that an information representation should fit both the task characteristics and the user’s cognitive abilities and style, literature has to a large extent focused on the fit between task characteristics and information presentation (e.g., [37]). In the information visualization literature, the lack of consideration of user characteristics has repeatedly been criticized (e.g. [38–40]). For example Baciˇ c and Fadlalla´ [41] note that the link between user characteristics and representation form has been ignored by a large body of literature. Similarly, Adnan et al. [42] point out the significance of individual cognitive differences as one of the key determinants of system success and state that the topic has not received enough attention. While evaluation is an important topic in the literature on information visualization, a survey of evaluation studies by Isenberg et al. [7] found that only about 5% of the studies used experiments with users for evaluation. In the comprehensive survey on evaluation of information visualization methods by Liu et al. [3], out of 19 papers using experiments, only one [43] considers individual characteristics of users and distinguishes between users of high and low numeracy.

Although rare, there are some studies in the information visualization literature that take into account user characteristics in various contexts. Some studies (e.g., [44]) consider information retrieval in databases and are therefore less relevant for our work. Several studies compare information acquisition using graphical and tabular formats taking into account individual factors like experience [45] or “spatial ability” [32] of users. Bak and Meyer [46] study the performance of subjects in a data mining task and find that users with stronger analytical orientation are better able to detect relationships using graphical representations. More closely related to decision making are studies that consider the performance of users in Bayesian inference tasks. In that context, Kellen et al. [47] compared various graphical displays for users with high and low spatial abilities, and Micallef et al. [43] textual and visual problem representations taking into account numeracy of users. Adnan et al. [42] explicitly refer to a DSS environment and compare decision times in a planning problem for users with different decision making styles when using visual representations showing different levels of detail.

Beyond this literature, there are also some studies that find differences in preferences for information representation due to cognitive styles (e.g. [48–51]). However, these papers do not actually test the performance of users with different cognitive styles, they only reflect preferences for receiving information in a certain form.

Clearly, the scarce literature that exists has not yet reached a consensus on which user characteristics to take into account. In this paper, we argue that relevant decision maker characteristics that influence the performance in a decision making task when using different forms of information presentation are best captured by the concept of cognitive style. Cognitive style is defined as: “ Consistent individual differences in preferred ways of organizing and processing information and experience.”([52] p. 4) Cognitive style is a stable property of each individual [53] that cannot be trained [54] and that is unrelated to intelligence [55]. However, it influences performance, because different tasks fit better to one style than to others [55]. If an individual is confronted with a problem representation that does not fit to one’s cognitive style, the individual has to perform additional steps to organize the information in the appropriate way [17]. These additional steps require cognitive resources, which are then no longer available for actual problem solving. This lack of cognitive resources could lead individuals to use less demanding, but also less reliable strategies for problem solving. In the language of the dual processing model [56], the resource consuming and effortful System 2, which handles conscious and analytical problem solving, might be replaced by the heuristic methods of System 1, which are more susceptible to biases in decision making.

Cognitive style can be measured in a variety of ways. It can be represented on a one-dimensional continuum (e.g., [9,57]), or in several dimensions (e.g., [10]). We argue that for the purpose of our research, a one-dimensional measure is more applicable. According to the cognitive continuum theory of Hammond et al. [58], each task can be positioned on an analytic - intuitive scale depending on its cognitive requirements. Human beings are usually neither completely analytic nor completely intuitive, so their preferred way of how to receive information and thus their cognitive style can be measured on the same scale. An optimal task performance is achieved, if scores on both scales are close to each other, i.e. the cognitive style matches the requirements of the task.

The Cognitive Style Index (CSI) of Allinson and Hayes [9] is an instrument to measure an individual’s position on the analyticintuitive scale. At the extreme points, analytics are described as conscious learners of information with a deliberate information acquisition process. At the other extreme, intuitive individuals have an unconscious information acquisition process. They are most likely to consider information as a whole and regard all elements simultaneously. Hence, while analytics are capable to report their patterns and rules for acquiring information, intuitives cannot do such reporting with ease. Individuals placed in the middle area of the scale are classified as having the “adaptive” cognitive style. Since the CSI was specifically developed to measure decision making styles in an organizational setting, we consider it particularly relevant for a DSS context.

Literature on decision making frequently assumes that the information processing capacity of decision makers, although perhaps limited, remains constant over time. However, there is empirical evidence that this is not the case and that the decision making strategy employed depends on the availability of cognitive resources [59]. A theoretical basis for this phenomenon is provided by the strength model of self control. According to this model, the ability to take control over one’s impulsive responses is a limited resource, that can be depleted by tasks that require cognitive effort. Baumeister et al. [14] define this phenomenon as ego depletion. Hagger et al. [60] report significant effects of ego depletion on task performance in a rich meta analysis of 83 ego depletion studies. Since decision makers in general tend to avoid cognitively demanding tasks [61], lack of self-control resulting from depleted cognitive resources will lead to an increased utilization of simple heuristics. Therefore, ego depletion caused by cognitively demanding tasks will lead to lower decision quality in subsequent decision tasks.

A mismatch between presentation format and the user’s cognitive style causes an increase in the cognitive load on the decision maker that can even be detected in electroencephalography [18]. We therefore expect a mismatch between information presentation and cognitive style to lead to increased depletion, with in turn will impede performance in subsequent decision tasks regardless of the presentation form used in these tasks. It should be noted that depletion cannot be measured directly, research on depletion only measures it indirectly by considering task performance and time needed in subsequent tasks (e.g. [59–62]). In line with this stream of research, we therefore study the impact of a match or mismatch between information presentation and cognitive style not only on the decision task for which information is provided, but also on performance in a subsequent task.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

The main focus of our study is on the fit between the (external) format used to present the task to the decision maker, and the decision maker’s cognitive style. Cognitive fit theory suggests that a mismatch between external representation and style will make the transformation of the problem to the decision maker’s internal mental problem representation more effortful and thus requires more cognitive resources. The resulting depletion in cognitive resources will lead to a degradation in performance in the solution of the task at hand, and also to a reduced performance in subsequent tasks. Fig. 1 provides an overview of our model of one decision task.

We distinguish between the transformation of the problem from external to internal representation and the actual problem solving step. Decision makers having different (intuitive or analytical) cognitive styles will use different internal representations. The more similar the external representation is to the preferred representation, the lesser the effort for this transformation. In line with existing literature, we consider an analytic decision maker’s internal representation, which focuses on detailed information about alternatives,

to be more easily created from a tabular display, and a more holistic internal representation of an intuitive decision maker to be more readily obtainable from a graphical representation. Thus we denote tables for the analytics and graphs for the intuitives as matching representation forms and the opposite as mismatching representation forms.

Once the mental problem representation is created, the decision maker can solve the actual problem. This process also requires cognitive resources, which come from the same stock that was already used for creating the mental representation. The amount of cognitive resources required for this task depends on the cognitive abilities of the decision maker. If cognitive resources were already depleted by previous operations, performance in the solution process and consequently solution quality will be lower. Since the problem solution process also consumes cognitive resources, the stock of cognitive resources available for subsequent problems will be reduced.

To study these depletion effects on solution quality in subsequent problems, we conduct an experiment consisting of two stages. In each stage, subjects are confronted with the same set of problems, but in different representation forms. Depending on the subjects cognitive styles, they are therefore in a matching condition in one stage and a mismatching condition in the other stage. Fig. 2 provides an overview of the experimental setup and the hypotheses.

We measure solution quality via errors in the ranking of alternatives. Our first hypothesis compares performance within the same stage. We expect that a mismatch between external representation and mental representation (and thus cognitive style) depletes cognitive resources, which are then not available for problem solving. Therefore we hypothesize:

H 1. Total errors will be higher if information is provided in a mismatching form as opposed to information in a matching form.

Since the experiment consists of two stages, we can split Hypothesis 1 into two sub-hypotheses as:

H 1a. First stage errors will be higher for subjects who are in the mismatching condition in the first stage in comparison to subjects who are the matching condition in the first stage.

H 1b. Second stage errors will be higher for subjects who are in the mismatching condition in the second stage in comparison to subjects who are in the matching condition in the second stage.

H 3. The difference in the number of errors between subjects under the matching condition in the second stage and subjects in the matching condition in the first stage (i.e., between situations C and B) will be larger than the difference between subjects in the mismatching condition in the second stage and subjects in the mismatching condition in the first stage (i.e., between situations A and D).

Note that in the presence of depletion effects, hypothesis H1b implies that the direct effect of matching is stronger than the depletion effect. According to hypothesis H3, subjects in situation C are more depleted than subjects in situation D, but their task is less demanding because they receive information in the matching format. Whether subjects in situation C or D will actually perform better therefore depends on the relative strengths of the depletion vs. the matching effect.

## 4. Study design and data collection

## 4.1. Study design

As we have already argued in the introduction section, effects of presentation formats are more important in the information acquisition part of the entire process than in the actual problem solving, which operates on the internal problem representation of the decision maker. We therefore use a task that requires subjects to acquire a considerable (and across subjects constant) amount of information, while actual processing requirements are comparatively low. The decision task we use is to rank a set of lotteries, which each involve two outcomes of equal probabilities, by their expected values. The information that needs to be conveyed to decision makers in this task consists of the two outcomes for each lottery, which is presented either in the form of a table or as a bar chart. By requiring a ranking of the lotteries, we make sure that subjects have to acquire and process information about all lotteries. Estimating the expected value of two equally likely outcomes is a very simple task. Furthermore, ranking uncertain alternatives is a very common type of decision to be made

in a managerial context, for which information provided by a DSS is commonly used. Our subjects are business students, therefore they are all familiar with the concept of expected values.

Since the aim of our study is to analyze the effects of matching between presentation form and cognitive style, we deliberately selected two very common presentation forms, tables and bar charts, rather than more elaborate visualizations. Thus, we avoid that the matching effect is confounded with possible effects of misunderstanding caused by unfamiliar problem representations. This level of familiarity is confirmed by a post experimental questionnaire, in which subjects (except for the two most complex problems) did not indicate a significant difference in the level of perceived problem difficulty for the two representations on average across all subjects. This result also indicates that the level of experience, which also might effect performance using different visualizations [45], is the same for both forms.

In each stage of the experiment, subjects had to solve six problems, which involved comparing from two to seven lotteries. As our hypotheses refer to two subsequent tasks, we employed a mixed within subjects and between subjects design. All subjects had to perform both stages of the experiment, corresponding to situations A and C, or B and D, in Fig. 2. Since the characterization of tables and graphs as matching or mismatching representation depends on the cognitive style of each subject, which was only measured as part of the experiment, subjects were randomly assigned to either the sequence graph-table or table-graph. Which of the two presentation forms consisted a matching or mismatching condition was determined after the experiment. However, each subject performed one stage under a matching and one under a mismatching condition. For additional control variables, we also obtained demographic information about subjects, and the cognitive reflection index of the subjects, measured by the items of Frederick [63].

The experiment thus consisted of four parts: cognitive style index measure, six ranking tasks in one format, six ranking tasks in the other format, and finally cognitive reflection index test and demographics. For table and graph formats, the same lottery set was used in order to preserve comparability. To mitigate learning effects, the labels and the order of appearance of the lotteries were changed randomly. Participants were instructed to work as quickly as they could, but without explicit time pressure (i.e., no countdown on the screen). They were also informed that an exact calculation of the expected values was not necessary. In order to ease participant’s cognitive burden, on-screen instructions were kept the same among all lottery types and complexities.

The participants ordered the lotteries by dragging and dropping the lottery labels to the corresponding slot on the experimental screen. The instruction text above the graph and table representations remained the same throughout the lotteries (for the screen instructions see Appendix A).

## 4.2. Measurement

Our hypotheses refer to matching and mismatching conditions as the main experimental factor. To determine which representation represents the matching (or mismatching) condition, we classified subjects using the Cognitive Style Index (CSI) of Allinson and Hayes [9]. The CSI questionnaire was used in its original wording with permission from the authors, and was administered to subjects at the beginning of the experiment. The CSI score ranges between 0 and 76 and covers the classifications analytic, quasi-analytic, adaptive, quasi-intuitive and intuitive. As a measure of cognitive abilities (which can be different from the cognitive style), we employed the Cognitive Reflection Index (CRI) of Frederick [63]. It has been shown in literature (e.g. [64]), that impulsive decision makers make more errors in ranking tasks in comparison to reflective decision makers.

Therefore, the CRI provides a good approximation of cognitive abilities in solving this type of task.

We measure decision performance by considering the errors in the subjects’ rankings compared to the ranking based on correct expected values. To measure the difference between two rankings, we used the Levenshtein distance [65], which counts the minimum number of edits in the sequence needed to obtain the correct sequence. To correct for the different number of lotteries to be ranked, we divided all distances by the maximum Levenshtein distance possible for a given problem size, and thus obtained an error ratio scaled between zero and one, where zero indicates absence of errors.

Following the literature on depletion (e.g., [13,60,66]) we consider the reduction in performance in the second stage as a measure of depletion. Although there are studies in that literature on ways to overcome depletion (e.g., by rest or consuming glucose), literature on depletion does not use indicators like blood glucose levels to measure depletion, but entirely relies on subsequent task performance as a measure of depletion. We follow this approach in our research.

## 4.3. Participants

137 female (age: l = 23.4, s = 3.2) and 90 male (age: l = $2 3 . 7 , \sigma = 2 . 8 )$ students from a university in central Europe took part in the experiment and were rewarded with extra course credits. Incentives were independent of their actual performance. 201 of the subjects came from business administration, 26 from other subjects. There was only one native English speaker in the sample. The self reported English fluency is l = 3.76 (s = 0.86) on a 5-point Likert scale, 1 meaning poor English knowledge and 5 meaning fluent English knowledge. In order to ensure that the participants understood the instructions, we allowed them to use an online dictionary of their choice during the experiment.

## 5. Results and discussion

## 5.1. Descriptive statistics

To validate the psychometric properties of the CSI scale in our data, we performed a confirmatory factor analysis and calculated the Cronbach alpha coeficient for the CSI questionnaire. Both the VSS criterion and the Velicer MAP criterion indicated a one factor solution, and the Cronbach alpha of 0.88 also confirms the consistency of that instrument. The average CSI score of the 227 subjects in the sample is 43.7 (s = 12.1). Using the thresholds provided by Allinson and Hayes [9], 50 subjects are classified as analytics, 29 as intuitives, 57 as quasi-analytics, 40 as quasi-intuitives, and 51 as adaptives. The average score in the CRI questionnaire measuring impulsiveness is 2.09 (s = 1.05). The average value of our dependent variable, the error ratio, across all subjects and treatments is 0.196 (s = 0.236).

Fig. 3 illustrates the error ratios of the different cognitive style groups for the two representation forms. This figure clearly reflects the matching between decision making styles and problem representation. Analysts and quasi-analysts perform better when using tables, while intuitive and quasi-intuitive decision makers exhibit fewer errors when information is presented in graphical form. A nonparametric Wilcoxon test indicates that the difference between representation forms is not significant for subjects having the adaptive style, and is significant at the 5% level $( p = 0 . 0 1 0 3 )$ ) for quasi-intuitive decision makers. For all other types, the difference is significant at the 1% level.

Based on these results, we consider a tabular representation for a subject having a CSI score > 38 as a matching representation, and a graphical representation for subjects below that threshold. The opposite situations are considered as mismatching representations.

## 5.2. Test of hypotheses

Fig. 4 provides first insights into the effects of matching problem representation and depletion on the error ratio as indicated in our Hypotheses 1 and 2a and 2b. A matching information presentation leads to considerably lower error rates in the first stage of the experiment. However, the difference is much smaller in the second stage. Therefore, it seems that the advantage of a matching representation in the second stage is mostly offset by the higher degree of depletion from a mismatching representation in the first stage.

To statistically test our hypotheses, we regressed the error ratio on dummy variables indicating the stage and the matching condition. We furthermore included problem complexity (the number of lotteries to be ranked) and impulsiveness as control variables and used a random effects model on subjects to account for other unobserved subject characteristics.

According to Hypothesis 1, subjects in a matching condition should exhibit lower error rates than subjects in a mismatching condition, thus the main effect of the matching dummy should be negative. Hypotheses 2a and 2b deal with differences between first and second stages and predict a significant positive main effect of the stage dummy. Since assignment of a presentation form as “matching” could be problematic for subjects in the middle range of the CSI score, we complemented the analysis of all data with a regression excluding the second terzile of subjects. Results of both regressions are very similar and are represented in Table 1. All calculations were performed using R statistical software [67]. We uses package lme4 [68] for linear mixed model regression analysis and packages robustlmm [69] and lmertest [70] for robustness checks.

Since the model was estimated using a maximum likelihood estimator, we tested the fit of the model by comparing it to a baseline model that contained only the random effect of subjects as explanatory variable. AIC of the baseline model is 350.93, likelihood ratio 457.65, which is significant at the 0.1% level. Results of both regressions confirm Hypothesis 1: subjects under a matching condition exhibit a significantly lower rate of errors than in a mismatching condition. Hypotheses 2a and 2b are not confirmed, there is no significant effect of stage. As could also be expected, both control variables have a significant influence on the error rate.

## 6. Conclusion

The aim of the present paper is to contribute to the well established research stream on information representation in decision support in two ways: first, by emphasizing that information presentation needs not only to match task characteristics, but also the cognitive style of decision makers and by proposing the Cognitive Style Index of Allinson and Hayes [71] as an adequate instrument to measure cognitive style. Second, we take into account that a poor fit of information representation to decision maker (and task) characteristics not only impedes performance in the decision problem at hand, but also causes a depletion of cognitive resources. This has a negative impact on the solution of subsequent problems.

Results of the empirical study we have presented provide evi dence for both effects. They indicate that (for non-depleted decision makers) the cognitive style has a clear influence on whether a subject performs better with graphical or tabular information. Selecting the right presentation form therefore makes a difference, and which form is better depends among other factors on personal characteristics of the recipient of the information. This result has clear implications for the design of decision support systems. On the one hand, offering a variety of different presentation forms can be beneficial, since it allows to avoid mismatches between information presentation and cognitive style. On the other hand, the choice becomes more complicated, since presentation forms have to fit not only characteristics of the problem, but also of the user. Detecting the actual cognitive style

Selecting the best way of presenting information becomes even more important since our research has shed light on the “hidden costs” of inappropriate presentation forms. They not only lead to weaker performance in the current decision task, but also have a negative impact on subsequent tasks, even if information for these tasks is presented in a better way. These delayed effects of a mismatch were not considered in previous studies.

Although the present paper has focused on user characteristics, this does not mean that we consider task characteristics to be irrelevant. Our study was conducted in the context of a specific decision making task, namely rank lotteries. This focus on one specific decision problem is clearly a limitation, and future research needs to consider different types of problems. Another possible limitation of the present research is the use of a student sample. The use of student samples is widely discussed in empirical economics [72], management research [73] and information systems [74], and there are no clear conclusions about this topic. For our particular problem, we note that since our sample consists of business students in a late part of their studies, these subjects are in fact future managers. Furthermore, studies in decision making found that advanced students are quite similar to actual managers in terms of decision biases like framing [75] and overconfidence [76], or in decision behavior in settings like negotiations [77] and auctions [78].

Further limitations apply to the depletion effects that we have studied. Following the tradition of the literature on depletion, we measured depletion only indirectly via subsequent task performance. Literature does not offer direct measures of depletion that we could have used. Of course, this leaves room for alternative explanations. However, even if the underlying mechanisms is somewhat different from depletion, the main fact that increasing the cognitive load in one task by using an information representation that does not fit to the decision maker’s cognitive style can impede performance on subsequent decision tasks remains an important aspect of our study.

Future research could apply additional manipulations for addressing depletion more specifically. While we did not impose an explicit time limit, experimental instructions discouraged subjects from long deliberations on each question, and thus might have created an implicit time pressure contributing to stress and cognitive depletion. Various aspects of time that might be relevant in this context need to be studied in the future. Periods of rest can reverse the effect of depletion [60], so the interval between decision tasks likely has an impact on performance in subsequent the task and should be varied in future studies. It would also be an interesting question to study whether and to which extent depletion effects accumulate if a decision maker performs not just two, but a longer sequence of tasks, as actual users of decision support system in practice frequently do. All these open questions show that information representation for decision making is still a topic that requires more research effort, and by considering depletion effects, we hopefully have added another dimension to this field of research.

He has published three books and more than one hundred peer reviewed papers in journals and collective volumes. He received the Informs GDN section award in 2012, and in the same year was ranked #8 among the university professors in Business Administration in German speaking countries in terms of lifetime research output. His main research interests are group decisions and negotiations, in particular the analysis of negotiation process, multicriteria decision analysis and decision models under incomplete information
