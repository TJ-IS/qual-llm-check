irtual communities continue to play a greater role in social, political, and economic interactions. However, how users value information from these communities and how that affects their behavior and future expectations is not fully understood. Stock message boards provide an excellent setting to analyze these issues given the large user base and market uncertainty. Using data from 502 investor responses from a field experiment on one of the largest message board operators in South Korea, our analyses revealed that investors exhibit confirmation bias, whereby they preferentially treat messages that support their prior beliefs. This behavior is more pronounced for investors with higher perceived knowledge about the market and higher strength of belief (i.e., sentiment) toward a particular stock. We also find a negative interaction effect between the perceived knowledge and the strength of prior belief on confirmation bias. Those exhibiting confirmation bias are also more overconfident; as a result, they trade more actively and expect higher market returns than is warranted. Collectively, these results suggest that participation in virtual communities may not necessarily lead to superior financial returns.

Key words: confirmation bias; overconfidence; investment decisions; virtual communities; stock message boards History: Michael Smith, Senior Editor. ## 1. Introduction

To the extent that the easy accessibility of comprehensive and diagnostic information about stocks and shares is relevant for making sound investments in them, it would appear that the advent of message boards and virtual investments communities is a blessing for investors. This is particularly the case for individual (as opposed to institutional) investors, who otherwise would not have access to such information. The amount of information available to individual investors on message boards, virtual communities, blogs, and the like is arguably higher than it has ever been. Businesses like Seekingalpha.com and leading business magazines (e.g., Fortune), evaluate, synthesize, and report comments made on message boards or blogs. Numerous online services (e.g., PredictWallStreet.com, Marketwatch.com, socialpicks.com, Stockpkr.com) have started to aggregate stock sentiments from these message boards and make the information available to online brokerages (e.g., TDAmeritrade) and other financial infomediaries. In 2010, Yahoo! Finance including Stock Message Boards recorded 37.5 million unique visitors (comScore Media Metrix 2012).

The large number of users on message boards and the increasing use of community sentiments raise the question of how investors value information from message boards and how it shapes their behavior and expectations. In particular, it is important to understand whether these message boards are beneficial or detrimental to investors’ financial health. Our paper investigates how investors value information from message boards and how their valuation impacts their trading behavior and their expected returns from their investments.

Prior studies in accounting and finance suggest that virtual communities (VCs) often provide more accurate information than do analyst forecasts (Bagnoli et al. 1999, Clarkson et al. 2006). Early evidence of Enron’s collapse from accounting tricks was first reported on message boards (Felton and Kim 2002). Some studies show that the volume of posts or the level of investor sentiments in VCs has a positive relation with market activity (Antweiler and Frank 2004, Bagnoli et al. 1999, Das and Chen 2007, Tumarkin and Whitelaw 2001). These findings indicate that message boards attract insiders and informed investors, and thus information in VCs can be valuable to investors’ investment decisions and trading activities. However, research explicating how this information is utilized is still evolving.

In the present paper, we consider two perspectives—economic and psychological—to postulate how consumers utilize information from message boards to make investment decisions. The economic perspective suggests that investors are rational in valuing information (Birchler and Butler 2007) and will be unbiased in evaluating the informational content of message boards. Specifically, investors will value different points of view (i.e., sentiments) on the message boards and will be objective and impartial in integrating these sentiments with their own opinions and beliefs. In particular, investors with prior beliefs are expected to update these beliefs in a purely Bayesian fashion, with a single-minded focus on making those investment decisions that have the best potential for maximizing financial returns.

In contrast, the psychological perspective suggests that investors may not be as objective and unbiased in valuing information from message boards. In this view, information that conforms to investors’ prior beliefs will be valued higher, whereas information inconsistent with their prior beliefs will be devalued or even ignored. In short, investors are expected to exhibit what is widely referred to as confirmation bias (Kunda 1999, Oswald and Grosjean 2004, Snyder and Swann 1978, Trope and Bassok 1982; see Nickerson 1998 for review). As Nickerson (1998) notes, confirmation bias can be deliberate or unwitting; that is, it can take place consciously (with the awareness of the investor) or nonconsciously (outside of the awareness of the investor).Regardless of whether investors are aware of engaging in confirmation bias or not, those who do will value conforming information on message boards more positively. As a result, investors are expected to walk away from the message boards with their prior beliefs reinforced.

The reinforcement of prior beliefs, in turn, is expected to increase investors’ confidence in the soundness of their investment sentiments (buy, sell, or hold decisions), resulting in enhanced expectations about returns from their stock investments and, therefore, engagement in active trading behavior.

The present paper explores the extent to which investors are subject to confirmation bias in the context of exposure to information on message boards. We hypothesize that although investors are likely to be guided by the forces described in the economic perspective in valuing and integrating information from message boards, they will also be susceptible to the forces described in the psychological perspective discussed earlier. However, not all investors are expected to exhibit confirmation bias to the same extent. For reasons we discuss shortly, we predict that those who believe that they are more knowledgeable about the stock market, or those with stronger beliefs, will exhibit higher confirmation bias. Further, we predict that confirmation bias will be low when the strength of belief and the perceived knowledge are low.

To test our hypotheses, we conducted a field experiment using real-life investors who participated in stock discussion boards at one of the largest financial portal websites<sup>1</sup> in South Korea. Prior to viewing the messages in the stock discussion boards, investors were asked their investment sentiments about a specific set of stocks (details of which follow). Then we asked investors to evaluate a set of messages with varying degrees of conformity to their prior sentiments. We then measured investor preference among messages, their expected return of the stock, and their trading frequency. These measures were used in our analyses to test our hypotheses. Our analysis revealed that despite the opportunities for exposing themselves to diverse perspectives and thereby making more accurate and objectively superior decisions, individuals exhibit substantial confirmation bias. The confirmation bias was greater with individuals’ strength of belief and perceived knowledge and led to greater discrepancies between an individual’s forecast and the actual stock performance.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we develop the main hypotheses. In §3, we summarize our survey methods and data. We present our main empirical results in §4 followed by discussions and conclusion in §§5 and 6, respectively.

## 2. Hypotheses Development

## 2.1. Strength of Belief

Our main hypothesis—i.e., investors will more highly value information that conforms to their prior beliefs—is motivated by the well-established literature in psychology on confirmation bias. Confirmation bias has been shown to occur in one of two main ways: through (1) selective seeking of information that is consistent with one’s prior beliefs, expectations, and hypotheses (Kunda 1999, Oswald and Grosjean 2004, Snyder and Swann 1978, Trope and Bassok 1982, see Klayman and Ha 1987 and Nickerson 1998 for reviews), a process known as selective searching, or (2) biased interpretation of ambiguous information in a manner that enhances confidence in one’s prior beliefs, expectations, or hypotheses (Klapper 1960, Risen and Gilovich 2007, Zillmann and Bryant 1985; see Bryant and Davies 2006 for a review), a process known as biased interpretation. Both subprocesses are geared toward enhancing confidence in one’s prior beliefs, expectations, and hypotheses. The behavior described by the two processes differs markedly from Bayesian decision theory in economics, which suggests that individuals are equally open to processing any and all relevant information and are also unbiased in interpreting such information (Akerlof and Dickens 1982).

Two broad classes of theories have been used to explain why confirmation bias occurs: the need for self-enhancement (e.g., Tesser and Martin 1996) and the need for consistency (Russo et al. 1996, 1998; Swann 1990; Swann et al. 1987). According to the selfenhancement account, people are motivated to hold positive views of themselves and their futures and thus value confirmatory information more because it enhances their perceived sense of knowledge about relevant information (Taylor and Brown 1988). Economic modeling of cognitive dissonance makes a similar argument. Akerlof and Dickens (1982), for instance, suggest that decision makers tend to stick to their prior decision and discount information that calls this decision into question, presumably because disconfirmatory information undermines individuals’ positive self-image as “a smart person,” whereas confirmatory information affirms this self-view.

The consistency-based account provides an alternative explanation for why people exhibit confirmation bias; according to this account, people experience psychological discomfort when exposed to information that is inconsistent with their prior beliefs, expectations, or hypotheses (Festinger 1957). In this view, people will value confirmatory evidence regardless of whether their self-image is enhanced through such endeavors or not. For instance, one set of studies (by Swann et al. 1987, study 2) pitted people’s desire for self-enhancement against their desire for selfverification and found that people often sought out information that confirmed their prior views, even when such information was detrimental to maintaining a positive self-image.

In sum, confirmation bias involves a process of case building that relies on preferential treatment to information that supports one’s prior views. Researchers have noted that decision makers are often unaware of having engaged in confirmation bias (e.g., Ambady and Rosenthal 1993, Cash and Kilcullen 1985; see Gilovich 1991 for a review); such lack of awareness is an important reason why confirmation bias is so prevalent and has been documented across a wide variety of disciplines from psychology and business to politics and education.

The above discussion is relevant for the context of our research interest. Investors vary with regard to their sentiments toward current or past stock holdings; i.e., they differ in terms of whether they wish to buy, hold, or sell them. Further, investors are also likely to vary in terms of the intensity of these sentiments—from neutral to strong buy or strong sell. The intensity of their sentiments affects the degree of psychological discomfort when they encounter contradictory information. As a result, we expect those with stronger (versus weaker) sentiments to be more biased. In other words,

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> Investors with stronger prior beliefs about a stock’s future performance are more likely to show confirmation bias in valuing information from the virtual communities.

It is important to note that we choose to remain agnostic about the reason underlying confirmation bias (i.e., whether it is triggered by the selfenhancement motive or the consistency motive). Further, we choose to remain agnostic about whether the confirmation bias is deliberate or unwitting. Our focus is to document the existence of the phenomenon in the context of message boards.

## 2.2. Perceived Knowledge

Social psychologists have long recognized the discrepancy between one’s actual knowledge-levels and one’s self-assessment (i.e., perception) of knowledge levels (e.g., Brucks 1985, Epstein et al. 1984, Park et al. 1988; see Moore and Healy 2008 for a review). Actual and perceived knowledge are expected to coincide during the early stages of knowledge accumulation (Park et al. 1988). However, as individuals accumulate more knowledge from exposure to new information (through, for example, the Internet, search engines, news media), the phenomenon of illusion of knowledge unfolds, whereby individuals’ subjective belief of knowledge levels (i.e., the depth and breadth, relevance, and accuracy of knowledge) becomes incommensurately higher than their objective knowledge levels (Barber and Odean 2001, Konana and Balasubramanian 2005). This discrepancy between perceived and actual knowledge has been the focal point of studies on judgment and decision making (e.g., Brucks 1985, Mahajan 1992) and underlies the well-known phenomenon of overconfidence bias (Adams and Adams 1960, Alpert and Raiffa 1982, Mahajan 1992, Moore and Healy 2008).

Prior studies suggest that when perceived knowledge is low, individuals are less overconfident about their knowledge levels (Kruger 1999, Moore and Small 2007, Windschitl et al. 2003). In one study (Windschitl et al. 2003, study 1), for example, the average participant reported just a 6% chance that he or she would beat a fellow college student in a quiz on the topic of indigenous vegetation of the Amazon—a topic on which most participants considered their perceived knowledge level to be low. Lower confidence with lower perceived knowledge leads, unsurprisingly, to greater motivation to value, process, and use new information (Park et al. 1988, Raju et al. 1995).

These findings suggest that those higher in perceived knowledge may be more overconfident about their knowledge levels and hence less willing to value new and potentially diagnostic information. Further, when exposed to disconfirming information, those with higher perceived knowledge are more likely to discount the value of such information in favor of that which confirms their sentiments. In sum,

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> Investors with higher perceived knowledge are more likely to show confirmation bias in valuing information from the virtual communities.

## 2.3. Strength of Belief and Perceived Knowledge Interaction

Thus far, we have predicted that the tendency to exhibit confirmation bias will be greater among those with (1) strong strength of belief or (2) higher perceived knowledge. A question that naturally follows is: what is the interactive effect of strength of belief and perceived knowledge?

Before addressing this question, we first turn to the possibility that strength of belief and perceived knowledge may be correlated with one another and therefore not independent or orthogonal constructs. One reason why perceived knowledge may be correlated with strength of belief is that those who have strong sentiments—and have therefore committed themselves to a particular investment decision (buy/sell)—may feel motivated to report a higher level of perceived knowledge, in order to assure themselves of the validity of their decision. Alternatively, investors may gain strength of belief because they perceive themselves knowledgeable about the stock market. Consider, however, that investors may also become less confident as they gain knowledge because the knowledge may make them more aware of the risks associated with the stock in question. Further, strength of belief may be based on factors (e.g., a tip from a friend, a hunch, emotional attachment or aversion to a company, etc.) that are completely independent of knowledge. Thus, overall, it is unclear whether strength of belief is correlated with perceived knowledge. We thus address the issue empirically—by assessing the statistical correlation between the two—in the methods section.

Returning to the interactive effects of strength of belief and perceived knowledge, we expect that the more motivated an investor is to make an objective and accurate decision, the less he will exhibit confirmation bias. Specifically, as discussed earlier, we expect investors with stronger beliefs to be less capable of objectivity since they are more wedded to their investment decision. Likewise, we also argue that because investors with low perceived knowledge are more motivated to learn and make good decisions, they will be less prone to confirmation bias. As such, we may expect that investors will be least susceptible to confirmation bias when their strength of belief is weak and when their perceived knowledge is low. In contrast, when either strength of belief or perceived knowledge are high, we expect investors to be less objective in their investment decisions, less motivated to learn, and hence more susceptible to confirmation bias. In other words,

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> Confirmation bias will be low when perceived knowledge is low and strength of belief is weak; in all other conditions confirmation bias will be at higher levels.

We have so far discussed (1) how exposure to information on message boards fosters the tendency for confirmation bias and (2) how the strength of prior belief and the level of perceived knowledge moderate this tendency. We now turn to a downstream effect of confirmation bias, its impact on the gaps between actual stock returns and investor forecasts and investor trading behavior.

## 2.4. Confirmation Bias, Trading Frequency, and Discrepancy Between Actual and Expected Returns

Those who engage in confirmation bias—that is, those who selectively search for confirmatory evidence or selectively value ambiguous information in a confirmatory manner—are naturally more likely to have greater faith in the accuracy of their beliefs once they have sought confirmatory information. Further, we expect confirmation bias to lead to greater perceived control (i.e., illusion) over random events (see Langer 1975). Prior research in finance supports these conjectures: investors who exhibit confirmation bias overestimate the precision of their beliefs and underestimate the role of random events in influencing stock prices (Ben-David et al. 2010, Glaser and

Weber 2007, Lichtenstein et al. 1977). The biased information valuation process and the enhanced confidence in the accuracy of one’s beliefs—both fostered by confirmation bias—are likely to make investors overconfident in their investment (buy, sell, hold) sentiments.

This greater overconfidence in investment sentiments is expected to have a twofold effect. First, we expect investors with greater overconfidence in their investment sentiments to underperform; specifically, we expect to find a greater discrepancy between the actual and the expected earnings among investors who exhibit confirmation bias—and are therefore overconfident—to a greater extent. Past findings on counteractive optimism are consistent with this expectation. Zhang and Fishbach (2007, studies 1 and 2), for example, found that, the more confident—and therefore, optimistic—one’s predictions are about the time needed to complete a project, the greater the discrepancy between actual and expected completion time. Conceptually replicating the Zhang and Fishbach (2007) findings, Shartot et al. (2007) find that confidence and optimism promote underestimation of risk and overestimation of positive events, leading once again to a bigger discrepancy between actual and expected performance. Kahneman and Reipe (1998) highlight the deleterious effect of overconfidence and optimism in the context of financial performance; they suggest that optimists often underestimate the likelihood of negative outcomes, leading to unwarranted higher expectations.

Second, the greater overconfidence fostered by confirmation bias is likely to lead to higher trading frequency. As past work by Glaser and Weber (2007) has shown, overconfident investors overestimate the precision of information and underestimate the volatility of random events in financial markets, and such miscalibration has been shown to lead to excessive trading (Varian 1989). There is another, related route by which confirmation bias leads to greater trading frequency. Those who exhibit confirmation bias can perceive themselves as more competent than those who don’t, and recent findings suggest that perceived competence is positively related to investor trading frequency (Graham et al. 2009). This is because investors who feel more competent in their investment-related skills tend to trade based on their subjective beliefs than do those who feel less competent. As such, investors with greater perceived competence are likely to engage in such behaviors as trying to time the market, which increases trading frequency.

Since investors with access to VCs can more easily find the confirming opinions that reinforce their prior beliefs, we expect that they will feel more overconfident and competent, leading in turn to both a higher trading frequency and a higher discrepancy between expected and actual performance.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4).</sup> The gap between the actual and the forecasted returns will be greater for those who more strongly exhibit confirmation bias.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5</sup> <sup>(H5).</sup> Higher confirmation bias exhibited by investors will lead to greater trading frequency.

To summarize, the gap between investors’ forecast and their actual performance and their trading frequency are influenced by confirmation bias, and the severity of confirmation bias is influenced by the strength of an investor’s beliefs and his perceived knowledge about a stock. We further conjecture a negative interaction effect between the strength of beliefs and the perceived knowledge on confirmation bias. ## 3. Data and Summary Statistics

## 3.1. Design of the Field Experiment

Our data collection involved a combination of survey instruments and a controlled field experiment to observe the information valuation of real investors participating in message boards. The participants came from stock message boards operated by one of the largest online portals in South Korea. The portal provides a wide range of services, including a financial news service, stock quotes, stock exchange rates, corporate press releases, reports, and recommendations. The company reports that its 1,992 stock message boards attract about 100,000 unique visitors per day.

With the help of the company, we posted a link to our research experiment at the top of each stock message board between October 7, 2009, and October 21, 2009.<sup>2</sup> All users of the portal were invited to click the link during the study period to complete the survey and participate in the experiment. To prevent responder fatigue, each investor is allowed to participate in the experiment only once. To facilitate participation and completion, we offered gifts (e.g., iPod Touch, iPod Nano, iPod Shuffle) of monetary values between \$100 and \$300, which were raffled among the participants who completed our field experiment. In total, 20 participants were given a reward. We also assured participants that the results would be reported only in aggregate and that their anonymity would be preserved.

The survey instrument was pretested with 24 employees of the company who were also actual retail investors. They were asked to comment on the questions, to raise concerns related to the questionnaire, and to describe any ambiguities. To address the concerns of content validity, the questionnaire was also pretested with three academic researchers familiar with virtual investment-related communities. The survey questions were then modified according to the comments from researchers and company employees.

At the beginning of the experiment, participants visiting a stock message board were asked to participate in an experiment and to complete an online questionnaire before being able to view messages. The questionnaire included investors’ demographic information, information search behavior, investment patterns, and perceived knowledge about investment activity (see Appendix A for the survey questions related to this study.) Participants were asked to answer questions specific to the stock related to the stock message board. For instance, if they participated in the experiment on the Samsung Electronics message board, the questions were related only to Samsung Electronics. The participants were then asked to report their current sentiment about the Samsung stock (e.g., strong sell, sell, hold, buy, and strong buy); their current investment amount in the stock; and their forecast of the stock returns (including dividend payout) one month following the date of participation in the field experiment.

The design of our field experiment is cross-sectional in nature because it requires substantial programming support from the company.<sup>3</sup> It is important to note that market movement does not affect our analysis. This is because our research model examines the relative gap between an investor’s forecast and actual stock performance and how this gap is related to the investor’s confirmation bias. Any change in market return will increase or decrease the gap for all investors, thus not affecting our analysis based on relative performance across investors. It is also useful to note that investors’ forecast for one month is likely to be noisy. However, we do not expect the forecasts to be systematically biased because of the noise. In such cases, identification of any effects despite the noise indicates the strength of the confirmation bias and its impact on investors, which is the objective of this paper.

The key issue in this experiment is to evaluate investors’ confirmation bias in how they value information from message boards. To assess this confirmation bias, we created a controlled environment where five messages selected by researchers were added to the top of each stock message board (unknown to the investors). These messages were randomly drawn from the database of stock messages for each stock and screened by one of the researchers and three company employees to ensure information distinctiveness and sentiment clarity. To ensure that the message we provided was orthogonal to the information investors used to form their original sentiments, we chose the five messages from new postings. The investors’ original decisions (to buy, sell, or hold) would likely be based on a variety of sources of information, including word-of-mouth and television, and not limited to messages posted on the message boards. Our past research suggests that investor sentiment persists over a long period of time, and it is unlikely that the messages presented to the investors in this experiment would have resulted in their prior beliefs. We further remove these messages from the public stock discussion boards so that they are only visible to subjects of our experiment. Among the five messages, two reflected positive opinion, two reflected negative opinion, and one neutral. These five new messages were presented in random order at the top of the stock message boards and marked as newly posted messages. Since we used actual stock message boards for our field experiment, we controlled for each message for the number of replies, sentiments, rating values, posting date, etc. A screenshot of the experimental setup is provided in Figure 2.

After showing the five messages, we asked participants to answer the following questions: (1) which opinion appears to have the most widespread support, (2) which opinion appears to be most strongly backed by news about the stock, and (3) which opinion appears to have the most convincing argument? Then participants were asked to click on one and only one of the top five messages for each of the above questions. The questions were asked in sequence. In addition, we did not allow investors to click other messages on the message board except for the five new messages. Therefore, we prevented the risk that investors may have been exposed to other messages on the message board while answering our questions.

## 3.3. Main Variables

To test our hypotheses, we obtain measures of investors’ strength of belief, perceived knowledge, and degree of confirmation bias on a given stock:

• Strength of Belief: This variable takes a value of 3 if an investor has a “strong sell” or “strong buy” opinion about a stock, 2 if he has a “sell” or “buy” opinion, and 1 if he has a “hold” opinion.

• Perceived Knowledge: An investor’s perceived knowledge is measured based on his/her responses to three items in the questionnaire: (1) I am well informed about the stock market; (2) I am familiar with the stocks I trade (e.g., their business, financial status); and (3) I am well informed about the major economic news that impacts the stock market. A seven-point Likert scale is used to collect these responses. To ensure the reliability and convergent validity of this scale, we use the Cronbach alpha measure and average variance extracted (AVE), respectively. We find that the scale has a Cronbach alpha of 0.81 for the sample, whereas the AVE indicates that 72.79% of the variance can be explained by items. Both Cronbach and AVE values meet the recommended threshold values.

• Confirmation Bias: Confirmation bias is measured based on how investors value information, which is reflected in the number of times an investor clicks on a message that supports their sentiment when responding to the three questions on confirmation bias. For each response, we assign a value of 1 if an investor clicks on positive (negative) messages when he holds a “strong buy” or “buy” (a “strong sell” or “sell” opinion), −1 if an investor clicks on negative (positive) messages when he holds a “strong buy” or “buy” (a “strong sell” or “sell” opinion), and 0 otherwise. Confirmation bias is measured as the sum of the values over the three responses. The maximum value of confirmation bias is 3 and the minimum value is −3. • Return Gap: Return gap is measured as the difference between the actual stock return and the forecasted stock return by an investor when he has a positive forecast and the difference between the forecasted stock return and the actual stock return when he has a negative forecast. Our operationalization captures the degree to which an investor falls short of his forecast in the direction of the forecast. The actual return (or realized stock return) is measured for the one month period following the participation date using the following equation: $R _ { i , t + 3 0 } =$ $( \bar { P } _ { i , t + 3 0 } - P _ { i , t + d } ) / \bar { P _ { i , t } } ,$ where $R _ { i , t } + 3 0$ represents the actual return of the stock from day t to $t + 3 0 . \ P _ { i , t + 3 0 }$ is the stock price at the end of the one-month period, $P _ { i , t }$ is the stock price on the date of participation, and d is any dividend amount during the one-month period. The prices of all stocks are obtained from the company stock price database. The forecast of stock return $\hat { R } _ { i , t + 3 0 }$ for the one month period following the participation date is reported by the investor in the questionnaire.<sup>4</sup>

• Trading Frequency: Trading frequency of each investor is measured based on his/her responses to one item in the questionnaire. Participants were asked to report their average weekly trading frequency. This self-reported measure is used to capture an investor’s trading behavior.

Our set of control variables includes time spent on stock message boards per day, investment experience, and amount invested in a stock. Each individual’s time spent on stock message boards is self-reported and coded from 1 to 5, where 1 represents spending less than 1 hour per day, and 5 represents spending more than 10 hours per day. Investors’ online investing experience is measured as 1 if an investor’s experience is less than one year, 2 if the experience is between one and two years, 3 if the experience is between three and six years, and four if the experience is greater than 6 years. Investors were also asked to report their average amount invested in each stock (in Korean won). We use the standardized value (i.e., the mean is set to zero and the standard deviation is one) of this variable in our empirical analysis.

## 4. Empirical Results

In this section, we summarize our empirical framework and report the results from our main analysis.

## 4.1. Estimation Framework

We estimate two systems of equations to test our key hypotheses. The first system of equation is the return gap system of equation, which contains two equations. The first equation assesses the relationship between investors’ confirmation biases and their strength of belief and perceived knowledge. The second equation assesses the relationship between investors’ degree of confirmation bias and the return gap. The first system of equations is specified as follows:

Return Gap System of Equations:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {ConfirmationBias} _ {i, t} \\ = \alpha_ {1} \text {StrengthOfBelief} _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {2} \text {PerceivedKnowledge} _ {i, t} \\ \quad + \alpha_ {3} \text {PerceivedKnowledge} _ {i, t} * \text {StrengthOfBelief} _ {i, t} \\ \quad + \alpha_ {4} \text {TimeOnBoard} _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {5} \text {Age} _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {6} \text {Sex} _ {i, t} \\ \quad + \alpha_ {7} \text {Education} _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {8} \text {Income} _ {i, t} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}; \\ \text {ReturnGap} _ {i, t + 3 0} \\ = \beta_ {1} \text {ConfirmationBias} _ {i, t} + \beta_ {2} \text {TimeOnBoard} _ {i, t} \\ \quad + \beta_ {3} \text {Age} _ {i, t} + \beta_ {4} \text {Sex} _ {i, t} + \beta_ {5} \text {Education} _ {i, t} \\ \quad + \beta_ {6} \text {Income} _ {i, t} + \delta_ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{1a}
$$

(1b)

In this set of equations, $i = 1 , 2 , \dots , N$ are the investors who participated in our experiment, t denotes each investor’s participation date, and $t + 3 0$ represents the period one month following the participation date. In Equation (1a), $C o n f i r m a t i o n B i a s _ { i , t }$ captures the confirmation bias of investor i at time $t , S t r e n g t h O f B e l i e f _ { i , t }$ denotes the strength of investor $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ sentiment on the stock at time t and $P e r c e i v e d K n o w l e d g e _ { i , \ l }$ identifies the level of knowledge investor i believes that he possesses about the stock at time t. We also include an interaction term between $S t r e n g t h O f B e l i e f _ { i , . }$ t and $P e r c e i v e d K n o w l e d g e _ { i , t }$ to estimate the interaction effect between these two variables on confirmation biases. We further include five control variables that may influence investors’ confirmation biases, including time spent on board per week $( T i m e O n B o a r d _ { i , t } )$ , investor’s age $( A g e _ { i , t } )$ , gender $( S e x _ { i , t } ) .$ , level of education $( E d u c a t i o n _ { i , t } )$ , and income $( I n c o m e _ { i , t } )$

In Equation (1b), $R e t u r n G a p _ { i , t + 3 0 }$ is the stock return gap of investor i for the 30-day period after participation.

Next, we investigate whether confirmation bias affects trade frequency. Hypothesis 5 suggests that investors with confirmation bias are more likely to be overconfident, which leads to excessive trading. To test the relation between confirmation bias and trade frequency, we estimate the following system of equations:

Trading Frequency System of Equations:

$$

Our field experiment approach allows us to better address possible endogeneity issues that often arise in cross-sectional analysis. First, we measure return gaps one month after our assessment of investors’ confirmation bias. The time precedence allows us to rule out the reverse causal effect that return gaps lead to confirmation bias. Second, we survey the investors before presenting them with messages to assess their degree of confirmation bias. This experiment design helps to rule out the possibility that investors form their opinion and belief after viewing the messages.

We estimate the systems of equations using path analysis in Amos $1 \dot { 8 } . 0 . 5$ The path estimates in our models have been obtained using maximum likelihood estimation. A path coefficient is a standardized regression coefficient (or beta coefficient) that shows the direct effect of an independent variable on a dependent variable. The most important reason for using a path analysis model instead of a traditional econometric model is that researchers can easily compare overall model fit to several alternative models because path analysis provides more statistics about overall model fit than other statistics do.

Table 3 reports the estimation results of the return gap system of equations. The model fit statistics in Table 3 indicate that our model is well specified. We first consider the relationship between confirmation bias and investors’ strength of beliefs and perceived knowledge. The estimates reported in column (3) indicate that the coefficient estimates of our key independent variables are consistent with the predictions of our hypotheses. Specifically, we find that the impact of the strength of beliefs on confirmation bias is positive at 0.01 significance level (coefficient estimate = 00214). When investors hold strong beliefs, they are more likely to be biased in their information valuation to confirm their beliefs. This result is consistent with H1, which posits that investors with stronger beliefs exhibit stronger biases in valuing information from VCs. The preferential treatment to confirming information demonstrated by the investors could be the result of either deliberate decisions or unwitting behavior by investors. However, regardless of its exact cause, our analysis demonstrates the presence of confirmation bias and its significant relationship with the strength of beliefs.

On the other hand, for investors with a higher level of belief strength, or a higher level perceived knowledge, the level of confirmation bias is positive and significant.

Finally, we examine the relationship between confirmation bias and both return gap and trading frequency. Our analysis reveals that the impact of confirmation bias on return gap is negative at the 1% significance level (coefficient = −00147). This result is consistent with H4, suggesting that confirmation bias increases the gap between an investor’s actual returns and his expected returns. Investors driven by confirmation bias become overconfident and overly optimistic about their prior beliefs, leading to higher expectation of returns than warranted.<sup>6</sup> Our analysis also reveals that investors who demonstrate confirmation bias often trade excessively. Columns (1) and (2) of Table 4 show that the impact of confirmation bias on trading frequency is positive at the 5% significance level (coefficient estimate = 00159). This evidence supports hypothesis H5.

Overall, our analysis suggests that investors show stronger confirmation bias when they hold stronger opinions (e.g., strong sell or strong buy) and when they perceive they have a higher level of knowledge.

Further, investors with strong confirmation bias find their actual returns significantly trail their expected returns, and they trade more frequently than an average investor. These results are consistent with the view that online investors are overconfident because of the illusion of knowledge and selective information processing. Those investors easily foster strong beliefs about their investment performance, which is detached from the reality of their performance.

Since our experiment was conducted when the market was bearish, a question naturally arises whether our main results can be replicated when the stock market is bullish. Because our dependent variable is the difference between the actual stock return and the forecasted stock return by an investor (when he has a positive forecast, as most investors do), our results will be replicable irrespective of the market condition. Consider a simplified case where all investors are investing in one stock. Any change to the actual return of the stock will only affect the intercept of the model (since it will change the dependent variable by the same amount for all observations) but will not affect any of the coefficients. This indicates that our result is not driven by the particular stock market conditions. As a robustness check, we re-estimate our model after removing the impact of market return (calculated as beta ∗ market return during the onemonth period) from the return gap. The Column (4) of Table 3 shows that the impact of confirmation bias on return gap (coefficient = −00154) is quantitatively the same as the one in our original model. This indicates that our results are not driven by market conditions (bullish or bearish).

## 5. Discussions

We ask two major questions in this paper: (a) whether investors participating in message boards value information preferentially based on their prior beliefs and perceived knowledge or are unbiased and objective in their assessment of information reported on the board and (b) how potential confirmation bias in information valuation influences investor expectations of future stock returns and their trading behavior. Using a combination of survey and field experiment on one of the largest message board operators for investors in South Korea, we show support for preferential treatment of messages that align with investors’ prior beliefs and perceived knowledge. This suggests that investors are subject to substantial confirmation bias. We also find that confirmation bias enhances overconfidence and optimism, resulting in excessive trading and higher expectation of returns than warranted.

These results raise important questions about the usefulness of these communities in helping investors make rational decisions. Although Bayesian decision theory predicts that individuals benefit from more information, which allows them to better estimate the states of the world, our analysis suggests that individuals, especially those with strong prior beliefs and/or a high level of perceived knowledge, are systematically biased in their evaluation of information. This biased process leads to wider gaps between their estimation of the states of the world and reality. This is in stark contrast to the extant literature that suggests VCs could be beneficial to users because they provide more information with much lower costs (Antweiler and Frank 2004, Tumarkin and Whitelaw 2001). However, these results are consistent with the psychology and behavioral finance literatures that have long argued that decision makers are often influenced by multiple psychological biases that distort their decision making and economic outcomes (Barber and Odean 2001, 2002; Kahneman and Riepe 1998; Raghunathan and Corfman 2006). Our findings suggest that the increasing trend to use market sentiments (e.g., PredictWallStreet.com, Motleyfool.com) from these communities to inform investors may not necessarily lead to better investment decisions within the communities.

There are a number of implications of confirmation bias. Our analysis reveals that confirmation bias in information valuation could be an important determinant of investor overconfidence. The literature suggests that overconfidence underestimates the volatility of random events in financial markets (Ben-David et al. 2010, Glaser and Weber 2007). Such miscalibration by investors is known to increase differences of opinion among investors, which, in turn, leads to more frequent trading (Varian 1989). Overconfidence is reflected in the illusion of control of random events (e.g., people believe that they can influence the outcome of chance events); the illusion of knowledge (e.g., when people have far more data, they believe that they are more knowledgeable than they really are); and self-attribution bias (e.g., people tend to attribute their success to their own abilities while attributing their failure to bad luck; Barber and Odean 2001). These illusions can be detrimental to investors because they encourage investors to trade more often (e.g., timing the market) even though doing so may lead to worse performance. Although individual investors are motivated to trade when they expect to earn high stock returns, overconfident investors experience poor overall performance because they trade frequently and speculatively and have greater trading costs (Barber and Odean 2000). Indeed, our analysis reveals a significant positive correlation between investor confirmation bias and their trading frequency. More than 67% of investors with confirmation bias in the top quartile trade more than once a week, while only 40% of investors in the bottom quartile trade more than once a week.

This study moves beyond existing information science (IS) research that has explored the motivations to participate in VCs (Bagozzi and Dholakia 2002, Butler 2001). These studies suggest that social and economic benefits are major motivations to participate. Individuals perceive both extrinsic (e.g., reciprocity, reputation) and intrinsic (e.g., self-efficacy, enjoyment of helping others) benefits from participation (Bock et al. 2005, Kankanhalli et al. 2005, Ma and Agarwal 2007, Roberts et al. 2006, Wasko and Faraj 2005). Although these motivations are relevant, this study adds to how information is valued and how it impacts individuals’ expectations and decision process. Beyond the behavioral finance literature, our results could provide guidance to online investors and financial infomediaries. Message board operators can help investors by presenting both confirming and disconfirming opinions and warning participants that the preferential information valuation may yield undesirable results.

The findings of this study can be generalized to other contexts where decision makers with prior beliefs access and value new information before making decisions. For example, when consumers with strong preferences about a particular brand visit online word-of-mouth networks (e.g., consumer review websites), they are more likely to accept opinions that are consistent with their prior beliefs. Thus, they may make purchase decisions based on biased beliefs and overlook or underweight different opinions that are related to their purchase decisions. Also, project managers within organizations may participate in internal knowledge management networks or project management networks where their team members discuss a project’s directions or decisions. However, if project managers have a strong belief or perceived knowledge toward a particular way of conducting the project, they may be disposed to value opinions that are aligned with their own opinions. Such behavior may lead to a project’s failure. As our model suggests, individuals with low perceived knowledge and beliefs are likely to use message boards to learn about products and services, and hence they serve a useful purpose. There is motivation to learn and to be informed to make the right choices. For instance, a user with little knowledge about a service (for example hotel quality in a tourist destination) may greatly rely on message board information to make decision. Our model, thus, provides a foundation that can be extended to other contexts.

Our results can also be expanded to the broader issue of how people self-select what they want to hear. It is likely investors self-select to message boards that align with prior beliefs. Sunstein (2002) suggests that technology has greatly increased people’s ability to filter what they want to read, see, and hear as discussed in the context of homophily and community fragmentation (McPherson et al. 2001, Van Alstyne and Brynjolfsson 2005). Homophily is defined as an individual’s tendency to associate with others who have similar backgrounds or beliefs (McPherson et al. 2001). Brown et al. (2007) suggest that users’ identity information may help to form a homophily of interests within VCs. They also argue that the virtual community itself may create homophily. Since VCs are formed based on a topic, activity, hobby, or ideology, the design encourages community members to form social interactions with people who share similar interests (Best and Krueger 2006, Brown et al. 2007). We extend this stream of research by showing that confirmation bias is a significant driver for homophilous behavior, influencing how individuals process and value information. New information or arguments that align with their prior beliefs are more readily accepted, and those that do not are discounted.

An understanding of individual information valuation behavior is also important for business practice. An increasing number of businesses leverage VCs and online social networks to facilitate networking and information sharing among their employees and customers (Nair et al. 2010). They need to be aware that individuals often unintentionally fall for psychological biases in information acquisition, sharing, and valuation (Kahneman and Riepe 1998). It is thus important for businesses to develop tools to help individuals avoid pitfalls and to improve the quality of information sharing and consumption.

## 6. Conclusions and Limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study in the literature to explore the impact of message boards on real-world investors as they are contemplating their investment decisions. In contrast, prior studies have either examined the impact of message boards on non-real-world investors (e.g., students participating in an experiment; Chiou and Cheng 2003) or have examined the impact of message boards on overall market performance without assessing their impact on individual investors (Antweiler and Frank 2004). We believe that our paper makes a significant contribution to our understanding of the impact of message boards on investment decisions—a topic we address in the general discussion above.

The study results provide greater evidence of the role of confirmation bias in investment activity or in general decision making in numerous contexts like project management. The results contribute not only to the IS field but also to finance and marketing literatures. The finance literature, in particular, has long debated the role of confirmation bias in information valuation, but this study explicitly evaluates those issues in the new age of VCs and social networks. As such, this paper provides greater guidance to the broader role of VCs.

Our study has several limitations and opportunities for further research. First, we do not observe the antecedents of investor beliefs. Such beliefs are potentially influenced by environmental factors such as stock market movements, firms’ announcements, news reports and other information sources. Future studies may develop a better understanding about how individuals form their beliefs on individual stocks. Second, prior studies on confirmation bias reveal that confirmation bias not only influences individuals’ interpretation and evaluation of information but also their information search process. Future studies can develop a better understanding of the influ ence of confirmation bias on information search and acquisition process. Third, our study is cross-sectional by design. We do not observe how investors’ confirmation bias changes over time and to what degree they learn from past mistakes. Obtaining longitudinal data on individual behavior in VCs and their investment performance will allow us to develop a more comprehensive understanding about the dynamic aspect of confirmation bias in VCs. Fourth, to prevent responder fatigue, we limit each responder to questions about only one stock. In reality, investors often have views and positions in multiple stocks at the same time. Future research could leverage this heterogeneity across multiple stocks of a given investor for better identification.<sup>7</sup> Finally, we consider a relatively simple model of how perceived knowledge and strength of belief influence investors’ confirmation bias and how such bias influences their forecast of stock performance. The underlying process that drives confirmation bias and its impact on investors’ belief is likely to be more nuanced. Future research to identify the underlying process will help expand our understanding of the behavior and belief of online investors.
