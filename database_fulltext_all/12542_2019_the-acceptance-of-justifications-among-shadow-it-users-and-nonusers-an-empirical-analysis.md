# THE ACCEPTANCE OF JUSTIFICATIONS AMONG SHADOW IT USERS AND NONUSERS – AN EMPIRICAL ANALYSIS

## ABSTRACT

Shadow information technology (IT) usage and justifications, which make shadow IT usage socially acceptable, are major challenges for managers. This study contributes to a better understanding of both the concepts. The results show significant differences between shadow IT users and nonusers in the degree to which they accept justifications in light of IT constraints, in particular, in the situational factors driving this acceptance. While perceived noncompliance of others influences shadow IT users’ acceptance of justifications, nonusers accept justifications of noncompliant fellows if they expect benefits of shadow IT usage.

New implications for managers are provided with the aim of reducing shadow IT usage.

Keywords: Justifications; shadow IT; neutralization; IT policy compliance; noncompliance

## INTRODUCTION

Shadow information technology (IT), or IT which users introduce into an organization in noncompliance with the organizational IT policies and then deploy to perform tasks [1,2], is becoming more pervasive in the IT landscape of organizations [3,4]. Increasingly, mobile- and cloud-based infrastructures enable users to take advantage of the flexible and convenient value propositions new IT services such as Dropbox, WhatsApp, or Google Docs propose, not only in private but also in work-life. These advantages ease and foster their collaboration with co-workers, customers, and partners, and thus, improve job performance [5,6]; however, when used in noncompliance with the formal IT polices (i.e., without approval and sometimes even without the knowledge of the IT management), these third-party IT services can threaten organizations’ information security or the fulfillment of regulatory requirements [7–9].

The behavioral consequences of utilizing shadow IT are thus ambiguous, something which research has demonstrated makes users engage in justifications of their noncompliant behaviors [10]. Justifications describe the statements of a social actor that claim the positive value of a behavior when it is called into question [11]. For example, users may defend their use of shadow IT by pleading necessity, the denial of any harm, or the injustice of organizational IT polices [1]. Despite the susceptibility of shadow IT usage to justifications, a recent study by Silic et al. [1] found no support for the most common types of justification invoked by users justifying IT-related noncompliance [e.g., 12–16].

However, one reason for those findings could be that this first study by Silic et al. [1] does not explicitly elicit shadow IT users’ justifications. Previous studies on justifications used in information systems (IS) research and criminology emphasize the need for a concrete situational stimulus that provokes users to deploy justifications of various types [14,15,17]. Thus, our current state of knowledge entails the risk that in practice IT managers will underestimate the importance of the acceptance of justifications for shadow IT users and infer measures that do not reduce but rather provoke shadow IT.

In our study, we, therefore, focus on the immediate concrete event of an IT constraint interfering with users’ task performance as a stimulator of shadow IT users’ acceptance of justifications. IT constraints are frequently observed in situations in which users employ shadow IT [5,18,19] and shadow IT users list them as one of the key drivers for their behaviors [3,5]. With this focus, we differentiate our study from prior work in IS research that exclusively considers the processed situational stimuli of organizational injustice, negative affect, and role conflicts [10,15,16,20].

Moreover, we look at the mechanisms underlying these justifying patterns of belief by exploring further key motivational factors that form shadow IT users’ justifications in light of IT constraints. As the first study in IS research that explores positive and social motivators as a driver for IT users’ justifications [14], we contribute to IS literature by providing an understanding of users’ acceptance

IT managers in organizations can make use of our new insights by deriving measures that are more targeted and effective in reducing IT users’ justifications and usage of shadow IT. Given that there is an increasing pervasiveness of utilizing shadow IT in organizations across all sizes and industries (with some estimates finding that shadow systems account for more than 80% of IT systems deployed by end users [21]), research can inform practice by suggesting well-grounded management actions. According to our results, IT managers could reduce shadow IT usage in light of IT constraints if they actively and consistently emphasize the normative climate of nonacceptance of shadow IT use and its justification among staff. Alternatively, they should incentivize users’ constant compliance with organizational IT policies and enhance initiatives to improve the illustration of advantages of compliance for both users and the organization.

Based upon our line of reasoning, our research poses the question:

What situational factors drive shadow IT users’ acceptance of justifications in light of IT

constraints and how do these factors differ from those of shadow IT nonusers?

The section below describes the literature on justifications in the fields of IS in general and shadow IT in particular. Following this discussion, we develop our hypotheses based on three well-established theories from social psychology. Next, we present our research methodology by describing our experimental research design, data analysis, and results. Finally, we discuss our contribution, their limits, as well as implications for research and practice.

## THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

To evaluate the acceptance and underlying motives of justifications regarding shadow IT usage, our research draws on the body of theoretical work on justifications in criminology and IS.

## The Acceptance of Justifications in IS

Based on Scott and Lyman’s [11] framework of accounts, justifications describe socially approved statements of a social actor that claim the positive value of a behavior subjected to negatively affected inquiry. This definition of justifications as an account implies that the individual accepts responsibility for the act in question but denies the derogatory quality associated with it [11].

Researchers across several disciplines have identified dozens of types of justification people invoke to justify acts that social norms call into question [22]. In the IS literature, the majority of justification types used reflect Sykes and Matza’s [23] original and extended “techniques of neutralization” from criminology [14]. Perpetrators use these “techniques of neutralization” to convince themselves and others that their criminal actions are justified [23]. Prominent examples are (1) “denial of injury,” which is used by perpetrators to minimize the extent of norm violation by stating that no one was actually hurt by the delinquent act (ibid), (2) “condemn the condemners,” in which the perpetrator scrutinizes the broken norm to redirect the attention and guilt to the people responsible for establishing rules (ibid), or (3) “defense of necessity” enabling the perpetrator (e.g., white-collar criminals) to justify rule breaking due to inevitable situational requirements [24]. IS scholars have transferred those types of justification to investigate four broad categories of users’ noncompliant behaviors: (1) the justification of illegal copying of digital media or software [e.g., 13,25,26], (2) the use of corporate Internet for private purposes, known as cyberloafing [e.g., 16,27], (3) the judgments and intentions of users concerning computer abuse [28,29], and (4) the violation of information security policies [e.g., 10,12,30].

Previous IS research has found that users who engage in illegal or illegitimate activities state one or more types of justification to account for them. The specific type of justification employed depends on the behavior as well as the situation under consideration [22,30–32]. Utilizing this approach, IS scholars have either counted how often various types of justification are used [25,33,34] or measured subjects’ degree of support for the acceptance of each type [e.g., 12,15].

Outside of the IS field, when studying the justifications used by sex offenders, Ward [35] argues that these approaches provide “little more than a list of common cognitive distortions used by offenders” (p. 492) but do not look at the source underlying these patterns of belief. As a result, both criminological and IS research have scarcely attempted to develop a theoretical account of the mechanisms underlying these deviance-justifying attitudes. However, without knowledge of the factors and mechanisms that drive IT users’ acceptance of justifications, we cannot derive effective management practices to reduce those justifications [1,22]. Various theoretical angles discussing processes of justifications, such as narrative identity [36,37] or Festinger’s [38] cognitive dissonance theory, point to potential factors referring to individuals’ goals, feelings, and social environment as being of great importance for persons’ acceptance of justifications [22].

Also of particular importance is the presence of an immediate situational stimulus, i.e., an external event or internal perception of such an event that can provoke deviant behaviors and their justifications when contemplating those behaviors [14,16,17,29].

In our review of the literature, we identified four existing IS studies that have taken a processed situational stimulus into account. Lim [16] and Willison et al. [29] investigate how the perceptions of organizational injustice in the work environment stimulate employees justifying their respective cyberloafing and computer abuse intentions. Two further IS security studies focus on the degree to which a negatively affected working situation stimulates employees’ acceptance of justifications. Teh et al. [20] found that employees’ disgruntlement manifested in their low commitment to the organization as well as role conflicts arising from contradictory expectations of relevant stakeholders and are significant motivating factors in the acceptance of justifications. D’Arcy et al. [10] evaluated how security-related stress including overload, complexity, and uncertainty led employees to apply moral disengagement as a mechanism to justify information security policy (ISP) violations [10,39].

In contrast to the existing approaches focusing on the processed triggers within individuals’ organizational environments, we apply the direct event of an IT constraint or the perception of it as a direct stimulus for users’ justifications of their deviant behaviors. Although prior research points to the frequent occurrence of IT constraints in real-world situations in which we observe ITrelated deviant behaviors in general [40,41] and, in particular, the use of shadow IT [5,18,19], no study so far has analyzed their role in stimulating the acceptance of justifications of those deviant IT behaviors.

## Justifying Shadow IT Use

In this study, we focus upon the deviant behavior of an individual’s usage of shadow IT systems. This phenomenon describes users voluntary noncompliance with the IT policies by using unapproved IT systems to perform an IT-based task [1,2]. In this situation, IT policies describe rules that prescribe or proscribe, which IT systems individuals should use or may not use to perform an IT-related task [42]. Although discussion of shadow IT use is increasingly found in toptier IS journals [e.g., 8], the literature on shadow IT is still in its infancy [43,44]. Prior publications have focused upon the causes [44–47], consequences [5,48], or IT governance approaches [43,49] at the corporate or departmental level of analysis while neglecting understanding of the drivers and consequences of individuals’ shadow IT usage behavior [1,2]. Prior to our study, there has only been one work that analyzed the impact of justifications on shadow IT usage [1]. In a field survey among managers, marketers, financiers, and administrative staff of four European organizations, this previous work demonstrated that those users who feel that their job performance and past compliance compensates for occasionally violating ISPs, known as the “metaphor of the ledger” type of justification, tend to use shadow IT. This work has found no support for the other types of justification in predicting shadow IT usage.

However, one reason for this finding may be that this field study does not properly elicit shadow IT users’ justifications. As outlined above, prior work on justifications found that an immediate situational stimulus is essential in order for users to invoke justifications [10,15]. In contrast to existing work in the shadow IT context, we, therefore, explicitly focus on justifications of shadow IT usage in reaction to IT constraints. IT constraints define concrete work situations in which users perceive the IT systems and/or IT policies within the organization as insufficient and interfering with their task performance [50,51].

Shadow IT usage in light of IT constraints is, therefore, especially susceptible for invoking justifications. The rationale is that the illegitimate but not criminal nature of shadow IT usage is a behavioral characteristic that previous work has found to be more prone to justifications than illegal ones [14,22]. More importantly, the consequences of engaging in using shadow IT are ambiguous [10]. On one hand, IT users deliberately deploy shadow IT for task achievement although the IT policies do not approve this deployment because it may, for instance, risk organizations’ information security and legal compliance [7,8]. On the other hand, however, the use of shadow IT enables the users to circumvent IT constraints and thus attain goals that were previously out of their reach [5,6].

Existing studies on shadow IT have found that users predominantly assign positive value to their shadow IT usage in face of a misalignment between task requirements and IT [1,5,18,19]. In short, the benefits of using shadow IT will lead a user to forget the illegal and risky nature of the act [7]. Shadow IT users may, thus, actively assert this positive value of their shadow IT usage by emphasizing the successful and/or creative accomplishment of their tasks, which would have failed to materialize if they had followed the IT policies. For instance, if waiting for a compliant solution implies market risk, users may claim that the IT constraint requires shadow IT usage in order to satisfy customers. In summary, the acceptance of justifications in light of IT constraints defines the attitudinal belief of the degree to which IT users accept justifications regarding noncompliance with IT policies in the face of IT constraints. In the next section, we hypothesize key situational drivers of shadow IT users’ acceptance of justifications in light of IT constraints.

## DEVELOPMENT OF HYPOTHESES

To develop our hypotheses, we rely upon three well-established theories from the field of organizational behavior. Next, we will review the expectancy-value model of attitudes [52], the model of organizational frustration [53,54], and the social-information processing theory [55] and utilize each of these theories to propose three respective hypotheses regarding the acceptance of justifications in the shadow IT usage context.

## Expectancy-Value Model of Attitudes and Justifications of Shadow IT Use

As we have argued, users form many different attitudinal beliefs to justify shadow IT usage.

The expectancy-value model of attitudes [52], mostly applied in the context of the theory of

reasoned action [56] and its successor the theory of planned behavior [57], assumes that at a

given moment only a relatively small number of salient beliefs that are readily accessible in

memory determine one’s attitude. These salient beliefs primarily consider the consequences of

performing a specific behavior. If the consequences are valued positively, individuals form a

positive attitude [58]. The model finds support in frequent studies in IS research, which show that

the perceived benefits of noncompliance with IT policies drive users’ attitude [e.g., 59,60].

We theorize that the expectancy-value model of attitudes can likewise be used to explain our

shadow IT usage context. Shadow IT usage is by definition a goal-oriented user behavior intended

to achieve work goals [1]; hence, we expect shadow IT users to have the work goals, and thus the

beneficial consequences of shadow IT usage, readily in their memory. Previous studies have

shown that the benefits of shadow IT usage enable the users to forget about the illegal and risky

nature of the act [7]. Thus, the benefits of goal achievement would be more salient in shadow IT

users’ mind than potential negative consequences.

We, therefore, follow the expectancy-value model of attitudes [52] to propose that users positive

belief that they can achieve their goal by using shadow IT will drive their acceptance of

justifications in light of IT constraints. Shadow IT users, who conceive benefits for their goal

achievement because shadow IT usage will help overcome the IT constraint, help them perform

their tasks better, and/or satisfy customers more rapidly, will be more inclined to assert this positive

value of shadow IT usage. The more benefits they perceive, the easier shadow IT users find and

accept the justifications that seek to neutralize the very shadow IT usage in reaction to IT

constraints by highlighting that positive value. Supporting our argumentation, D’Arcy et al. [10]

offer the following argument with regard to ISP violations: “[B]ecause ISP violations can help employees conserve time and energy that would otherwise be exerted to comply with these policies, the positive outcomes of such violations are likely to be salient and facilitate moral justification” (p. 297).

## Model of Organizational Frustration and Justifications of Shadow IT Use

Prior literature has demonstrated that negative emotions positively influence the acceptance of justifications regarding ISP violations [10,20]. These findings are theorized as existing because of the similarities in the conceptual rational (i.e., the situational stimuli) supporting the linkages between those negative emotions and justifications [10]. The situational stimuli in our study are IT constraints. The model of organizational frustration [53,54] proposes that the IT constraints that prevent an employee from goal attainment will trigger the experience of frustration. Accordingly, if the model of organizational frustration is correct, it is likely that IT constraints trigger the experience of frustration that, in turn, serves as progressed situational stimuli to, in addition, give rise to accepting justifications in light of those constraints [61]. Adopting the model of organizational frustration by including the experience of frustration in our research model should thus help us to better depict the factors most salient in driving shadow IT users’ acceptance of justifications in light of IT constraints.

The greater the emotional arousal of experiencing frustration, the higher the motivation to reduce it by coping with this state of frustration [62]. Mild levels of frustration provoke workarounds to help people reach their goals [53]. In light of an IT constraint, we, therefore, expect IT users experiencing mild levels of frustration to be particularly tempted to circumvent the IT constraint by

reacting with shadow IT use to reach their goals, which they, in turn, will utilize as justification. Higher levels of frustration provoke aggression [53]. We expect that aggressive users experiencing higher levels of frustration devalue the organization because of their unsatisfactory provision of IT resources and justify their shadow IT usage on such grounds. So overall, the more frustration shadow IT users perceive in face of an IT constraint, the more they will accept justifications th neutralize the shadow IT usage in reaction to IT constraints. Defining experienced frustration as the extent to which individuals perceive frustration while performing their job, we thus hypothesize:

H2: The experience of frustration is positively associated with shadow IT users’ acceptance of justifications in light of IT constraints.

## Social Information Processing Theory and Justifications of Shadow IT Use

Finally, in addition to emotions and expectations about consequences, social information processing theory [55] suggests that the social context provides expectations and conventions that influence the degree to which individuals justify and accept their own and others’ actions. Among others, people construct acceptable justifications from perceiving (i.e., cognitively evaluating any information about their social environment), such as their own or others’ past expressions of attitudes and/or behavioral responses. Individuals derive their perceptions in a retrospective process by recalling that information and reconstructing their environment [55]. Corroborating this theory, workplace deviance scholars find that individuals perceiving their coworkers breaking rules evaluate the rule-breaking behaviors as less risky in terms of social consequences [63,64]. This, in turn, will lessen their need to justify those acts [55].

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

## Data Collection

To test our hypotheses, we identified a valid and operationalizable research design that combines two methods of data collection. First, we performed a quasi-experimental lab scenario designed to objectively identify participants that use shadow IT in reaction to an IT constraint. The objective measurement enabled us to minimize social desirability bias common in self-reports of noncompliant behavior [65,66]. As an additional precaution, we guaranteed participants’ anonymity. A paper-based questionnaire gathered the psychometric scales of the latent constructs and participants’ demographic information. The retrospective measurement was essential in order to prevent sensitizing participants to the quasi-experiment’s focus on noncompliance [67]. The subjects in our study were 112 undergraduate and graduate IS students from our university, who received grade points for participating.

Lab Quasi-Experiment Scenario. To observe IT users’ noncompliance with the IT policies by using shadow IT, we created a realistic IT-based task. Subjects were required to send two PDF files using a provided web-based email system within a specific time frame. One of the files,

#

however, exceeded the maximum attachment size of the provided email system. So, as soon as participants tried to send this file they faced an IT constraint, which served as the situational stimuli of our study.

To create positive motivation to work around the IT constraint and reinforce a common goal, we raffled off an iPad mini among participants who were able to send both the files. However, this was only possible if participants violated the experimental task description and used shadow IT to perform the task. The experimental task description served as the IT policy prescribing the exact step-wise conduct of the task and specified IT usage restrictions explicitly prohibiting the use of any other hardware, such as private USB sticks, mobile devices, or smartphones, any other email client, or other web and cloud services as well as downloading software from the Internet (see Appendix).

Subjects performed the task in an isolated room, equipped only with one PC granting Internet access. We pretested our scenario with five pilot runs. This allowed us to determine an appropriate time frame of 12 minutes and to control for the operability of the task, the proper (mal)functioning of the set-up email system, and the criticality of the IT constraint integrated in this system. We also performed runs without the IT constraint and found all participants acting in compliance with the experimental task description and performing the task without shadow IT usage.

Shadow IT Users and Nonusers. To detect shadow IT usage, we secretly recorded the PC’s desktop during each subject’s task execution<sup>1</sup>. Thus, we could watch participants’ actual shadow IT usage in the screen recordings in order to objectively measure if the subjects voluntarily violated the experimental IT policy by performing the task using an explicitly prohibited shadow IT system. Subjects who adhered to the experimental IT policy were classified as shadow IT nonusers.

#

Survey Measures. To maximize reliability and validity of the latent variables, we relied heavily on established scales of prior IS literature, which we adapted to our shadow IT context. Perceived benefits of shadow IT usage were based on Guo et al. [59] and measured using two indicators specifically tailored to two key advantages of participants’ shadow IT usage, which in our experiment were: (1) the successful task performance and (2) the chance to win the IPad mini<sup>2</sup>. The remaining latent constructs were all measured with three items. Experienced frustration was captured with Peters et al.’s [51]’s three item rating-scale, such as “Trying to accomplish the task done was a very frustrating experience.” The three items of perceived noncompliance of others were transferred from Dahling et al. [64]’s construct “perception of rule-breaking performed by others,” for instance, “I think that participants of the study intentionally violate the IT policies to accomplish the task”.

To operationalize the acceptance of justifications in light of IT constraints, we focused on those types of justification within our experimental scenario design that were most likely to invoke their use by noncompliant participants. This approach is consistent with prior work that found that the specific situation determined which exact type of justifications IT users deployed [12,14,15,30,31]. For example, as there is no history of compliance in our one-time experiment, we excluded the “metaphor of the ledger.” We also expected the “appeal to higher loyalty” to be less relevant in our setting because participants’ act of noncompliance primarily served their individual goals and the isolated experimental environment should have minimized social pressures and claims. Moreover, we omitted the “denial of victim” type because previous work points to its similarity with “denial of injury”: “When offenders deny people victim status, they deny the amount of harm done” [20, p. 284]. Likewise, justifications of the “denial of responsibility” and the “denial of necessity” types are discussed to include similar verbalizations [24]. In the end, we theorized three types of justification to be the most relevant mechanisms in our experimental shadow IT setting: the “condemn the condemners”, the “denial of injury”, and the “defense of necessity”. We used Siponen and Vance’s [12] and Siponen et al.’s [13] validated instrumentations to measure each of these types of justification with three items. As there are theoretical arguments for reflective and formative second-order constructs of justifications [10,12], we reviewed those items and, building on criteria for formative and reflective constructs [68,69], determined that all of the first-order constructs were reflective. Consistent with the conceptually similar moral disengagement construct [10], we used these three interrelated, yet distinct, types as

manifestations of the higher-order construct of acceptance of justifications (i.e., a reflective firstorder, reflective second-order construct [70]). The rationale follows Petter et al. [70], whereby, in our study, each type by itself can be used as justification because all types share the common theme of justifications acceptance as well as same antecedents. Users need not accept all three types to accept justifications of shadow IT use.

The exact operationalization of each construct is included in the Supplementary Appendix. All items were measured on a five-point Likert scale from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree” and were coded so that high scores indicated a higher degree of acceptance of justifications, perceived benefits, experienced frustration, and perceived noncompliance by others.

## Data Analysis

We analyzed our hypotheses using structural equation modeling (SEM) with the partial least squares (PLS) algorithm, given the predictive and exploratory nature of the study [71]. According to the ten-times-rule heuristic, sample size requirements for our model were 30, and according to Cohen [72], 59 or 48 for a statistical power of 80% to identify minimum $\mathsf { R } ^ { 2 }$ values of 0.25 in the endogenous constructs in the structural model with an error probability of 5% and 10%, respectively. Furthermore, PLS is a useful tool for our scenario, where sample size constrains the use of robust SEM, given that we have overcome the established requirements for our model to reject the null hypothesis. Overall, 98 participants in our experiment recognized the IT constraint in the email system and were included in our final sample for data screening, preparation, and subsequent analyses. On average, the 30 men and 68 women were 21.03 years old, had 2.69 email accounts, and have been sending emails for 4.20 years.

Assessment of Shadow IT Users, Nonusers, and their Acceptance of Justifications. We started by analyzing the screen recordings of participants’ IT system usage during task performance. Two-thirds of the 98 participants complied with the experimental IT policy and,

instead of using a shadow IT system, waited for the time to pass, tried to send the file over and over again, or notified the email addressee of the problem. However, 39 subjects used shadow IT for performing the task and, thus, did not comply with the experimental IT policy by disobeying the explicit prohibition of private USB sticks, mobiles, or smartphones, any other email client, or other shadow IT users converted the PDF into a smaller text or document copy in another software, compressed the PDF deploying a downloaded or a web-based tool, or sent the file using another email system.

Assessment of Measurement Models. In addition to the theoretical examination as outlined above, we empirically tested the reflective three-factor structure for the acceptance of justifications construct proposed in prior studies by conducting a series of confirmatory factor analyses using AMOS 21. ## DISCUSSION

## Results and Contribution to Research

The results of our study have three major contributions. First, we contribute to shadow IT research by gaining a deeper understanding of shadow IT users’ acceptance of justifications in reaction to IT constraints and its difference to the acceptance of justifications of shadow IT nonusers. We show that compared to nonusers, shadow IT users accept justifications in light of IT constraints that defend shadow IT usage in case of necessity, no injury, or perceived injustice of the prevalent IT policies. This result is different from the single existing work on justifying shadow IT usage that found shadow IT users only invoke the “metaphor of the ledger” type of justifications [1]. However, our approach deliberately included a specific situational setting and stimuli. Our findings, thus, support previous results of studies on individuals’ acceptance of justifications in IS research and criminology that found that the nature of the noncompliant behavior as well as the specific situation, in which the noncompliance occurs, influence the types of justification users invoke [22,30–32].

Moreover, our results provide support for the essential importance of a concrete situational

stimulus that provokes users to deploy justifications of various types [14,15,17]. In contrast to the

prior work in IS research that has exclusively considered processed situational stimuli of

organizational injustice, negative affect, and role conflicts [10,15,16,20], we focused on the

acceptance of justifications in light of an immediate concrete situational event. We, thus, add that

IT constraints interfering with users’ task performance serve as such a stimulator for shadow IT

users’ acceptance of justifications. IT constraints may reinforce the ambiguity of the consequences

of shadow IT usage and make this noncompliant behavior even more prone to provoking

justifications. Shadow IT usage allows IT users to circumvent those IT constraints and thus to

attain goals that were previously out of their reach [5]. The prospect of this goal achievement may

place potential negative consequences on shadow IT usage, such as threat to information

security, into the background of shadow IT users’ minds. The IT constraint will, thus, invoke

shadow IT users to assert the positive value of shadow IT usage when other social referents

question this noncompliant shadow IT usage behavior.

Second, we contribute by analyzing situational factors that drive shadow IT users’ acceptance

of justifications in light of IT constraints. As the first study in IS research that focuses on exploring

positive and social motivators of users’ acceptance of justifications, we provide a better

understanding of the underlying mechanisms that influence justifications regarding shadow IT

usage. In particular, our empirical analysis sheds light on the substantial role of perceived

noncompliance of others in shadow IT users’ social work environments that enhance their

acceptance of justifications to use shadow IT. Although prior IS research has emphasized the

various types of user justifications of noncompliant behaviors [14], the mechanisms underlying the

justification processes have not yet been fully understood. In particular, prior research has found

#

that negatively afflicted situations trigger IT-based noncompliance and the corresponding justifications, such as organizational injustice, security-related stress, and role conflicts [10,15,16,20]. However, especially in light of IT constraints, noncompliance with existing IT policies could also imply that users are simply not satisfied with the IT systems offered by their organizations and, therefore, look out to other shadow IT in order to better satisfy their needs. Using shadow IT can then result in positive values, such as productivity gains or innovative task solutions. Referring to those positive benefits, people could be more inclined to use justifications regarding noncompliance with IT policies prescribing specific IT systems. Although our results did not find initial support for this relationship for shadow IT users, our posthoc test shows that the benefits of noncompliance shape the acceptance of justifications of the compliant nonusers of shadow IT, who are the recipients of shadow IT users’ justifications.

Third and finally, we contribute to research on justifications by showing the differences in the underlying mechanisms between shadow IT users’ and shadow IT nonusers’ acceptance of justifications. As this investigation was the first of its kind, and thus of an exploratory nature, we did this in a posthoc analysis. This exploratory analysis gives us a better understanding of how the same situational factors construct and influence the normative climate of IT policy noncompliance in organizations (i.e., people who engage in shadow IT use + people who tolerate shadow IT use). With respect to noncompliance in social systems, it is not only about these people who act in noncompliance and justify this behavior but also about those who socially approve of these justifications [11]. This is of particular relevance for decision makers with the objective of reducing noncompliance in their institutions because they need to consider both groups of people. Interestingly, research in the IS field has only focused on noncompliant users without considering the externalities and social influence of other referents who comply with the rules but accept and tolerate the justifications of those who are noncompliant.

#

Our empirical comparison finds that the perception that other individuals in organizations also use shadow IT drives shadow IT users’ acceptance of justifications to a great extent. Shadow IT nonusers, by contrast, accept justifications regarding shadow IT usage the more they expect benefits from shadow IT use. Thus, to convincingly frame justifications toward managers not using shadow IT usage.

## Limitations

A key limitation is the scenario of the lab quasi-experiment. Although we created a realistic experimental IT-based task to trigger participants’ shadow IT usage and the desire for justifications, questions about external validity exist. In particular, our results might be restricted to our experimental task of working around a constraining email system. Future research will benefit by verifying the generalizability of our results in field settings of employees’ shadow IT usage.

While we objectively recorded shadow IT usage during the IT-related task, the construct measures of situational factors and acceptance of justifications share common methods. We guaranteed response anonymity throughout our study and controlled for the effects of method variance using established statistical tests, none of which indicated the presence of substantial amount of common method variance (see Supplementary Appendix). Due to doubts about their evidential value [79], however, research designs with separated measurement contexts, locations, and points in time to collect the independent and dependent variables, would strengthen our results [80].

Moreover, in our study, we selected the acceptance of three types of justification, “condemn the condemners,” “denial of injury,” and “defense of necessity” because these types best fit with our experimental shadow IT usage scenario. This is a common approach due to the dependence

#

of the types of justification on the situational and behavioral context [12,14,22,30,31]. Our analyses and results have supported this selection and the multidimensional measurement approach. As prior studies have also measured the types of acceptance of justifications each as single constructs, we controlled for the possibility of different effects by regressing perceived benefits, “denial of injury,” and “defense of necessity” each in a separated structural modal. These reexaminations (see Table A10 in Supplementary Appendix) found equal effects regarding relative importance and the significance of each situational factor for predicting the acceptance of justifications of shadow IT users and nonusers. We are, therefore, confident that the findings of our study are robust across justification types. Future research could focus on other types of justification invoked by shadow IT users in light of different situational stimuli as well as in realworld settings.

Finally, we focused on three specific situational factors of perceived benefits, experienced frustration, and the perceived noncompliance of others. Social psychologists found these factors to be among the most relevant theoretical antecedents of attitude changes in situations closely related to our shadow IT setting. However, we are one of the first studies focusing on understanding situational factors driving individuals’ acceptance of justifications [22]. As a consequence, our investigations are to a great extent of an explorative nature. Future research should, therefore, build on our initial approach and examine further potential drivers of and barriers to the acceptance of justifications specific to the respective work and/or social environment of the study. Moreover, as D’Arcy et al. [10] and Detert et al. [81] point to their importance, individual differences in the way users’ accept justifications should be studied.

## Implications for Future Research

Our findings and contributions offer several implications for future research on individuals acceptance of justifications. Within this work, we discovered that different types reflect the

#

acceptance of justifications of shadow IT users. Silic et al. [1] showed that those users tend to use

shadow IT who think that their good level of job performance and past compliance compensates

for occasionally violating ISPs. By contrast, we show in our approach that those individuals who

use shadow IT to work around IT constraints justify their actions by scrutinizing the violated IT

policies to redirect attention and guilt on to the authorities responsible for rule establishment as

well as by minimizing the degree of noncompliance using claims that no one was (perceived to

be) hurt by this act, and that the noncompliance is an indispensable requirement in the particular

situation. Our results, thus, suggest that future research needs a deeper understanding of how

specific situational and technological as well as immediate and processed stimuli trigger shadow

IT usage in organizations and how shadow IT users justify their actions. It would be interesting to

examine how many and how intense these stimuli have to be in order to transform a compliant IT

user into a noncompliant shadow IT user.

Consistent with the expectancy-value model of attitudes [52], we have found that favorably evaluated consequences of noncompliance for goal achievement can lead to a higher acceptance of justifications to use shadow IT. Thus, as people accept justifications of shadow IT use if the outcome is positive, we need to adjust our pattern of thought whereby noncompliance is first and foremost seen as risky and harmful to organizations. Instead, shadow IT users may act with good intentions and the benevolent objective of helping their organizations boost performance. For example, shadow IT use can help identify inefficiencies in the process structure and IT landscape of an organization and, thus, improve the overall performance of IT and business processes alike. However, in our work, only those individuals complying to ISPs and not engaging in shadow IT use accept justifications based on positive situational factors, while perceived benefits had no significant impact on the acceptance of justifications of actual shadow IT users. Consequently, this result suggests that if individuals have used shadow IT once, their acceptance of justifications of future shadow IT use is not a question of potential benefits that could have been gained by this

#

act, but, as our further findings support, it is about the perception of others’ behavior in the same situation. In this regard, future research should investigate whether the first noncompliant act of shadow IT users not only changes their justifications but also their evaluations of whether shadow IT use could be supportive of themselves and their organization. Another fruitful research endeavor could be to investigate the interaction between positive and negative situational factors in shaping individuals’ acceptance of justifications. Such a reexamination may also help to explain the insignificant effect of positive situational factors on shadow IT users’ acceptance of justifications in the case that the relative importance of positive drivers is mitigated or even destroyed by negative ones.

Another important finding of our work is the strong but unbalanced impact of the social informal environment on shadow IT users and nonusers. Our results displayed a highly significant association between shadow IT users’ perceptions of noncompliance of others and their degree of acceptance of justifications. In contrast, our posthoc analysis reveals that compliant individuals’ acceptance of justifications is not influenced by the perceived noncompliant acts of shadow IT users. Therefore, our results are only partly in line with social information processing theory [55], which suggests that expectations and observations in the social context shape the justifications of one’s actions. This result is somewhat consistent with prior research that found an insignificant influence of informal sanctions on IT-related noncompliance in the presence of justifications [12]. Shadow IT users tend to justify their actions to clear themselves from self-blame as well as the blame of other referents, such as peers or supervisors and, thus, to prevent potential informal sanctions. When noncompliance is prevalent among significant peers, this justification process eases because people may perceive a normative environment that socially approves shadow IT use and projects less informal sanctions. Therefore, in the presence of high levels of acceptance of justifications, descriptive norms of noncompliance are perceived as more salient and, according to the focus theory of normative conduct [82], surpass the influence of injunctive norms describing what ought to be done to avoid informal sanctions. Thus, future research is needed to analyze, on the one hand, what constitutes a normative climate justifying shadow IT use despite users being compliant themselves and on the other hand, how this climate influences shadow IT users, their actions, and acceptance of justifications.

## Implications for Practice

The ambiguous nature of shadow IT (potential for performance and innovation versus threat to security and compliance) requires managers to focus on one of two distinct strategies, as gaining the best of both worlds may be hard or even impossible to achieve: Managers need to follow either a disruptive approach containing the almost entire repeal of restricting IT policies or a clear concept to enforce compliance with IT policies.

When managers set out to follow the latter path of compliance, our results suggest tracking IT users’ behavior and intervening directly and immediately after the first incidence of shadow IT deployment. Instant counteractions in the form of verbal confrontations demonstrating the serious disapproval of, and intervention against, those actions, for example, by imposing formal sanctions, may suppress the emergence of a normative climate of socially approving shadow IT use among coworkers. Consequently, shadow IT users will be less likely to justify their actions with the noncompliant actions of others. If ative climate of justifying shadow IT use has already emerged, measures need to be tailored to reduce the acceptance of justifications of both the shadow IT users and nonusers. Toward actual shadow IT users or those with an assumed high propensity, supervisors need to explain that shadow IT use is a noncompliant act and not accepted and possibly even sanctioned because the IS policies are important and necessary, for instance, to save the organizations’ digital assets and ensure conformity with laws and accountancy rules. Managers should further highlight the typical compliant behaviors of colleagues not engaging in shadow IT use and emphasize that no excuses for shadow IT use are approved even if users’

#

have good motives in terms of organizational objectives like meeting deadlines of important projects.

To ensure that the compliant users continue to stick to the policies and do not socially approve justifications regarding shadow IT use by asserting its positive value, expectations of benefits from ensure that the approved IT systems are as beneficial, useful, and convenient as those systems users employ in their private lives and know from past task settings. Moreover, the advantages of compliance for both users and the organization should be stressed or even rewarded. This includes building up disadvantages, such as monetary penalties or dismissals even in the case of tolerance of shadow IT use that individuals need to consider. Moreover, incentives should be set for constantly complying with the organizational IT policies as well as constantly improving them.

## CONCLUSION

Despite the noncompliance with organizational IT policies, shadow IT usage can provide positive value for organizations, in particular, if IT constraints impede employees’ job performance. This makes shadow IT usage prone to socially accepted justifications that assert its positive value by pleading necessity, harmlessness, or injustice.

This study offers a better understanding of the acceptance of those justifications. We find significant differences between shadow IT users and nonusers, not only in the degree to which they accept justifications in light of IT constraints but also in the situational factors driving this acceptance. While perceived noncompliance of others is the most important factor for shadow IT users’ acceptance of justifications, nonusers accept the justifications of their noncompliant fellows if they expect benefits from the shadow IT usage. Our findings provide new implications for managers aiming to reduce shadow IT usage. Shadow IT users, in turn, can find help in convincingly framing their justifications to management.

## Authors Biography

Steffi Haag is an Assistant Professor of Information Systems at the Friedrich-Alexander University Erlangen-Nürnberg (FAU), Germany. Her research focuses on the use and the value of shadow IT, behavioral IS security, and the management of innovation in IS. Her research has been published in Information & Management, Business Information Systems Engineering, Communications of the AIS, the Journal of Business Economics, and in the proceedings of leading IS conferences, such as the International Conference on Information Systems.

Andreas Eckhardt is a Professor at the German Graduate School of Management and Law (GGS), Heilbronn. His research on IS adoption and use, behavioral security, technostress, and virtual work, has been published in two books, several conference proceedings, and scientific journals including Journal of Information Technology, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, and Information & Management. He received multiple prizes and awards for his research, teaching and community work, among others the Magid Igbaria Award by ACM SIGMIS and the Best Research-in-Progress Paper Award at ECIS 2018. He currently serves as an Associate Editor for Information Systems Journal and is a member of the AIS standing committee on Diversity & Inclusion (D&I).

Andrew Schwarz, Milton J. Womack Developing Scholar, is a Professor of Information Systems at the E. J. Ourso College of Business, Louisiana State University. His research interests focus on the adoption of new technology, IT-business alignment, and IT outsourcing. Previous work by him has been published in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, the Journal of AIS, and the European Journal of Information Systems, among others. Prior to his career in academia, he was a consultant in the market research industry, working on projects for Fortune 100 companies on topics related to market segmentation, brand imaging, and brand awareness.
