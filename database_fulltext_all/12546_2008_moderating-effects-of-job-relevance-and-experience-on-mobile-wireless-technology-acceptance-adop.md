# Moderating effects of Job Relevance and Experience on mobile wireless technology acceptance: Adoption of a smartphone by individuals

Sang Hyun Kim \*

School of Business Administration, Kyungpook National University, 702-701 Sankyuk-dong, Puk-ku, Daegu, South Korea

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 8 May 2006 Received in revised form 1 June 2007 Accepted 29 May 2008 Available online 10 July 2008

Keywords: Technology acceptance model (TAM) Mobile wireless technology Job Relevance Experience Perceived Cost Savings Company’s Willingness to Fund

## A B S T R A C T

My study extended TAM to include individuals’ intention to use mobile wireless technology (MWT). It added two new constructs, Perceived Cost Savings and Company’s Willingness to Fund, and two causal relationships, Job Relevance and Experience, as moderating effects. The 286 sets of data collected in an online survey were tested against the model using SEM. Results supported my new model: the new constructs and variables accounted for 62.7% of the variance found in an individual’s behavioral intention to use MWT. The path coefficients between the constructs ranged from 0.26 to 0.85 also supporting the model.

## 1. Introduction

Many people and organizations today use mobile wireless technology (MWT) via WAP phones, handheld devices, smartphones, wireless laptops, m-commerce, etc. However, despite the market growth, research examining key factors affecting user behavior and its adoption are rare. What is missing is an understanding of the motivation resulting in its adoption from a user viewpoint. I used one MWT – the smartphone – as a target technology: it is a wireless telephone set with computer-enabled features. My unit of analysis was the employee in an organization.

## 2. TAM and related empirical studies

Fig. 1 shows the well-known TAM; in it, the PEU is assumed to affect the user’s intention indirectly through PU. These two beliefs influence a user’s attitude (A) toward using and this, in turn, determines BI to use the system. Then, information systems usage is determined by behavioral intention. Also, the PEU influences the PU, which has been found to be a more important factor than PEU in determining whether to use a technology [24].

Kwon and Chidambaram [13] modified TAM to examine the extent of the adoption of cellular phone technology. They added other variables, such as enjoyment, social pressure, and apprehensiveness. Liang et al. [15] added personal innovativeness, compatibility, and supports and found that PU and PEU were the main factors that determined actual usage by health professionals. PU mediated PEU, Job Relevance, and compatibility for PDA usage. Lu et al. [16] investigated the mobile wireless Internet, suggesting that technological complexity, individual differences, facilitating conditions, social influences, and wireless trust environment had a positive effect on both long- and nearterm usefulness and ease of use; these, in turn, had a positive influence on attitude and intention to use the Wireless Internet via Mobile Technology. Pedersen [19] added subjective norm and behavioral control to examine the reliability of TAM in analyzing mobile wireless service, adding a subjective norm and behavioral control; however, only the former had a significant effect on intention to use.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

My model is presented in Fig. 2. It introduces two new variables – Perceived Cost Savings (PCSs) and Company’s Willingness to Fund (CWF) – as affecting a user interest in adopting or rejecting use of a smartphone. Costs and similar variables, such as resource availability and user support, have been seen as important determinants influencing a user in adopting new technology. For example, Wu and Wang [23] integrated innovation and diffusion theory, perceived risk, and cost to examine factors determining users’ m-Commerce acceptance; they found that cost significantly affected the user’s BI. Moreover, various types of support, such as technical, legal, management, etc., were found to be important [8].

I assumed therefore that PCS and CWF would have a positive impact on use of a smartphone and retained PEU and PU in the model to determine whether their impact predicted user adoption of a smartphone. Finally, Job Relevance and Experience were introduced as moderators.

## 3.1. Model variables

## 3.1.1. Perceived usefulness

In the model, PU is the extent to which an individual believes that using MWT will improve his or her performance, i.e., helps them conduct their tasks or jobs. The concept of PU was expanded to include both near- and long-term consequences. In the context of an acceptance of MWT, PU is a near-term consequence since MWT allows people to blur the boundaries once imposed by time and space. By using it, users expect to perform better in their jobs. Thus, my first hypothesis was

Hypothesis 1. PU is directly related to BI to use MWT.

## 3.1.2. Perceived ease of use

Prior work has shown that PEU is significantly linked to intention via its impact on PU. It directly influences user acceptance and indirectly affects usefulness, where the direct impact was most relevant. Empirical studies, however, found mixed results about PEU: some found it to be non-significant [5]. Therefore, I decided to investigate the effect of PEU on PU and BI to use the MWT. This resulted in a pair of hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2a. PEU is positively related to PU.

Hypothesis 2b. PEU is positively related to BI to use MWT.

## 3.1.3. Behavioral intention

TRA originally suggested that the use of a technology was determined by BI: the strength of an individual’s intention to perform a behavior. In my study, the attitude construct was not included in order to simplify the model. Actual usage was measured in terms of the frequency of use (how often) and its volume (how much). Thus, the third hypothesis was

Hypothesis 3. BI is positively related to Actual Usage (AU) of MWT.

## 3.2. Job Relevance as a moderating variable

Individuals have different perceptions of outcomes they expect to obtain from MWT because of the different nature of their job; also they are exposed to external information, which may affect them in choosing which technology they need. The elaboration likelihood model (ELM) suggested that external information was a primary reason that individuals reinvestigated their prior beliefs and attitudes. Bhattacherjee and Sanford [1] drew on ELM as a framework to examine the moderating effects of job relevance on PU and attitude. They claimed that potential users would be motivated to find out about a technology to help them make an informed decision on its usefulness.

Venkatesh et al. [22] noted that there was little evidence of a direct relationship between attitude and intention. Other research has empirically linked user acceptance to variables similar to Job Relevance [14], and task-technology fit [9]. Therefore, I contended that individuals’ perceptions about the relevance of an MWT to their job would reinforce the relationship between PU and BI toward the MWT. Thus, I hypothesized:

Hypothesis 4. Job Relevance (JR) moderates the effect of PU on BI to use MWT.

## 3.3. Perceived Cost Savings

Costs of a technology have always been a primary consideration in its adoption. In my study, PCS referred to an individual’s perception about the way that an MWT provided cost-effective communication and information exchange. Costs include more than monetary factors, e.g., time and emotional effort. Indeed, costs were not found to be significant, in the adoption of mobile wireless financial services [12]. In my study, PCS includes time and emotional effort. Palen [17] claimed that MWT allowed businesses to develop a connection by transcending time and place, providing ubiquity, reachability, convenience, and localization for individuals who participated in mobile wireless communication and service activities. Furthermore, Rogers [20] claimed that decreasing costs and the capability of mobile wireless phones to save time offered a relative advantage. Therefore, it seems important to examine how individuals perceived costs when deciding to adopt MWT. Thus, I proposed:

Hypothesis 5. PCS is positively related to BI to use MWT.

## 3.4. Company’s Willingness to Fund

Without adequate funding, it is virtually impossible for individuals, employees, or organizations to adopt and use new technologies. If funding is available, however, people’s attitudes and behaviors may change. Therefore, the essential question is, ‘‘Will individuals or employees adopt a new technology if the company is willing to fund it?’’ CWF is defined here as the degree to which a company believes that funding would enhance an individual’s adoption of MWT. A lack of funding has been found to impede the success of IT adoption. Iacovou et al. [10] found that organizational readiness affected adoption of technology. However, individuals in a high economic status may not care about costs and this makes funding unimportant. Also individuals who are familiar with an older technology may resist change. Thus, I proposed:

Hypothesis 6. CWF has a positive effect on BI to use MWT.

## 3.5. Experience as a moderating effect on CWF and B

The effect of CWF might change with prior experience, the level of an individual’s satisfaction when adopting prior technology. Experience has been regarded as important in identifying individual differences. For example, prior experience with a similar technology was found to be a major factor influencing an individual’s attitude in adoption decisions [7]. However, the positive effect of prior experience weakened over time as the individual started to understand and evaluate benefits and costs of the new technology. It was, however, important to look closely at the effect of prior experience. Thus, to examine a user’s beliefs concerning BI on MWT, prior experience was considered by adding:

Hypothesis 7. Experience (E) moderates the effect of CWF on BI to use MWT.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Study context and sample

An on-line survey was constructed and validated before using it to test the research model and its hypotheses. An online survey service was used to solicit 302 questionnaires; 16 of these had to be discarded because of missing responses and being outside the context of the study. All participants were working adults. They represented a racially diverse group with an average age of 32.6, ranging from about 20 to 55 years old. Sixty two percent of the respondents were male. Most of the participants (87%) used a smartphone daily. Demographics of the respondents are shown in Table 1.

## 4.2. Measures of the research variables

Items to measure constructs in the model were mainly adopted from prior research. Some minor wording changes were made for a smartphone context. New constructs in the model, however, had to be constructed. For example, the three items to measure PCS were developed from Parasurman [18] and IS literature on technology appropriation criteria [4]. Items were modified to measure an individual’s psychological feeling in reference to his or her possession of a smartphone. Each individual was asked to indicate the extent of agreement with statements about the adoption of a smartphone, using a seven-point Likert scale ranging from (1) Strongly Disagree to (7) Strongly Agree for each factor. All items are shown in Appendix A.

## 5. Findings and discussion

## 5.1. Analysis of the measurement model

The measurement model for the constructs was created and tested using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). AMOS 5.0 was the main statistic tool used in the analysis. To demonstrate a reasonable fit for the model, a number of factors were computed, including Chi-square/degrees of freedom, Goodness-of-fit Index (GFI), Adjusted Goodness-of-fit Index (AGFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Root Mean Square of Approximation (RMSEA). A very good fit is normally deemed to exist when GFI and CFI are greater than 0.90 [4], Root Mean Square of Approximation (RMSEA) is around 0.1 [2], and AGFI is greater than 0.80. The chi-square was not used because it is sensitive to sample size [11]. Thus, the use of relative $\chi ^ { 2 }$ $( \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d . f . } )$ seemed appropriate; it is assumed that values from less than 3 to as high as 5 were indicative of an acceptable fit [3].

The indices for the measurement model 1 with all items (23) showed that the data did not fit well. Some of the indices, such as GFI (0.81), and AGFI (0.74) were below acceptable levels. The modification indices indicated that one item (PU4) used to measure perceived usefulness had a cross-loading issue. This parameter representing a possible cross-loading of item PU4 on perceived ease of use stands apart from the rest and accounted for substantial misspecification of the hypothesized factor loading. Such misspecification may mean that PU4, in addition to measuring perceived usefulness, measured PEU. Therefore, the measurement model was reevaluated by deleting this item. Furthermore, the first item of perceived usefulness (PU1), and the first item of perceived ease of use (PEU1) had large residual values. Therefore, a respecification of Model 1 without these items was necessary to improve it. Each item was discarded one at a time and Model 1 was reevaluated.

NFI (0.93), GFI (0.93), and CFI (0.97) were all greater than 0.90, AGFT (0.89) was greater than 0.80, and RMSEA (0.057) was less than 0.10, while the relative chi-square (1.88) was less than 3. The measurement model was based on the revision of 20 items. The summary of the overall fit indices for the measurement Models 1 and 2 is shown in Table 2. With the values of all indices improved, Model 2 was a better fit for the data than Model 1.

## 5.2. Psychometric properties of measures

After purifying the measurement model, the construct validity was assessed by three measures: item loading, construct reliability, and average variance extracted (AVE). If the items had factor loadings greater than 0.5 for their expected factor and less than 0.4 on the others, then construct validity is demonstrated [6]. To test the reliability, Cronbach’s alpha was used. Even when there is no set level of alpha being used as a minimum for reliability, the minimum acceptable level of alpha is 0.7 [21]. AVE measures the variance captured by the indicators relative to the measurement error, and it should be greater than 0.5 to justify using the construct [2]. Tables 3 and 4 show the results of the convergent and discriminant validity test.

All constructs have higher mean scores than that of the average. This implied that the respondents considered a smartphone to be useful, easy to use, and cost saving in performing the job, and that they had a positive intention to use the technology, which in turn lead to using it.

Lastly, the discriminant validity was tested. The latent variables described the shared variance among the constructs and did not exceed the AVE. Thus discriminant validity was justified.

## 5.3. Analysis of the structural equation model

From the primary data $( n = 2 8 6 )$ , the SEM was examined to test the relationships between user intention to use mobile wireless technology and PU, PEU, PCS, and CWF with two moderating effects: job relevance and experience. I used AMOS 5.0 to analyze the proposed model. Significant paths were found. As expected, TAM variables were consistent with previous studies. Additionally, new variables and two moderating factors had a significant effect on users’ behavior. Table 5 and Fig. 3 show the results of hypothesis-testing.

PU was significantly related to an individual’s behavioral intention to use a smartphone $( \beta = 0 . 5 2 2$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1 $ . Moreover, PEU had a significant impact on BI and PU $( \beta = 0 . 4 9 9 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ $\beta = 0 . 6 0 8 , \ p < 0 . 0 0 1$ , respectively). Finally, BI was significantly related to actual usage of a smartphone $( \beta = 0 . 8 5 4 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ Thus, H1, 2a and b were supported. Additionally, PEU explained 49.1% of the variance in PU.

Second, job relevance was found to be significant $( \beta = 0 . 2 6 1$ $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ as a moderator. Therefore, H4 was supported. Job Relevance made the relationship between perceived usefulness and users’ behavior strong. As individuals use a smartphone to perform their job, they feel that the technology is useful, which affects their positive intention to use it. The respondents were occupied in various jobs. Therefore, job relevance was an important factor regardless of occupation in considering the usefulness of the technology.

Third, respondents agreed that mobile wireless technology was more cost effective than other technologies in performing their job.

## 6. Conclusion

## 6.1. Summary of findings

My study introduced PCS and CWF as factors, which affect the social effects motivating users considering adoption of MWT. The data collected supported the overall validity of this model and all hypotheses were supported. The new constructs explained about 62.7% of the variance in BI. Finally, it explained 70.0% of the variance in the actual use of MWT.

The findings also supported the new additions and casual relations in the model. Some relationships in the model were found to be significant: perceived usefulness $( \beta = 0 . 5 2 2 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and perceived ease of use (b = 0.499, p < 0.001) continued to have an impact on a user behavior, and behavioral intention (b = 0.854, p < 0.001) had a significant impact on the actual use of mobile wireless technology.

The moderating effect of job relevance was also found to be significant $( \beta = 0 . 2 6 1 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The effect of this construct has not been examined in previous studies. In the workplace, individuals are more willing to adopt a technology that is useful. I also found that an individual’s intention to use mobile wireless technology was significantly affected by CWF. A company must, of course, provide technology to their employees to help them perform their jobs. Finally, the individuals adopt a technology if it is within their prior experience, which was found to be significant moderator between CWE and BL

## 6.2. Contributions and implications

My study proposed new constructs and causal relationships in adopting a new technology, particularly mobile wireless technology. The findings help by revealing the effects of new technology on human attitude and behavior.

## 6.3. Limitations

This study, however, had several limitations which must be noted. One is common to all survey research: a possible selfreporting bias: some of the variables were self-reported. Another limitation was that some constructs were measured using only two items; there are some risks in this, particularly in reliability and validity. However, the two-item scales generally exhibited adequate reliability: the Cronbach’s Alpha was over 0.79 in all cases and other results mitigated validity concerns.

PEU1 – I would find it easy to get a smartphone to do what I want it to do.

## Behavioral Intention

## Actual Usage

AU1 – How many times do you believe you use a smartphone?

AU3 – How frequently do you believe you use a smartphone?

## Job Relevance

JR1 – In my job, usage of a smartphone is high.

JR2 – In my job, usage of a smartphone is relevant.

## Perceived Cost Savings

PCS1 – In my job, I can avoid any unnecessary cost and time by using a smartphone.

PCS3 – A smartphone is more cost effective than other technologies in my job.

CWF1 – In my job, funding from my company is crucial for me because with new technology, I too often risk paying a lot of money for something that is not worth much.
