# Strategic bene<sup>W</sup>ts to SMEs from third party web services: An action research analysis

Amy W. Ray <sup>a,¤</sup>, Julian J. Ray <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Bentley College, 175 Forest Street, Waltham, MA 02452, United States <sup>b</sup> C2GLogistics, Inc., Natick, MA 01760, United States

Accepted 17 July 2006 Available online 14 September 2006

## Abstract

Prior research provides evidence that large organizations can derive strategic bene<sup>W</sup>ts from developing new applications with web services. This research contributes to the literature by demonstrating that small and medium enterprises (SMEs) can derive strategic bene<sup>W</sup>ts from using publicly available web services, and that SMEs may be best able to take advantage of the inexpensive, user friendly third party web services that are emerging. In this paper we describe how one small golf retailer used web services to achieve strategic bene<sup>W</sup>ts including higher pro<sup>W</sup>t margins and better competitive positioning relative to a large competitor. Speci<sup>W</sup>cally, action research methods were used to devise and implement an innovative strategy for applying the web services o<sup>V</sup>ered by eBay in tandem with web services o<sup>V</sup>ered by related third party companies. The business and IT strategies, <sup>W</sup>rst year results, and key success factors are described followed by a discussion of the generalizability of the strategies employed. In conclusion, possible directions for future research are discussed. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Web services; Third party; SME; eBay; Action research

## 1. Introduction

Many large e-businesses have begun o<sup>V</sup>ering web services that allow trading partners of all sizes to access various data sources and applications. For example, Amazon, Google, and eBay o<sup>V</sup>er either free or inexpensive developer kits that include standard web service APIs, demonstration applications and code as well as complete instructions for connecting to general data sources or speci<sup>W</sup>c account information. In addition, a number of new third party web service companies have emerged, o<sup>V</sup>ering tools that work in conjunction with the web services of the larger e-businesses to support activities such as data mining and sales assistance.

These o<sup>V</sup>erings present a window of strategic opportunity for small and medium enterprises (SMEs). Small budgets and limited technical expertise have historically been barriers to SMEs interested in IT-enabled business strategies, but emerging web service o<sup>V</sup>erings provide inexpensive, user friendly access to information for all organizations, including SMEs. While prior research provides evidence that large organizations can derive strategic bene<sup>W</sup>ts from developing new applications with web services, this research contributes to the literature by demonstrating that SMEs can derive strategic bene<sup>W</sup>ts from simply using publicly available web services, and that SMEs may be best able to take advantage of the inexpensive, user friendly third party web services that are emerging.

In this study, action research methods were used to devise and implement an innovative strategy for applying the web services o<sup>V</sup>ered by eBay in tandem with web services o<sup>V</sup>ered by related third party companies for a small golf retail and service company, Golf Masters. Golf Masters is a privately owned golf retail and service company and has operated since 2000 in its present location. It serves a local market with a variety of golf-related products and services. Annual revenues approximate \$1 million, with retail sales generating 30% of the revenues and services such as golf lessons, repair, and maintenance of golf equipment, and operation of a private Golf Club and indoor golf facility generating the remaining 70% of revenues. In 2003, a major golf-specialty chain-store opened a 24,000 sq. ft. retail space within 1/2 mile of Golf Masters’ store. This competitor threatened to reduce Golf Masters’ retail revenues by drawing customers and repeat business away.

How Golf Masters used the publicly available web services of eBay and related third parties to meet this competitive challenge is described in the remainder of the paper. Golf Masters’ innovative strategies, <sup>W</sup>rst year results, and key success factors are presented in this paper followed by a discussion of the generalizability of the strategies employed to other SMEs, including discussion of some reasons why these speci<sup>W</sup>c strategies may not work for large companies. We conclude with some possible directions for future research.

## 2. Literature review

Lessons learned from prior research on strategic investment in information technology (IT) and from prior research on SME investment in IT form the theoretical foundation for this research. The literature on strategic investment in information technology is extensive. Two areas particularly relevant to this research e<sup>V</sup>ort are studies on IT-enabled business strategies and research on the valuation of IT investments. After a discussion of relevant research from these areas, a summary of how this study <sup>W</sup>ts with prior literature is presented.

## 2.1. IT-enabled business strategies

A steady stream of research on IT-enabled business strategies has been produced since the 1980s, in part because the information technologies employed have changed rapidly in this period of time. As new technologies and technology paradigms emerge, researchers are inspired to study their strategic implications (e.g., Barney, 1991; Barua et al., 2004; Bergeron et al., 1991; Brown et al., 1995; Chatterjee et al., 2002; Clemons and Row, 1991; Hackbarth and Kettinger, 2004; Kimberly and Evanisko, 1981; Levy et al., 1999; Porter, 1985; Tavakolian, 1989). More businesses are using web services to increase information availability and exchange among di<sup>V</sup>erent applications and systems, so research on the potential value of web services has increased. Research on web services has either focused on development of theoretical frameworks for attaining bene<sup>W</sup>ts (Hagel and Brown, 2001; Huang, 2004) or on the analysis of bene<sup>W</sup>ts achieved by larger <sup>W</sup>rms (Anderson et al., 2005; Iyer et al., 2003; Lim and Wen, 2003). Yet at the time of this literature review, no other research on the strategic value of web services to SMEs was found.

Just as the technologies businesses use has changed since the mid-1980s, the theoretical frameworks used for studying strategic value of technologies has also shifted. Speci<sup>W</sup>cally, early frameworks focused more on external factors such as a company’s competitive positioning relative to others in the industry (e.g., Porter, 1985), while more recent frameworks (e.g., the resource based view of <sup>W</sup>rms) emphasize the importance of an internal focus to achieve synergies between IT investments and other organizational resources (e.g., Barney, 1991). Consideration must be given to both external and internal in<sup>X</sup>uences in most cases. Pressures from customers and trading partners are often the source of a company’s decision to adopt an accelerated strategy for IT investment and implementation (Hackbarth and Kettinger, 2004) and can be particularly in<sup>X</sup>uential for SMEs with smaller market shares, but without considering how IT can leverage unique organizational competencies, a company may merely survive rather than thrive (Barney, 1991).

According to the resource based view (Barney, 1991), a <sup>W</sup>rm’s success is determined by their ability to e<sup>V</sup>ectively combine technological resources with organizational and environmental resources (Barney, 1991; Barua et al., 2004; Sambamurthy et al., 2003) and emphasizes that coordinating existing internal resources is vital to strategic success (Brown and Eisenhardt, 1997; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Venkatraman and Henderson, 1998). For example, one organizational resource, the agility of personnel to recognize and seize new, pro<sup>W</sup>t-enhancing business opportunities, has been shown to be e<sup>V</sup>ectively improved through innovations in information technology that provide more or better information to managers. Non-technical resources have also been shown to in<sup>X</sup>uence successful adoption of technology. For example, strong leadership is an organizational resource that can have a powerful e<sup>V</sup>ect on an organization’s ability to leverage IT investments (Armstrong and Sambamurthy, 1999; Chatterjee et al., 2002). Numerous other studies con<sup>W</sup>rm the importance of coordinating IT investments with business knowledge throughout an organization (Chatterjee et al., 2002; Hackbarth and Kettinger, 2004; Nelson and Cooprider, 1996; Pinker et al., 2002).

## 2.2. Valuation of IT investments

If evidence of <sup>W</sup>nancial bene<sup>W</sup>t can be linked to IT investments made to enhance organizational resources, then the IT investment has strategic value (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). Several studies have considered alternative measures of IT investment success as well as factors that might increase the likelihood of <sup>W</sup>nding measurable relationships between strategic IT investments and <sup>W</sup>nancial returns (Barney, 1991; Barua et al., 2004; Brandyberry et al., 1999; Rai et al., 1997; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). However, meta-analyses of research attempting to identify a <sup>W</sup>nancial return on strategic investments in information technology show mixed results (Brown et al., 1995; Strassman, 1997). Contributing factors include the di<sup>Y</sup>culty of disaggregating IT investments from other related investments that may simultaneously a<sup>V</sup>ect a <sup>W</sup>rm’s success (Rai et al., 1997), and the lack of standard methods for measuring returns on IT (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1998). As <sup>W</sup>rms grow in size, the measurement challenges may grow as well because the complexity of the IT infrastructure and the related organizational business processes grows. Thus, theoretically, one would expect fewer challenges when attempting to measure the success of IT investments in SMEs.

Some research looking at inter-organizational IT investments indicate that the success of those investments may be in<sup>X</sup>uenced by the technological sophistication and readiness of trading partners and customers and that greater e<sup>Y</sup>ciency and e<sup>V</sup>ectiveness may be achieved when trading partner technologies and related business processes are aligned (Barua et al., 2004). However, the technologies supporting inter-organizational business systems are increasingly standards-based and capable of opening the <sup>X</sup>ow of data between any heterogeneous systems and applications. The public web services o<sup>V</sup>erings of Amazon and eBay are prime examples of this paradigm shift in inter-organizational information exchange as virtually any organization can use the web services of these companies to access available inventory and account information, regardless of the systems platforms they use. Accordingly, as the use of web services and similar standards and protocols increases, the amount of pre-implementation planning to align technologies and business processes may decrease and the ability to connect with a wider variety of trading partners, including SMEs, may increase. It is thus conceivable that an organization may pro<sup>W</sup>t from using web service o<sup>V</sup>erings of other organizations without inter-organizational coordination of systems and processes, and indeed without the other organizations even knowing about the strategy.

## 2.3. SME investment in IT

SMEs have smaller budgets for IT investments than their larger counterparts (Raymond, 1985), so <sup>W</sup>nancial limitations have traditionally prevented SMEs from making investments in large and complex IT applications. In addition, smaller companies are historically managed and operated by personnel with rich product and domain expertise but limited IT skills (Auger and Gallaugher, 1997), so the human resources necessary to champion and coordinate strategic IT investments may not exist in smaller organizations. Other research <sup>W</sup>nds that ease of use is a dominant factor in explaining system usage and perceived usefulness in SMEs (Igbaria et al., 1997). Part of the reason that web services are growing in popularity is that they are more user friendly and less expensive than alternatives. These attributes are attractive to large companies, but vital to small companies with limited budgets and technical expertise. Yet prior studies on large <sup>W</sup>rm use of web services are not generalizable to SMEs because those studies focus on using web services to solve ‘large <sup>W</sup>rm’ problems such as integrating complex, enterprise-wide applications (Anderson et al., 2005) or facilitating communications across diverse and multifaceted b2b systems (Lim and Wen, 2003).

SMEs have simpler management structures and fewer personnel which may be a catalyst for rapid, e<sup>V</sup>ective implementation of IT when an owner/manager is highly motivated to act as champion for a new IT strategy. In contrast, larger <sup>W</sup>rms frequently run into political con<sup>X</sup>icts over IT initiatives and may even experience sales channel con<sup>X</sup>icts where IT initiatives involve development of web-based sales or purchasing systems (Pinker et al., 2002). Also, functional activities are often not organized and managed separately by di<sup>V</sup>erent teams of personnel in SMEs, which may work in their favor when investing in new ITinfused business strategies.

Research also indicates that SME managers are often unaware of the potential bene<sup>W</sup>ts of e-business investments to their organizations (Gribbins and King, 2002) and may therefore need outside assistance to initially identify suitable IT investment strategies. In addition, more than 70 percent of respondents to a survey of SMEs identi<sup>W</sup>ed external business pressure from customers or others as one of the primary reasons for adopting one particular integrative technology, EDI. (Iacovou et al., 1995). Together, these <sup>W</sup>ndings along with other prior research on levels of IT knowledge in SMEs suggest that collaborative e<sup>V</sup>orts with external experts on strategic IT opportunities such as use of web services may be a good investment.

## 2.4. Expectations based on prior research

Based on <sup>W</sup>ndings from prior research, we posit that SMEs in general and Golf Master in particular should have some advantages over larger counterparts in the strategic appli cation of web services. Golf Masters, like most SMEs, has a simpler management structure and fewer personnel. The owner was highly motivated to act as the champion for the new IT strategies, and their size and structure further facilitated rapid, positive strategy implementation. Accordingly, we expected that strategic application of publicly available web services and related third party services should be less cumbersome for an SME than for larger, less agile companies. Also, since functional activities are not organized and managed separately by di<sup>V</sup>erent teams of personnel in most SMEs, we expected Golf Masters to have an advantage when trying to apply an integrated e-business strategy through application of web services.

## 3. Methodology

To complete this research, a pluralist methodology (Mingers, 2001) is used with action research serving as the primary approach (Lewin, 1947a,b), supplemented by case analysis methods. Information systems studies using action research methods are growing (Avison et al., 1999; Baskerville and Wood-Harper, 1996; Checkland, 1981; Hult and Lennung, 1980) and several di<sup>V</sup>erent approaches to action research have emerged recently (Davison et al., 2004; Iversen et al., 2004; Susman and Evered, 1978). In this study, an approach similar to the collaborative practice research (CPR) method (Iversen et al., 2004) was used, where the researchers and organizational participants worked closely together to identify new ways to support and improve business and technology processes. This approach involves completion of several iterative steps during the implementation phase, which helps reduce the risks of a study becoming uncontrollable and too subjective (Kock, 2004). Table 1, an adaptation from Iversen et al. (2004), shows a comparison of prior studies employing CPR and includes an outline of steps followed in this study.

To avoid other pitfalls frequently associated with action research, including lack of researcher impartiality, lack of discipline, conversion of research project into a consulting project and inability to generalize <sup>W</sup>ndings (Baskerville and Wood-Harper, 1996; Iversen et al., 2004), we adopted similar guiding principles as those used by Iversen et al. (2004).

Speci<sup>W</sup>cally, we identi<sup>W</sup>ed clear roles for the two researchers and the two managers, documented data collection e<sup>V</sup>orts at each step, identi<sup>W</sup>ed criteria for selecting systems options for implementation and regularly checked to ensure that implementation strategies were theoretically defensible.

Formal interviewing techniques consistent with case study methods (Yin, 2003) were applied in the initial stages of this research to collect data regarding the business problem. Initial data were collected in a series of formal interviews and informal working sessions over a period of approximately 5 weeks. Data collection e<sup>V</sup>orts were organized into two distinct phases. During the <sup>W</sup>rst phase, information was collected regarding existing business processes, perceived competitive threats faced by the company and the technical capabilities of the owner and sta<sup>V</sup>. An analysis of the company’s strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats (SWOT) relative to the large retailer was performed. The results of this analysis were in turn used to sharpen their business strategy and to consider the potential strategic value of a variety of di<sup>V</sup>erent business-enhancing IT investments. This phase resulted in an understanding of the organization’s existing structure, core business processes and lines of business, and management’s perceived and actual technical capabilities.

The second (iterative) phase focused on presenting a series of business strategies and technical options to the owner’s for consideration of varying technical requirements, necessary modi<sup>W</sup>cations to core business processes and short term versus long term costs. A number of strategies were initially presented to management including automation of accounting and sales systems, inventory management systems, customer relationship management systems and applications for enhancing management of service operations such as scheduling golf lessons with 10–12 golf professionals and scheduling equipment maintenance. Various levels of sophistication and degrees of integration of these strategies were presented to the owner along with expected costs and bene<sup>W</sup>ts and expected ROIs for each option. After several iterations in this phase of the CPR process, a suitable set of strategies were identi<sup>W</sup>ed and implementation plans were made.

The third (closing) phase involved assessing the e<sup>Y</sup>cacy of the selected strategies over the <sup>W</sup>rst year and identifying where the greatest value was being created. Comparison of Golf Masters’ new strategies to competitor strategies were conducted to identify new strategic strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats relative to the larger competitor as well as to consider how lessons learned from Golf Masters’ strategies might be generalized to other businesses.

To establish the quality of any empirical research e<sup>V</sup>ort, tests of a study’s construct validity, internal validity, external validity and reliability are commonly used (Yin, 2003). In qualitative research studies, construct validity can be established through a variety of means including establishing a chain of events and having key informants review study <sup>W</sup>ndings (Yin, 2003). The smallness of the company made it relatively easy to establish the chain of events. The only operational changes at Golf Masters during the time of this research were the ones described in this report. In addition, the owner of Golf Masters was asked to review this paper for fact accuracy. Establishing internal validity in qualitative studies generally means considering whether there are alternative explanations for the phenomena studied (Yin, 2003). Since there were no operational changes other than those described in this report during the time of the analysis, we feel sure that there are not alternative explanations for the results described. External validity means establishing whether the study’s <sup>W</sup>ndings are generalizable beyond the current analysis (Yin, 2003). While we do not attempt to replicate this study in other settings, we do provide speci<sup>W</sup>c details regarding the tools employed and how they were used so that replication of the study by others is possible. We also present arguments for why we think the results are generalizable to other SMEs. Finally, reliability means establishing whether another researcher conducting the exact same study would conclude the exact same results (Yin, 2003). While this is an academic argument since it would be impossible for another scholar to implement the same technologies at Golf Masters, the provision of speci<sup>W</sup>c details regarding the technologies employed is useful for providing assurances of reliability in addition to providing opportunities for determining external validity.

## 4. Results from the initial phase

The results of the SWOT analysis revealed that, as with many small to medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), Golf Masters’ key weaknesses relative to the retail giant included a limited IT budget, a sta<sup>V</sup> with few technical skills and higher inventory costs since purchase discounts from suppliers are based on the volume of the purchase. Key relative strengths were the sta<sup>V</sup>’s domain expertise, the owner’s willingness to champion new technology, Golf Masters’ strong reputation in the local community, including long-standing relationships with customers, and the organizational <sup>X</sup>exibility that comes with being unencumbered by complex infrastructure and bureaucracy.

The most serious threat was potential loss of the company through declining market share and pro<sup>W</sup>t margins. Traditional golf product distributors provide discounts on volume purchases. Golf Masters purchased enough goods to receive modest discounts, but even a small loss of their existing market share could lead to lower discounts on future purchases. If regular customers were lured to the large retailer with the promise of lower prices, Golf Masters risked lower pro<sup>W</sup>t margins.

## 5. Results from the iterative phase

Strategic opportunities for Golf Masters included capitalizing on customer relations and exploring opportunities for improving pro<sup>W</sup>t margins. Investing heavily in IT activities or specialized technical sta<sup>V</sup> was not an appropriate option, consequently the team decided to explore and use existing web-based technology wherever possible including use of nontraditional electronic markets for product acquisition.

IT-centric strategies were employed to address both of these competitive opportunities. First, a simple, web-based customer relationship management (CRM) system was employed to track customer information such as purchases, past services, preferences and inquiries. This investment now enables the managers of Golf Masters to build on one of their strengths, strong customer relationships, at a relatively low cost. This was a relative strategic opportunity as it is more di<sup>Y</sup>cult for large chain stores to deliver highly customized service to each customer.

The second IT-centric strategy was to employ readily available third party web services to improve Golf Masters’ product acquisition and sales strategies. The Golf Masters team decided that web services could provide an appropriate IT strategy for reducing inventory costs through an e-business solution. Web services were also deemed appropriate for Golf Masters because the third party solutions identi<sup>W</sup>ed addressed their business needs and the applications, once in place, would not require highly technical personnel to operate and maintain. Thus, the third party web services were considered a good <sup>W</sup>t with Golf Masters overall organizational strategy. In addition, the team decided to employ the third party web services in unconventional ways to quickly and e<sup>Y</sup>ciently identify and acquire streams of lower-priced, new golf products to supplement the inventory acquired directly from manufacturers. Speci<sup>W</sup>cally, Golf Masters’ employed a combination of data mining, automated bidding and online auction tools designed to support selling products to acquire products at signi<sup>W</sup>cant discounts over manufacturer-direct pricing. As a result, Golf Masters increased their sales margins on those products and e<sup>V</sup>ectively by-passed the in<sup>X</sup>exible price controls levied by traditional suppliers (i.e., hierarchical discount programs based on purchase volume only). In the following sections we discuss details of their strategy and <sup>W</sup>rst year results.

## 5.1. Product acquisition strategy and related issues

As part of their strategy to increase sales margins, Golf Masters wanted to explore possible web-based alternatives for acquiring golf products. The owners of Golf Masters have purchased goods from eBay for personal use and were aware of the availability of new golf products at signi<sup>W</sup>cantly discounted prices. Thus, a closer analysis of several specialized and general online auction sites was conducted. The results indicated that a large volume of new golf products are available through a variety of online auctions such as The Golf Club Exchange (www.golfexchange.com), 3-Balls Golf (www.3balls.com) and eBay (www.ebay.com). Given a suitable management system, Golf Masters decided that these online auction sites could be used as alternative sources of merchandise. The challenge was to identify systematic approaches for acquiring the right goods at prices below the manufacturer wholesale prices already available to the owner. This product acquisition challenge can be broken down into three more speci<sup>W</sup>c technical challenges: identifying suitable product lines, identifying speci<sup>W</sup>c product items, and then acquiring identi<sup>W</sup>ed items. Identifying long-term auction patterns of potentially suitable product lines is an important <sup>W</sup>rst step to ensure a consistent supply of products over time. Final auction prices for certain brands of golf clubs can vary as much as 50% throughout the year and the number of items available for auction can also vary considerably from season to season. In order to identify product lines which can be included in the purchasing process, each candidate product line sold by Golf Masters is analyzed to determine if there is su<sup>Y-</sup> cient quantity of product available at auction which can be won at a price signi<sup>W</sup>cantly discounted from manufacturer prices. Key information necessary to qualify a product line includes average number of items available for auction, average <sup>W</sup>nal auction price, and percentage of auctions which are completed. Generating this information requires access to historical auction data. However, access to auction information is usually limited to current or just completed auctions and historical data for auction items is not usually available. eBay, for example, stores 30 days of auction data in its live database and the previous 30 days in another accessible online database, providing at most 60 days worth of auction information. Attempting to determine the annual auction pattern for a product is di<sup>Y</sup>cult using conventional auction tools as there is at most 60 days worth of data available at any one time.

Once product lines have been quali<sup>W</sup>ed, identifying speci<sup>W</sup>c products available at auctions that meet Golf Masters’ purchasing criteria must be identi<sup>W</sup>ed. Determining how many items of a certain type are for sale at any given point in time is often di<sup>Y</sup>cult. The primary user-interface for most auction sites is a browser for searching on auction title key words supplied by the seller. Since auction titles are supplied by the seller they are not standardized, making it impossible to identify products in any consistent or systematic fashion. Searching for speci<sup>W</sup>c items in high-volume categories such as golf clubs poses speci<sup>W</sup>c problems. Popular golf club brands might have as many 80 auctions closing per day. Given that auctions are typically 7 days long, a particular golf club brand might have over 500 active auctions. Searching for speci<sup>W</sup>c products then requires hit-or-miss keyword searches and manual paging through many screens of auction items. Moreover, a successful acquisition strategy requires identifying a subset of available auctions which will provide the greatest success rate and lowest average auction price while ensuring a certain proportion of auctions are won. E<sup>V</sup>ectively managing this process requires the buyer to predict the expected <sup>W</sup>nal price of each auction with some degree of accuracy and then <sup>W</sup>lter out those auctions which provide the lowest chance of winning.

The last challenge is to identify an e<sup>V</sup>ective, systematic mechanism for acquiring auction items. In the traditional auction model, bidding processes require a signi<sup>W</sup>cant degree of user interaction; a user manually enters and re-enters bids whenever current auction price exceeds their last bid which is both time consuming and ine<sup>Y</sup>cient. Bidding strategies are also important. Early and frequent bidding is shown to result in higher average <sup>W</sup>nal auction prices and is, therefore, counterproductive. Experienced auction bidders wait until the last few seconds to place a bid thereby reducing the likelihood of being outbid. This process, called “auction sniping”, is perfectly legal but often incurs the ire of traditional bidders. Within a retail business framework, attempting to micro-manage many simultaneous auctions is prohibitively time-consuming and impractical; many auctions end on evenings, weekends, and in di<sup>V</sup>erent time zones, requiring some level of attention to the bidding process 24 h a day, 7 days a week. To this end, an automated means of bidding is far better for business use of auction sites.

## 5.2. eBay

eBay provides a rich set of web services-based application programming interfaces (APIs), allowing pre-quali<sup>W</sup>ed applications access to their internal data over the Internet. Access to eBay’s API is via SSH using XML for encoding data. A full description of the API can be downloaded from www.ebay.com. The following is a detailed explanation of the third party web services which use eBay’s API to provide new functionality to individual consumers and organizations such as Golf Masters.

## 5.3. Online auction data mining

Building a data mining application can be expensive; however, several third party providers are now o<sup>V</sup>ering data mining applications and tools at low cost, providing SMEs with insights otherwise available only to large companies. The data mining tool selected by Golf Masters, Andale (www.andale.com), is designed to be an eBay sales assistance tool but the Golf Masters team discovered that the tool also has very powerful search and analysis capabilities allowing it to be used in a variety of innovative ways. Golf Masters uses Andale’s data mining tools to perform initial product quali<sup>W</sup>cation and product identi<sup>W</sup>cation.

To initially qualify product lines, Golf Masters uses Andales’ online eBay data miner application to analyze auction patterns of each selected product line over a year. The data miner queries historical auction data previously cached from eBay’s databases, providing average <sup>W</sup>nal auction price by month, percentage of auctions which result in a sale by month and average number of items for sale each month for each product (Fig. 1).

The data mining results are used to determine if a product line is consistently available from the auction at a price signi<sup>W</sup>cantly below direct manufacturer prices, to ensure that a su<sup>Y</sup>cient volume of each product is potentially available in steady supply, and to determine the best acquisition strategy for seasonal products. The average auction price of seasonal products vary greatly over the course of a year, consequently a higher volume of a seasonal product can often be acquired at a lower price and stored until needed.

Example results of a product quali<sup>W</sup>cation query using Andale’s tools are shown in Fig. 2.

Once a product line has been quali<sup>W</sup>ed for the purchasing strategy, the next step is to develop a means whereby items can be acquired e<sup>V</sup>ectively and e<sup>Y</sup>ciently. The online data miner is also used for this process but queries live data rather than historical data from the auction site (Fig. 3).

## 5.4. Automated bidding

Once speci<sup>W</sup>c auctions have been identi<sup>W</sup>ed by the data miner Golf Masters has to be able to bid on them in a manner which is both manageable and e<sup>Y</sup>cient. Golf Masters elected to use an automated online bidding tool, Auction Sniper, to manage the bidding process by proxy. Auction Sniper automatically manages the bidding process by placing bids on speci<sup>W</sup>ed auctions based on parameters set by Golf Masters’ managers. Auction Sniper is web-based and, similar to the data miner application, integrates into eBay’s internal data via eBay’s web services-based API. The bidding tool accesses Golf Masters’ eBay account, again via eBay’s web-service API, and loads pre-selected items from the watch list into the automated bidder application. The user enters maximum bid prices for the auction item and sets a bidding lead time which is the time before the end of the auction at which the bid will be placed (Fig. 5). An illustration of the watch list interface is shown in Fig. 6. Maximum bid price is de<sup>W</sup>ned using the same 80% rule as in the product identi<sup>W</sup>cation search procedure.

Once an auction price and lead time is set, the automated bidder places bids on behalf of Golf Masters through eBay’s web services interface. If the lead time is extremely small (<5 s) there is more likelihood of winning an auction but an increased chance that network congestion or busy eBay servers will cause a delay in the bid being processed, possibly resulting in a missed opportunity. Conversely, a longer lead time increases the chance that a bid will be placed but also increases the risk of being outbid by other proxy or traditional bidders. Through trial and error, Golf Masters settled on a 10 s lead time, which in turn is yielding wins in approximately 30% of bids.

## 6. Closing phase and results

In the <sup>W</sup>rst year, Golf Masters’ product acquisition exceeded expectations. Concentrating on 10–12 product lines from four major golf manufacturers, the company managed to purchase a steady supply of new golf products from eBay at 20–30% below manufacturerdirect wholesale prices. By using a mix of one-third to one-<sup>W</sup>fth of auctioned product to manufacturer direct products, Golf Masters now sells sets of golf clubs at an average cost of 5–10% below their competitor without decreasing sales margins or violating manufacturer’s pricing controls.

Golf club sales are cyclical with the highest volume of sales in early spring and the lowest volume of sales in winter. Average auction prices follow a typical demand curve with higher prices during the spring and lower prices during fall and winter. Accordingly, Golf Masters has adopted a pro<sup>W</sup>t maximizing strategy where the majority of products are acquired during the o<sup>V</sup> season when prices are at their lowest and stored until the sales start to pick up in the spring. While such inventory management strategies are expensive for large companies and are particularly problematic for retail organizations carrying bulky and/or low turnover products, this strategy suits Golf Masters very well as golf clubs are a relatively high-pro<sup>W</sup>t and physically compact item that can be easily stored until needed.

The IT investment necessary to implement this strategy was negligible requiring little more than an Internet connection and small investment in time for training. Amortized costs amount to just a few dollars per successful transaction while average realized savings range between \$100 and \$200 per transaction. The online data mining application costs less than \$5 per month and places no restriction on the number of searches or data mining queries placed. The automated bidding process is priced on a per-win basis costing 1% of the <sup>W</sup>nal sale price up to a maximum cost of \$5. Only bids resulting in wins are charged. Costs of managing the process are also surprisingly small; Golf Masters’ sta<sup>V</sup> can manage a day’s activity, including processing any wins from the previous day, in about 30 min and these operations can be performed outside of normal store retail hours. Finally, while training costs are often signi<sup>W</sup>cant for implementation of new IT strategies, integrating this new process into Golf Masters’ inventory procurement processes took less than 2 days.

Golf Masters achieved a 30% increase in pro<sup>W</sup>ts from retail sales<sup>1</sup> within the <sup>W</sup>rst 12 months and anticipates a three-fold increase in pro<sup>W</sup>ts from retail sales over a 3–5 year period. Golf Masters’ owner believes that the driving force behind this increase in pro<sup>W</sup>ts was the decision to use third party web services to re-engineer their core inventory purchasing process. Since use of the web services lowered purchasing costs, Golf Masters was able to o<sup>V</sup>er some equipment at discounts below the retail prices o<sup>V</sup>ered by the large competitor. These lower priced items were then advertised locally and on their web site. The advertisements were e<sup>V</sup>ective in bringing increased tra<sup>Y</sup>c through the store, which in turn led to increased sales on other products as well. The sta<sup>V</sup> have made a conscious e<sup>V</sup>ort to leverage the new CRM system to provide personalized attention to new as well as old customers by keeping an integrated record of retail and service transactions, available for quick retrieval when customers return.

The new large retailer could not e<sup>V</sup>ectively use Golf Masters’ new product acquisition strategy because it is one retail outlet in a retail chain with centrally managed purchasing policies and processes. Hypothetically, if the local retail store reduced their prices on the same products to match Golf Masters’ new prices, they would have to take a loss on those products while Golf Masters could still receive a pro<sup>W</sup>t on those goods. Realistically, Golf Masters is not a threat to their viability, so it is not likely that they would use this strategy even if they could.

As the sta<sup>V</sup> gained experience using the purchase acquisition strategy they started to track sales of used equipment on the auction sites and eventually experimented with developing an alternate sales channel by taking trades of used equipment as part payment for new. The trades are re-conditioned in the service center and sold on eBay via a specialized eBay online store. This strategy was a direct outcome of a deeper understanding of auction patterns and <sup>W</sup>nal auction prices, as well as pricing and advertising strategies identi<sup>W</sup>ed using the data mining application. In order to increase their online competitiveness and return a higher pro<sup>W</sup>t on the trades, Golf Masters decided to make use of a core strength and excess capacity in their service center, and provide customization services for auction winners. This strategy allowed the company to elicit higher <sup>W</sup>nal auction prices and increase the average pro<sup>W</sup>t per trade. This strategy has the added advantage of extending the market reach of Golf Masters to national and inter-national customers. While the pilot proved highly successful, in practice this strategy has led to only modest gains in revenue as limited access to trade-ins have restricted the overall growth of the channel. Again, this opportunity is a direct result of <sup>W</sup>rst engaging in the new web services-based product acquisition strategy and utilizes the same data mining tools to determine pricing, auction timing and online presentation.

Ultimately, Golf Masters was able to leverage their small business strengths, including business agility and closer customer relations, by strategically aligning their investments in web services and related technologies with their carefully articulated business goals and capabilities. This strategic alignment allowed Golf Masters to derive <sup>W</sup>nancial bene<sup>W</sup>ts from their product acquisition strategy that far exceeded the <sup>W</sup>nancial bene<sup>W</sup>ts of reducing product costs alone. As a result, Golf Masters not only survived the arrival of a new competitor, but expanded their business.

## 7. Conclusions

This study extends the literatures on the resource based view of the <sup>W</sup>rm (Barney, 1991), on strategic use of web services and on the IT-enabled business strategies of SMEs by demonstrating that SMEs can use web services to e<sup>V</sup>ectively leverage other organizational resources such as agility and customer service. The <sup>X</sup>exibility of small business infrastructures like Golf Masters makes them ideally suited to adoption and use of available third party web services. Further, the accessibility of publicly available web services from large <sup>W</sup>rms like eBay makes it possible for small companies like Golf Masters to ‘partner’ with those <sup>W</sup>rms without needing to align and coordinate technological infrastructures.

At the beginning of this research e<sup>V</sup>ort, Golf Masters had a very simple sales transaction information system as their only information technology investment. Since the technological infrastructure of Golf Masters is far less complex than that of a large <sup>W</sup>rm, it was easier to isolate and measure the <sup>W</sup>nancial e<sup>V</sup>ects of systems enhancements than in studies involving larger, more complex organizations and serves to strengthen the validity of research <sup>W</sup>ndings.

Golf Masters’ new business strategy of purchasing products from indirect sources such as eBay to supplement direct purchases from manufacturers is e<sup>V</sup>ective for several reasons. First, a relatively large volume of new golf clubs are available through auction sites, due in part to the nature of the golf industry and the large volume of golf products made available but never used through promotional activities. Also, the level of technology skills necessary to manage the process is low, so it is appropriate for an SME with few IT resources (Auger and Gallaugher, 1997; Raymond, 1985).

Prior to initiating the new acquisition process Golf Masters’ sta<sup>V</sup> already had experience with online auctions and eBay in particular, so they understood general auction procedures and were familiar with the process of using sophisticated browser-based applications over the Internet. Extending the traditional consumer-focused auction model and developing a relatively high-volume product acquisition strategy, however, required familiarity and experimentation with more sophisticated analysis tools and third party web-services which Golf Masters sta<sup>V</sup> did not possess. Consistent with prior research (Gribbins and King, 2002), obtaining technical expertise from outside the company proved fruitful in helping Golf Masters consider the strategic value of new information technology options within the context of their business goals. However, once the tool-set was identi<sup>W</sup>ed and an overall process developed, minimal additional training was required to enable Golf Masters sta<sup>V</sup> to perform data mining functions, create new product searches and transition seamlessly from one online application to another. The owner was able to strategically leverage these investments in numerous ways once he was aware of the opportunities. In fact, the owner’s leadership played a key role in the successful deployment of web services strategy. This <sup>W</sup>nding is also consistent with research on successful integration of technology in large organizations (Armstrong and Sambamurthy, 1999; Chatterjee et al., 2002).

Research indicating that external pressures are often the source of a company’s decision to adopt an accelerated strategy for IT investment (Hackbarth and Kettinger, 2004) are also applicable here, as the competitive pressure caused by the large retailer moving into the same town was the primary impetus for Golf Masters’ decision to make the strategic investments in web services. All of these factors contributed to Golf Masters’ success in making the product acquisition process e<sup>Y</sup>cient and manageable and, therefore, sustainable.

## 8. Further research

Research on possible uses of web services by companies of all size is in early stages. This research illustrates how some web service applications may be strategically used by SMEs in ways that are potentially generalizable to other SMEs but are probably not generalizable to large organizations. Other research into web services has revealed strategic uses of web services by large organizations that may not be easy for SMEs to employ. Yet while speci<sup>W</sup>c strategies may be more or less appropriate based on organizational size, it may be noted that companies of all sizes are <sup>W</sup>nding web services to be an inexpensive tool to support closer collaboration and integration with multiple partners and suppliers across multiple systems. Additional theoretical research analyzing the relative bene<sup>W</sup>ts of large and small company use of web services is needed.

Additional research is also needed to determine the extent to which the results obtained by Golf Masters are repeatable by other SME retailers. We suggest that the business model employed by Golf Masters may work well for other SME retailers that sell commodity or semi-commodity products, but only additional research could con<sup>W</sup>rm this hypothesis.

There are a number of reasons why consumers of golf clubs may prefer to purchase their clubs from Golf Masters rather than from the large retail chain or from the auction site directly, despite the fact that golf clubs are a commodity product. Firstly, the fact that Golf Masters can now o<sup>V</sup>er a 5–10% discount over the large retailer on many items gives them a small local price advantage. Golf Masters also has in-house experts in club <sup>W</sup>tting and customization, so once the clubs are purchased, the clubs can be tailored to <sup>W</sup>t the individual. Such services are not usually o<sup>V</sup>ered by large retailers or by sellers at online auctions. Another reason why a customer may continue to prefer buying clubs from Golf Masters rather than going to the auction sites is that golf clubs, while commodity products, are relatively expensive, so many consumers may be reluctant to purchase their clubs from an auction site. Other consumers may be uninterested in investing the time to deal with the auction process while still others do not generally think of the Internet as a safe or viable option. However, further research would be necessary to consider the value derived by customers from SME use of web services. The continued rapid growth of physical eBay shops in Europe, Australia and the US is a testament to the number of people who still prefer buying and selling at a physical location to buying and selling items through an auction site. This leaves a number of opportunities for SMEs willing to invest in similar product acquisition processes as those employed by Golf Masters. Any related applied research may be useful to business owners as well as other applied research scholars.

Golf Masters employed one possible business model. The potential for research related to development of other innovative business models employing web services for SMEs seems virtually limitless. In any case, a key to the company’s success was the alignment between the web services solutions selected and the strategic needs of the organization.

There are several opportunities for more technical future research as well. Today, Golf Masters uses a simple rule-based approach in determining which items to bid on within a product line resulting in an average 30% of auctions being won. A more systematic understanding of the correlation between winning an auction, the auctions characteristics and product characteristics could increase the e<sup>V</sup>ectiveness of the bid process and result in lower average prices and better product quali<sup>W</sup>cation. The cost e<sup>V</sup>ectiveness of automated auction sniping also warrants further analysis as our experience indicates that their service is perhaps undervalued to organizations which use them to perform bulk bidding such as Golf Masters.

The success experienced by Golf Masters is predicated on a strategy of technology adoption rather than development, favoring use of existing low-cost third-party tools available online. Today, the number and type of such third-party applications which can be adopted by SMEs is increasing as companies such as Salesforce.com, SAP, Microsoft and Oracle are providing web-based access to a variety of high-value hosted applications, allowing businesses of all sizes access to sophisticated business applications without the overhead of developing or managing the technical aspects of their deployment.
