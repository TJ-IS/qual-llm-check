# Why do we trust new technology? A study of initial trust formation with organizational information systems

Xin Li <sup>a,\*</sup>, Traci J. Hess <sup>b</sup>, Joseph S. Valacich <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Accounting and Information Technology, University of North Carolina at Pembroke, P.O. Box 1510, Pembroke, NC 28372, United States <sup>b</sup> Department of Information Systems, Washington State University, P.O. Box 644743, Pullman, WA 99164-4743, United States

Received 27 January 2007; received in revised form 15 January 2008; accepted 16 January 2008 Available online 5 March 2008

## Abstract

Recent trust research in the information systems (IS) field has described trust as a primary predictor of technology usage and a fundamental construct for understanding user perceptions of technology. Initial trust formation is particularly relevant in an IS context, as users must overcome perceptions of risk and uncertainty before using a novel technology. With initial trust in a more complex, organizational information system, there are a number of external determinants, trusting bases, that may explain trust formation and provide organizations with the needed levers to form or change individuals’ initial trust in technology. In this study, a research model of initial trust formation is developed and includes trusting bases, trusting beliefs, trusting attitude and subjective norm, and trusting intentions. Eight trusting base factors are assessed including personality, cognitive, calculative, and both technology and organizational factors of the institutional base. The model is empirically tested with 443 subjects in the context of initial trust in a national identity system (NID). The proposed model was supported and the results indicate that subjective norm and the cognitive–reputation, calculative, and organizational situational normality base factors significantly influence initial trusting beliefs and other downstream trust constructs. Factors from some of the more commonly investigated bases, personality and technology institutional, did not significantly afect trusting beliefs. The findings have strategic implications for agencies implementing e-government systems and organizational information systems in general.

Keywords: Initial trust; Technology adoption; Trusting intention; Trusting beliefs; Subjective norm; Trusting attitude; Trusting bases; e-Government; Organizational information systems; National identity systems

## 1. Introduction

Recent information systems research indicates that trust plays an important role in helping users overcome perceptions of risk and uncertainty in the use and acceptance of new technology (Gefen et al., 2003; Pavlou and Gefen, 2004). Numerous studies have investigated the influence of trust on perceptions and/or use of technology in various contexts including eCommerce (Jarvenpaa and Todd, 1997; McKnight et al., 2002a; Pavlou, 2003), online marketplaces (Pavlou and Gefen, 2004), and recommendation agents (Komiak and Benbasat, 2006). This research suggests that understanding how initial trust toward an information system is formed is essential for promoting the adoption of a new system.

Research on technology adoption and innovation has noted that the determinants of initial adoption difer from the determinants of continued use (Agarwal and Prasad, 1997; Chin and Marcolin, 2001; Karahanna et al., 1999). The adoption literature suggests a strong relationship between users’ pre-implementation expectations of a new system and post-implementation experiences with the system (Ginzberg, 1981; Staples et al., 2002). From a difusion of innovations perspective, users initially seek or are provided with information about an innovation which forms their attitude toward the innovation and subsequent adoption decision (Rogers, 1995). The decision to readily adopt a new technology is influenced by users’ initial perceptions of the technology characteristics (Moore and Benbasat, 1991; Rogers, 1983), and users ‘‘will be less likely to experiment with new technologies if they perceive a significant risk associated with such exploration. . .” (Agarwal and Prasad, 1997). This initial exposure to a system is also said to be a time of importance as this is when adaptation of the system and user learning is most likely to occur (Tyre and Orlikowski, 1994; Weick, 1990). In the context of organizational systems, adoption research has also found that in the early stages of technology adoption, indirect information such as perceptions of the organizational sponsor, organizational structure, and social influence will have the greatest influence on adoption decisions (Gallivan, 2001).

This perspective on initial adoption of technology aligns with research on initial trust. While trust is a dynamic concept that develops over time, researchers have noted the importance of studying initial trust, especially in cases of novel technology where users must overcome perceptions of risk and uncertainty before using the technology (McKnight et al., 2002b; Wang and Benbasat, 2005). Trust research in organizational contexts has demonstrated that individuals form trusting beliefs prior to having first-hand experience with another party and that high levels of initial trust exist (Berg et al., 1995; Kramer, 1994) and thus may be cultivated. The determinants of initial trust are believed to difer from the determinants of trust with a more familiar party (Gefen, 2004; McKnight et al., 2002a), as individuals do not have direct experiences to draw upon in forming initial trusting beliefs. Research on initial trust is also relevant to later trust with a familiar party as all trusting relationships have a starting point (a beginning), and influential events often occur at the beginning of a relationship that may afect the continuance and/or later phases of a relationship (McKnight and Chervany, 2006). In the context of IS trust, organizations have an opportunity to create a positive, first impression of a new system and generate high levels of trust with users before they interact with the system.

In order for organizations to take advantage of the opportunity to create a trustworthy first impression of a new system and encourage adoption, the relevant determinants of trusting behaviors in the context of initial IS use need to be studied. McKnight et al. (2002a) developed and tested a model of initial trust in an IS context based on the theory of reasoned action (TRA) (Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980). Their model included two trusting bases as external determinants (personality and institutional), trusting beliefs, and trusting intentions. This model provides a parsimonious, essential starting point for studies of initial IS trust.

Ajzen and Fishbein (1980) suggest that to better understand a behavior or the intention to perform a behavior, it is important to develop a thorough understanding of the underlying external variables in the behavioral context. The organizational trust literature suggests several theoretically-grounded trusting bases, such as cognitive, calculative, institutional, and personality, that may influence initial trusting beliefs (Mayer et al., 1995; Rousseau et al., 1998). Studies of IS-related trust have investigated only a subset of these trusting bases, and the object of trust in these studies (the trustee) has ranged from virtual team members to web vendors and online sellers. The operationalization of trusting beliefs and intentions has also varied across IS trust studies, and recent empirical research provides evidence that more parsimonious, one-dimensional representations may produce erroneous results (Serva et al., 2005). While many advances have been made in IS-related trust, there is still much to be learned about the determinants of initial trust in information systems.

The objective of this research is to provide insight on how to build trust in new technology prior to actual use of the technology. An empirical investigation on the formation of initial trust toward an organizational information system is conducted using a research model of trusting bases, trusting beliefs, trusting attitudes and subjective norm, and trusting intentions. Eight trusting base factors are assessed including personality, cognitive, calculative, and both technology and organizational factors of the institutional base. The model is tested with survey data collected in the context of a national identity (NID) system; the findings provide insights to organizations implementing or updating information systems.

An NID system was selected as the context for the study due to its organizational scale, complexity, and the lack of an NID system within the United States (U.S.) where the study took place. An NID system is a large-scale, public information system used at the governmental-level to facilitate public services, enhance national security, and guard against illegal immigrants (Clement et al., 2001). Many European and Asian countries already utilize such systems and advanced user features such as online access to personal data, other government services, and electronic funds transfer with NID cards are often provided (European eGovernment Services, 2005; Finnish Population RC, 2005; Hong Kong Smart ID GIC, 2004). An NID system has been advocated in the U.S. for several decades, but opposition to such a system has remained strong. Campaigns against NID systems have capitalized on citizens’ fear and distrust of such systems (Burns, 2001; Dority, 2002) and have played a part in preventing the adoption of NID systems. In the specific context of NID systems, an understanding of the external factors that influence citizens’ initial trust in these systems will be of assistance to government agencies implementing e-government systems. In a general context, an understanding of these determinants of initial trust will be insightful for organizations implementing new, complex systems.

The paper is organized as follows: the theoretical foundation, research model, and hypotheses are first presented, followed by a discussion of the research methodology. The data analysis is then described, and a discussion of the results is provided. Lastly, the conclusion, limitations, and directions for future research are discussed.

## 2. Theoretical foundation and model development

Trust is a broad and multi-faceted concept that has been widely studied in many disciplines. In this study, we adopt Mayer et al.’s (1995) definition of trust, which emphasizes the common characteristics of trust across diferent disciplines and all trusting situations (Johnson-George and Swap, 1982) and implies diferent components of trust (Brown et al., 2004):

Trust refers to ‘‘the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party (p. 712).”

Empirical evidence shows that the level of trust does not necessarily develop gradually over time (Berg et al., 1995; Kramer, 1994). Instead, trust at diferent stages is formed based on diferent factors and processes (McKnight et al., 1998). In this research, we focus on the initial stage of trust and the factors that influence initial trust in an organizational information systems context. We will generally refer to the party who grants trust as the trustor and the party that receives trust as the trustee. The initial trust model developed and tested in this research is shown in Fig. 1. This model consists of four levels of constructs: (1) eight trusting base factors, which are the external variables that form beliefs, (2) trusting beliefs, (3) trusting attitudes and subjective norm, and (4) trusting intention.

## 2.1. Initial trust

McKnight et al. (2002a, 1998) defined initial trust as trust in an unfamiliar party. When the trustor has no prior interaction with a trustee, he/she cannot develop trust based on direct experience with or first-hand knowledge of the trustee. Instead, the trustor will depend on other sources, such as second-hand information, contextual factors, or personal intuition to make trust inferences. For example, before having direct interaction with an information system in a specific context, a trustor can build initial trust in this system based on their experiences with other systems, their knowledge about this system used in other contexts, and/or others’ opinions about the system. McKnight et al. (1998) emphasize that trust in diferent stages is built on diferent factors. Therefore, a detailed investigation of the initial trust circumstance as well as the factors involved in initial trust formation will enhance our understanding of why people initially trust in the context of information systems.

## 2.2. Trust in technology

Trust research was initially advanced in the context of interpersonal relationships, where the trustee was a human being. Most IS trust research has focused on a web vendor or virtual team members and thus the trustee has been human, or an organization of humans. More recent IS research has discussed trust in technology where the trustee is a technological artifact such as a recommendation agent or an information system (Corritore et al., 2003; McKnight, 2005; Wang and Benbasat, 2005). Trust in this context has been defined similarly to interpersonal trust in that it reflects the willingness of the trustor to ‘‘behaviorally depend on a piece of software (e.g., a statistical system) to do a task. . .” (McKnight, 2005). Models of trust and technology acceptance have been applied to the relationship between humans and technology in the context of recommendation agents (Komiak and Benbasat, 2006; Wang and Benbasat, 2005). The empirical results support the nomological validity of applying such models to technology, with significant loadings for the three dimensions of trusting beliefs (benevolence, competence, and integrity) and replications of the significant relationships among trusting beliefs and technology acceptance constructs (Wang and Benbasat, 2005).

Research in the Computers are Social Actors (CSA) paradigm provides a rich foundation for applying trust and other social responses to technology. In a series of laboratory studies summarized in Nass and Moon (2000), individuals were found to respond to computers in a social manner despite acknowledging that the computers were in no way human. The subjects in these studies responded with social behaviors such as politeness, stereotyping, and similarity preference to their interactions with computers. While the recommendation agent trust studies described above utilized anthropomorphic interfaces with computer-generated speech and animation, many of the CSA studies obtained social responses to technology using simple, text-based interfaces. Humans tend to anthropomorphize technology even in the absence of more human-like features such as voice and embodiment through (Nass et al., 1995). These studies suggest that trust in an information system is a viable concept (McKnight, 2005) and that a model of initial trust formation should be applicable to technology.

## 2.3. Trusting bases

In this research, we use trusting bases, described in existing literature within sociology, psychology, management, and information systems research, as the determinants of trusting beliefs and subsequently trusting attitude and trusting intention in an information systems context. Prior trust research in various disciplines has suggested five major categories of trusting bases with sub-components that provide a foundation to general trust formation. The five bases are personality base, cognitive base, calculative base, institutional base, and knowledge base (Kramer, 1999; Lewis and Weigert, 1985; Mayer et al., 1995; McKnight et al., 1998; Rousseau et al., 1998; Williamson, 1993). While the knowledge trusting base has been shown to influence trust, it is excluded from our research model because in an initial trust context no direct knowledge of or experiential interaction with an unfamiliar trustee is available (McKnight and Chervany, 2006; McKnight et al., 1998). In this study of initial trust, we investigate a total of eight distinct antecedents to trusting beliefs based on the four general categories of bases, as shown in Fig. 1, and refer to these antecedents as trusting bases. We acknowledge that these bases might not be an exclusive or exhaustive consideration of the foundations to initial trust.

In conceptualizing these bases, we work within a theoretically grounded framework of initial trust (McKnight et al., 2002a) and operationalize these bases in a general context as recommended in prior trust research (McKnight and Chervany, 2006). More specific and technical attributes such as customer service, digital certificates, authentication, and product characteristics (Jarvenpaa and Todd, 1997; Kim et al., 2005), would serve as determinants or instances of these general bases. This approach makes it easier to generalize our findings to other information systems, and provides a framework for categorizing context-specific IS attributes.

Existing research on trust in information systems has examined some of the trusting bases individually, but has not investigated this set of external determinants simultaneously in an efort to understand the relative influences of these bases. For example, McKnight et al. (2002a) examined personality and institutional trusting base factors in an eCommerce context, but did not include cognitive and calculative bases. Table 1 summarizes prior IS studies on the various trusting bases and their empirical findings. As shown, most of these studies investigated only 2–4 trusting base factors, and many other IS trust studies have not assessed these external determinants of trusting beliefs and thus are not included in the table. The trustee and context has also varied greatly, and in many studies the full measurement scales (with multiple dimensions) for constructs such trusting beliefs, intentions, and some trusting bases, were not used.

The current study investigates eight trusting base factors applicable to initial IS trust within a more complete model of antecedents, beliefs, attitudes and subjective norms, and intentions. Our objective is to develop a trust model that accurately explains the formation of initial trust in an organizational information systems context, and to investigate the relative influences of these trusting bases within this more complete theoretical model with an IS trustee. We now describe the eight trusting bases and the expected relationships with trusting beliefs.

## 2.3.1. Personality trusting base

Trust research in psychology suggests that individuals difer in their general tendency to be willing to depend on others across a broad spectrum of situations and persons/objects (Erikson, 1968; Rotter, 1967, 1971, 1980). This tendency is formed by a person’s lifelong experiences with important referents and normally remains stable over time (Rotter, 1971). It influences how much trust one has for others prior to any interaction (Mayer et al., 1995) and is most influential in the initial phases of a relationship (Gefen, 2000; Rotter, 1971). The personality trusting base has also been referred to as one’s propensity to trust (Mayer et al., 1995) and disposition to trust (McKnight et al., 2002a). Prior research distinguishes two subcomponents of the general personality trusting base (Brown et al., 2004; McKnight et al., 1998), faith in humanity and trusting stance. These two subcomponents have been shown to have distinct empirical properties (McKnight et al., 2002a) and are treated as two separate constructs in our model to better understand their individual efects on trusting beliefs.

Faith in humanity (Wrightsman, 1991) refers to people’s beliefs about general human nature (McKnight et al., 1998). As with trusting beliefs, benevolence, competence, and integrity have been identified as the primary dimensions of this individual difference construct (McK night et al. 2002a). A trustor with strong faith in humanity is more likely to trust others in general. This trusting base is more salient in a novel situation in which less or no situational information is available, because in this situation people’s basic beliefs in human nature are more dominant in forming their initial trusting beliefs (Mullins and Cummings, 1999; Rotter, 1971; Wrightsman, 1991). In this research, we proposed that faith in humanity can be generalized to people’s perceptions of nonhuman objects, such as an information system. People with strong faith in humanity also have faith in the nature of an information system that was designed, developed, and/or acquired by people. For example, with strong faith in humanity, an end user of a novel information system tends to believe that the organization implements the system because it can improve work efectiveness and user satisfaction. When there is no direct knowledge of or experiential interaction with the system available, users with strong faith in humanity are more likely to form positive trusting beliefs towards the system in general.

## H1: Faith in humanity positively afects trusting beliefs.

Trusting stance, the other type of personality base (Cummings and Bromiley, 1996; Deutsch, 1958; Rosenberg, 1980), refers to people’s belief that better outcomes will result from dealing with trustees as though they are well meaning and reliable, regardless of the trustees’ real attributes (Riker, 1971). A trustor with a positive trusting stance will choose to initially trust every trustee in general because he/she believes that initial trust will lead to a friendly relationship or reciprocal trust from the other party, which will then lead to a positive result (Deutsch, 1958). Although the trustor may change this trusting strategy later with increasing information about the trustee or the trust situation, trusting stance has a great impact on his/her initial trusting beliefs before the other information is available. This trusting strategy can also be extended to a nonhuman object such as an information system. For example, end users may be inclined to trust a novel system such as an NID system, until pitfalls or misuses are found, as full use of the system should yield greater benefits than a more cautious, piecemeal approach to adoption. Users with a positive trusting stance are more likely to develop positive trusting beliefs towards the system initially before any experiential interaction with the system is available.

H2: Trusting stance positively afects trusting beliefs.

## 2.3.2. Cognitive trusting base

Social psychologists see trust as a cognitive process and suggest that various cognitive cues and impressions will afect how people form their trust (Brewer, 1981). Trust involves a degree of cognitive familiarity with the trustee that is somewhere in between total knowledge and total ignorance (Simmel, 1978). When people lack direct information and repeated experience with the trustee, they will establish their cognitive familiarity based on second-hand knowledge, impressions, cognitive cues, and cognitive processes. The degree of cognitive familiarity determines their initial trusting beliefs towards the trustee (Gefen, 2000). From this perspective, the cognitive trusting base is a substitute for the knowledge trusting base when dealing with an unfamiliar trustee.

Prior research on the cognitive trusting base suggests that a trustor may categorize an unfamiliar trustee as trustworthy or untrustworthy based on the reputation of the trustee. The reputation categorization process infers that a trustee with a good reputation is believed to be trustworthy (McKnight et al., 1998). When no direct experiential information is available, the trustee’s reputation may afect people’s beliefs about the trustee’s competence, benevolence, or integrity (Barber, 1983; Dasgupta, 1988; Powell, 1996). Thus, reputation is considered an important subcomponent of the cognitive trusting base.

Prior trust research has explored other subcomponents of the cognitive trusting base, such as unit grouping and stereotyping that are applicable in an interpersonal context. With the categorization process based on unit grouping, people tend to trust individuals who share common goals and values with them (e.g., people within the same organization or the same team) (Brewer and Silver, 1978; Kramer et al., 1996). People also exhibit diferent trusting tendencies based on positive or negative stereotypes that are formed when they initially meet someone, such as gender and occupations (Orbell et al., 1994), or physical appearance (Dion et al., 1972; Riker, 1971). These two components are not readily adaptable to the current research context as we are investigating users’ trust prior to their having any observation of the system.

In this research, when people have no direct knowledge of or experiential interaction with an information system, they may rely on the substitute of cognitive familiarity (Gefen, 2000) and make their trust inferences by categorizing based on reputation. For example, people may be influenced by the reported opinions of oth ers who have used an NID system in another country and develop their initial trusting beliefs accordingly.

H3: Reputation positively afects trusting beliefs.

## 2.3.3. Calculative trusting base

Transaction cost economists posit that rational people will perform trust-related behaviors according to economic principles (Williamson, 1993). A key assumption of this view is that rational individuals act in their own self-interest (Lewicki and Bunker, 1995). When making a decision to trust an unfamiliar trustee or a familiar trustee in a new situation, the trustor will generally assume that the trustees are rational and calculative and that the trustees will act in their own best interests. The trustor also assumes that these rational trustees will not engage in opportunistic behavior when it is not worthwhile (Shapiro et al., 1992). Therefore people tend to trust when the trustee is thought to have nothing to gain, or the cost overwhelms the benefit, from being untrustworthy.

While calculative trust is typically applied in volitional interpersonal or inter-organizational trust, recent research also generalizes it to trust relationships involving non-volitional trustees, such as a product (Wang and Emurian, 2005), an electronic agent (Jones, 2002), an eCommerce web site (Gefen et al., 2003), or a technology (e.g., the Internet) (Marcella, 1999). Although these trustees do not have their own volitions to choose how to perform in a trusting relationship, they are normally controlled by a volitional entity or operator. Trust in the non-volitional trustee and its underlying operator is inseparable, and the operator is rational and has benefits/costs that are derived from operating the non-volitional trustee in diferent ways.

Recent research also suggests that calculative trust is applicable to initial trust. Paul and McDaniel (2004) evaluate calculative trust in an initial interpersonal trust context and argue that as long as the trustor is vulnerable to the non-performance of the trustee, calculative trust is efective. In the context of initial trust in an information system, users may be vulnerable to the potential misuse/abuse of the system (e.g., by providing personal information), and therefore, their calculation of the potential costs and benefits for misuse/abuse of the system will afect their initial trusting beliefs towards this system. If users believe that the system and underlying stakeholders (i.e., the government and related agents in the case of an NID system) have nothing to gain, or the costs overwhelm the benefits, from being untrustworthy in operating a system, they will be more likely to build positive, initial trusting beliefs towards the system.

H4: The cost/benefit calculation positively afects trusting beliefs.

## 2.3.4. Institutional trusting base

Sociologists see trust as a social structure and suggest that the impersonal structures that are inherent in a specific circumstance will facilitate trust building in this circumstance (Lewis and Weigert, 1985; Shapiro, 1987). When necessary institutional structures are in place and the social environment is in proper order, trustors are more likely to grant trust in general (McKnight et al., 1998). This trusting base is especially important in situations where there is no previous interaction or where the trustor and trustee are not familiar with each other (Zucker, 1986).

Prior IS research examining the institutional trusting base has mostly focused on a single institutional context – the internet environment (Gefen et al., 2003; McKnight et al., 2002a). When studying trust in a nonhuman object manipulated by an underlying human operator, we believe more complex institutional contexts should be considered. For example, in an organizational environment, two sets of impersonal structures are involved in forming users’ trust in a novel system – the technology structures and the organizational structures. The technology structures establish expectations for information systems in general. These general expectations guide users’ trusting perceptions of a specific, novel information system. On the other hand, organizational context is an important factor in using an information system. When adopting a novel information system, the organizational resources and procedures, system sponsorship, and stakeholders’ interests are all influential factors in forming users’ trust. Thus, we believe a thorough investigation of both technology and organizational institutional contexts will improve our understanding of initial trust formation in the current research.

Prior research suggests that there are two subcomponents of the institutional base that afect trust formation, situational normality and structural assurance (Lewis and Weigert, 1985; McKnight et al., 2002a; Shapiro, 1987). Similar to the personality trusting base, the two forms of institutional trusting base have been represented as separate constructs in our model to better understand their individual efects on trusting beliefs.

Situational normality means that the situation is normal and everything is in its proper order (Baier, 1986; Lewis and Weigert, 1985). When the trustor has no knowledge about the trustee, he/she will rely on his/her feelings about the situational setting to build trusting beliefs (McKnight et al., 1998). Dimensions of beliefs relating to situational normality have included benevolence, competence, and integrity (McKnight et al., 2002a). People are typically more comfortable in a customary and anticipated setting and are more likely to trust others in this kind of setting (Baier, 1986; Lewis and Weigert, 1985). On the other hand, when people face inexplicable, abnormal situations, they are uncomfortable and tend to not trust (Garfinkel, 1963).

Existing research on trust has operationalized situational normality by referring to general examples of the trustee being studied. For example, in studies of trust in a specific online vendor or web site, the referenced object in the situational normality scales was web sites in general, or other internet vendors in general (Gefen et al., 2003; McKnight et al., 2002a). The subjects in these studies had experience with other web sites and online vendors and could respond to these survey questions based on their past, direct experience with similar technology. Following this approach, we operationalize two situational normality constructs – one for each institutional context – by referring to general objects. Situational normality in a technology context (referred to as technology situational normality) is operationalized with general information systems. For example, users are more likely to have positive, initial trusting beliefs in an NID system if they believe that most information systems are employed with integrity in a benevolent, competent manner. Situational normality in the organizational context (referred to as organizational situational normality) is operationalized with general government agencies and services because of the current study context of NID systems. For example, users are more likely to have positive, initial trusting beliefs in an NID system if they believe that most government agencies are operated with integrity in a benevolent, competent manner. Thus, we propose that if users are comfortable interacting with information systems and government agencies in general, then they are more likely to build positive initial trusting beliefs towards a new NID system.

H5: Organizational situational normality positively afects trusting beliefs.

H6: Technology situational normality positively afects trusting beliefs.

Structural assurance means that safeguards such as promises, contracts, regulations, and guarantees are in place (Shapiro, 1987; Sitkin, 1995; Zucker, 1986). These safeguards are the institutional ‘‘side bets” (Shapiro, 1987) of trust, especially when the trustor has no other information with which to make trust inferences. Legal safeguards like regulations, laws, guarantees, and contracts will facilitate trustors’ beliefs that the trustee will make every efort to fulfill the promise (Sitkin, 1995). Similar to situational normality, two reference objects – general information systems and general government agencies and services – are used in the current study for assessing structural assurance in a technology context (technology structural assurance in the model) and in an organizational context (organizational structural assurance in the model). In a technology context, technological safeguards such as encryption, specific system development processes and procedures (McKnight and Chervany, 2000), third-party certifications (Luo, 2002), and feedback mechanisms (Pavlou and Gefen, 2004) have been found to facilitate initial trusting beliefs in a web vendor or site. When using a system in an organizational context, end users may also seek organizational policies, norms, regulations and sometimes even legal means to protect their interests. Thus, we propose that the structural assurances associated with information systems and government agencies and services in general will facilitate initial trusting beliefs in a new NID system.

H7: Organizational structural assurance positively afects trusting beliefs.

H8: Technology structural assurance positively afects trusting beliefs.

Based on the trust literature, we have generally proposed that all trusting bases will positively influence trusting beliefs. It seems likely, however, that in investigating many trusting bases simultaneously, the efect of some trusting bases may overshadow or subsume others.

## 2.4. Trusting beliefs, attitudes, and intentions

McKnight et al. (1998) decomposed the concept of initial trust into two constructs based on a parsimonious version of the TRA (Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980). The two constructs were trusting intention, the trustor’s will ingness to depend on the trustee, and trusting beliefs, the trustor’s perception of whether the trustee has the desired attributes to be trusted. In their study, trusting intention was operationalized with trustor’s general willingness to trust the trustee, as well as one’s subjective probability to perform trust-related, context-specific behaviors. Three dimensions of trusting beliefs (benevolence, competence, integrity) have been extensively studied in various trusting contexts and together provide an overall measure of trusting beliefs (Gefen, 2002; Mayer et al., 1995; McKnight et al., 2002a). The McKnight initial trust model (2002a) was constructed such that trusting intention was determined by trusting beliefs, which are formed based on external variables. While this trust model is parsimonious, subjective norm and attitude, known determinants of behavioral intentions, were omitted and may provide additional insight on how initial trust is formed.

According to the Ajzen and Fishbein (1980), behavioral intentions are a function of two determinants – attitude and subjective norm. Prior IS research has included attitude and/or subjective norm within their models of online trust and purchasing intentions (Jarvenpaa et al., 2000; Pavlou and Fygenson, 2006; van der Heijden et al., 2003). A recent study by Li et al. (2006) compared the McKnight initial trust model with an extended model and found that the inclusion of trusting attitude and subjective norm explained more variance in trusting beliefs and trusting intentions. Thus, in an efort to better depict initial trust formation, both trusting attitude and subjective norm are included in the current research as determinants of trusting intention.

The three dimensions of trusting beliefs – benevolence, competence and integrity – have been assessed in various IS contexts and recently extended to trust in technological artifacts (Wang and Benbasat, 2005). While there has been debate as to whether human attributes such as benevolence can be ascribed to technology, recent conceptual and empirical research suggests that these attributes are applicable to technology, especially given that technology is designed and operated by humans. Trusting attitude is defined as a trustor’s positive or negative evaluation of trust-related behaviors. Thus, we propose that users’ trusting beliefs in system attributes, including benevolence, competence and integrity, form their attitude towards the system.

H9: Trusting beliefs positively afect trusting attitude.

Trusting attitude represents the trustor’s personal judgment that performing these behaviors would result in good or bad consequences. It is the personal determinant of behavioral intention to perform trust-related behaviors (Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980). In the context of NID systems, trust-related behaviors might include advocating the NID system, providing personal information, and using an NID card. Thus, users’ attitudes towards these behaviors determine their intention to trust such a system.

H10: Trusting attitude positively afects trusting intention.

## 2.5. Subjective norm

The other determinant of intention is subjective norm, defined as a ‘‘person’s perception that most people who are important to him think he should or should not perform the behavior in question” (Ajzen and Fishbein, 1980, p. 3). This theory posits that people may choose to perform a behavior when they believe their important referents think they should, even if they are not themselves favorable towards the behavior or its consequences. This direct relationship between subjective norm and intention is based on compliance (Kelman, 1958), which assumes that people will be suficiently motivated to comply with these important referents. Empirical studies in diferent contexts have supported this compliance relationship (Albarracin et al., 2001). Taylor and Todd (1995) also found a significant efect from subjective norm to usage intentions in an information systems context. When the trustor has no direct knowledge of a system and the related trust situation, he/she may seek others’ opinions and tend to comply with the important referents’ opinion. In addition to the compliance efect, Kelman (1958, 1974) also suggests an indirect influence of subjective norm on intention, which is called internalization. The influence of social reference groups is believed to result from both compliance and internalization efects (Bagozzi, 2007). Internalization refers to the process through which one incorporates the important referents’ beliefs into one’s own belief structure. Thus, subjective norm would directly influence one’s beliefs and attitude, and indirectly influence intentions. For example, Venkatesh and Davis (2000) proposed that when the important referents think a system is useful, one may come to believe that this system is actually useful and in turn form an intention to use it. Their empirical findings strongly supported this indirect relationship from subjective norm to intention. Similarly, Li et al. (2006) found a direct relationship between subjective norm and trusting beliefs and attitude in an IS context. If people have not enough direct knowledge about a system, they may rely on the important referents’ opinions and may incorporate the referents’ beliefs into his/her own trusting beliefs and attitude, and then develop trusting intentions towards the system accordingly. ## 3. Methodology

A survey-based study was conducted to test the initial trust model in the context of trust in a future NID system. NID systems refer to a type of information system used for supporting e-government at a national level. While most NID systems share some standard features, such as centralized databases, security measures, identity verification, and recognition processes, there is also a great deal of variability with these systems due to the actual NID system design and the wide-range of additional features and functionality provided with these complex systems.

In this study, the context of NID systems was selected for three reasons. First, there is an increasing, nationwide interest in the topic of NID systems and a global interest in e-government systems in general. Second, NID systems are large-scale, complex, and novel to the user population (i.e., citizens). Third, the topic is relevant and salient to many as deployment of such a system involves a strong, sponsor or stakeholder – the government – and actual use of the system would have considerable consequences for end users – the citizens. All of these characteristics make an NID system a good context for the current study of initial trust, as the U.S. has yet to adopt an NID system and most residents would not have direct experience with a nation-wide, full-function NID system. Thus, the study was designed to assess initial trust for this prospective system prior to actual use or direct experience. Study interventions were developed to insure that the subjects had enough second-hand information about the future system to form opinions. Difering opinions among the subjects were expected and should reflect the varying opinions of potential users of a U.S. NID system.

## 3.1. Study procedure

Undergraduate students enrolled in a sophomore-level, business course at a university in the United States served as subjects in the study. Class credit was awarded for voluntary participation in the study. Pre-tests for the study disclosed that subjects did not have experience with NID systems, as expected, given that the U.S. does not utilize such a system. In addition, many subjects were unfamiliar with the basic concepts related to NID systems prior to the study.

The subjects were first asked to complete a pre-survey, then were given study interventions – an NID lecture and an assignment, and lastly were asked to complete a post-survey. All aspects of the study were conducted within a single laboratory session. The pre-survey included demographic information and scales measuring six constructs (faith in humanity, trusting stance, technology situational normality and structural assurance, and organizational situational normality and structural assurance), as these constructs could be assessed independent of the specific trustee. The NID system lecture introduced the subiects to the general topic of NID sys: tems, including how these systems work, the technological components, advantages, and potential problems. An assignment was then given for subjects to search online for additional information on NID systems. The detailed lecture content and the assignment instructions are presented in Appendix A. Lastly, a post-survey was conducted to assess the subjects’ second-hand knowledge and to measure all remaining constructs, including reputation, cost/benefit calculation, subjective norm, trusting beliefs, trusting attitude and trusting intention.

## 3.2. Measures

The scales used in this study (Table 2 and Appendix B) were adapted from existing scales or developed according to the guidelines provided in previous research. Each item was measured by a 7-point Likert-type scale (anchored with 1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). The scales for trusting intention, trusting beliefs, faith in humanity, trusting stance, situational normality, and structural assurance were adapted from McKnight et al.’s (2002a) eCommerce trust measures. Trusting intention and trusting beliefs were operationalized with references to a specific object – the future U.S. NID system, while the two personality bases and four institutional bases referred to more general objects (i.e., general others, general information systems, and general government agencies and services, respectively). As suggested in the literature (McKnight et al., 2002a; McKnight et al., 1998), trusting beliefs, faith in humanity, and situational normality were measured with separate scales of three to four items for each dimension of beliefs (benevolence, competence, integrity). Trusting intention was measured with items assessing general willingness to depend on the NID system and the subjective probability of three common trust-related behaviors for NID systems (advocating the system, providing personal information to the system, and using NID cards). The use of multiple trusting behaviors within this scale provides for a better measure of trusting intention as recommended by Ajzen and Fishbein (1980) and other trust-related research (McKnight et al., 2002a).

An existing scale was used to measure subjective norm and trusting attitude (Li et al., 2006). The trusting attitude items assessed subjects’ evaluations of whether the three trust-related behaviors will have favorable consequences, while the subjective norm items assessed whether important referent others would want the subjects to trust the NID system, in terms of the three behaviors (advocating the system, providing personal information to the system, using NID cards) as recommended by Ajzen and Fishbein (1980). The scale for the cost/ benefit calculation was adapted from Gefen et al.’s (2003) measures of calculative-based trust in online shopping and assessed perceptions of whether NID systems and the related government agents have anything to gain by not being trustworthy. The scale for reputation was developed based on a review of reputation-based measures in IS research (McKnight et al., 2002b; Pennington et al., 2003; Sarker et al., 2003). These items generally referenced an individual or vendor having an overall good reputation (McKnight et al., 2002b), or being known for having certain qualities such as being honest or concerned for their customers (Pennington et al., 2003). Our items refer to NID systems having a reputation for the attributes of benevolence, competence, and reliability.

## 3.3. Pilot study and main data collection

A pilot study with 390 data points was first conducted to refine the study design and to validate the scales through an assessment of internal consistency and factor analysis. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) with AMOS 4.01 was used to make these preliminary assessments. One item measuring the cost/benefit calculation was discarded as the loading was below .70 and significantly afected overall construct reliability. After dropping this item, the composite reliabilities for all constructs were >.7 and the factor analysis supported the convergent and discriminant validity of the measured constructs.

The full data collection was then conducted with 443 new subjects with an average age of 20.3. This sample included 286 males and 157 females. Of the 443 subjects, 399 were U.S. citizens. On average, subjects reported a high level of second-hand NID knowledge obtained from the lecture and assignment (average responses ranged from 5.78 to 6.27, out of 7, with standard deviations of 0.838–0.949). A set of control variables (age, gender, and citizenship) was included in the structural regression model to eliminate the influences of sample characteristics.

## 3.4. Common method bias

Podsakof et al. (2003) suggested that studies using single source, self-reported data may have a potential for common method bias. Some temporal separation<sup>2</sup> was provided in the study procedure to help reduce the potential for common method variance (CMV) and two assessments of CMV were conducted. Harman’s single factor test was first performed with the full data set by conducting an exploratory factor analysis. The unrotated factor solution resulted in 11 factors with no general factor accounting for the majority of the variance – the first factor accounted for 30.4% of the variance, while all 11 factors accounted for 72.8% of variance.

A second, more rigorous form of assessment was conducted as recommended by Lindel and Whitney (2001), and the results are provided in Appendix C. This approach uses marker variable analysis to parcel out the potential efect of CMV from correlations between independent and dependent variables. In the first analysis (Table A, Appendix C), the smallest correlation for each dependent construct was selected as a marker construct and conservatively assumed to represent CMV. Trusting stance was identified as the marker for all three dependent constructs (trusting intention, attitude, and beliefs). The correlations of the marker construct with the associated dependent constructs were assumed as method variance and parceled out from other correlations. Sensitivity analyses with 95% and 99% confidence intervals of the marker correlations were also performed by repeating the above procedure. All but three constructs had significant corrected correlations and sensitivity analyses, indicating that the correlations were not merely due to CMV. The three constructs that did not have significant correlations were faith in humanity, technology situational normality, and technology structural assurance, and the hypothesized relationships for these constructs were not supported as reported later in the results.

A second marker analysis was also performed using an alternative method suggested by Lindel and Whitney (2001) (Table B, Appendix C). The smallest correlation between all pairs of constructs (correlation between trusting stance and subjective norm, 0.054) was identified as the marker correlation and the same analysis procedure was performed again. All corrected correlations and sensitivity analyses were significant after parceling out the method variance.

Based on these assessments, common method bias does not appear to have a serious influence on the study results. The discriminant validity results for this study (reported in Appendix F) further minimize the possibility of this bias as the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ values increase in size as constructs are combined, despite the reduction in model complexity.

## 4. Data analysis

Structural equation modeling with maximum likelihood estimation using AMOS 4.01 (Arbuckle and Wothke, 1999) was conducted to test both the measurement model and the structural regression model with the full data set. A two-step approach was used where the measurement model was first evaluated to assess internal consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. The structural regression model was then used to test our hypotheses.

## 4.1. Measurement model

The survey items along with the means and standard deviations for each construct are provided in Table 2 and Appendix B. CFA was conducted on the measurement model and showed acceptable model fit (SRMR .058, CFI .966, RMSEA .043), supporting both convergent and discriminant validity. Composite reliabilities were used to assess the internal consistency for each construct<sup>3</sup> (Gefen, 2003; Werts et al., 1974). As shown in Table 2, all reliabilities were greater than .7, and all but one were greater than .8, thus internal consistency was acceptable.

In this study, scales for the first-order dimensions for four constructs (trusting beliefs, faith in humanity, technology situational normality, and organizational situational normality) were used, as previously mentioned, to provide a more accurate measure of these multi-dimensional constructs (McKnight et al., 2002a; McKnight et al., 1998; Serva et al., 2005). The constructs were not represented as second-order constructs in the measurement or structural regression models, however, as this would have added an additional 12 constructs to the model and required a substantially larger sample. Instead, the mean response for each dimension was calculated and then treated as a direct observation, thus the dimensions are listed in Table 2 instead of the individual items. The individual items used to measure each dimension and descriptive statistics for these items are provided in Appendix B. Individual measurement models with direct items for the four constructs are presented in Appendix D. When working with a large number of observed variables in structural equation modeling, the averaging of items may provide adequate estimates and prevent extreme sample size requirements (Bagozzi and Heatherton, 1994; Yuan et al., 1997).

The factor loadings for the indicators are shown in Table 2 and Appendix D, respectively, and support the convergent and discriminant validity of the constructs. All standardized factor loadings were >.7 with the exception of two items. Trusting beliefs-competence and faith in humanity-competence were slightly below the 0.70 benchmark (Straub et al., 2004). We retained these items as their loadings were significant at $p < . 0 0 1$ and were higher than twice their standard errors. Other empirical studies have reported similar loadings for these items and have retained the items for content validity (Gefen et al., 2003; McKnight et al., 2002a).

The Average Variance Extracted (AVE) was also calculated<sup>4</sup> and compared to construct correlations to assess convergent and discriminant validity (Fornell and Larcker, 1981; Straub et al., 2004). As shown in Appendix E, the AVE for each construct was greater than 0.5, confirming convergent validity (Straub et al., 2004). The square root of the AVE for each construct was above 0.7 and greater than the correlations with other constructs, supporting the discriminant validity of the constructs (Chin, 1998; Straub et al., 2004). Some pairs of constructs, however, had relatively high correlations (i.e., subjective norm, trusting beliefs, trusting attitude, and trusting intention). While intention theories and prior literature support strong relationships among these constructs, two additional analyses were conducted to further assess discriminant validity (see Appendix F). First, the estimated correlation parameter between each pair of trusting bases and subjective norm was constrained to 1.0 and a $\chi ^ { 2 }$ diference test for the constrained and original models was conducted (Anderson and Gerbing, 1988). Second, the original model was compared with alternative models in which each pair of trusting bases and subjective norm was combined into one (Gefen et al., 2000; Segars, 1997). The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ values of the constrained and alternative models were all significantly larger than the original model, thus further supporting the discriminant validity of the constructs (Straub et al., 2004). Lastly, diagnostics were performed to assess potential multicollinearity among the independent variables. All variables had a variance inflation factor (VIF) less than 3, lower than the recommended threshold of 10 (Kline, 2005), thus, multicollinearity does not appear to be a serious concern.

## 5. Discussion and implications

The primary objective of this research was to study a more comprehensive set of trusting bases and the relative efects of these bases on initial trust in a nonhuman trustee, a complex and novel system. The findings indicate that less comprehensive studies of trust may provide misleading results and that practitioners and academics should focus their attention on reputation, cost/benefit perceptions, social influence, and organizational factors in building initial trust in a novel technology. All of these factors can be influenced by indirect exposure to a new system (e.g., second hand information with no direct use of the system) and afect users’ pre-implementation expectations and initial trust in the system. As previously summarized in Table 1, most prior IS studies on trust have reported institutional technology factors and personality factors as signif icant determinants of trusting beliefs. Our results suggest that when a more comprehensive set of factors are examined, the technology institutional and personality trusting bases may not influence trusting beliefs. In addition, few IS trust studies have assessed social influence as a determinant of trusting beliefs, attitudes, and intentions, and we found that it had a stronger efect on trusting beliefs than any of the trusting bases in our context of initial trust.

These results have significant implications for practice and research, as we know that pre-implementation perceptions and expectations of a system influence later perceptions of the system (Ginzberg, 1981; Staples et al., 2002) and that the initial exposure to a new system represents a critical phase for adaptation of the system and the users (Tyre and Orlikowski, 1994; Weick, 1990). This study provides several strategic implications for organizations implementing technological innovations and insight for trust researchers as outlined below.

## 5.1. Strategic implications for practice

Our findings suggest that when users have no prior interaction with, or direct knowledge of, a new system, they will acquire information and use cognitive processes such as categorizing based on reputation to make their trust inferences. Users will look for other ‘‘good reasons” constituting evidence of trustworthiness (Lewis and Weigert, 1985), including available second-hand information (McKnight et al., 1998). Organizations implementing a large-scale, novel system should therefore publicize positive, second-hand information about the system to potential users to help build initial trust in the system. This second-hand information might include user accounts of system usability, security, and reliability in other organizations or contexts, which should help establish a more positive reputation for the system in general. Users’ experiences with prior large-scale systems may also provide useful information. In addition, multimedia could be used to demonstrate the functionality and usability of the system. For example, SAP.com provides video demonstrations for major enterprise applications. Management can introduce these demos to provide a preview of the system for the end users and build appropriate expectations of system functionality.

Users’ calculative assessment of the benefits and costs to various system stakeholders for (un)trustworthy behavior were also found to influence trusting beliefs and has significant implications for practice. Users will have greater initial trust in a complex, novel system if they believe that there are significant consequences or penalties for designers, managers and other users of the system to employ the system in an untrustworthy manner. These system stakeholders are assumed to be rational, calculative, and follow economic principles (Gefen et al., 2003). If they have much to lose by abusing the system, they will choose to design and use the system in a predictable and reliable way, and therefore the system will be viewed as trustworthy.

Organizations should thus establish and promote policies related to internal system use and include significant penalties for inappropriate use within these policies. Technical safeguards should also be established to increase the costs of system misuse. By establishing such policies and safeguards, and providing evidence that they are well-enforced, management can foster user initial trust in these systems. Similarly organizations could establish and promote policies that reward employee good behavior related to security, privacy and interactions with users.

Two institutional trust contexts were investigated in this study, organizational factors and technological factors. The results suggest that organizational factors have a strong efect on initial trust with a novel, organizational system and that technological factors may not be a significant determinant of trust for some users. Given the number of complex organizational systems and every day use of technology, many users are more comfortable with technology in general and thus view the technical parts of a novel system (i.e., databases, scanners, registration interface, etc.) as similar to other systems. Instead, they may focus on how the organization implements and administers the technology. Within the organizational institutional factor, situational normality was found to afect trusting beliefs while structural assurance had no efect.

The subjects in our study rated the technology institutional items relatively high (5.35 situational normality; 4.95 structural assurance), while the organizational institutional factors (i.e., governmental factors in an NID context) were rated lower (4.41 situational normality; 4.68 structural assurance). This suggests that the subjects were less concerned with trusting assessments of technology, and in fact, most debates of NID systems have focused on how the government would design and use such a system. Strong concerns about privacy and autocracy exist in a large population, as well as in our study sample. Concerns with the organizational sponsor, the government in this context, and general comfort with technology may have made subject perceptions of government agencies and services more salient in forming initial trust.

These findings suggest the importance of system developments and routine operating procedures, sponsors and stakeholders, and decision making processes in an organizational context. When users are of aware of these underlying stakeholders and procedures, they will assume a normal and stable institutional context, and are more likely to form initial trust in a system. Organizations should provide open information about key stakeholders and decision making processes associated with the new system. The more users feel comfortable with the stakeholders and their decision making and find the government organization to be in proper order, the more likely they are to trust and potentially accept a new system deployed by the government. Systems are technologic artifacts manipulated by people, and in an organizational context, it is usually the key sponsors and decisions makers who control the purpose of a system. Therefore in promoting a complex, novel system, organizations should focus on building users’ positive perceptions about the organization in general, high-level executives, and board members, in terms of their competence, benevolence, and integrity.

Social influence showed the most significant efect on trusting beliefs. In the absence of first-hand knowledge or experience, the trusting beliefs of a few may have a far-reaching impact on the overall trusting beliefs of a group. Additional training and promotion of a novel organizational system to influential individuals such as group leaders, respectable seniors, key members, and organizational partners, would appear to have significant downstream efects on the trust and acceptance of new systems. Formal marketing campaigns could also be employed featuring influential individuals to further promote the trustworthiness of the system to all users.

## 5.2. Implications for trust research

The results from this study suggest that partial investigations of trusting bases as determinants of trust may be misleading. Our results showed that the personality trusting base and the technology institutional trusting base were overshadowed when cognitive-reputation, calculative, and organizational institutional trusting bases and social influence were also assessed. Some trusting bases may appear to have a significant efect on trusting beliefs when tested in isolation or when a less comprehensive set of determinants is studied. Prior research has also posited that the efects of some trusting bases may be overshadowed or diminished when studied in the presence of the other trusting bases (Gefen et al., 2003; Johnson-George and Swap, 1982; McKnight et al., 2002a). To empirically support this speculation and the findings of our study, additional analyses were performed. Specifically, nine separate structural regression models were run in which only one trusting base factor or subjective norm was included as a determinant of trusting beliefs. However, when all of the bases and subjective norm were included in the model, five of the determinants no longer had a significant efect on trusting beliefs, and all path coeficients (column 2 in Table 3) were reduced from those in the individual analyses (column 3 in Table 3).

## 5.2.1. Institutional determinants of trust

There were mixed results for the efects of institutional determinants on trusting beliefs. One component of institutional trusting base, organizational situational normality, had a significant efect on trusting beliefs, while the other components, technology situational normality, technology structural assurance, and organizational structural assurance, did not have a significant efect. These mixed results have important implications for researchers studying the institutional base as there has been great variation in the operationalization and representation of these trusting bases (e.g., one component vs. two components; second-order vs. first-order constructs; exclusion of one or more belief dimensions; and so on). Given the findings of our study, conclu sions relating to the institutional base when all dimensions are not measured, or the measurement representation is not consistent, may not be reliable.

In this study, institution-based trust may have had a smaller efect on trusting beliefs than in other less comprehensive studies due to the presence of cognitive-based trust. The cognitive process of reputation categorization is based on the general reputation of NID systems, whereas institutional trust is assessed using the more general information systems and government contexts with which subjects are familiar. NID systems are a more specific reference object than general information systems or government agencies and services and thus may have a stronger relationship with trusting beliefs about a specific NID system.

The insignificant influence of organizational structural assurance may be explained by the younger subject pool in this study not having developed informed perceptions about formal safeguards (laws, regulations, etc.) that regulate government activities. One would expect that in an organizational context, establishing and promoting policies and regulations of organizational behaviors would provide necessary safeguards and foster initial trust in new systems. Additional studies with more diverse samples and diferent contexts may provide more insight.

## 5.2.2. Personality determinants of trust

Both personality base components, faith in humanity and trusting stance, did not have significant efects on trusting beliefs. The trust literature and the number of trusting bases investigated provide an explanation for these results. The influence of the personality base may be lessened in the context of a complex system and in the presence of other trusting bases and subjective norm. With a complex, novel system there may be considerable consequences to users for mis-granted trust based on personal tendency or intuition. Instead, users may seek other trusting bases, with more reliable trust inferences, to form their trusting beliefs. Given that humans are social beings, most will find other social and cognitive factors that are more pertinent to a trust decision than their own disposition (Williamson, 1993). Thus, users’ cognitive and calculative processes and subjective norm may have overcome their personal trust tendency.

Another explanation is a mediation efect. ## 6. Conclusions and limitations

This study proposed and tested a model of trusting bases with initial trust formation in the context of NID systems. The empirical investigation of the trusting bases and social influence as well as the relative efects on trusting beliefs, attitudes, and intentions enhances our understanding of initial trust formation. The findings provide practical implications to government agencies implementing new systems, and the initial trust model can be generalized to organizations implementing other types of information systems.

The specific findings related to cognitive and calculative bases of trust have significant academic and practical implications. Past research on trust in information systems has focused less attention on cognitive and calculative bases and has not evaluated these bases in a more comprehensive model that includes other trusting bases and complete measures of trusting beliefs, attitudes, subjective norm, and trusting intentions. From a practical perspective, the findings related to cognitive and calculative bases provide governments with actionable recommendations for improving trusting beliefs in an NID system. Cognitive-based beliefs can be enhanced through reputation-building mechanisms, while calculative-based beliefs can be enhanced by establishing and publicizing policies and procedures that make untrustworthy behavior with an NID system very costly. Past studies of trust in information systems have focused more on personality and institutional bases, and findings related to these bases are less actionable, as personality-based beliefs are typically stable over the lifetime of a trustor, and the institutional context is larger in scope and more dificult to control or manipulate.

Limitations of the study may include construct operationalization. In this study, the trusting base factors were operationalized in a general manner. Specific system and product characteristics were not included in the model or measured, as a U.S. NID system does not yet exist. Technology structural assurance and situational normality, two factors of the institutional base, were operationalized in the context of general information systems, instead of NID systems, as most subjects in the study did not have prior experience or knowledge of NID systems. A more complete, specific set of external determinants could explain more variance in trusting beliefs and thus provide more insight to institutions implementing organizational systems.

A limitation in the study design is the use of student subjects. While the students represent valid, potential users of an NID system, broader samples could be used in future studies to better address population diferences such as age, occupation and computer experience. Another limitation in the design includes the use of a field survey rather than an experiment. While field surveys ofer external validity, internal validity is limited, as a controlled manipulation of the causal factors is not conducted (Blackburn, 1987; Zmud et al., 1989).

Future research is needed with the actual use of an NID system to investigate the reciprocal relationships that can exist among trust constructs after initial trust is formed and a long-term trusting relationship is established (Gilliland and Bello, 2002; Verhoef et al., 2002). Cultural issues should also be investigated. While subjective norm may subsume some cultural issues, such as an increased concern for privacy in the U.S., future research could investigate cultural diferences by comparing initial trust across cultures. Lastly, confirmatory studies of the research model and the relative influence of the trusting bases should be conducted in other contexts where new, complex forms of information technologies are being deployed. Different system contexts could influence the relative importance of the various trusting bases as determinants of trusting beliefs.

## National identification systems (NIDS)

## Introduction

National identification (NID) systems have long been advocated in many countries around the world as a means to facilitate public services, enhance national security, guard against illegal immigrants, and so on. They are already in use in most European countries, Hong Kong, Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand. This idea has also been advocated in the U.S. for many years, but an NID system has yet to be implemented in the U.S.

Now, you may be wondering what a NID system looks like; how it works; why some of the people like it, but other people don’t; and what the future of the NID systems is. The purpose of this lecture is to answer these questions to you.

## What is a NIDS?

 A combination of government-issued identifications: NIDS have been used or proposed for handling routine administrative transactions between government agencies and citizens, with benefits claimed in the areas of convenience, cost savings or fraud reduction. NIDS could combine the functions of a driver’s license, social security registration, immigration documents, and other government-issued identification.

 Two aspects of NIDS: On one hand, NIDS is a type of information system consisting of information verification technology, database, scanning technology, and so on. On the other hand, this information system is developed, administered and maintained by governmental agencies.

 NIDS can either be mandatory or voluntary: NIDS can either be mandatory or voluntary. In a mandatory scheme, everyone is required to carry and present a card when asked; not doing so is an ofense. In a voluntary scheme, those who do not have a card will be subjected to additional background checks while those with a card can more easily obtain services or pass security checkpoints.

## Technological Components of NIDS

 An identity verification system: Before a card can be issued, there must be some means of assuring that the person receiving the card is who he or she claims to be. Because of this, any ID system is only as good as its ability to verify an identity in the first place. The issuance of the ID is accompanied by the entry of the person’s ‘unique identifier’, such as fingerprint or retinal image, that can be read by a scanning machine and matched with the person presenting the card to confirm his/her identity.

 A database: A NID database stores basic information about the person identified: weight, height, hair and eye color, address, etc. It may also contain some personal record, such as credit history or criminal history.

 A card: While the card is the most visible of these components, without the other components working together, the card is not especially useful. Diferent information is included with the NID cards from diferent countries. However, some basic information such as name, date of birth, sex, address, photograph, etc. are common among cards. Biometric data such as fingerprints and retina scans can help in verifying that the card actually belongs to the cardholder.

 A card verification system: Finally, a massive card verification system is needed to connect the cardholders with their records in the database.

## How does a NIDS work?

 Registration process: There are at least two distinct processes in a functioning NIDS. First is a one-time registration process in which everyone is required to present themselves to the authorities along with their existing identification documentation, such as birth certificate or citizenship papers. If the authorities believe the documentation is valid, they create an individually identified entry in a database and issue the person a card which, in most systems, would be linked to this entry.

 Authentication: The second process is authentication. This occurs whenever the cardholder is required to show the card to verify his or her identity. A first check is made to ensure that the card actually belongs to the person presenting it. This is done by comparing the information on the card with the person, for example by visual comparison of the cardholder with the photograph on the card, or by digital comparison of a live finger scan with the finger print recorded on the card. If there is a satisfactory match, the card is used as a link to a database. A second check then determines whether there is anything on file that raises suspicion about the cardholder. If not, the person can proceed.

## Advantages

 Enhance public services: For example: improving the accuracy of public health information; etc.

 Enhance national security: For example: helping confirm the alibis of criminal suspects; fighting terrorism; guarding against illegal immigrants; etc.

## Potential problems

 Efectiveness: Does the system work efectively? Using biometric data such as fingerprints and retina scans can help in verifying that the card actually belongs to the cardholder. However, this is not 100% reliable. There is always a margin of variation between the original sample obtained during registration and any subsequent sample used at the point of authentication. Another problem is that the issuance of a high-security ID card is based on the presentation of low-security documents. For example, if someone shows a convincing passport or birth certificate, they could misrepresent their identify and obtain an ID card.

 Security: Is the system secure? As more and more smart cards are put into operation, more and more people know how to break them. If the card is used to check the information against a central database, then the security of this database becomes crucial. It must be accessible nationwide in order to support security checkpoints all over the country. Therefore it will have to be on some network, probably the Internet or telephone system. The security necessary to prevent people from breaking into such a sensitive networked system may be hard to achieve.

 Invasions of privacy and civil liberties: The system may invade privacy and civil liberties. NIDS would allow individuals to be easily tracked. Some people believe that those who are easily and constantly tracked by a central authority are not free people. A NIDS would make everyone vulnerable to the problem of incorrect data in the database. If the data on the card or in the database is incorrect, then innocent people could be victimized even through they have no fault themselves.

 Cost: Another potential problem could be the cost. The more secure a card is, the greater its value, and the greater the incentive and reward for breaking the card. Any card or device in the public’s hands long enough will be cracked. The more secure the card, the more expensive it will be to roll out, and the more costly would be a failure.
