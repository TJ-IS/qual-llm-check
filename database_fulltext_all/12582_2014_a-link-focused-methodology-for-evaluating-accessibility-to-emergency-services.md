# A link-focused methodology for evaluating accessibility to emergency services

## a b s t r a c t

This paper considers the development of a new measure for evaluating accessibility to emergency services via a road network called critical closeness accessibility (CCA). CCA quanti<sup>fi</sup>es the relative importance of each link in a roadway network with respect to its system-wide contribution to emergency service accessibility and is based on the network science/graph theory concept of closeness. CCA is a link-focused measure that used to evaluate accessibility on a link-by-link basis and can be used for disconnected networks. We introduce a relative importance-based weighting approach for both origins and destinations that can be applied to different types of nodes including generic link endpoints that represent individual intersections and roadway segments in GIS-based roadway maps as well as to physical locations. CAA offers a unique approach for evaluating accessibility that accounts for the spatial distribution of emergency service locations, the topology of the road network, and the engineering characteristics of the road network such as road types, capacities, volumes, and travel speeds.

## 1. Introduction

Many man-made systems including transportation infrastructure systems take the form of networks. As our world has become more highly connected there has been a great deal of interest in studying the principles that govern the underlying spatial characteristics and structure of different types of networked systems and how these characteristics are affected by planning and policy decisions. Roadway networks are extremely complex and are comprised of interconnected components that can interact with one another in intricate and often non-intuitive ways; and obtaining a better understanding of how accessibility is impacted by the complexities associated with the topology and physical characteristics of the roadway network is important with respect to making informed and accurate transportation planning decisions [15,35]. While interest in the spatial characteristics and structure of transportation networks is not new, the application of network science/complexity analysis and measures to transportation problems is limited [16]. Complex network theory or “network science” can offer unique approaches for evaluating different aspects of transportationbased accessibility.

This paper considers the development of a new approach for measuring accessibility to emergency service facilities (ESFs) via a road network called critical closeness accessibility (CCA). CCA quanti<sup>fi</sup>es the relative importance of each link in a roadway network with respect to its system-wide contribution to emergency service accessibility and is based on the network science/graph theory concepts of connectivity and closeness. The research provides a number of value-added contributions.

• CCA is a link-focused measure that quanti<sup>fi</sup>es accessibility on a link-bylink basis. Many existing transportation accessibility measurement approaches are link-based in that they utilize link impedance in the accessibility calculation; however, they evaluate the accessibility of a particular node or set of nodes (or zones) as opposed to assigning an accessibility value to the individual links in the network. We argue that place-based measures that use links in the accessibility calculation, but directly assign an accessibility value to a node or a set of nodes, are link-based as opposed to link-focused. As noted in [73] much of the research related to roadway networks tends to be node-centric and fails to recognize the link-centric nature of roadway infrastructure systems where the links as opposed to the nodes are the active elements.

• CCA directly builds on Dangalchev's [14] alternative formulation of closeness and the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of residual closeness. First, residual closeness extends the concept of closeness to an arc where disconnected networks are addressed by allowing closeness to go to zero; however, it does not consider the importance of the disconnected nodes. Dangalchev points out that residual closeness is not substantially impacted by losses in connectivity resulting from disconnected networks. We feel that this is unlikely to be the case for many realworld networks such as transportation networks where disruptions can isolate portions of the population from essential services. Second, residual closeness is formulated in a manner that considers all nodes in the entire graph and weights them equivalently. We address both of the issues above by introducing a relative importance-based weighting approach that can be used for different subset typologies of nodes.

• The CCA weighting approach can be used for generic link endpoints representing intersections and individual roadway segments in GIS-based roadway maps as well as for actual physical locations. This approach provides an important contribution to the accessibility literature as only measures of destination attractiveness are currently considered. We use the actual endpoints of each roadway link in the CCA calculation as opposed to arti<sup>fi</sup>cially creating origin and destination nodes via abstraction, thus preserving the actual connectivity of the roadway network being modeled.

• CAA offers a unique approach for evaluating accessibility that accounts for the spatial distribution of critical/important nodes, the topology of the road network (relative locations and connections between links and nodes), and the engineering characteristics of the road network such as road types, capacities, volumes, and travel speeds.

We believe that this work provides a unique, highly relevant and usable decision support methodology that <sup>fi</sup>lls a niche at the union of network science, emergency service, and transportation accessibility research. CCA can be applied at different geographical scales, to all types of critical/important facilities, to different types of nodes, and at vastly different scales. The methodology is described and illustrated on a small hypothetical network and is then demonstrated in a realworld application on portions of an actual GIS-based transportation planning network using an off-the-shelf software for transportation network applications called TransCAD™. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides a discussion on the use of travel models in transportation planning and how transportation infrastructure investment and policy decisions are shaped by outputs from these models. A discussion of relevant literature is presented in Section 3. The CCA methodology is introduced in Section 4, which includes an example implementation of the CCA along with detailed calculations. Section 5 focuses on a real-world application of CCA. A summary of our research contributions, concluding remarks and suggestions for future work is outlined in Section 6.

## 2. Transportation planning, travel models and decision-making

Timely access to emergency services including police, <sup>fi</sup>re, rescue, and medical care is essential to the overall health, safety, and general welfare of any population; and the roadway infrastructure system can greatly affect accessibility to these services. Distance-comparable locations within a given road network are not equally accessible. Some locations are more or less accessible than others based on factors such as the structural characteristics of the roadway infrastructure, network topology, and the surrounding geographical features [39]. Consequently, the everyday lives of people are impacted by not only the distances between where they live and where they are going but also by the characteristics of the existing roadway system, as the road network can enhance (or diminish) accessibility to emergency services and other important locations. GIS-based modeling has been successfully applied in a variety of residential and business planning decisions as well as in the context of some transportation planning problems [25,40,67]; and in recent years accessibility has become more of a focus in the landuse/transportation planning and policy arenas as the realization of the magnitude of economic, social and environmental implications associated with accessibility issues has become more evident [54,61,67]. While accessibility is a well-known concept, there is no consensus on how it should be measured or even de<sup>fi</sup>ned. How accessibility is de<sup>fi</sup>ned and measured as well as the level of detail of the analysis are critical in the context of planning decisions, as different types of decisions require different performance measures, different assumptions, as well as different analysis tools and solution techniques.

In the United States, state, regional and municipal transportation agencies are largely responsible for the planning, construction, maintenance and governance responsibilities associated with the various transportation infrastructure systems within their respective jurisdictions [70]. To facilitate accurate and timely travel demand forecasts as well as to gain a better understanding of the current operational status of existing transportation systems, decision makers often rely on sophisticated, large-scale, statewide transportation planning models which are ideal for projecting current travel patterns and estimating travel demand on the roadway network. Outputs from these models are often used to direct funding and policy decisions such as new roadway construction and the selection and prioritization of roadway maintenance projects. Unfortunately, these models do not speci<sup>fi</sup>cally address accessibility or emergency service issues. It is therefore challenging to predict and/or evaluate emergency response and emergency service routing. This can result in planning decisions that ignore the most critical roadway links in the network with respect to facilitating accessibility to emergency services.

This research is largely motivated by the lack of accessibility-based decision support modeling tools available to transportation planners [25,61,67]. The CCA can be used within the existing travel modeling framework to speci<sup>fi</sup>cally evaluate roadway-based accessibility concerns such as: identifying speci<sup>fi</sup>c links and/or portions of the road network that are the most important in terms of facilitating system-wide access; identifying speci<sup>fi</sup>c links and/or portions of the road network and their corresponding populations and locations that are at an inherent disadvantage for receiving critical emergency services; and assisting with new ESF location decisions and emergency route planning.

## 3. Background

In this paper we connect concepts from a number of different problem domains including transportation planning, emergency service management, facility location decisions, and accessibility modeling/ measurement using a methodological approach based on network science and graph theory. The body of literature in each problem domain, as well as the body of literature addressing network science/graph theory methodologies and their applications is quite extensive and a range of solution methodologies are employed depending on the scope of the problem, the type of network being considered, and the availability of empirical data. We do not attempt to provide a comprehensive review of the literature in each problem domain or to detail the history and evolution of network science. Instead, we focus on providing a general background on network science and include examples of how network analysis and graph theory has been applied to transportation network planning problems, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally with regard to accessibility-based performance measures for roadway systems. The literature review is organized into three subsections: 3.1. Network science and infrastructure networks, 3.2. Accessibility and roadway networks, and 3.3. Emergency services and facility location.

## 3.1. Network science and infrastructure networks

The term network science describes an evolving research area that includes applications and problems that are largely inspired by the study of non-trivial, real-world networked systems such as transportation, physical, biological and social networks [31]. Like other complex networks, transportation networks possess topological and geometric variations that can be described by a set of nodes representing distinct spatial locations and a set of links that characterize the connections between the nodes [73]. While much of the network science related research authored by physical scientists tends to focus on the statistical properties of different systems; much of the network science related research authored by transportation engineers, planners, and geographers tends to focus on evaluating the spatial structure of physical infrastructure systems and measuring and describing the complex relationships between the nodes, links and <sup>fl</sup>ows within those systems [69].

Centrality has been a fundamental concept in network science since the late 1970s when Freeman introduced degree, closeness and betweeness centrality, and discussed the intuition behind the use of these measures in the context of modeling social networks [19,20]. The basic premise was to model social interactions between people by employing a graph theoretic approach where the nodes of the network represent individuals and the links of the network represent relationships between those individuals [19,20,54]; and increasingly, graph theory and centrality measures have been used to describe the structural properties and design of various transportation systems [13,16,36,47,48,50,73]. We refer interested readers to several sources that offer reviews of the spatial aspects of transportation systems. Barthélemy (2011) [4] provides a comprehensive review of spatial networks that breaks out research studies on different categories of transportation and infrastructure networks including airline, transit, shipping and roadway infrastructure. Xie and Levinson [72] provide a perspective on complexity/network-science concepts and methodological approaches with respect to the transportation research domain. Mishra et al. [41] utilize existing graph theory measures to determine connectivity of multiple levels of transit service coverage including routing, scheduling, demographic and spatial activity patterns. The paper offers an excellent summary of the literature on centrality and connectivity measures in transportation and social networks.

As the <sup>fi</sup>eld of network science has advanced, computers have become more powerful, and data collection, analysis, visualization and storage techniques have improved, the ability to analyze extremely large and complex data sets and network structures has led to a number of advancements in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research related to the spatial analysis of the structure and design of transportation networks. This has facilitated the application of network analysis methodologies and concepts to examine the underlying structural and spatial properties of urban street networks [13,47,55]; to study relationships between street centrality and the densities of service and commercial activities [49]; to explore relationships between land use intensity and street centrality [68]; and to examine the dynamics of traf<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fl</sup>ow, urban form and commuting, congestion, and commercial vehicle movement with respect to road-network design and topology [8,15,23,30,34,35,46]. Centrality based approaches and measures have also been used to explore the evolutionary structure and growth of roadway networks in a spatial and temporal context [73] and to demonstrate that road networks have the property of self-organization and evolution and feature path dependency based on changing economic conditions [36].

## 3.2. Accessibility and roadway networks

Accessibility analysis has been widely used in transportation and urban planning for several decades and has directly bene<sup>fi</sup>ted from the evolution of complexity analysis and the development of powerful network analysis tools in GIS-based transportation modeling and land use software [8,19,25,67]. Within the transportation-based accessibility literature there are differences in how accessibility is de<sup>fi</sup>ned and measured depending on the mode of transportation used and whether transportation is the sole focus of the study or is treated as a component of the overall land-use strategy. For example, accessibility may be measured differently depending on whether bus transportation, light rail transportation, air transportation or roadway transportation is being considered [1,22,32,39,67]. Our interest is on link-focused accessibility in a roadway network and we concentrate speci<sup>fi</sup>cally on literature that considers roadway accessibility.

Niemeier [43] de<sup>fi</sup>nes accessibility in the context of transportation engineering and planning as: accessibility is the ease with which desired destinations may be reached. Given that transportation-based accessibility measures are context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c and can include factors such as the mode of travel, time constraints, and the topology of the transportation network, others have de<sup>fi</sup>ned accessibility as: accessibility is the relative ease of reaching various services, destinations, and/or activities from a particular origin [32,38,67]. The following de<sup>fi</sup>nition is offered in [65] for regional and rural areas, which rely on the road/highway network as the primary transportation system: accessibility is the ease with which services and facilities can be reached while using the road network. It is this de<sup>fi</sup>nition that forms the basis for the CCA measure where “ease of reach” is quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed via travel time. Conceptually, CCA is similar to the Accessibility/Remoteness Index of Australia (ARIA) discussed in [65]; however, CCA is link-focused and can be used to identify the exact roadway links in the network are most important in terms of facilitating emergency service accessibility. This is an important distinction because roadway links in immediate proximity of one another can have very different CCA values based on factors other than their Euclidean distance or the mileage from the surrounding ESFs.

Kwan et al. [33] provide an excellent overview on accessibility research based on the representation of geographical space, the methodological approach employed, and the speci<sup>fi</sup>c application considered. Geurs and van Wee [22] offer a comprehensive review of accessibility measures and separate accessibility measures according to different components and discuss different perspectives for measuring accessibility that focus on one or more of the components. While Geurs and van Wee argue that ideally, accessibility measures should take all components into account, this is often not a practical approach based on data limitations. In a recently published review paper, Páez et al. [45] note that in practice there are typically just two basic components to accessibility measures: 1) the cost of travel which is affected by spatial distributions of travelers and opportunities/destinations, and 2) the quality/quantity of opportunities/destinations. There is a considerable body of work that explores the application of different accessibility measures in the context of roadway networks [2,6,14,19,32]. For a more comprehensive review of roadway-based accessibility measures we refer readers to [45] and [67] where accessibility measures are discussed according to the class of indicator (cumulative opportunities, gravity-based, and utility-based), and to [41] where accessibility measures are organized according to centrality and connectivity measures (node-based versus link-based). Accessibility measures are also used to evaluate vulnerability and reliability in degraded roadway networks including [10,11,28,29,53,56,62–64,67]. In this paper we indirectly address the accessibility components suggested in [22] and directly address both of the accessibility components discussed in [45]. Depending on the application, the problem domain and the exact de<sup>fi</sup>nition of accessibility that is used, transportation accessibility measures may or may not be directly related to the network science concepts of centrality and connectivity. There is a smaller subset of accessibility literature that directly ties accessibility measures to network science/graph theory concepts. For example, [8,36,41,46–48,73] directly consider transportation network complexity and structure and explicitly discuss and de<sup>fi</sup>ne linkages between known graph theory measures and transportation-based accessibility concepts.

## 3.3. Emergency services and facility location

The literature addressing facility location modeling is vast. While we do not explicitly consider facility location in this research as the locations of the ESFs are <sup>fi</sup>xed, we rely on location science and emergency logistics literature for theoretical background and ideas. We refer readers to a number of comprehensive reviews that address different aspects of facility location modeling and problem formulation [17,44,51,52,58]. Although most of these papers do not speci<sup>fi</sup>cally consider emergency services, they discuss different techniques for location modeling that can be directly applied to emergency services. As opposed to facility location modeling, emergency logistics modeling explicitly addresses questions related to the location of emergency support facilities, the distribution and routing of emergency resources, evacuation and relocation of populations, and the transportation of casualties. Emergency logistics problems tend to differ from more traditional facility-location problems in the objective – for example, maximizing accessibility or minimizing response time versus minimizing cost or maximizing pro<sup>fi</sup>t respectively; and also in how vehicle availability, congestion, and temporal and spatial constraints are handled [8]. In many cases, access to emergency services is measured according to the “coverage” provided, with a common objective being the ability to respond to some fraction of all emergency calls within a minimum time period [26]. An example is the goal of responding to 95% of all emergency calls in less than 10 min. A comprehensive review of emergency logistics problems for both pre and post-disaster operations is found in Caunhye et al. [9].

Accessibility can factor into location analysis and modeling the location of important facilities and services can involve performance criteria such as minimizing average travel distances or travel times to the closest facility or service and minimizing average response times to customers, etc. [5,21,63]. Measuring accessibility to critical facilities by minimizing travel time or distance is consistent with the concept of “normative accessibility” introduced in Páez et al. [45]. Hierarchical planning issues can also be addressed via location modeling where more important or more critical facilities are associated with higher hierarchical levels [3,9] and the concept of hierarchical importance is incorporated into the CCA methodology. Summary reviews of emergency services logistics modeling literature that include references to wellcited papers in emergency facility location modeling and vehicle districting appear in [5,21,26,63]. Although CCA does not explicitly measure the accessibility to/from a single ESF in the network, the notion of critical facilities is pertinent to the development of the CCA methodology.

## 4. Methodology

CCA is a measure for evaluating the accessibility provided by each roadway link with respect to the link's system-wide relationship to all ESFs in a roadway network. CCA is based on the network science/ graph theory concepts of connectivity and closeness and incorporates the network topology and the actual engineering characteristics of the roadway network into the calculation. The CCA measure is implemented in a GIS-based travel modeling software, TransCAD™, and is demonstrated on both a small hypothetical network and on portions of an actual statewide transportation planning network. The development of CCA was largely motivated by the alternative de<sup>fi</sup>nition of closeness and the formulation of residual closeness provided by Dangalchev [14]. CCA draws on a number of important foundational concepts. Basic graph theory measures of connectivity and how they relate to accessibility are discussed in Subsection 4.1. Travel cost and trip valuation based on important facility locations are examined in Subsection 4.2. The CCA measure is described in detail in Subsection 4.3.

## 4.1. Centrality measures and accessibility

In transportation planning the general concept of accessibility relates to how easily a particular location can be reached, and foundational measures of transportation accessibility are often derived from closeness centrality measures in graph theory [47]. Graph theory provides a basis for network science where graphs are used as abstract structural models for a wide range of phenomena [37]. A complex network such as a roadway network can be formally represented as a graph. Graph $G = ( N , L )$ is characterized by a nonempty set of N nodes, which are connected to some extent to one another by a set of links, L; where the elements of $N \equiv \{ n _ { 1 } , n _ { 2 } , . . . , n _ { N } \}$ and the elements of $L \equiv \{ l _ { 1 } , l _ { 2 } , . . . , l _ { L } \}$ . Each node in N is referenced by its order, i, in the set. In undirected graphs each link is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by two nodes i and j and denoted as $( i , j )$ or $l _ { i j } { \cal A }$ path through the graph from node i to node j is an alternating progression of adjacent nodes and links that begins with i and ends with $j .$ Shortest paths play an important role in transportation networks and are notable in characterizing the structure of a graph [10]. In a valued graph where links are quantitatively weighted based on attributes such as distance, travel time, or impedance, the shortest path $d _ { i j }$ between nodes i and j is the smallest path length for all possible paths in the network from i to j. For a non-valued graph where links have no speci<sup>fi</sup>c weighting to describe the strength, frequency, distance, etc. of the relationship between different nodes, $d _ { i j }$ is measured by the fewest nodes traversed from i to j. The characteristic path length $L ,$ shown in Eq. (1), is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the mean of the shortest path lengths for all node pairs in the graph [7,47,72].

$$
L = \frac {1}{N (N - 1)} \sum_ {i, j \in N, i \neq j} d _ {i j}\tag{1}
$$

A serious <sup>fl</sup>aw with this measure is that it can only be used for connected graphs, so $d _ { i j }$ must be assigned a <sup>fi</sup>nite value or there must be a path connecting nodes i and $j .$ The fact that the measure can only be used on connected graphs prevents it from being used in linkremoval scenarios where one or more links are temporarily or permanently removed based on some type of disruption to the network, traf<sup>fi</sup>c is re-routed over the functioning links and network performance is re-assessed.

Closeness centrality $C ^ { C }$ measures centrality with respect to minimizing the distance to all other nodes in the network. $C _ { i } ^ { C }$ is the sum of the graph-theoretic distances from node i to all other nodes in the network where distance is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the length of the shortest path [7,54]. This measure is based on the idea that more important nodes will be centrally located with respect to minimizing the distance to all the other nodes in the network. The closeness centrality of node i is de<sup>fi</sup>ned in Eq. (2) where $D _ { i }$ is the average distance from node i to all other nodes in the graph [19,41]. $C _ { i } ^ { C }$ is meaningful for connected graphs only.

$$
C _ {i} ^ {C} = \left(D _ {i}\right) ^ {- 1} = \frac {N - 1}{\sum_ {j \in N , i \neq j} d _ {i j}}\tag{2}
$$

Nodes with relatively high closeness scores have short distances from other nodes in the network and are assumed to be more accessible. In the context of <sup>fl</sup>ows, nodes with high closeness scores receive more <sup>fl</sup>ow than those with low closeness scores, all else being equal. This interpretation of closeness is useful for processes where <sup>fl</sup>ows are measured along shortest paths such as vehicle traf<sup>fi</sup>c on a road network. The closeness value for the graph/network as a whole is then calculated as:

$$
C = \sum_ {i} C _ {i}\tag{3}
$$

This de<sup>fi</sup>nition can be used for non-connected networks where connected node pairs will be greater than 0 and non-connected node pairs will have a closeness value of 0, following $\begin{array} { r } { ( \frac { 1 } { d _ { i j } } = \frac { 1 } { \infty }  0 ) } \end{array}$ : according to [14]. The concept of centrality closeness is particularly relevant to the CCA measure because CCA is designed to be used with non-connected graphs resulting from various disruption scenarios where links may be completely removed from the network.

## 4.2. Travel cost and trip importance

Travel cost is a general economic reference to a non-market monetary estimate associated with a vehicle trip based on the time spent traveling from origin to destination. Generally speaking, the greater the time spent traveling, the greater the cost incurred by the traveler [27,66,74]. The measurement of travel cost is complicated by the fact that transportation-based stated preference studies<sup>1</sup> indicate that travelers place different values on different types of trips, and nondiscretionary trips that are deemed more important are treated differently than less important, purely discretionary trips. For example, a trip to work may be valued more highly than the exact same trip to the mall. The relative value of a particular trip can depend on a number of things including the purpose of the trip, travel time, distance, variation in travel-time delay, income level, and the end destination [71].

Several approaches are typically used to value trips. One approach is to assume that each unit of time has a monetary value and then assign a monetized weighted value to each trip based on the equilibrium travel time or travel-time delay. This approach is generically referred to as a value-of-time approach. An extension of this approach is to assume that the monetary value of time varies by user, vehicle and/or by travel mode. Another way of assigning value to a trip is based on the value-ofaccess to the destination. The value-of-access approach holds that trips to important destinations should be valued more highly than trips to less important destinations regardless of the travel time or delay associated with the individual trip itself. Studies that value trips by purpose implicitly utilize a value-of-access approach as the trip purpose or destination determines the importance weight. An example of the value-ofaccess approach is the traditional functional classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of roadway types by the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA), where the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is based on the access provided to/from important facilities like airports [18]. There are also approaches that distinguish between emergency trips and other trips. This valuation approach is an example of a subset of methods for assigning trip importance based on value-ofaccess referred to as critical access. Roadway links that are in close proximity to important facilities like hospitals, police stations, <sup>fi</sup>re houses, etc., are considered to be more important than links farther away. An obvious consideration in assigning an importance value to links in a roadway network based on access is the frequency of the trips that traverse each of the links. Arguably, all else being equal, a link that is frequently used to access emergency services should be more highly valued than a link that is rarely used to access emergency services. The concepts of value-of-access and critical access are foundational to the CCA methodology. Readers are referred to the text, Critical Infrastructure [42] for a compilation of research papers on critical infrastructure.

## 4.3. The critical closeness accessibility method

In this section we introduce the CCA methodology used to evaluate each roadway link based on its relative closeness to all ESFs in the network. For simplicity, destination nodes fall into one of two possible categories: 1) critical (ESFs), and 2) non-critical, where critical destinations include hospitals, police departments, ambulance houses and <sup>fi</sup>re stations. While the type and number of ESFs most certainly impact our results, the methodology is independent of the type and number of critical facilities being examined and can be extended to a hierarchical application where critical facilities are tiered with respect to their relative importance. CCA is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a number of factors including: 1) Each link's proximity to all ESFs in the network; 2) The topology of the road network with respect to all ESFs and to high-capacity roadways such as major arterials and highways; 3) The physical and spatial characteristics of each link in the network as well as the physical and spatial characteristics of the links in close proximity to the link being evaluated; and 4) Each link's relative contribution to the shortest path routing calculations between all other links to all ESFs in the network. The CCA also accounts for changes in routing that result when the link being evaluated is removed from the network and whether the link provides a single connection point to any ESFs in the network. The CCA of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c link being evaluated, $l _ { i j } ,$ in undirected graph $G = ( N , L )$ is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as:

$$
CCA_{ij} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l}\sum_{\substack{i\neq j\\ i,j\in N\\ l_{ij}\in L}}\sum_{\substack{p\neq P\\ P\subset N}}\frac{\gamma(ij),p}{2^{\left[\min(d_{(ij)}) , p,d_{(ji)},p + t_{ij}\right]}} \text{when} i \text{or} j\neq p\\ \sum_{\substack{i\neq j\\ i,j\in N\\ l_{ij}\in L}}\sum_{\substack{p\neq P\\ P\subset N}}\frac{\gamma(ij),p}{2^{t_{ij}}} \text{when} i \text{or} j = p \end{array} \right.\tag{4}
$$

We use a variation of standard notation that allows us to consider three nodes in the CCA calculation as opposed to just two, where link, $l _ { i j } ,$ has origin endpoints (i,j), and p represents an ESF destination node in the subset of ESF nodes P, for $p \in ( i , j )$ . The variables i and j refer to the opposite endpoints of link $l _ { i j } ,$ as opposed to place-based (origin, destination) locations, as typically noted. Each link, $l _ { i j } \in L ,$ is represented by the unordered pair $( i , j ) = ( j , i )$ ) where $i \neq j .$ The free <sup>fl</sup>ow travel time is the minimum travel time (in minutes) it takes to travel the path from the closest endpoint node of link $l _ { i j }$ to ESF destination node $p$ through the network. The variable $d _ { ( i j ) , p }$ is the shortest path free flow travel time between the closest link endpoint in the pair (i,j) and ESF destination, p. The shortest path free <sup>fl</sup>ow travel time is often used for evaluating distances traveled by a vehicle when modeling traf<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fl</sup>ows and is especially useful for simulating travel of emergency-response service vehicles [4].

Because we are evaluating a link, and not a speci<sup>fi</sup>c endpoint of the link, the point of origin for the shortest path calculation can be either i or j depending on the location of $p ,$ as noted by the term min $( d _ { ( i j ) , p } ,$ $d _ { ( j i ) , p } )$ . As the closest endpoint of link $l _ { i j }$ and ESF destination p does not include the free <sup>fl</sup>ow travel time on link $l _ { i j }$ itself, $t _ { i j }$ is used to represent the free <sup>fl</sup>ow travel time on $l _ { i j } .$ It is possible for an ESF node to be located at one or both endpoints of a link; however, since the shortest path from each minimum link origin point, (i,j) is calculated separately for each unique ESF destination and $( i , j ) = ( j , i )$ , it is possible for $j =$ $p , \mathrm { o r } i = p$ , but not simultaneously for a particular link origin/ESF destination (OD) pair, (i,j), p. For a given iteration of the CCA calculation, a link endpoint can never be both an origin and a destination. If one endpoint of link $l _ { i j }$ is an ESF node where either $i \ : 0 \Gamma j = p ,$ , the shortest free <sup>fl</sup>ow travel time path for that particular OD pair is the link itself, $t _ { i j } .$ Note that the denominator introduces a non-linear aspect to the CCA calculation that is sensitive to the link's proximity to all ESFs in the network – when a link is in close proximity to many ESFs, CCA increases dramatically in a non-linear manner. The opposite is true when a link is far away from most ESFs.

We introduce γ as a normalized weight to capture the relative importance of all OD nodes involved in the shortest path calculation. This includes both origin endpoint nodes associated with each link, (i, $j ) ,$ as well as the ESF destination node, $p ,$ where $0 \leq w \leq 1$ . The weighting is independent of the distinction between ESF and noncritical nodes, and therefore allows any node in the network to vary in importance with respect to its contribution to the CCA. The average weight of endpoints of the link being evaluated, (i,j), and the ESF destination node, $p ,$ is used to calculate a normalized importance weight for each link OD pair in the network using Eq. (5). The weights associated with the link endpoints, w and $w _ { j } ,$ remain constant for each link being evaluated, but the weight associated with each destination ESF, $w _ { p } ,$ changes based on the exact destination. There are p unique γ values for each link in the network. The weighting approach also works in cases where one of the endpoint nodes of the link being evaluated is an ESF. Recall that $j = p , \mathrm { o r } i = p ,$ but both i and $j \neq p$ at the same time.

$$
\gamma_ {(i j), p} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l} \left[ \frac {w _ {i} + w _ {j} + w _ {p}}{3} \right] \text {   when   } i \text {   and   } j \neq p \\ \left[ \frac {\left(w _ {i} \text {   or   } w _ {j}\right) + w _ {p}}{2} \right] \text {   when   } i \text {   or   } j = p \end{array} \right.\tag{5}
$$

A variety of techniques and attributes can be used to weight destinations according to the literature, including population, the number of opportunities at a particular destination, market share and GDP among others [8,10,12,24,25,45,61,63–65,67,73]. Correspondingly, there are many different approaches one could use to weight different origins and destinations. The use of γ offers a <sup>fl</sup>exible approach for normalizing the relative importance of different types of nodes without having to explicitly de<sup>fi</sup>ne and quantify a speci<sup>fi</sup>c set of attributes for each node. Because the nodes used in the CCA calculation range from GIS-based roadway link endpoints and intersections to different types of ESF locations – each of which has its own unique set of attributes – the use of many traditional place-based accessibility attributes may not be applicable in the context of the CCA. Our approach allows for a great deal of <sup>fl</sup>exibility in modeling different types of locations where weights can be estimated subjectively or objectively using traditional place-based attributes. For example, normalized objective weights can be derived from a quanti<sup>fi</sup>able attribute such as the population around a node [64,67]. Alternatively, weights can be based on a subjective assessment of the relative importance of a particular node.

After the normalized weights for all OD pairs are calculated, the $C C A _ { i j }$ values for each link are calculated based on the shortest path free <sup>fl</sup>ow travel times from link $l _ { i j }$ to all ESFs in the network. Higher CCA values indicate increasing importance with respect to the link's contribution to network-wide accessibility to the complete set of ESFs. Weights can be applied to all nodes in the network, to different sets of nodes in the network, or not applied at all. If no weighting is desired (or appropriate), all values of $w = 1$ and therefore all $\gamma = 1$ . The unweighted CCA is shown in Eq. (6).

$$
CCA_{ij} = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll}\sum_{\substack{i\neq j\\ i,j\in N\\ l_{ij}\in L}}\sum_{\substack{p\in P\\ P\subset N}}\frac{1}{2\left[\min \left(d_{(ij),p},d_{(ji),p}\right) + t_{ij}\right]}  when  i  or  j\neq p \\ \sum_{\substack{i\neq j\\ i,j\in N\\ l_{ij}\in L}}\sum_{\substack{p\in P\\ P\subset N}}\frac{1}{2^{t_{ij}}} &  when  i  or  j = p \end{array} \right.\tag{6}
$$

We next incorporate a link-removal component into the CCA to address situations where disruptions in the roadway network may deprive certain segments of the population of the ability to reach some set of ESFs. For example, a link with a relatively low initial CCA value could end up being essential to network-wide accessibility if its removal severely restricts, or completely eliminates, access to some set of ESF destinations. Link removal addresses important aspects of network connectivity and is consistent with the logic behind network disruption-based vulnerability/robustness measures discussed in [10,28,29,55,57,59,60,62–64,67]. The CCA calculation therefore involves two distinct components: 1) a base case calculation, and 2) a network disruption or link removal component. The <sup>fi</sup>rst component, $C C A ^ { B }$ is obtained by summing all link-speci<sup>fi</sup>c $C C A _ { i j }$ values where all links in the network are present using either the weighted or unweighted pproach from Eq. (4) or Eq. (6) respectively via Eq. (7).

$$
C C A ^ {B} = \sum_ {l _ {i j} \in L} C C A _ {i j}\tag{7}
$$

The second component is obtained by recalculating all $C C A _ { i j }$ values for all links in the network each time a link is removed. This involves recalculating all the shortest path routes from all link origin points to all ESF destinations, $d _ { ( i j ) , p } ,$ as each link is sequentially removed from the network. The CCA link-removal component is illustrated in Eq. (8) where the superscript $D ( r s )$ indicates the removal or disruption of link $l _ { r s } .$ Note that the link-speci<sup>fi</sup>c $C C A _ { i j } ^ { D ( r s ) }$ value is not de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the case where the link being evaluated is also the link that has been removed from the network, i.e. when $l _ { r s } = l _ { i j } = D _ { r s }$

$$
CCA_{ij}^{D(rs)} = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll}\sum_{\substack{i\neq j\\ r\neq s\\ i,j,r,s\in N\\ l_{ij}\in L\\ l_{ij}\neq l_{rs}\\ l_{rs\not\in d_{(ij),p},d_{(ji),p}}}}\frac{x_{(ij),p}^{(rs)}}{2\left[\min(d_{(ij),p},d_{(ji),p}) + t_{ij}\right]} & \text{when $i$ or $j\neq p$}\\ \\ \sum_{\substack{i\neq j\\ r\neq s\\ i,j,r,s\in N\\ l_{ij}\in L\\ l_{ij}\neq l_{rs}\\ l_{rs\not\in d_{(ij),p},d_{(ji),p}}}}\frac{x_{(ij),p}^{(rs)}}{2t_{ij}} & \text{when $i$ or $j = p$}\\ \\ undefined & \text{when $l_{ij} = D_{rs}} \end{array} \right.\tag{8}

## 5. Real-world application of the CCA

In this section, we provide a real-world example of the CCA using the Vermont state road network scripted using the TransCad™ Development Kit. A network module containing the exact engineering speci<sup>fi</sup>cations (traf<sup>fi</sup>c signals, lanes, speed limits, turning restrictions, capacities, volumes, etc.) of all interstate highway, major arterial, secondary arterial and rural roadway links is used to calculate the shortest free <sup>fl</sup>ow travel-times between all link endpoint origins and all ESF destinations for the entire state. This is particularly important with respect to the shortest path free flow travel times, as our calculations incorporate the physical and topographical features of the actual road network. The scope of the application is fairly large and TransCad™ is designed to model large, real-world roadway networks. The application covers a land area of 9250 mile<sup>2</sup> that includes 718 ESF destination nodes and 144,365 separate roadway links. The GIS module integrated into TransCad™ allows very <sup>fi</sup>ne granularity with respect to modeling the exact location of the endpoint nodes for each link as well as the exact location of each ESF and provides detailed results using a spatial unit of analysis at the link level. The OD pairing is technically feasible as it is possible for residents in the state to independently reach each ESF within a three- to four-hour time period. The methodological approach can be employed using census-based zones or traf<sup>fi</sup>c analysis zones if the analysis were to be scaled up to the regional or national level. The CCA can be readily adopted for congestion-based routing using a different traf<sup>fi</sup>c assignment technique build into TransCAD™.

## 6. Conclusions

In this paper, we introduce a link-focused approach for evaluating accessibility to emergency services via a road network called critical closeness accessibility (CCA). The research is directly motivated by the lack of accessibility-based modeling tools in transportation management and land use planning. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we focus on access to emergency services since trips to/from emergency service facilities are omitted from transportation planning models. The CCA approach can be used within an existing travel modeling framework to identify speci<sup>fi</sup>c links that are the most important in terms of facilitating system-wide access to emergency services; identifying populations that are at an inherent disadvantage for receiving critical emergency services; prioritizing maintenance projects; and assisting with new facility siting decisions and emergency route planning.

CCA is derived from the network-science/graph theory concept of closeness. The CCA can be used for non-connected graphs, is monotonic, and provides noticeably unique results which account for the disconnection of portions of the network due to isolating links. Unlike traditional accessibility measures that are node-focused, CCA measures the relative importance of each link in a road network in terms of its system-wide contribution to emergency service accessibility. The CCA is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by: 1) Each link's proximity to all critical facilities in the network. 2) The topology of the road network with respect to all critical facilities and to all high-speed, high-capacity roadways such as major arterials and highways. 3) The physical and spatial characteristics of each link as well as the physical and spatial characteristics of the links in close proximity to the link being evaluated. 4) Each link's relative contribution to the shortest path routing calculations between all links and all critical facilities in the network where the shortest path routing calculations account for disconnected paths.

• CCA is a link-focused measure. Existing transportation accessibility measurement approaches utilize link impedance in the accessibility calculation but assign an accessibility value to a particular node or set of nodes (or zones) and not directly to the links in the network.

• CCA builds on an alternative formulation of closeness and the residual closeness measure introduced by Dangalchev [14]. While an approach for extending the concept of closeness to links is presented in [14], disconnected networks are addressed by allowing closeness to go to zero. This approach will not work well with real-world networks such as transportation networks where disruptions can isolate portions of the population from critical services. We introduce a relative importance-based weighting approach that can be used to evaluate losses in accessibility resulting from disconnected graphs and can be employed with different node typologies. The approach provides a novel contribution to the accessibility literature as it takes into account the relative importance of both the link origin endpoints and critical destination node, while only measures of endpoint destination attractiveness are considered in existing accessibility measures.

The formulation for the CCA is based on [14] and we have employed standard/acceptable techniques for weighting and normalizing variables. It is important to note; however, that there are a variety of mathematical approaches that could be used to weight node importance and to normalize different variables in the model. It would be interesting to explore the sensitivity associated with different mathematical formulations and functional forms and the extent to which different weighting and normalization formulations might produce different results.
