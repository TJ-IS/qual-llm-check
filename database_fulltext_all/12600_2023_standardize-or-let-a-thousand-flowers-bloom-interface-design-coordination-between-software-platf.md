# STANDARDIZE OR LET A THOUSAND FLOWERS BLOOM? INTERFACE DESIGN COORDINATION BETWEEN SOFTWARE PLATFORMS AND HOSTED APPS<sup>1</sup>

Cheng Zhang School of Management, Fudan University Shanghai, China {zhangche@fudan.edu.cn}

Peijian Song Business School, Nanjing University Nanjing, China {songpeijian@nju.edu.cn}

Kai Lim Faculty of Business, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University Hong Kong, China {kai.lim@polyu.edu.hk}

Software platform ecosystems are emerging as a dominant model for IT-based services. To accelerate third-party development, platform owners often waive restrictions on app user interfaces to provide app developers with more autonomy in interface design. The literature has indicated, however, that standardized interface design across products is a strategic necessity for firms to introduce and manage their product lines, suggesting that platform owners should not waive restrictions on app user interfaces. This tension between the two approaches raises a crucial research question: Does standardized interface design used by apps and their host platform benefit the platform and apps that it hosts? Specifically, does the coordinated design approach enhance or hinder usage between the platform and apps such that apps attract usage from their host platform and vice versa? Drawing on the theory of basic Gestalts, we theorize the three interface design properties of interface similarity, embeddedness, and synchrony, and extend the theory to investigate how these properties help both apps and their host platform attract each other’s users, thus reflecting derivative usage between them. We performed a randomized field experiment to test the proposed hypotheses. The results reveal that interface similarity, embeddedness, and synchrony of the app-platform interface design strengthen users’ grouping perception of the platform and its apps, thus enhancing platform-to-app forward derivative usage. Contrary to our expectations, we found that this strengthened grouping perception does not improve the app-to-platform backward derivative usage. These findings have crucial implications for the value of interface design in software platform ecosystems.

Keywords: Software platform, interface design, derivative usage, visual perception, theory of basic Gestalts

## Introduction

Software platforms such as Google’s Chrome and Android are extensible codebases of software systems that provide core functionalities for third-party apps that can run on them (Baldwin & Woodard, 2009). Leveraging this technological architecture, platforms extend their product boundaries by attracting numerous third-party apps that can create complementary value (Boudreau, 2012; Ceccagnoli et al., 2012). To accelerate third-party development in software platform ecosystems, platform owners often waive their restrictions on the app user interface (UI) design, providing app developers with more autonomy in product development (Boudreau, 2010). While this approach has been viewed as a great strength of software platforms in “letting a thousand flowers bloom” (e.g., Boudreau, 2012), the literature on product line design, which is relatable to general platform design (Bosch & Bosch-Sijtsema, 2010; Jiao et al., 2007), has found that standardized UI design across products provides strategic advantages and is a necessity for firms introducing and managing their product lines (Nambisan, 2002).

Specifically, these integrated design features such as interface standardization and interface linkages are favored because they provide a seamless user experience and thus help newly developed products attract more users of existing products (Sengupta, 1998). In particular, studies on software product lines have established that UI standardization across existing and new software products, as well as the interface linkage between them, enhance the performance of new products (Gopal & Gosain, 2010; McIntyre & Subramaniam, 2009), which also reduces the cost of adopting and learning to use a new product. Moreover, increasing the use of a new product can consolidate users’ willingness to stick to existing products. That is, satisfaction and familiarity with a new component can motivate users to stick with the integrated system (Schilling, 2000). The product line literature has further indicated the critical role that cross-product design plays in integrating the focal product with complementary (new) products at both interface and function levels (Nambisan, 2002). This design strategy has been widely applied in product family software applications, such as Microsoft Office software suites (Schilling, 2000), and internet-based product and service linkage, such as hypertext links (Song et al., 2009; Stewart, 2006).

The tension between app autonomy and cross-product interface design principles reflects the “seesaw” problem of platform owners who “must strike a delicate balance between autonomy granted to app developers and the integration of their work with the platform” (Tiwana, 2013, p. 42). While the literature has argued that this tension can be managed by aligning architecture and governance at the app level (Wareham et al., 2014), the role that integrated or coordinated interface design plays in relationships between the platform and its apps—termed integrated app-platform interface design in this study—has yet to be adequately explored. Specifically, our research question is whether or not the integrated app-platform interface design can reinforce derivative usage between a platform and its apps, defined as the association between platform usage and app usage, which mutually benefit each other.

We draw on and extend the theory of basic Gestalts (TBG) in the perceptual psychology domain (Pomerantz & Portillo, 2011) to investigate how users perceptually group a platform and its apps according to the app-platform interface design. Along with the theoretical perspective of addressing the human visual perception of an object as a whole rather than the sum of its parts, we propose three key integrated interface design principles: interface similarity, interface embeddedness, and interface synchrony. These design principles are mutually exclusive and exhibit unique yet interdependent properties. Interface similarity refers to the degree to which apps and the platform share visual properties (e.g., color, size, or shape). Interface embeddedness refers to the degree to which an app is surrounded by the platform’s interface region. Interface synchrony is the extent to which an app changes simultaneously with the change in users’ actions on its platform’s interface. We theorize that these three interface design principles affect users’ perception in terms of whether an app is part of the platform or a separate entity, which the literature has termed grouping perception.

We further extend TBG to better understand the downstream effects of grouping perception on users’ usage behavior by delineating the mechanisms underlying its influence on two directions of derivative usage between the platform and hosted apps. We propose derivative usage to reflect mutually beneficial app-platform relations/associations, where its two directions include platform-to-app forward derivative usage—which relates to the usage derived from platform usage to app usage (the benefit an app derives from platform usage)—and app-to-platform backward derivative usage, which refers to the usage derived from app usage to platform usage (the benefit a platform derives from app usage). To test our hypotheses, we conducted a randomized field experiment with 541 participants that explicitly manipulated interface similarity, embeddedness, and synchrony to examine their effects on integrated interface designs.

This research makes several theoretical contributions. First, it supplements the software platform literature by providing a theory-based elucidation of the value of app-platform integrated interface design. The study further extends Gestalt theory to postulate the downstream effects of grouping perception on user behaviors. It also enriches the humancomputer interaction (HCI) literature on the interrelated design stimulus and the platform literature on the balance between autonomy and integrated or standardized functions. Finally, it advances the knowledge of multiple technology usage and conceptualizes the associations between software platform use and hosted apps as a bidirectional derivative usage. The study also contributes guidelines on how software platforms can flexibly leverage apps’ integrated interface design to achieve a stronger mutual benefit in software ecosystems.

## Theory and Hypothesis Development

## Value Creation from a Platform and its Apps

A software platform ecosystem consists of two key subsystems: platform and apps. Both may provide end users with values or benefits depending on whether the product or service meets users’ needs (Song et al., 2018). Specifically, users may find a software platform a valuable option when its core (the platform) or peripheral components (the apps) directly satisfy their needs. A platform can decide whether to provide value to its users directly or via third-party apps by evolving its core and boundary (Tiwana, 2013).

Accordingly, there are two typical types of value creation paths. As shown in Appendix A, the first platform type (Type 1) relies mainly on the platform’s core functions to engage end users, while the second type (Type 2) enhances support for the apps and relies more on its interfaces to engage end users. Although end-user values can be derived from both the platform’s core functions and various apps operating on the platform, most successful platform ecosystems, such as Facebook, Firefox, and R, contain their own stand-alone products or services for native value creation within a system of use (Tiwana, 2013; Wareham et al., 2014). These Type 1 platforms and apps also provide unique UI visible to end users. Conversely, Type 2 platforms mainly support apps’ UI at functional levels that are invisible to end users. As Type 1 platforms are better suited to our purposes, this study focuses on integrated app-platform interface design in Type 1 platforms.

## App-Platform User Interface

As noted, value creation can be implemented at both function and interface levels (Nambisan, 2002). The core function work performed by an app is distinctive to the platform, including the app functionalities that make it uniquely valuable to end users. For example, a messaging app’s core function is to enable communications between two or more client devices, while a social networking app’s core function is to support social interactions and relationship management between two or more users. The functions are thought to represent the application logic of an app, whereas the UI is the app’s representation logic, where almost all of the interactions with the end users occur (Tiwana, 2013). The UI is the part of the application that manages input from end users and presents the app’s output to end users. In this way, the UI helps end users perceive the app’s use values.

In practice, platforms such as Firefox and Chrome have completely relinquished control of the display properties of the interfaces of individual apps, such as color, button shape, and button size. Nevertheless, allowing such autonomy in interface design directly contradicts the principle of crossproduct interface standardization established in the product innovation and software development literatures (e.g., Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013).

This study is particularly interested in interface-level design for platforms and apps for two reasons. First, as the UI represents the presentation logic of the applications where visual interactions between the apps and end users happen (Tiwana, 2013), it can play a key role in facilitating user perceptions of interactions between the platform and its complements (McIntyre & Srinivasan, 2017). In contrast to the rich discussions on the interdependency between core functionality design and peripheral functionality development (e.g., Tiwana et al., 2010; Wareham et al., 2014; Jacobides et al., 2018), the value of UI design on both the platform and its apps has been largely overlooked in the platform literature.

Second, while the literature on product lines and HCI has, for decades, articulated the importance of UI design, new contexts in the emergence of software platforms require a careful reexamination of the role that the UI plays in app-platform interactions. Since the evaluation of apps places demands on users’ cognitive resources, and as apps usually have many product features to be evaluated, users tend to rely on simple heuristics to assess product value (Kahneman, 2000). Besides functional features that reflect a product’s quality, visual interfaces provide intuitive and valuable heuristics that users can apply to evaluate the interdependent relationship between two products (i.e., an app and its platform) (e.g., Stewart, 2006). We therefore argue that it is important to examine the impact of UI designs on usage behavior in the platform context.

## App-Platform User Interface Design and Grouping

Irrespective of the methods used to link and facilitate communication between platforms and apps, users perceive— primarily visually—the relationships between apps and platforms via their interactions. The essence of this visual perception process is grouping objects as a whole entity based on their observed relationships (Wagemans et al., 2012), called Gestalt phenomenon in the vision science and perceptual psychology fields. The literature has indeed confirmed that humans are more likely to perceive grouping through the interrelationships among objects than through the objects themselves (Greene & Oliva, 2009). This means the perceived interrelationships between or among objects can be viewed as topological properties, which Gestalt theory positions as “emergent features” that influence human grouping perception. Taking software platforms as an example, users are more inclined to group a software platform and its apps as a whole through perceiving the interrelationship between them (e.g., an app is displayed inside a mobile operating system interface, creating a spatial relationship between the app and the operating system) than through the properties of the apps themselves, such as the size of app’s clickable button.

In formally introducing the theory of basic Gestalts (TBG) to define and measure Gestalt principles as emergent features, Pomerantz and Portillo (2011) focused on topological properties that arise from the interrelations among objects (rather than singular properties of individual objects) that affect human grouping perception (Chen, 2005). As the underlying logic behind TBG relies on uncovering wholes that can be more easily distinguished in human visual perception than the parts from which they are built (Pomerantz & Portillo, 2011), this phenomenon is also referred to as the “part-whole” relationship of objects, in terms of associations between/among objects (parts) that further influence the human grouping perception (whole) of the objects.

Although TBG has so far received little attention in IS research, the theory provides a particularly valuable information processing framework to investigate how users perceive the app-platform (parts) relationship in an ecosystem (the whole) through specific topological properties reflected in UI designs. In the following sections, we develop arguments on how the salience of three topological properties in UI design (interface similarity, embeddedness, and synchrony) can significantly affect users’ grouping perception of the platform and its apps (see Appendix B).

## Interface Similarity and App-Platform Grouping

While the Gestalt literature has identified the impact of topological properties on overall perception (Pomerantz & Portillo, 2011; Chen, 2005; Boremanse et al., 2013), software platforms’ unique characteristics need to be considered when adapting these properties to the app-platform UI design context. First, in app-platform environments, the functional relationships between a platform and its apps are usually invisible to end users. For example, application programming interfaces (APIs) present a typical case in which how apps exploit the platform’s functionality is not visible to end users, while the UI where these interactions occur creates the user’s visual perception field (Tiwana, 2013; McIntyre & Srinivasan, 2017). This is particularly true for Type 1 platforms whose UIs are also directly visible to end users.

Related to this characteristic is the topological property of similarity, wherein people are more likely to group objects with similar visual properties such as color and texture than elements that differ in these dimensions (Wagemans et al., 2012). In the app-platform context, we define interface similarity as the degree of similarity between visual attributes (such as fonts and buttons) in the UI of a platform and the apps it hosts. Taking the Google Chrome add-ons Google Scholar Button (GSB) and Playerline News (PN) as examples, GSB provides easy access to Google Scholar from any web page in Chrome, while PN offers timely football news from top news websites in a single feed (see Appendix C). But the two addons have completely different interface styles: the GSB system’s default font format and white background are the same as Chrome’s interface settings, while PN uses its own font format and a black background, which looks totally different than Chrome’s default settings. Although platform owners’ allowance of autonomous design has rendered interface similarity less controllable, an app can still customize its interface properties (such as button size or interface color) to make it appear similar to the platform’s interface. Drawing on the argument related to topological property in similarity and grouping, we propose that the higher the similarity of an app-platform interface design, the more likely it will be that users will perceive a platform and its apps to be grouped as a whole. Conversely, users will likely perceive a weak association between a platform and its apps or perceive them as independent parts when their interface properties are visually different. We therefore posit:

H1: The level of interface similarity between a platform and the apps that it hosts strengthens users’ grouping perception of the platform and its apps.

## Embeddedness and App-Platform Grouping

Another unique characteristic of software platforms is the limited screen size a platform and its apps can use for display, especially on mobile devices. Computer and cellphone screens are limited whether they use Type 1 platforms like Chrome, Facebook, or WeChat or Type 2 platforms like iOS, Android, Windows, and the associated apps. Limited screen space thus causes closed and continuous (rather than open and discrete) boundaries in interface designs, creating properties that reflect salient relationships (such as inside/outside the boundary) between the platform and app space boundaries.

Related to this characteristic is the topological property of interface embeddedness in the Gestalt literature, which proposes the tendency for visual elements that lie within the same bounded area (or region) to be grouped together perceptually (Palmer, 1992; Palmer & Rock, 1994). Like similarity, embeddedness is not a property of the objects themselves but a holistic perception consisting of the spatial relationship of objects (such as being inside/outside) that affects users’ overall perception of the objects. The theory suggests that regardless of any individual object’s physical attributes (e.g., shape, size, or color), its appearance in a particular location is important for helping people group the parts as a whole (Chen, 2005). The embeddedness of an object also has an ecological rationale: When a bounded region encloses a number of visual objects, they are likely to be elements on the surface of a single object, such as human facial features or a leopard’s spots (Wagemans et al., 2012). Empirical research has confirmed that embeddedness is a salient property that facilitates people’s grouping perception (e.g., Palmer, 1992; Pomerantz & Portillo, 2011).

In the platform context, an app is activated by clicking or tapping an icon in the app-platform interface design. Upon activation, the app then moves to the front of the platform interface, where it can be designed to change its size, shape, or color. Given the various development rights granted to app developers, apps can also vary their display location on the screen. Accordingly, we define embeddedness on a software platform as the degree to which an app stays within or inside a platform’s interface boundary when a user activates or uses it. Low embeddedness refers to a relationship in which apps work outside the platform border when they are activated; high embeddedness refers to a relationship in which apps work within the platform border when activated. For example, while GSB and PN both display a fixed interface inside Google Chrome (thus causing users to perceive high embeddedness on Chrome’s platform), the company’s add-on 2048—Retro Classic Games (RCG)—provides flash games that pop up in a separate window outside of Chrome (refer to Appendix C), causing users to perceive low embeddedness on the platform. In some cases, where a software platform completely occupies the screen (e.g., on a mobile device), the app that appears on the upper layer of the platform and fully covers the platform interface with its running window will be perceived as outside the platform and will thus be perceived as having low embeddedness.

Embeddedness influences the perception of the platform and its apps as a whole, such that when an app is displayed within the platform borders for use, it exhibits a higher perception of “togetherness.” This causes embeddedness to become a salient topological property that users can perceive (Palmer & Rock

1994), which leads to a stronger grouping perception. In contrast, when an app is displayed outside the platform borders after being activated, users are more likely to perceive the app as a separate part of the platform. We therefore propose:

H2: Higher levels of embeddedness strengthen users’ grouping perception of a platform and its apps.

## Synchrony and App-Platform Grouping

The third unique characteristic of software platforms is that they allow app use only, but apps cannot change the platform through APIs (Tiwana, 2018). This means that temporal topological properties need to be designed with the understanding that apps will not be able to manipulate or change the platform. As shown in Appendix C, when a user activates an app on a platform, there may be temporal changes in the app (e.g., responding quickly and displaying its operation window) but the software platform will remain stationary and cannot be controlled or changed by the app. This implies a unilateral motion of apps rather than the platform in this context.

Related to this characteristic is interface synchrony, originally defined as the tendency for parts to change simultaneously or in a similar manner (Alais et al., 1998; Lee & Blake, 1999); part changes do not have to involve motion or be in the same direction of change (Wagemans et al., 2012) but are nonetheless grouped together. The underlying mechanism of synchrony is the perception of association arising from human detection mechanisms: when humans observe that the temporal coincidence of changes is unlikely to be based on chance alone, they perceive it as having some common underlying cause related to the changing events. According to this mechanism, the temporal contiguity of events, rather than the changes themselves, is central to synchrony (Alais et al., 1998; Gegenfurtner, 1998), wherein the association perception decreases as the time interval between events increases. For instance, research has shown that when intervals are longer than approximately 0.1 seconds, human association perception begins to decrease (Cravo et al., 2015). This also implies that the association perception is intrinsically assumed for changes occurring with temporal coincidence regardless of whether those changes are causally related. Neural-level studies recognizing the synchrony of neural firings as the physiological mechanism through which the brain codes all forms of grouping have confirmed this phenomenon at a subconscious level (Singer & Gray, 1995; von der Malsburg, 2021).

Accordingly, we define synchrony on a software platform as the degree to which an app changes simultaneously in response to a user’s actions on the platform’s interface. The temporal contiguity between the platform and the app begins when users activate an app on the platform (such as clicking on the app-related menu/button on the platform) and ends when the app being displayed is ready for use. The app’s responsive time during the process therefore reflects the extent of synchrony between the platform and the app. For example, Google Chrome’s add-on Evernote Web Clipper (EW) helps users save webpage content (see Appendix C). Unlike some other add-ons that display interfaces immediately, EW displays a pop-up window indicating that the app is loading. This waiting time before the add-on is loaded causes users to perceive it as an external stand-alone app. Longer response times often arise from the app checking the user’s account externally or loading features (e.g., if the app determines that the user has not yet logged in, it will generate a pop-up window with a separate login screen).

Rapid response times between a platform and an app would likely cause users to perceive the two as constituting a single entity in the time dimension (Sekuler & Bennett, 2001; Wagemans et al., 2012). In this case, high synchrony should strengthen the perception of a platform and the app as a group. In contrast, response times between a platform and an app would likely cause users to perceive a weak association between the platform and the app, leading them to view them as independent entities. Lower synchrony would therefore be expected to lead users to perceive a weaker grouping perception of the platform and the app. Consequently, we propose:

H3: Higher levels of synchrony strengthen users’ grouping perception of a platform and its apps.

## Grouping and Derivative Usage

While Gestalt theory presents topological property as a basis for our study to develop UI design principles in the app-platform context, the theory does not further explain user behaviors after they form grouping perceptions. We therefore extend TBG to postulate the downstream effects of grouping perception on user behaviors by discussing the mechanisms underlying the influence of grouping perceptions on platform and app usage. Furthermore, as both platforms and apps create use values for their users, two directions of derivative usage emerge: (1) platform-to-app usage, which is beneficial for apps in that it allows apps to attract increased usage from platform users, and (2) app-to-platform usage, which is beneficial for platforms in that the apps can encourage users to increase their usage of the host platform (Li & Agarwal, 2017).

The nature of app usage in driving platform usage and vice versa closely relates to the concept of extended use in the IS literature, defined as using more of a technology’s features (Hsieh & Wang, 2007). Although extended usage was originally conceptualized for single-technology use, since platforms and apps are different technologies and complement each other in terms of functionalities, the association between their use is more appropriate when viewed from the multitechnology use context. In adapting the concept of extended use to the platform context, this study describes the phenomenon as derivative usage and defines it as the mutually beneficial association between platform and app use.<sup>2</sup>

Furthermore, while the extended use literature has largely defined a unidirectional association of use, it is the interdependence and coordination between platforms and apps that causes bidirectional cross-product use. We thus propose that platform use enhances app use as forward derivative usage and that app use enhances platform use as backward derivative usage. Based on the trust and the complementary relationship between the focal and the secondary product, we argue that two explanatory mechanisms, trust transference (Stewart, 2003) and synergistic specificity (Schilling, 2000), can be applied to the bidirectional effects, respectively. Trust transference is a cognitive process wherein users’ trust in an existing object (e.g., product or service) can be transferred to a new object (i.e., from a platform to a new app in our context) through perceiving the association between them. Synergistic specificity is the degree to which a system functions more effectively through specific components that are inseparably connected to each other (Shilling, 2000). It refers to users being more likely to use an integrated system (the platform in our case) after perceiving a complementary relationship between the components (i.e., the apps) and the system.

## Platform-to-App Forward Derivative Usage

In a platform ecosystem, users usually adopt a platform before starting to use new apps and accessing apps through the platform. Users are therefore more familiar with the platform and will consider it more trustworthy than its apps, particularly for new apps, making it more likely that trust transfer will be unilateral from the platform to the apps. Stewart (2003) theorized and empirically validated that when a relatively well-known website provides a hyperlink to an unknown website, users’ trust in the known website can be transferred to the unknown website via their perceived association or indirect sense of endorsement. The literature has also confirmed that trust transference enhances users intention to use new services and products (Song et al., 2009; Stewart, 2006). Likewise, just as the initial trust in one entity can be transferred to an unknown or less-trusted entity when the two are linked or perceived to be related (Lim et al., 2006), trust transfer can also occur from a platform to its apps. Accordingly, we postulate that in the platform context, the trust transferred from a known platform to an unknown app would result in a corresponding forward derivative usage from the platform to the app.

This discussion raises the following question: Do users’ grouping perceptions moderate platform-to-app forward derivative usage? As grouping perception increases, the extent of users’ perception of the part-whole relationship between the platform and its apps is strengthened. This part-whole relationship therefore promotes the transfer of user trust in the known platform to unknown apps. That is, when users perceive that a new app is part of the platform, they should be more likely to trust and use it. Consequently, users’ favorable evaluation of the platform should transfer to the app and lead to a greater intention to use it (Hamilton & Sherman, 1996; Crawford et al., 2002), resulting in enhanced platform-to-app derivative usage.

In contrast, when users’ grouping perception is weak, they are less likely to recognize the platform and its apps as belonging to the same group. Consequently, they are not likely to immediately trust the unknown app because they cannot predict its effect or usage value. In this situation, transferring the initial sense of trust from the platform to the app is difficult, which weakens platform-to-app forward derivative usage. When an app and platform are perceived as completely unrelated, users are likely to be more skeptical of their value. In this case, they will think more carefully about the security risks associated with trying out this completely unknown app. We therefore predict that a weak grouping perception will make users less likely to transfer their platform usage behavior to the app, while a strong grouping perception will enhance platform-to-app usage. Consequently, we posit:

H4: The grouping perception between a platform and its apps enhances platform-to-app forward derivative usage in terms of use behavior, such that a stronger grouping perception will result in stronger platform-to-app forward derivative usage.

## App-to-Platform Backward Derivative Usage

A platform’s potential comes not just from its inherent qualities but from the variety of apps that end users access (Wareham et al., 2014; Jacobides et al., 2018), suggesting that a platform’s attractiveness to end users can be influenced by the apps it hosts (Tiwana, 2013). App-to-platform backward derivative usage can be explained by the synergistic specificity mechanism, where the platform’s overall value or utility to users will also increase because of the increased value of its specific components. The positive value engendered by an app adds weight to the platform and prompts users to evaluate it more positively (Tiwana, 2013). Because favorable evaluations of a product lead to a greater intention to use it (Hamilton & Sherman, 1996; Crawford et al., 2002), the effect from a particular app to a platform in terms of usage will be enhanced. Consequently, users’ willingness to explore other platform features (Boudreau, 2012; Ceccagnoli et al., 2012) confirms app-to-platform backward derivative usage.

This discussion raises the corresponding question: Do users’ grouping perceptions moderate app-to-platform backward derivative usage? The literature on synergistic specificity has argued that providing greater user understanding or confidence that components will work well together is one critical way to achieve component-system synergy (Mikkola, 2006). Users will be more likely to have confidence in a system assembled by components when they understand how the components work together. In cases where the nature of the interaction between components is uncertain, users will be less likely to use the components together. Instead, they may seek other integrated solutions such as, in our context, alternative functionality provided by the platform or app alone (Schilling, 2000).

When the grouping perception between an app and its platform is strong, such as when an app is thought to be an integrated part of the platform, users’ favorable evaluations of the app will likely primarily be attributed to the platform (Hamilton & Sherman, 1996; Crawford et al., 2002), resulting in an increased synergistic effect between app usage and platform usage. Hence, app-to-platform usage will be strengthened. In contrast, when users’ grouping perception between an app and its platform is weak—i.e., when the platform and app are perceived by users as two separate entities—the app’s positive evaluation may be largely attributed to the app only rather than the platform. In this case, app-to-platform usage will be weakened. We therefore posit:

H5: The grouping perception between a platform and its apps enhances app-to-platform backward derivative usage in terms of use behavior, such that a stronger grouping perception will result in stronger app-to-platform backward derivative usage.

## Field Experiment

To test the proposed hypotheses, we conducted a randomized field experiment in which interface similarity, embeddedness, and synchrony were explicitly manipulated to examine the

effect of integrating interface designs. The experiment used a 2×2×2 between-subjects factorial design with three independent variables: interface similarity (low/high), embeddedness (low/high), and synchrony (low/high). To ensure sufficient power and predicted medium effect size for the 2×2×2 factorial design, 541 subjects participated in the experiment. Prior to the study, we informed the participants that they would receive RMB ¥50 (approximately US\$8) as an incentive for their participation.

![](/api/attachments/J3VFZ6XQ/fulltext/images/770392ce494c2933ba54c2459ac88e336d4acf3ef726ee949172eb6bb2e9579a.jpg)

We developed an information-type app to collect real usage data in a natural, unobtrusive environment with manipulation of the independent variables. We made the app available to users of WeChat, the largest social network platform in China. Like Facebook, WeChat is a popular mobile-based social networking platform that allows third-party developers to add functionalities to the platform’s core functionalities by registering an Official Account (similar to the Facebook Page) (refer to Appendix D) for their business. Although WeChat is an app that runs on Android and iOS platforms, it operates as a platform business model by encouraging third parties to develop customer services, e-commerce, payment functionalities, and CRM using WeChat’s Official Accounts. Through providing API integration with the platform, the accounts provide users with app-like experiences within WeChat. In 2020, the number of Official Accounts (business accounts) for this platform reached 20 million.<sup>3</sup>

We developed an information-type app that provides an entertainment service by publishing 10 jokes every day. We manipulated interface similarity, embeddedness, and synchrony by developing eight versions of the app specifically for this study. The eight versions shared the same features and only differed in their interface in terms of the three factors under investigation: (1) in the high-similarity versions, the app shared the same font size and background color as those of the WeChat platform; (2) in the low-similarity versions, the app’s font size and background color contrasted with those of the WeChat platform (refer to Appendix E); and (3) in the highembeddedness versions, the app’s inputs and outputs were completely embedded within the WeChat platform and users could browse the app’s content without leaving the platform. In the low-embeddedness versions, the app appeared on the upper layer of the platform and fully covered the platform interface after the app was activated, and users browsed the app content in a separate off-platform window. This practice is not unique to WeChat; it also appears on other platforms with the LINE MINI and TikTok Jump apps, for example. As manipulating the response time between a platform’s activation and an app’s display varies the extent of synchrony perception, synchrony in our experiment was operationalized as how quickly the platform allowed users to visit the app. In high-synchrony versions, platform users could visit the app immediately; in low-synchrony versions, there was a fivesecond wait time before the app loaded and was ready to use. Since apps cannot manipulate or change the platform (i.e., when a user activates an app on the platform), the software platform remained stationary and only temporal changes happened in the app. The temporal-synchrony manipulation is thus in line with platform reality.

Taking the synchrony and embeddedness combination as an example of how multiple factors are manipulated in the experiment, in the Low-synchrony × Low-embeddedness condition, users had to wait five seconds before the content was displayed on an external webpage, whereas in the High-synchrony × Low-embeddedness condition, the external webpage was displayed to users immediately on an external HTML webpage. Accordingly, in the Low-synchrony × High-embeddedness condition, users had to wait five seconds before the content was displayed; in the high-synchrony × High-embeddedness condition, the content was displayed immediately.

## Procedure

The study’s field experiment had two phases (see Appendix F). Before the experiment started, study participants were recruited through the WeChat platform. They were asked to complete a background questionnaire about their demographic characteristics, experience, and platform (i.e., WeChat) usage. When the first phase (P<sub>1</sub>) of the experiment started (T<sub>0</sub>), participants were invited to install the app and start using it. The eight versions of the app were randomly distributed to the study participants. The platform only listed one app title, but when subjects installed the app, our system randomly allocated one of the eight versions to them. The participants could log onto the app at any time and from anywhere. Based on the participants’ unique ID, we were able to ensure that they accessed the same version in subsequent visits.

After using the app for the first time, the study participants were prompted to complete an online questionnaire, which included items for manipulation checks and their grouping perceptions. The first phase of the experiment ended (T<sub>1</sub>) when the subjects successfully installed the app and completed the questionnaire. The second phase (P<sub>2</sub>) started immediately after the questionnaire was completed. Participants were then encouraged to keep using the app for the next two weeks. During this period (second phase), the app recorded each participant’s objective app usage behaviors. At the end day of the second phase (T<sub>2</sub>), we invited the study participants to complete a second questionnaire about their platform usage behaviors and then performed the manipulation checks again. In the introduction section of this questionnaire, we clearly indicated that the platform refers to WeChat and the app refers to the joke app.

We initially recruited a total of 992 subjects. At T<sub>1</sub>, 788 participants had successfully installed the app and completed the first questionnaire. Between T<sub>1</sub> and T<sub>2</sub>, some participants uninstalled and reinstalled the app. These participants were removed from subsequent data analysis. After the survey was completed at T<sub>2</sub>, we received 541 valid questionnaires, a response rate of 54.5%. As the WeChat platform has strict constraints on collecting platform usage data due to privacy and security concerns, we used questionnaires to collect users’ subjective perceptions about their platform usage.

All constructs were measured using validated items from prior literature, with minor wording changes to fit our context. The measures for platform usage were adopted from Wixom and Todd (2005). We adapted items dealing with grouping perception from Overvliet et al. (2012) and Yang et al. (2016).

Although we were unable to access objective (actual) platform usage data, actual app usage behavior was collected based on the app login data. This worked in our case because the app was developed by the research team for the sole purpose of this study and the participants agreed to let the team collect their usage data when they signed up for the experiment. To more comprehensively capture usage intensity, we followed Venkatesh and Morris (2000) in employing the duration and frequency of use and applied the adapted objective formative measures to measure app usage. Based on the app login data, we calculated and employed the frequency and duration of app usage by participants during the second phase of the experiment, which lasted two weeks.

Factors such as gender, age, platform use experience, time spent on the platform, and the number of apps used on the platform were incorporated as covariates in the data analysis. To check the extent of response bias, we treated participants who did not complete the entire experiment as the no-response group and compared the no-response group and the response group on these five factors. The results indicate no significant differences between the two groups.

The eight versions of the app were randomly distributed to the study participants so that any differences across groups could only be attributed to the integrated interface designs. By looking at changes in grouping perceptions among different groups, our field experiment design allowed us to control for various sources of confounding factors, including selection bias and peer influence, making it possible to rule out most alternative explanations, such as users’ preference or confirmation bias. We used univariate analyses of variance (ANOVA) to test H1, H2, and H3. H4 and H5 were tested by structural equation modeling (SEM).

## Results

We conducted independent sample t-tests to confirm the manipulations of the independent variables. Results show a significant difference between the means for different levels of interface similarity (t = -11.837, p < 0.01) confirming that the manipulation of interface similarity was effective. In the same way, we also confirmed that the manipulation of embeddedness and synchrony were all effective. The t-test statistics are t = -17.208 (p < 0.01) and t = -3.870 (p < 0.01), respectively.

We next examined the reliability and validity of the constructs in the study. Cronbach’s alphas for grouping perception and platform usage intention were 0.90 and 0.93, respectively, demonstrating adequate internal consistency reliability for the two constructs. We conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to assess the validity of these two constructs convergent and discriminant validity, utilizing the principal components method with varimax rotation. The EFA consistently provided three factors: within the rotated component matrix, loadings of items on their corresponding factor were higher than 0.70, which was higher than loadings of other items on this factor and higher than loadings of these items on the other factors. Average variances extracted (AVEs) for grouping perception, platform usage, and app usage were above 0.50, demonstrating convergent validity. All square roots of AVEs were greater than the corresponding correlations, thus demonstrating discriminant validity.

We used univariate analyses of variance (ANOVA) to test H1, H2, and H3. As shown in Table 1, for H1, H2, and H3, we found significant main effects of similarity (F = 23.307, p < 0.01), embeddedness (F = 14.122, p < 0.01), and synchrony (F = 6.239, p < 0.05) on grouping perception, supporting H1, H2 and H3. We did not find any significant two-way or threeway interaction term on grouping perception.

The full model was tested by SEM using PLS using the bootstrap resampling method.<sup>4</sup> Figure 2 presents the estimated standardized path coefficients.<sup>5</sup> Platform usage at T<sub>0</sub> had a significant positive effect on app usage from T<sub>1</sub> to T<sub>2</sub> (0.264, $p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ , suggesting the existence of platform-to-app forward derivative usage. App usage from T<sub>1</sub> to T<sub>2</sub> further significantly enhanced platform usage at T<sub>2</sub> (0.361, p < 0.01), indicating the existence of app-to-platform backward derivative usage. Grouping perception at T<sub>1</sub> had a significant moderating effect on platform-to-app forward derivative usage (0.365, p < 0.01).<sup>6</sup> Thus, H4 is supported.

In contrast to our expectations, the moderating effect of grouping perception on app-to-platform backward derivative usage was not significant (0.041, p > 0.1). Therefore, H5 is not supported. One plausible explanation for this result is that the predicted synergistic effect may have followed the law of diminishing marginal utility in our research context (Greene & Baron, 2001). According to the law of diminishing marginal utility, the consumption of most goods follows a pattern in which the additional benefit or amount of utility of goods decreases with each additional unit of consumption. Similarly, we believe that the synergistic effect initially positively influenced app-to-platform backward derivative usage. However, when numerous apps are operating on a platform, the additional utility provided by new apps is diminished. Accordingly, the grouping perception of the marginal utility is attenuated, leading to no significant app-to-platform backward derivative usage.

## Discussion

## Theoretical Implications

The theoretical implications of this study are manifold. First, it contributes to platform ecosystem research by providing a novel perspective on third-party product development and usage. The open platform development strategy of “letting a thousand flowers bloom” (Boudreau, 2012)—wherein platform owners seek product variety and reach by aggressively pursuing thirdparty app developers—often results in undermining product interface design principles. This, in turn, may be a root cause of chaos for platforms and thus hinder their long-term development (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013). This autonomy-development tension has received scant attention in the literature. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to develop theory-based arguments and empirical evidence substantiating the need for interface design standardization in platform ecosystems. Our research thus provides a novel theoretical perspective to the discourse and understanding of the difficulty of fostering rapid growth while avoiding potential chaos in the platform ecosystem.

Second, this study contributes to the platform ecosystem literature by advancing the understanding of platform ecosystem management and growth from the perspective of technical design. While it has been argued that technical design may impact platform ecosystem performance (Tiwana et al., 2010), studies exploring the role of technical design in software platform ecosystems are scarce (Boudreau, 2010; Ceccagnoli et al., 2012). Nevertheless, with the expansion of product boundaries, software platform ecosystems offer a unique opportunity to accentuate interface design in IS theory development (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Tiwana, et al. 2010). We contextualize the theory of basic Gestalts (Pomerantz & Portillo, 2011) to platform ecosystems and extend the theory to reveal inherent linkages between app-platform interface design and platform-to-app derivative usage. This approach provides an in-depth exploration of the role of app-platform interface design in the software platform ecosystem. We thereby contribute to platform ecosystem research by developing a theory-based understanding of how certain design principles can benefit the ecosystem as a whole. This study thus confirms that searching for and validating design principles can serve as a benchmark and foundation in future quests for comprehensive platform ecosystem design components.

Third, our study supplements TBG by extending it to the platform ecosystems context. While we incorporate the theory of basic Gestalts from the perceptual psychology field as a new perspective in the platform interface design literature, we also recognize the theory’s limitations in associating grouping perception with behavioral consequences. By integrating trust transference and synergistic specificity mechanisms, the study applies and extends the original theory to postulate the downstream effects of grouping perception on user behaviors in the app-platform context.

This unique theoretical framework provides the missing link between the topological properties of interface design and app-platform usage. By integrating the psychology and IS literatures, the study also contributes to the perceptual psychology literature on two fronts: (1) By applying the original TBG to examine the technical interface of platform ecosystems, we extend the scope of applicability of TBG, and (2) by theorizing and validating the downstream effects of grouping perception on user behaviors, we render the original TBG more functional and accessible to research disciplines that are more applied in nature and more interested in user behaviors in general.

Finally, the study advances our knowledge on the multitechnology usage literature. Technology usage is one central research theme of the IS domain. Within this research stream, studies have examined the path from adoption to extended use and explored the use of a single technology to make associations between different technologies (e.g. Hsieh & Wang, 2007; Song et al., 2009, 2013; Stewart, 2003). Reflecting on the nature of software platforms and the inadequacies of the existing conceptualization of use associations in studying platform ecosystems, this study coins a new term, derivative usage, to characterize the mutually beneficial interactions between platform and app use, which better captures the essence of use in our research context. This reconceptualization and extension of the existing concept will better serve future research on platform ecosystems in particular as well as research in other multi-technology contexts in which the technologies involved are interdependent and complementary.

## Practical Implications

This study has crucial implications for platform owners and app developers. First, it confirms that integrated app-platform interface design is a valuable strategy for supporting platform growth. This approach has often been overlooked by platform and autonomous developers in practice. As the user base is widely considered to be the most valuable asset of a platform (McIntyre & Subramaniam, 2009), determining approaches to maintain and grow the user base while balancing the tension between app development autonomy and standardized/ integrated app-platform interface design is crucial for healthy growth and platform development. This study shows that an efficient app-platform interface design implemented from a grouping perspective may be the key to balancing this tension. This study further reveals interface similarity, embeddedness, and synchrony as three app-platform interface design principles that can benefit platform ecosystems. The results provide specific guidelines for practitioners on how software platforms can leverage interface design principles to achieve strong derivative usage and a successful software ecosystem.

Second, the study shows that platform boundary resources— such as software tools and regulations that serve as the interface for the relationship between the platform and apps (Ghazawneh and Ola Henfridsson, 2013)—are important resources for governance. Recent studies have also recommended that software platforms utilize their boundary resources, such as APIs and software development kits (SDKs), to curb strategic exploitation by apps or competing platforms in order to retain competitive advantages. For instance, Google defended itself against Amazon’s exploitation by increasing its control over program interfaces and wrapping them inside client libraries (Karhu et al., 2018). Platform firms are encouraged to consider integrated design as a new cooperative governance strategy to facilitate mutual benefits between platforms and apps by developing different restriction and control strategies according to apps’ interface similarity, embeddedness, and synchrony with the platform. For example, to ensure the entire ecosystem’s synchrony, platform owners could place restrictions on apps, such as excluding apps with long response times. At the same time, the platform should encourage apps to adopt a similar interface style that can be run within the platform’s interface boundary.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations that indicate directions for further study. First, while we recognize two types of value creation paths in platform ecosystems, the study’s boundary is the Type 1 ecosystem in which platforms offer core value creation. Caution is warranted when extrapolating this study’s findings to Type 2 ecosystems that primarily include complementary value creation. We encourage scholars to explore this in future research. Second, in the field experiment, we manipulated the app-platform interface design of only one app. The experimental findings for a single app may underestimate the value of the app-platform interface design, particularly regarding the effect of app-to-platform usage. Hence, future research should manipulate a larger number of apps to obtain more realistic and meaningful results. Third, given the current state of practice and feasible managerial implications, we only consider how the UI design of the platform and app satisfies user needs. It would be interesting to study diverse situations, such as when the UI design of a platform is shoddy or inadequate. Furthermore, interface design properties may vary their display forms in various contexts. We thus encourage future research to further explore diverse platform interface designs.

## Conclusion

This study addresses the platform ecosystem paradox wherein platform owners are challenged to strike a balance between offering autonomy for app developers to “bloom” and the successful (regulated) integration of their work with the platform. We build on TBG from the psychology literature and expand it to examine the effects of design principles—interface similarity, interface embeddedness, and interface synchrony—on bidirectional derivative usage between platforms and their apps. Four of the five formulated hypotheses are supported, thus validating most of our theoretical arguments. This research contributes to the growing platform ecosystem literature by offering a new design perspective in conjunction with theoretical accounts of the topological relationship between platforms and their apps. By offering a fresh approach to investigating these issues, the study enriches the ecosystem paradox debate.

## Measurements

Platform usage (intention) source: Wixom and Todd (2005):

1. I intend to use this platform at every opportunity in the future.

2. I plan to increase my usage of this platform in the future.

3. I will use this platform to do more things in the future.

App usage (objective) source: Venkatesh and Morris (2000):

1. On average, the frequency that subjects used the app over the last two weeks.

2. How much time each subject spent using the app over the last two weeks.

Grouping perception source: Overvliet et al. (2012); Yang et al. (2016):

1. According to my vision, the platform and this app are bonded together as a coherent group.

2. According to my vision, the platform and this app have a strong relationship with each other.

3. According to my vision, this app is an integrated part of the platform, making it hard to distinguish their boundary.

4. According to my vision, I can easily group the platform and this app perceptually as a whole.
