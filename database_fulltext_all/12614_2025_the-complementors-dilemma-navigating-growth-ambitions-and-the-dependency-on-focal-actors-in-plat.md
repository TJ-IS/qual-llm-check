# THE COMPLEMENTOR’S DILEMMA: NAVIGATING GROWTH AMBITIONS AND THE DEPENDENCY ON FOCAL ACTORS IN PLATFORM ECOSYSTEMS<sup>1</sup>

Shiyuan Liu College of Business and Public Management, Wenzhou-Kean University, Wenzhou, Zhejiang, CHINA {lshiyuan@wku.edu.cn}

Ola Henfridsson Miami Herbert Business School, University of Miami, Coral Gables, FL, U.S.A. {ohenfridsson@miami.edu}

Jochem T. Hummel and Joe Nandhakumar Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry, U.K. {jochem.hummel@wbs.ac.uk} {joe.nandhakumar@wbs.ac.uk}

The literature on platform ecosystems increasingly recognizes that nonfocal actors such as complementors may have growth ambitions. Such ambitions, if successfully advanced, may even elevate the complementor’s position in the platform ecosystem to that of a focal actor. However, transitioning from nonfocal actor to focal actor is challenging. Along the way, the nonfocal actor may need to choose between the seemingly unfavorable alternatives of acting on its growth ambitions—risking losing the focal actors’ support needed for that growth—or relinquishing its ambitions to ensure the support of the focal actors continues. Referring to this phenomenon as the complementor’s dilemma, we unpack the process whereby a nonfocal actor can pursue growth ambitions while successfully managing relationships with the focal platforms in the ecosystem. To address this research problem, we conducted an in-depth, embedded case study of a Chinese short-form video platform, Douyin (known as TikTok outside of China), from its inception as a complement in 2016 to its establishment as a focal actor in 2018. During this two-year period, Douyin grew spectacularly from 0.75 million to 208.28 million users. We examined the process through which Douyin navigated the complementor’s dilemma multiple times within the confines of its dependency on Weibo and WeChat, the focal actors in China’s social network platform ecosystem. We contribute to the platform ecosystem literature by offering a process perspective that conceptualizes the complementor’s dilemma and theorizes how to navigate the dilemma when transitioning from a nonfocal actor to a focal actor in a platform ecosystem.

Keywords: Complementor’s dilemma, nonfocal actor, focal actor, platform ecosystems, embedded case study, process-tracing, nonfocal to focal model

## Introduction

The relationship between focal <sup>2</sup> and nonfocal<sup>3</sup> actors in a platform ecosystem is characterized by mutual benefit. Focal actors, such as platform owners, benefit from the contributions made by nonfocal actors, such as complementors, to the viability of the platform ecosystem (Jacobides et al., 2018; Rietveld & Schilling, 2021; Teece, 2018). In particular, complementors help increase the scope and diversity of the platform ecosystem through innovation (Gawer, 2014;

Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Hukal et al., 2020). In this regard, the focal actor and the platform ecosystem benefit from heterogeneous innovation capabilities and knowledge resources (Cennamo & Santaló, 2019; Hurni et al., 2022; Wareham et al., 2014; Yoo et al., 2010), shared innovation risk (Boland et al., 2007; Boudreau, 2012), and network effects (Eisenmann et al., 2006; Gregory et al., 2021; Parker & Van Alstyne, 2005). At the same time, complementors benefit significantly from the platform ecosystem. They enjoy low-cost innovation because they can use and benefit from the focal actors’ technical resources (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Rolland et al., 2018; Soh & Grover, 2020) and existing user base (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012; Cennamo, 2021; Rietveld & Eggers, 2018).

Despite this mutual benefit, the relationship between focal and nonfocal actors is asymmetric; complementors are “at the mercy of the platform owner’s actions” (Hurni et al., 2022, p. 310) and portrayed as “platform-dependent” (Cutolo & Kenney, 2021). The scale advantages of platform owners, underpinned by winner-take-all logics (Schilling, 2002), are challenging to factor out in most circumstances. Yet, as pointed out by Hurni et al. (2022), complementors are not powerless but may exhibit self-determination and proactivity in creating their success within a platform ecosystem (cf. Eaton et al., 2015). In particular, a complementor might have its own growth ambitions (cf. Constantinides et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2017; Hurni et al., 2022; Qiu et al., 2017; Schilling, 2002) that, if realized, could potentially evolve its existing position into a more central, powerful one. The case of PayPal offers an anecdotal example of this. PayPal started out as a complementor to the eBay platform but eventually established itself as a focal platform in the wider ecosystem of payments.

Notably, the platform ecosystem literature reports that despite considerable asymmetry, nonfocal actors can thrive in their ability to make autonomous decisions within the confines of their dependency on focal actors in the platform ecosystem (Cutolo & Kenney, 2021; Eaton et al., 2015; Hurni et al., 2022; Kude & Huber, 2024). This paper extends this stream of the platform ecosystem literature by examining the evolution of a complementor from a nonfocal actor, one of many in a platform ecosystem, into a focal actor that operates side-by-side with established focal actors. We thus respond to Cutolo and Kenney’s (2021) call to investigate how nonfocal actors deal with the uncertainties related to platform dependence. We surmise that the more a nonfocal actor grows, the greater the risk of it becoming the target of countermoves that influence its relationship or coupling (e.g., Baldwin & Clark, 1999; Brunswicker et al., 2019; Orton & Weick, 1990;

Tiwana et al., 2010) with the focal actor (cf. Eisenmann et al., 2011; Rietveld et al., 2019). It is therefore important to research the process through which the nonfocal actor’s coupling with focal actors evolves, driven by the interplay between the nonfocal actor’s ambition to outgrow the platform ecosystem and the focal actor’s ambition to maintain the established asymmetry. This motivates the following research question: What is the process through which nonfocal actors pursue their growth ambitions while successfully managing relationships with focal actors in platform ecosystems?

We propose that the evolution of the complementor into a focal actor requires it to deal with “the complementor’s dilemma.” The dilemma<sup>4</sup> manifests as the conundrum experienced by the nonfocal actor as it deliberates on the unfavorable alternatives of (1) acting on growth ambitions at the risk of losing the focal actors’ support needed for that growth, or (2) deciding against acting on growth ambitions to maintain the support of focal actors. The nonfocal actor’s navigation of the dilemma largely deals with changes in coupling with the focal actors of the platform ecosystem. We develop our understanding of coupling through relevant works in the literatures on platform ecosystems (Brunswicker et al., 2019; Jacobides et al., 2018; Tiwana et al., 2010), modularity (Baldwin & Clark, 1999; Brunswicker & Mukherjee, 2023), and social psychology (Orton & Weick, 1990; Weick, 1976), stressing both the social and technical nature of couplings between actors in platform ecosystems. In pursuit of growth, the nonfocal actor will have to develop a path to outgrow the platform ecosystem by strategically and opportunistically adopting specific types of couplings to propel growth.

We conducted an embedded case study (Gerring, 2006; Scholz & Tietje, 2002) of the Chinese short-form video platform, Douyin (internationally known as TikTok), to study its spectacular growth from its inception as a complement in 2016 to its establishment as a focal platform actor in 2018 (from 0.75 million to 208.28 million users). We zoom in on four episodes in Douyin’s growth to examine the ways in which the firm changed its couplings, and ultimately its complementarity, to Weibo and WeChat, the two focal platforms of China’s social networking ecosystem. Our process-tracing analysis (George & Bennett, 2005) of both quantitative and qualitative archival data from 2013 (three years before Douyin launched) to 2020 (when TikTok was first threatened with a ban by the U.S. government) involved multiple corroboratory data sources. Through examining Douyin’s evolution from nonfocal to focal actor in China’s social networking ecosystem, we developed a process model, which we refer to as the NFTF (nonfocal to focal) model. The model explains how to navigate the complementor’s dilemma in a complementor’s evolution from a nonfocal actor to a focal actor in platform ecosystems.

The research contributes to the innovation stream of the platform ecosystem literature in general (Agarwal & Kapoor, 2023; Constantinides et al., 2018; Thatchenkery & Katila, 2023; Tiwana, 2015) and to the literature on nonfocal actors in particular (Cutolo & Kenney, 2021; Eaton et al., 2015; Hurni et al., 2022; Kude & Huber, 2024). First, we offer a conceptualization of the complementor’s dilemma. Second, we theorize on how to navigate the dilemma. Our process model explicates four paths through which nonfocal actors can transform their couplings and growth strategies as they deal with the complementor’s dilemma in an iterative way.

## Conceptual Background

## Complementarity in Platform Ecosystems

A platform ecosystem can be seen as “a collective of firms that is inter-linked by a common interest in the prosperity of a digital technology for materializing their own product or service innovation” (Selander et al., 2013, p. 184-185). In this regard, platform ecosystems consist of firms that are influenced by each other’s activities as they pursue their own goals (Eaton et al., 2015; Kapoor et al., 2021; Li & Kettinger, 2021), frequently enabled by digital technologies (Wang, 2021). In view of the multilateral nature of ecosystems, complementarity thus represents a complex interdependence structure through which three or more actors rely on each other to function properly or fulfill their objectives (Adner, 2017; Márton, 2022). The platform ecosystem represents a setting in which actors align with each other as they benefit from shared technologies and/or technical standards (Alaimo et al., 2020; Jacobides et al., 2018; Selander et al., 2013; Wang, 2021).

Resonating with the innovation stream of the platform ecosystem literature (Agarwal & Kapoor, 2023; Constantinides et al., 2018; Thatchenkery & Katila, 2023; Tiwana, 2015), we view the interaction between nonfocal and focal actors in a platform ecosystem in terms of a modular system (Baldwin & Clark, 1999; Garud et al., 2009; Langlois, 2002). As such, the actors are interconnected through interlinkages—couplings— that hold them and their focal and complementary innovations together as a complex system (cf. Brusoni & Prencipe, 2013; Jacobides et al., 2018). The couplings represent both social and technical interfaces through which information is interchanged to allow actors to adapt their behavior to each other. Focal actors are central nodes with many couplings, yet these couplings are mostly asymmetric in that nonfocal actors are more dependent on the focal actor than the other way around.

Despite the asymmetric couplings among actors (Cutolo & Kenney, 2021; Hurni et al., 2022), the complementarity of nonfocal actors is often vital to the long-term prospects of platform ecosystems. Platform ecosystems are therefore frequently open-ended (Henfridsson et al., 2018), with complementors being offered design flexibility (Henfridsson et al., 2014; Kallinikos et al., 2013; Langlois, 2007) to enable generativity (Yoo et al., 2010; Zittrain, 2006) through recombination (Lyytinen et al., 2016). In this regard, the innovation potential of a platform ecosystem cannot be fully leveraged without the participation of complementors (Boudreau, 2012; Cennamo & Santaló, 2019; Hein et al., 2020). The focal actor’s offering is sometimes not even viable without the complementary products of nonfocal actors (cf. Teece, 1986).

## Nonfocal Actors in Platform Ecosystems

The platform literature typically takes the perspective of focal actors, which are central to studies of platform governance (Foerderer et al., 2019; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Huber et al., 2017; Sarker et al., 2012) and platform entry into complementary markets (Foerderer et al., 2018; Shi et al., 2023; Wen & Zhu, 2019). A significantly smaller stream of literature takes the perspective of nonfocal actors. Early work by Selander et al. (2013) proposed a model of capability search and redeem to explain how Sony Ericsson navigated its nonfocal device business across four platform ecosystems. Later studies focused on application developers (Benlian et al., 2015; Kude & Huber, 2024; Qiu et al., 2017; Soh & Grover, 2020) as nonfocal actors in platform ecosystems.

In recent literature on nonfocal actors, there is increasing recognition that the complementor is not entirely powerless in its relation to the focal actor(s) (Cutolo & Kenney, 2021; Eaton et al., 2015; Hurni et al., 2022). For instance, Hurni et al. (2022) highlighted how the proactivity of nonfocal actors may lead to “surprising outside innovations” (p. 1), motivating repeated evaluations of whether the benefits outweigh the downsides of the platform owner’s dominance. Specifically, when the pursuit of a business opportunity requires endorsement beyond the existing relationship, a complementor can deliberately entice the platform owner to tilt toward a partnership mindset by “over-subjectifying” itself. In some cases, the anticipated benefit does not cover the cost of such subjectification, triggering the complementor to resist or even abandon the platform ecosystem. Similarly, Eaton et al. (2015) observed dualities in the relationship between complementors and platform owners. While complementors such as small software developers might find their choices restricted by the boundary resources offered by the platform owner, there is still some leeway to challenge and circumvent them by mobilizing technical, financial, and legal resources in the ecosystem. This allows a degree of destabilization and a leveling of the asymmetric power structure between the platform owner and complementors.

In a similar vein, Cutolo and Kenney (2021) show that “platform-dependent entrepreneurs” can develop responses to mitigate their power disadvantage vis-à-vis the focal platform. Such responses include multihoming, income diversification, and collective action (by joining forces with other complementors) to resist the platform owner’s hegemony. These strategies provide some countervailing power for complementors seeking short-term growth. Complementors can mitigate the power disadvantage relative to the focal platform by reducing the importance of the platform owner’s resources to them, or by pressuring the platform owner to reverse unwelcome changes to the ecosystem (cf. Kude & Huber, 2024).

Cutolo and Kenney (2021), Eaton et al. (2015), and Hurni et al. (2022) all note that complementors can enjoy a certain level of autonomy in platform ecosystems. In this paper, we build on this observation by looking at the evolution of the complementor, as a nonfocal actor, into a central, focal actor in the platform ecosystem. Few, if any, studies have examined the growth ambitions of nonfocal actors and how they can be realized. It is important to address this gap in the literature because nonfocal actors often represent digital ventures with high-growth ambitions (Huang et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2022; Kim & Kim, 2022; Lehmann et al., 2022) seeking to continuously develop their offering (Lehmann & Recker, 2022) beyond the status quo of a platform ecosystem.

Accepting that dependency on focal actors is a significant challenge for a nonfocal actor with growth ambitions, we can envision a conundrum, which we refer to as the “complementor’s dilemma,” manifested by tensions in a nonfocal actor’s relationship with the focal actor(s) of the ecosystem as it seeks to grow (cf. Hurni et al., 2022; Kude & Huber, 2024; Li & Kettinger, 2021; Selander et al., 2013). The complementor’s attempt to change position relative to the focal actor is challenging because, as a nonfocal actor, it must build on the technologies of the focal actor (e.g., Constantinides et al., 2018; Hukal et al., 2020; Tilson et al., 2010) as it materializes its own growth ambitions. Focal actors that perceive their position to be threatened will likely try to reinforce the current power asymmetry (e.g., Eaton et al., 2015; Hurni et al., 2022; Karhu et al., 2018; Leong et al., 2019). To manage this tension with focal actors, complementors need to make difficult decisions about how to grow within the confines of the platform ecosystem without losing the support of the focal platform.

## A Coupling View

Consistent with the innovation stream of the platform ecosystem literature (Agarwal & Kapoor, 2023; Constantinides et al., 2018; Thatchenkery & Katila, 2023; Tiwana, 2015), we conceptualize the platform ecosystem as comprised of modular systems (Baldwin & Clark, 1999; Garud et al., 2009; Langlois, 2002) where focal and nonfocal actors are interconnected through linkages that hold them and their core and complementary innovations together as a complex web of dependencies (cf. Brusoni & Prencipe, 2013; Jacobides et al., 2018). We refer to the specific interconnections between two actors in a platform ecosystem as couplings (Brunswicker et al., 2019; Schilling, 2000). A coupling between actors is not binary but rather a certain state along a continuum. It manifests as a combination of the degree to which two systems have variables in common (Glassman, 1973) and the degree to which the components of systems can be decomposed and recombined into new configurations (Henfridsson et al., 2014; Langlois, 2002; Sanchez, 1995; Schilling, 2000; Tiwana et al., 2010). The fewer the variables two systems have in common, the looser the coupling between them, and vice versa. The larger the extent to which configurations of the systems’ components can continue to function after being decomposed and recombined, the looser the coupling between the two systems, and vice versa.

Synthesizing the views on coupling across literatures on platform ecosystems (Brunswicker et al., 2019; Jacobides et al., 2018; Tiwana et al., 2010), modularity (Baldwin & Clark, 1999; Brunswicker & Mukherjee, 2023; Schilling, 2000), and social psychology (Orton & Weick, 1990; Weick, 1976), the coupling between two or more actors in a platform ecosystem exhibits two dimensions. First, there is a technical dimension, where the coupling between actors is dependent on the technical interfaces between the products of the actors. A technical interface is a description of how parts of a system will interact (Baldwin & Clark, 1999, p. 73), and it typically manifests as a standard (Hanseth, 2000; Lyytinen & King, 2006). For instance, an application programming interface or API (Benzell et al., 2024; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Yoo et al., 2010) is a technical interface common in platform ecosystems. A specific API allows a product to connect with and access data from another product. For instance, a property listing service such as Zillow can use the Google Maps API to render a detailed map on which to plot property listings and obtain geographical information relevant to specific properties. In addition, it can open interfaces that allow other actors in the platform ecosystem to use features of its product. In this regard, any actor in a platform ecosystem can shape its coupling with any other actor in the platform by making its product interoperable with other actors’ products. However, a nonfocal actor is more dependent on the coupling with a focal actor than the other way around. For instance, the property listing service is more dependent on its coupling with the Google Maps API than vice versa.

Another dimension for understanding coupling is social, pertaining to how actors are interrelated in terms of identity and the user base. As Weick (1976) outlines, coupling implies that actors are responsive to the moves of others, yet each “preserves its own identity” (p. 3). In modularity, the distinctiveness of the identity of a complement is often discussed in terms of specificity (Schilling, 2000), referring to the uniqueness of the functionality offered by a component within a modular system. The more distinct the identity of a complement, the looser the coupling between the nonfocal and focal actors (Brusoni & Prencipe, 2013). A nonfocal actor that can offer a complement with a distinct identity is likely to successfully serve the platform ecosystem over time (James et al., 2023; Li et al., 2022). At the same time, the relationship between the user base of the product offered by one actor and the user base of the product offered by another actor in the platform ecosystem is another significant factor that determines the coupling between actors. Defining a user base as the number of customers that have acquired the product and still use it, the user base overlap (Cennamo, 2021) between two products is the intersection between the total user base of each product. We can think of the user overlap between the nonfocal and focal actor as a continuum. On one end of the continuum, we might have a unilateral user overlap, tightly coupling the nonfocal actor with the focal actor. For instance, a photo sharing app only available in the Apple App Store will have a 100% user overlap with Apple as the focal actor, while Apple’s user overlap with the app is likely minuscule. But when the nonfocal developer of the photo sharing app pursues multihoming (Bakos & Halaburda, 2020) by also offering it in the Android App Store, the user overlap with Apple will be smaller. At the other end of the continuum, where the user overlap is balanced or bilateral, the nonfocal actor is loosely coupled with the focal actor because users overlap in both directions. For instance, Tencent launched the Tencent App Store with high user overlap with Android application marketplaces in China, and vice versa (cf. Tiwana, 2013).

In this regard, the interdependence between actors in a platform ecosystem rests on their coupling actions. Tighter coupling enables a nonfocal actor to draw upon the technical resources and user base of the focal actor to function and grow, which in turn expands the focal actor’s platform scope and diversity, leading to higher interdependence. When there is more than one focal actor, the nonfocal actors need to consider their coupling decisions with each focal actor. It can be lucrative for a complementor, for example, to multihome across multiple platforms to reach a wider user base. This creates a complex structure of multilateral interdependencies between focal and nonfocal actors in the ecosystem (Adner, 2017).

A coupling perspective builds on the idea that actors operating in an ecosystem interpret their environment (Daft & Weick, 1984, p. 286). Apart from the existing couplings (across technical and social dimensions) to the focal actors in the platform ecosystem, a nonfocal actor with growth ambitions will likely pay attention to the broader momentum of the ecosystem. Leiponen (2024, p. 199) refers to ecosystem momentum as the situation where “stakeholders find the innovation increasingly compelling, useful, and worth exploring and supporting.” While a positive ecosystem momentum will likely grow the overall value of the platform ecosystem, the nonfocal actor with growth ambitions may perceive ecosystem momentum as an opportunity to overturn established couplings and evaluate the value of having bilateral or multilateral dependencies with focal actors in the ecosystem.

In view of this conceptual background, we next report on an embedded case study of the process through which a nonfocal actor, Douyin, evolved into a focal actor as it navigated its couplings with the focal actors of the ecosystem, WeChat and Weibo, across four episodes of growth.

## Research Methodology

## Case Selection

We conducted an in-depth, embedded case study (Gerring, 2006; Scholz & Tietje, 2002) of a Chinese short-form video platform called Douyin (known as TikTok outside of China) from its inception as a nonfocal actor (i.e., complementor) in 2016 to its growth as a focal platform alongside WeChat and Weibo in 2018. There are several reasons that Douyin is an ideal case for our research objective. First, it is paradigmatic of the phenomenon of interest (Gerring, 2006). Douyin became a focal actor in a short time. In just two years, it had established itself as the number one short-form video platform in China, with over 250 million active users and an annual turnover of 20 billion RMB (3 billion USD). Douyin’s growth journey contains ample evidence to help us understand how a nonfocal actor navigates the complementor’s dilemma. In addition, the competitive positions of the focal platforms in the broader social networking ecosystem remained stable over time so we could see which focal actors—WeChat and Weibo—we should focus on in our unpacking of Douyin’s evolution from a complementor to a focal platform. Lastly, we were able to collect extensive and unique archival data in the original language; this made in-depth qualitative analysis possible.

Douyin is embedded in China’s social networking ecosystem, and the firm’s actions to shift its position were influenced by other ecosystem actors (i.e., users, focal actors, nonfocal actors). We therefore used temporal bracketing (Langley, 1999) to distinguish our units of analysis; these were the episodes marking Douyin’s growth from nonfocal to focal actor. Temporal bracketing supports theory development grounded in the comparison of episodes within a single case. We analyzed episodes using process-tracing to find the contingent mechanisms at play in the case (George & Bennett, 2005). As such, our research design brings forth novel insights with logical coherence (Eisenhardt, 1989).

## Data Collection

Inspired by Vaast et al. (2017), we developed an extensive research database using digital trace data (Berente et al., 2019) to systematically and comprehensively explore Douyin’s growth journey within the broader context of China’s social networking ecosystem. Our primary source of data was publicly available archival sources, which prior research has shown to be ideal for analyzing longitudinal processes in platform ecosystems (e.g., Eaton et al., 2015; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Karhu et al., 2018). The archival records have time stamps and date back to the birth of the Chinese short-form video market in 2013; thus, we were able to trace, in detail, the growth trajectory of Douyin and other actors in the social networking ecosystem. Sensitizing the activities, related events, antecedents, and impacts of key ecosystem stakeholders was instrumental to understanding Douyin’s growth. This enabled an embedded view (Scholz & Tietje, 2002) that would not have been possible had we only considered Douyin’s internal stakeholders (cf. Monteiro et al., 2022).

Combining qualitative and quantitative data, we developed a high-quality and relevant research database (Vaast et al., 2017) integrating five data sources (Table 1). First, we used a China-based application analytics platform called Analysys to acquire platform evolution data for 40 main (if not all) actors in the short-form video ecosystem between 2014 and 2019. This dataset contained panel data (daily, weekly, and monthly) for 55 variables (e.g., active user base, average use time per user, average open frequency per user, user retention rate, active user overlap between two platforms, user portrait). We triangulated the dataset with three other application analytics platforms. For the purposes of this research, we primarily used this data to map the growth trajectory of Douyin in terms of user base and user commitment over time. We also used it to corroborate the findings generated from other data sources. The second data source was the Douyin website and internet archives. These allowed us to make sense of Douyin’s views on key growth activities and events and provided clues about the main ecosystem actors relevant to Douyin’s growth.

Specifically, we identified, transcribed, and translated 23 long-text and 65 brief growth reports published by Douyin. Third, we collected 75 archival interviews with executives and employees of Douyin, its parent company ByteDance, and external stakeholders (e.g., content creators and competitors) in China’s social networking ecosystem. These interviews were conducted by journalists between 2014 and 2020. We systematically collected, transcribed, and translated the interviews to help us trace actions and follow reflections of relevant stakeholders in the ecosystem during Douyin’s growth. Consistent with the principle of multiple interpretations (Klein & Myers, 1999), this data source offered the perspectives of different actors in the wider social networking ecosystem about Douyin’s growth trajectory. Fourth, we recorded and translated 31 public speeches and presentations made by executives at Douyin and ByteDance between 2014 and 2020. We were thus able to capture the key messages (i.e., values, vision, stance, practices, and reasons) that Douyin conveyed to outsiders during its growth journey. This served as an important data source for our analysis of the timing and logic used by the firm to interact with related ecosystem actors. Finally, our study included 1,220 published articles, blog posts, and technical reports on Douyin, the shortform video market, the social networking ecosystem, and the overall mobile internet market in China over the period 2013 to 2020. This data source helped corroborate, enrich, and triangulate the data synthesized from other sources regarding the interactions between Douyin and the other actors in the social networking ecosystem.

## Data Analysis

We adopted an iterative, grounded, five-step analysis through which we derived process theory (Langley, 1999). First, we mapped the growth rate of the monthly user base and monthly user commitment level over time to examine the growth trajectory of Douyin between 2016 and 2018. This helped us uncover specific periods of rapid growth and slowdown, which served as a backbone for our temporal bracketing (Langley, 1999) of the case narrative. Consistent with the literature on digital ventures (e.g., Huang et al., 2017, 2022; Lehmann et al., 2022), we measured the Douyin user base as the number of users that actively opened the application at least once a month. User commitment was measured by two indexes: the monthly average frequency of opening the Douyin application and the monthly average use time per user. In addition to these main indexes, which capture digital venture evolution over time, we traced other industry-level indexes (e.g., user portrait, application number, user overlap between apps) to facilitate our understanding of the short-form video ecosystem evolution over time. We cross-checked the accuracy of our indexes against data from other third-party application platforms.

In the second step of our analysis, we identified key activities and events in the growth of Douyin. In doing this, we followed Van de Ven (1992) and Van de Ven and Poole (1995), who describe development as a progression of subsequent events marked by empirically observed changes in the form, quality, or state of an entity. We identified 86 growth activities and events marked by Douyin insiders to guide our coding of related ecosystem stakeholders (e.g., users, focal platforms, competitors, and complementors) and their respective activity timelines parallel to Douyin’s growth. During this open coding process (Strauss & Corbin, 1998), we concentrated on identifying the views of Douyin insiders (e.g., Douyin employees, senior managers, executives) on the contextual background, interpretation, and consequence of each growth activity/event. The reflections of other related ecosystem actors (e.g., content creators, users, WeChat, Weibo, Weishi, Miaopai, Kuaishou, Taobao) on Douyin’s growth trajectory were also gathered, especially their thoughts about actions/events that were aimed at supporting or competing with Douyin, and/or which influenced the short-form video ecosystem. Such reflections incorporated contextual backgrounds, interpretations, and consequential outcomes at the organizational and ecosystem levels. All emergent codes were further triangulated across the different data sources.

As a third step, we visualized the event chronology (Langley, 1999). This allowed for the simultaneous representation of many dimensions of our process data, including event ordering, parallel tracks across different entities, and their mutual effect over time. In particular, we traced and coded the timing and logic of Douyin’s growth activities relative to other related ecosystem actors’ activity timelines. This process-tracing practice (George & Bennett, 2005) revealed the mutual shaping between Douyin’s growth trajectory and wider ecosystem conditions, particularly the link between the activities of other ecosystem actors and Douyin’s growth actions. We used these indicative activities as a descriptive organizing device to decompose Douyin’s growth trajectory into four successive adjacent periods embedded in broader ecosystem dynamics (as shown in Figure 2). For instance, in response to user outflow and WeChat’s decision to close down Weishi, its own short-form video application, Douyin added entertainment features in April 2017 to attract new users, leading to a new period of growth.

Identifying these periods of growth in Douyin’s history enabled us to analyze Douyin’s journey from nonfocal to focal actor in the social networking ecosystem, including (1) tensions in the interactions between Douyin and focal actors and (2) how these tensions evolved as Douyin’s position in the ecosystem evolved from nonfocal to focal. This allowed for temporal bracketing (Langley, 1999) of Douyin’s growth into episodes. Building on the emerging findings, we constructed a comprehensive case storyline (see Figure 2).

In the fourth step, we used a coupling view of platform ecosystems to examine and interpret the observations identified in step three. Our coding process was iterative as we continually went back and forth between first-order categories (e.g., “Douyin launched instant messaging functionality that was core to WeChat’s offerings”), second-order themes (e.g., “imitating focal actors’ core offerings”), and the coupling view in literature (e.g., “distinctiveness of product identity in the platform ecosystem”). This allowed us to further conceptualize the complementor’s dilemma and how nonfocal actors can navigate it as third-order themes, which we then synthesized into aggregated dimensions (see Appendix). The aggregated dimensions involved contextual conditions (ecosystem momentum) situated at the broader category ecosystem level (Wang, 2021), the conceptualization of the complementor’s dilemma (going for decoupling versus going for coupling), and two mechanisms for navigating the dilemma (user scoping and product interfacing) at the business ecosystem level (Wang, 2021). Since space limitations prevent us from providing detailed data structures for these concepts, they are provided in the Appendix.

The final step of analysis specified and conceptualized the relationships between the constructs, from which we could understand the interplay of ecosystem momentum, the navigation mechanisms, and the consequences for nonfocal actor growth. Contrasting and comparing the four episodes revealed different enactments of user scoping and product interfacing by Douyin. We concluded that when a nonfocal actor paused its growth ambitions as a way of dealing with the dilemma, user scoping and product interfacing were unchanged and the actor remained tightly coupled with the focal actor, mirroring the growth of the platform ecosystem. When a nonfocal actor decided to pursue growth through decoupling from the focal actor, ecosystem momentum influenced whether user scoping was selective, imitative, or unrestricted, and if product interfacing was selective, opportunistic, or unrestricted. These different enactments of user scoping and product interfacing manage the dilemma by enabling the nonfocal actor to increasingly decouple from focal actors. The outcome of this stage helped generate a coherent analytical story and process model.

## Episodes of Growth

By 2016, the online social networking market in China had evolved into a relatively stable platform ecosystem that consisted of an array of firms providing social networking, entertainment, and lifestyle services, mainly under the governance of two focal platforms, WeChat and Weibo (see Figure 1). That year also marked the broader inception and development of the short-form video format, at the center of which was Douyin, which gained more than 200 million active users in the two years that followed its establishment in September 2016 (Figure 2). This extraordinary growth transformed Douyin from a niche complementor into a new focal actor in the ecosystem.

The evolution of Douyin was neither smooth nor linear. It consisted of four episodes (Figure 2), during which Douyin had to balance its growth ambitions against its relationships with the established focal actors—WeChat and Weibo—upon which it depended. In what follows, we present Douyin’s rapid growth trajectory and use the case findings to trace how four dilemmas became salient for Douyin and were subsequently navigated by the firm. To show the link between the empirical data and the theoretical constructs that emerged from our analysis, we created process-tracing models for each episode (Figures 3-6) and interspersed our narrative of Douyin’s growth with references to the theoretical codes illustrated in Figures 3-6 in parentheses.

## Episode 1: Complementor to WeChat and Weibo

Douyin was created in 2016 by ByteDance, a Chinese technology company known for its news feed platform Toutiao. From the outset, ByteDance viewed short-form video as a core offering. It thus differed from WeChat and Weibo (the dominant focal platforms in China’s social networking ecosystem at that time), which saw short-form video as a novel medium to complement their existing text- and imagebased social networking services. As such, their early initiatives in the short-form video area lacked clarity about the targeted users and product design. For instance, in 2013, WeChat’s parent company, Tencent, had opportunistically created Weishi, a short-form video app. As a Weishi team member recalled from the initial meeting with the team’s director: “He told us we would build another Vine<sup>5</sup> in China. However, when you asked about his concrete plan, his answer was: foreign countries have it, so we must have it too.” The people at ByteDance, on the other hand, had observed changes in user preferences from text to short-form video on their news feed platform Toutiao. They saw short-form video as their next core competence (“all in short-form video”). As

Douyin’s General Manager noted on the 36Kr tech-news website prior to the founding of the company:

User-generated content short-form video is a potential offer we have been giving attention to for some time. The popularity of the big-screen smart phone and the developing internet infrastructure is allowing more people, especially young people, to express themselves through short-form video.

![](/api/attachments/2N22B7ZE/fulltext/images/fcdfe65108c4b7d8262c004ce6fff04577a5a62980f6cd55eab483bbc80ada55.jpg)  
Figure 2. Timeline of Douyin’s Growth

ByteDance launched Douyin, a carefully designed short-form video app, in September 2016. In order to reach users, Douyin relied heavily on the support (i.e., API connections) of the two focal platforms, WeChat and Weibo; the connections enabled unrestricted marketing activities and user multihoming. With granular user targeting (i.e., young, talented, and fashionable users) and corresponding product function design, Douyin became a popular short-form video application within six months. As a founding team member explained in a blog posted on Douyin’s website:

We didn’t have big ambitions at first. We envisioned just creating a music short-form video community; it was enough to just build a community. Most of our group members are the generation after the 90s. At the launch stage, the 11th content you browsed would repeat the first. We therefore sought talent creators from art academies and other short-form video platforms, and made friends with them. If they couldn’t give us feedback online, we invited them to visit us face to face. In this way, we targeted young pop culture fans. The content boom that followed was just a natural chemical reaction.

In March 2017, Douyin’s user growth was catapulted when a comedy star decided to share a funny Douyin video on Weibo. The interface connection between the two applications triggered Douyin’s first round of explosive user growth (from 7 million to 10 million in one month (48.49%) by piggybacking on Weibo’s user base (stakeholder interest). As the Huxu journal highlighted:

The unexpected sharing activity of Yue Yunpeng on Weibo gave Douyin’s cold start a boost. This halo effect inspired the operations team to enhance promotion activities on Douyin by inviting more stars to participate, which expanded the target users of Douyin from niche to broader groups. It could be said that without this forward activity, the growth of Douyin would have been much slower.

At the same time, the unexpected user boost brought a new challenge for Douyin’s future growth. Douyin designers were hesitant about accommodating in their product offerings the preferences of these emerging users, who had piggybacked over from Weibo in search of a unique complementary shortvideo community for entertainment interaction. Douyin knew then that it would have to strengthen its entertainment features while also offering distinctive community features to engage and retain users. As a third-party business report pointed out:

It’s hard to expect users from Weibo to spend much time on Douyin. Users will not cultivate social relations on a new platform just because its content creation is convenient. Unless Douyin develops strong community interactions similar to Weibo and WeChat, they will still be inclined to create content there while sharing on existing social platforms such as Weibo and WeChat.

However, the prospect of adding community functionalities analogous to those of Weibo and WeChat was problematic, not least because it would propel Douyin into the existing competition between WeChat and Weibo. Douyin, therefore, had a dilemma (i.e., Douyin’s first complementor’s dilemma). Embracing these emergent users from Weibo and mimicking some of the focal actors’ social networking features would enable Douyin to shoulder its way into the market. But the move would risk the loss of support from Weibo and WeChat, which might begin to see Douyin as a threat or, even worse, as a competitor. As a product manager at Douyin recalled: “Blocking is nothing new and we had experienced it multiple times on the Toutiao platform before when we positioned similar content and users to Weibo and WeChat.” At the same time, the decision not to embrace these users would go against Douyin’s ambitions to grow.

At that time, Weibo and WeChat were continuing to develop their own short-form video complements (WeChat: Weishi; Weibo: Huangka) with the goal of becoming the dominant social networking platform (stakeholder interest). As a team member of Weishi recalled: “Weishi was endorsed with intense connections with WeChat, QQ, and Tencent Microblog.” Similarly, the CEO of Weibo stated: “We have noticed short-form video as an important medium to extend social media and we have indeed experienced more intense competition over the last months.” With WeChat and Weibo focusing on developing short-form video, it became clear to Douyin that for now, it had to remain a complementor. In addition, text/image-based instant messaging remained the fundamental technology underlying widely adopted applications (e.g., WeChat, QZone, Weibo, Douban, TianYa, Taobao) in the social networking ecosystem (technical architecture). Douyin therefore could not independently attract users to its application. As a Tencent business analyst highlighted in a Baidu news article:

Douyin keeps drawing on the connection with Weibo and WeChat as its main user acquisition sources. It recruits experienced content creators from those platforms. More than 56 percent of users directly attribute their download of Douyin to friends’ sharing activity at existing social platforms. More importantly, this part of users is more active (i.e., use Douyin more than 1 hour every day) and is willing to recommend Douyin to others voluntarily.

![](/api/attachments/2N22B7ZE/fulltext/images/11d12e6b4991e87411a737ed3783bea7cdde7c18e95d85228d088d73f715180b.jpg)

In view of WeChat’s and Weibo’s significance for user acquisition, Douyin decided to sacrifice the prospect of rapid growth in favor of securing continued support from the focal actors (going for coupling strategy). Lacking sufficient momentum to break away, Douyin carried out functionality optimization and improved the API linkages with WeChat and Weibo by adding a 3D shaking effect on forwarded videos (intensive product interfacing). The core interaction on Douyin (i.e., short-form performance video creation and sharing) was further refined to conform to the preference of the originally targeted user group (unchanged user scoping). As such, Douyin repressed its growth ambitions in exchange for consistent interface linkages from both WeChat and Weibo to enable its future development. This meant that Weibo users, who had started to use Douyin in parallel, had little incentive to fully engage with the product, leading to user outflow (from 10 million to 7 million in April 2017) to other competitive short-form video products and an average growth rate (6%) similar to that of the short-form video market (5.8%) between February and April (mirrored growth). These actions made Douyin even more dependent on Weibo and WeChat’s users for its future growth, eventually prompting the reemergence of the complementor’s dilemma several months later, as we will discuss in Episode 2. Moreover, by moving alongside Weibo and WeChat, short-form video remained a peripheral technology that did not substantially influence the ecosystem’s momentum.

## Process-Tracing Analysis of Episode 1

Douyin had been launched within the broader social networking ecosystem in China, and its initial user growth was highly dependent on its coupling with the two focal platforms: WeChat and Weibo. In March 2017, Douyin faced its first complementor’s dilemma when the user inflow from the focal actors’ platforms made it feasible for it to grow as a short-form video community; however, capitalizing on this by building community features would risk the existing interface connections and therefore the support from focal platforms (see Figure 3). Since onboarded users at Douyin multihomed with WeChat and Weibo, Douyin could not function and attract users on its own. Moreover, WeChat and Weibo recognized that shortform video was a novel, potentially valuable, complement in the ecosystem and wanted to keep Douyin as a complementor for their platforms. Lacking sufficient ecosystem momentum in terms of stakeholder interest and technical architecture, Douyin decided not to decouple from WeChat and Weibo. Its user scope and intense product interfaces with these focal actors were left unchanged. These coupling actions kept Douyin as a nonfocal actor, mirroring the growth of WeChat and Weibo. It remained dependent on the platforms and therefore did not change the ecosystem momentum.

## Episode 2: Growing Despite Blocking from Weibo

Although Douyin had decided to take a conservative path, it had not lost its growth ambitions. It simply aimed to grow without risking the support of WeChat or Weibo. In April 2017, a change in the social networking ecosystem presented an opportunity: WeChat closed its short-form video product, Weishi. This decision allayed Douyin’s concerns about competing head-to-head with two focal platforms and losing crucial access to their user bases. WeChat, at that time, had failed to compete with Weibo in the short-form video market, something that had implications for Douyin’s position. A former Weishi team member recalled the difficulty of competing with Weibo in an interview in Jiemian Journal:

Weishi was seen as the very last try [by WeChat] to cope with the barbaric growth of Weibo in the short-form video market. However, the disproportional user outcome from heavy investment and effort kept disappointing the executives at Tencent. We were all agonizing, followed by an on-going descending of its strategic priority at Tencent (e.g., ceasing WeChat endorsement to Weishi and decreased financial investment).

WeChat’s decision provided Douyin with new momentum to confront the dilemma. Weibo clearly saw short-form video as a service with significant potential. WeChat, on the other hand, showed less commitment to the offering (stakeholder interest). This meant that Douyin could be sure of retaining WeChat’s interface support if it started to grow its own autonomous short-form video product. But Douyin did not know if it could convince Weibo users to come over in the absence of full compatibility between the two applications. A specific fear was that users would leave Douyin altogether, as had been the case with Meipai and XiaoYing in April 2015 (i.e., Douyin’s second complementor’s dilemma). To address this threat, Douyin had to develop product performance comparable to Weibo and strengthen its interconnections with WeChat.

In May 2017, Douyin undertook a set of actions to materialize this growth ambition (going for decoupling strategy). First, it added new community functions (e.g., soundtrack-sharing and live-streaming) that were carefully adapted to engage Weibo users without interfering with WeChat’s core offering (i.e., instant messaging) (selective user scoping). These data-intensive functionalities had become more feasible with the growing adoption of 4G connectivity among China’s smartphone providers since

2016 (technical architecture). Moreover, to speed up scaling, Douyin launched a campaign specifically targeting Weibo users. It included inviting entertainment stars who were popular on Weibo to lead original entertainment content creation and sharing on Douyin (selective user scoping). Both activities elevated Douyin’s position from a content creation and sharing tool that complemented WeChat and Weibo to a short-form entertainment video community that staged content-based user interactions that were similar to Weibo’s.

Second, as Douyin opened up its advertising interface to third parties, it became more valuable to WeChat because it could potentially promote other applications owned by WeChat’s parent company Tencent (e.g., game apps, long video apps, entertainment apps), creating a reciprocal relationship between the two companies (selective product interfacing). Although embracing novel users directly threatened the existing support of Weibo, Douyin was able to balance the risk through increased interface couplings with WeChat and its first-party applications (stratified growth). In June 2017, Douyin’s user base rebounded significantly with a 95.54% increase, taking the number of users from 10,044,400 to 19,640,900.

This significant growth in Douyin’s user base had started to shake up the platform ecosystem. In August 2017, the increasing user overlap, especially with Weibo, and the imitation of a Weibo functionality (i.e., the story mode to create and share daily-life videos for 24 hours) caused the platform to block Douyin’s API connection. As noted by a consulting analyst in an interview: “The competition between Douyin and Weibo became more apparent since both platforms tilt toward a media feature with weak social chains.” As a result, Douyin had grown its own user base, decreasing its dependence on Weibo and WeChat and almost turning itself into a potential competitor for these platforms. These changes in competitive dynamics would cause a new complementor’s dilemma to emerge some months later, as we will show in Episode 3. With its own user network and increasing independence from Weibo and WeChat, Douyin’s short-form video format and its algorithmic recommendation feature became increasingly popular in China’s social networking ecosystem, changing the ecosystem momentum in a way that was favorable for Douyin’s future growth.

## Process-Tracing Analysis of Episode 2

In April 2017, Douyin faced a second complementor’s dilemma, where it again questioned whether or not to develop social community features (see Figure 4). It was able to leverage the focal actors’ contradictory views about the importance of short-form video in the social networking ecosystem. WeChat apparently viewed short-form video as unimportant and uninteresting, as evidenced by the sudden closing of its own application. Weibo, on the other hand, increased its investments in short-form video and saw it as a very relevant offering to its users. Such contrasting stakeholder interests provided Douyin with an opportunity to maintain the interface connections with WeChat, from which it could realize its growth ambitions. This, in combination with the enhanced adoption of 4G technologies and smartphones by mobile internet users over the past year, added to an ecosystem momentum that enabled Douyin’s decoupling decision to function independently from Weibo and selectively target Weibo’s users. The move triggered resistance from Weibo, but the selective product interfacing with WeChat helped Douyin compensate for the loss through further user acquisition on WeChat, leading to stratified growth (Figure 4). Douyin thus became less dependent on Weibo and WeChat and influenced the ecosystem momentum in favor of its own growth by popularizing short-form video and algorithmic recommendations among users of China’s social networking ecosystem.

## Episode 3: Growing Despite Competition from WeChat and Weibo

To continue its early success as a growing short-form entertainment video product, Douyin designers added new community functionality (e.g., soundtrack-sharing and livestreaming tags) in September 2017. Significantly, Douyin, once again, carefully introduced this functionality with the aim of not interfering with WeChat’s core offering, which was instant messaging. As the general manager of Douyin pointed out on the company’s blog:

Instead of competing with other social platforms, we are still a short-form video community. The live-streaming functionality does not influence the identity of Douyin. We are not making a pure and professional livestreaming social platform but try to contextualize this functionality in Douyin (e.g., setting live-streaming functionality on a peripheral entrance rather than the home page, replacing the usual ranking rule for the number of likes given by fans, designing special effects and tags on live-streaming page) in order to enhance the interaction between talent content creators and fans.

As Douyin engaged more users from WeChat and Weibo in real-time social interactions, more usage data was generated, which in turn helped the accuracy and speed of the recommendation system to evolve through algorithmic filtering. As a result, while WeChat and Weibo still positioned short-form video products as an essential complement to the mainstream text/image-based social interaction they provided, users were increasingly appreciating Douyin as the next generation of social networking applications (stakeholder interest). In view of this positive momentum, Douyin had to figure out which position in China’s social network ecosystem it wanted to occupy. As a team member of Douyin pointed out in an interview with a journalist from 36Kr:

Social and community are utterly different from each other. The social tie with content creators is the antecedent for a user to consume content on the social platform, while content quality is the antecedent for a user to follow content creators in the content community. Therefore, one key issue for Douyin now is to decide whether we should focus on social or community. Should we create a contentdriven or social-driven platform? Which one is better? Is it possible to find a balance point between community and social?

Adding more social features to the application would pose a direct challenge to the market positioning of WeChat and Weibo because it would interfere with their core offering and further increase user overlap. Note that at that time, other platforms in similar situations were threatened by envelopment. For example, Weibo attempted to envelop Miaopai into Kuran, its own short-form video product, by suddenly restricting Miaopai’s access to its users in November 2017. As a former strategic analyst at Tencent remarked to Sina News about the envelopment logic:

Short-form video is a medium that can complement diverse products. It is information when the contents are news; it is entertainment when the contents are edits of variety and films; it is community when users present their interests and interact with other users with similar interests; it is social networking as well when the interactions are user-centralized (e.g., WeChat and Weibo).

The less risky path for Douyin was to maintain its position as the main, nonfocal content community complementor in the social networking ecosystem. This would allow it to retain the support of WeChat and avoid being enveloped by Weibo. At the same time, it would confine its growth to the short-form video market. The decision whether to remain content-driven or move in the direction of social was important because this would define its future path (i.e., Douyin’s third complementor’s dilemma).

In September 2017 and the months that followed, Douyin began to recognize its strengths and the momentum that shortform video was enjoying in China. The popularity of the format among users in China’s social networking ecosystem was sky-high (360 million users in September 2017) (stakeholder interest). Moreover, it turned out that users developed strong personal social ties on short-form video products (e.g., Douyin, Kuaishou, HuoShan, XiGua) driven by algorithm-based content distribution technologies (technical architecture), even compared against the focal actors—for instance, only 1 in 10 users followed their WeChat or Weibo contacts on Douyin. All in all, this indicated to Douyin that users would stick with Douyin even if WeChat and Weibo tried to envelop the application (stakeholder interest). It was time for Douyin to aggressively speed up its user acquisition in the social networking ecosystem before WeChat and Weibo had time to catch up and pull support for the application (going for decoupling strategy).

To this end, in January 2018, Douyin launched multiple cash reward campaigns to attract users served by WeChat and Weibo (imitative user scoping). In February 2018, it spent 4 million RMB (i.e., 0.5 million USD) per day to acquire users using the interfaces from every platform to which it could connect in the Apple App Store and opened its own advertising interfaces targeted at gaming applications (opportunistic product interfacing). As a result, within two months, Douyin’s user base had doubled. It had also become more diverse in terms of age, gender, city distribution, and consumption power. After its successful user acquisition drive, Douyin imitated Weibo’s and WeChat’s core offerings (private chatting and instant messaging, respectively) (imitative user scoping) and interconnected the account systems of all ByteDance’s platforms (opportunistic product interfacing). As the CEO of Bytedance reasoned in March 2018: “We are going to evolve to satisfy diverse user demands on Douyin from now on. By giving priority to social functionalities, we oriented user interactions to personal relationships between content creators and consumers.”

Douyin popularized itself as a short-form video social product, similar to WeChat and Weibo, through a series of product redesign actions (enveloped growth). But even as Douyin was becoming more independent, it was still trying to preserve its tenuous relationships with WeChat and Weibo because it knew that it still relied on them to compete with Kuaishou, the then-leading short-form video platform. As one of the team members recalled in a Douyin blog post:

Figure 5. Process-Tracing of Episode 3

After the spring festival, the daily active users of Douyin grew to 40 million. But Kuaishou was still the leading player at that time with daily active users of over 100 million. No one in our team shouted slogans about becoming the number one short-form video platform in China. We always thought of ourselves as runners-up and stayed calm.

The growth and popularity of Douyin surprised Weibo and WeChat, and they became increasingly reluctant to support Douyin’s future growth. In the months that followed, their opposition would lead to a new complementor’s dilemma. But Douyin’s actions had popularized the product with users and other nonfocal actors, tipping ecosystem momentum in favor of its future growth (see Episode 4).

## Process-Tracing Analysis of Episode 3

In the third episode of our analysis, Douyin faced the dilemma that if it developed social features to become a more independent short-form video and social media application, it would risk being enveloped by the focal actors in the ecosystem and lose access to the users of WeChat and Weibo (see Figure 5). While WeChat and Weibo continued to regard short-form video products as essential complements to textand image-based interaction, users were increasingly appreciating and switching to short-form video as the next social application driven by innovative algorithm-based content distribution technologies. The progressive momentum in stakeholder interest and the technical architecture underlying the social networking ecosystem enabled Douyin to enact imitative user scoping by integrating functions offered by WeChat and Weibo into the product and opportunistically interfacing its own product toward various other nonfocal actors in the ecosystem. This led to Douyin’s enveloped growth by attracting the users of focal platforms. Through these decoupling actions, Douyin became increasingly independent from Weibo and WeChat, triggering the emergence of a final complementor’s dilemma in the following months. At the same time, its decoupling actions had made the ecosystem momentum highly conducive to future growth by turning short-form video into a mainstream technology in China’s social network ecosystem.

## Episode 4: Growing Despite Isolation from WeChat and Weibo

In response to the rapid user growth of Douyin at the beginning of 2018 (30 million per month), WeChat relaunched the Weishi short-form video product in February 2018, fueling its growth through substantial investment. Weibo followed suit with Aidong, its own short-form video product. These countermoves threatened Douyin’s positive momentum. Douyin was particularly troubled by WeChat’s and Weibo’s blocking of content sharing, which had been fundamental to its earlier growth. As the CEO of ByteDance complained: “There were more than 10 million users sharing short-form videos on WeChat and Weibo before. So, their blocking is really harmful for user experience.” As a result, Douyin’s monthly growth rate decreased from 32.7% to 9.52% in April 2018.

![](/api/attachments/2N22B7ZE/fulltext/images/8cd61b453dd621f684907cfbb967dbda7c8a63b8cabda2544cf4c135ed3a094e.jpg)

At around the same time, however, some of Douyin’s users started to combine the application with e-commerce activities (stakeholder interest). They embedded their Taobao (an ecommerce platform owned by Alibaba) accounts into their personal Douyin pages and created short-form innovative marketing videos for Taobao commodities. This novel user behavior propelled Douyin into a delicate new relationship with the ecosystem’s focal actors. Douyin now had to choose between saving its relationships with WeChat and Weibo by not capitalizing on this new user behavior or generating growth in submarkets such as e-commerce, which would challenge WeChat and Weibo head-to-head and thus risk completely losing their support (i.e., Douyin’s fourth complementor’s dilemma). A technology analyst outlined this risk to 36Kr:

As Douyin expanded from a pure content community to “short-form video+,” it already highly threatened all Tencent’s [i.e., WeChat] core businesses in the social and entertainment market. Such threat was further reflected in the increasing use time on short-form video platforms and decreasing use time in other submarkets (i.e., reading, games, long-form video, social). Douyin is no longer a pure product for content publishing and consumption, but has become the new interaction platform for users. Although Douyin just sprouted an instant messaging functionality, it could go further in opening social relations to other complementary markets with the form of short-form video.

For Douyin, it had become evident that short-form video had its own value and was the next fundamental technology in the ecosystem (technical architecture). Moreover, its interfacing with e-commerce platforms facilitated marketing, something that was highly appreciated by users and other applications in the ecosystem (stakeholder interest). As Alibaba reported: “There are many posts in the comments sections along the lines of ‘this is the 99th item I’ve been recommended and also attracted to at Douyin’; Douyin has already become a popular marketing place for many goods on Taobao and Tmall.” Furthermore, in May 2018, Douyin had scaled its user base to over 180 million highly active users; this undeniably strong support from ecosystem users made Douyin confident about the upcoming battle with WeChat and Weibo (stakeholder interest). As a team member of Weishi pointed out in an article on Tencent News:

The re-launch of Weishi in March 2018 was more as a strategically defensive product to slow down the pace of Douyin’s entry to the social market. However, although Tencent could provide user flow and resources for Weishi, it was still an issue of matching one competitor’s product logic against another one. As Douyin already generated a network effect with abundant and high-quality UGC and community ecosystem in the short-form video market, it’s unlikely that a defensive product can replace a dominant competitor.

Eventually, Douyin’s management became convinced that the company could grow without the support of WeChat and Weibo (going for decoupling strategy). In fact, the CEO of ByteDance made an overt declaration in May 2018 that “deliberate blocking by WeChat and imitation by Weishi cannot stand in the way of Douyin.”

Douyin made its final steps toward becoming a focal platform between May and July 2018. First, it further supported its B2B users by providing them with new means of monetization, such as launching the Xingtu platform, which allowed businesses in China seeking brand exposure and brand credibility to come into contact with “influencers” on Douyin (unrestricted user scoping). Second, Douyin built innovative social functionalities that went beyond what WeChat and Weibo offered. This included allowing users to develop a “top music list” and the popular “duet” functionality, in which content creators could watch a video of other content creators and react to it (unrestricted user scoping). Finally, Douyin started to support and attract diverse complementors in the ecosystem by offering software development kits and APIs. The software development kits allowed developers in more than 200 submarkets to create applications (e.g., photo editors) that worked within the Douyin interface (unrestricted product interfacing). Many smaller application developers were excited by this free and open access to Douyin’s considerable user base and became complementors to Douyin.

It did not take long for WeChat and Weibo to cease business cooperation with Douyin in every submarket and launch their own short-form video platforms, effectively isolating the company from their ecosystem resources. However, this isolation had little negative effect on Douyin’s position in the social networking market because it had established its own technical resources and user base to support further growth. In September 2018, it had grown into a focal platform with 200 million monthly active users, which was comparable to Weibo’s 360 million (sovereign growth). As one of the senior managers explained to 36Kr news:

Nowadays, Douyin is no longer a music short-form video community, or even a short-form video platform. Its newest version name has changed from “Douyin Short-form Video” to “Douyin.” It has become a “monster” with lots of arms such as video, livestreaming, e-commerce, social, local life, and is trying to grow more arms for the future. Today’s Douyin is beyond anyone’s expectation, even for those who participated from the beginning.

## Process-Tracing Analysis of Episode 4

Douyin confronted the dilemma that achieving growth as a short-form video social platform would both require and jeopardize access to the supporting resources of WeChat and Weibo (see Figure 6). However, the ecosystem momentum was favorable for Douyin. The benefits that short-form video offered, both in itself and to other markets such as e-commerce, were almost beyond question, and the format was widely adopted by both users and nonfocal actors in the ecosystem. Douyin’s increasing ability to stand on its own feet made the dilemma less perilous. Douyin therefore decided to enact unrestricted user scoping to gain users who were unserved by the focal platforms, and unrestricted product interfacing to invite other nonfocal actors onto its own platform and support them with its capabilities. Both activities allowed Douyin to grow autonomously into a focal platform in the ecosystem, rendering it immune to the isolating countermoves of incumbent focal actors. Douyin was no longer dependent on WeChat’s and Weibo’s technical resources and user base for its functioning and growth; in short, it had become a focal actor in China’s social networking ecosystem.

## Cross-Episode Analysis

The case narrative offers an account of the process through which Douyin navigated its growth ambitions around its dependence on WeChat and Weibo. In particular, the processtracing analysis for each of the four episodes synthesizes the key events related to that episode’s complementor’s dilemma with Douyin’s navigation of the dilemma. In what follows, we provide a cross-episode analysis in which each episode is compared with the others, as an intermediate step in the development of our process model.

The complementor’s dilemma manifested slightly differently in each episode because the actions taken by Douyin in the context of an evolving platform ecosystem shaped the contextual conditions of the next episode’s dilemma. In Episode 1 (Figure 3), for instance, the creation of a short-form video community seemed to be a logical next move; however, seizing that opportunity would risk the support of WeChat and Weibo and therefore the interfaces supplying Douyin users. This instantiation of the dilemma was situated in an ecosystem where the two focal actors viewed short-form video as a novel complement. Episode 2 (Figure 4) is reminiscent of Episode 1 in that Douyin was confronting the dilemma of choosing between its role as a tightly coupled short-form video tool and its evolution into a more independent short-form video community by initiating its own user network. Such a development would risk the loss of its API linkages with the focal actors. In Episode 1, Douyin decided to pause its growth ambitions by keeping its user scope and its product interfaces to WeChat and Weibo unchanged. In Episode 2, changes in the short-form video interests of WeChat and Weibo provided Douyin with the momentum to prioritize its growth ambitions. In Episode 3 (Figure 5), Douyin confronted the dilemma of remaining an interoperable content-driven application versus becoming more independent by adding social-driven functionalities that would compete with the user networks of WeChat and Weibo, thereby risking envelopment. In Episode 4 (Figure 6), Douyin faced another instantiation of the dilemma when it discovered the opportunity to become more independent by growing into other markets, but it was aware that this would likely prompt WeChat and Weibo to isolate it from their ecosystem. Figures 3-6 and the Appendix provide details of how the complementor’s dilemma was empirically derived.

On a general level, we note that Douyin’s navigation of the dilemma can be described by reference to the relative degree of coupling with the focal actors. Going for a coupling strategy (Episode 1) meant increasing its dependency on the focal actors to retain their support and grow with the platform ecosystem as a whole. Going for a decoupling strategy (Episodes 2-4) meant trying to relax dependency on the focal actors in order to outgrow the platform ecosystem, but at the risk of losing the support necessary to achieve that growth.

We identified two mechanisms through which the nonfocal actor put into action its strategy for navigating the dilemma. First, user scoping refers to the nonfocal actor’s actions to identify its market and attract the users of that market through seeding and novel product functionality. For instance, in Episode 2, Douyin implemented features that were similar to Weibo’s with the intention of selectively seeding its users while still maintaining its dependence on WeChat. In Episode 3, Douyin went a step further in implementing its decoupling strategy by imitating the functionality of both WeChat and Weibo, seeking to seed the users of both. Second, product interfacing refers to the actions through which a nonfocal actor changes its product’s degree of interoperability with the products of other actors (focal and otherwise) in the platform ecosystem. For instance, in Episode 2, Douyin opened advertising interfaces with WeChat as part of its intention to selectively commit to WeChat rather than Weibo. In Episode 3, Douyin took the strategy further by connecting to the advertising interfaces of nonfocal actors across the ecosystem. The Douyin case indicates that a nonfocal actor pursuing growth ambitions needs to eventually enact user scoping by seeding the users of focal actors. It can do this by introducing a competing functionality that services similar user markets while maintaining its product interfaces with focal actors. However, in most circumstances, user scoping alienates the focal actors, jeopardizing the interoperability (product interfaces) between the nonfocal actor’s product and those of the focal actors (see Episode 4).

Synthesizing what we learned in the cross-episode analysis, four types of growth strategies emerged: mirrored growth, stratified growth, enveloped growth, and sovereign growth. In Episode 1, enabled by a tight coupling strategy, Douyin went for mirrored growth; that is, its growth as a nonfocal actor largely reflected the growth of the platform ecosystem. Episodes 2 and 3 reflected more sophisticated coupling strategies in that the existing interrelationships with the focal actor(s) were challenged. For instance, in Episode 2, Douyin opted for stratified growth, which we define as growth in tandem with one focal actor at the expense of another. It occurs when the nonfocal actor partitions the platform ecosystem with the intention of growing in a promising subset of the platform ecosystem. Taking this a step further, in Episode 3, Douyin pursued enveloped growth enabled by competitive coupling. That is, the nonfocal actor grows by serving and capturing value from the same market as the focal actors. Finally, in Episode 4, Douyin went all out with a loose coupling strategy. It pursued sovereign growth by growing its own novel ecosystem in parallel to WeChat and Weibo within the broader social networking platform ecosystem in China.

## Process Model

We offer a process model<sup>6</sup> (see Figure 7) that focuses on (1) contextual conditions that shape the circumstances in which the nonfocal actor with growth ambitions faces the complementor’s dilemma, (2) the complementor’s dilemma, and the strategies of coupling and growth that the nonfocal actor can deploy to navigate the dilemma, (3) the dynamics of navigating the complementor’s dilemma, and (4) the tipping-point when the nonfocal actor becomes a focal actor.

Contextual conditions: A starting point for our theory development is the existence of a nonfocal actor with ambitions and resources. As noted in work on digital ventures (Huang et al., 2017, 2022; Kim & Kim, 2022; Lehmann et al., 2022), growth ambitions in nonfocal actors might be driven by design attitude (Boland & Collopy, 2004) and visionary foresight, as well as by pressure from investors, analysts, users, business press, and other stakeholders of the company. The status of being a nonfocal actor in the platform ecosystem cuts both ways. On the one hand, nonfocal actors enjoy being able to pursue low-cost innovation, as they benefit from the technical resources (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Rolland et al., 2018; Soh

& Grover, 2020) and the existing user base (Ceccagnoli et al., 2018; Cennamo, 2021; Rietveld & Eggers, 2018) of the focal actors. On the other hand, complementors are “at the mercy of the platform owner’s actions” (Hurni et al., 2022, p. 310), and their coupling with the focal actors is dependent on their access to the user bases and technical interfaces of the focal actors. Ecosystem momentum is another factor contributing to the contextual conditions that form the complementor’s dilemma. Ecosystem momentum is the progressive movement in the ecosystem enabling substantial growth, fueled by changing stakeholder interest—that is, ecosystem members’ sentiment about the attractiveness and value of using and exploring an innovation (Leiponen, 2024; Shi et al., 2024) and the underlying technology architecture, which involves the technological components essential to a platform ecosystem and how these relate to each other (Yoo et al., 2010; Baldwin & Woodard, 2010). In the case of Douyin, the changing stakeholder interest was assessed from changes in the focal actors’ commitment to short-form video as well as from end users’ engagement with short-form video and the interconnections of nonfocal actors with short-form video. Assessment of the technical architecture, on the other hand, drew on the layered technological components underlying the social networking ecosystem (e.g., smartphones and 4G networking devices, algorithmic-driven functionalities, short-form video as a new content medium) and the relationship between social networking technologies underlying the ecosystem. For instance, in our findings, shortform video was increasingly being upgraded from a peripheral technology that functioned on and extended the text/imagebased social technology to a core technology that offered similar complementary benefits to diverse nonfocal applications. Our operationalization of technical architecture as a key technical dimension of ecosystem momentum extends the work by Leiponen (2024), who defined ecosystem momentum by focusing on the social dimension of stakeholder interest. Please see the Appendix for further insight into our empirical assessment of the construct.

The complementor’s dilemma and strategies of coupling and growth: The contextual conditions set the scene for the complementor’s dilemma. We think of the complementor’s dilemma as the nonfocal actor’s conundrum as it deliberates on the unfavorable alternatives of (1) acting on growth ambitions but losing the support of focal platforms by going for decoupling or (2) pausing its growth ambitions by going for coupling. The ultimate goal for the complementor is to navigate the complementor’s dilemma within the confines of its dependency on focal actors and eventually elevate its position to that of a focal actor in the ecosystem.

Figure 7. The Nonfocal to Focal (NFTF) Process Model

Informed by our coupling view (Baldwin & Clark, 1999; Brunswicker et al., 2019; Orton & Weick, 1990; Tiwana et al., 2010) and our in-depth case study, we find that two mechanisms are imperative for making the nonfocal actor engage with the complementor’s dilemma: user scoping and product interfacing. User scoping, that is, the nonfocal actor’s actions to identify its market and attract the users of that market through seeding novel product functionality, influences the social dimension of coupling between the nonfocal and focal actors through shaping the product identity and user overlap in the ecosystem. Product interfacing, that is, the actions through which a nonfocal actor changes its product’s degree of interoperability, changes the nonfocal actor’s technical coupling with the focal actors in a platform ecosystem.

To become a focal actor, nonfocal actors must patiently navigate the complementor’s dilemma, enacting the user scoping and product interfacing mechanisms in ways that help them outgrow the platform ecosystem without losing access to the necessary resources of focal actors. Ecosystem momentum has a key role here. For instance, when the platform ecosystem’s stakeholders have limited interest in the nonfocal actor’s area of innovation and the ecosystem’s stable technical architecture is dominated by focal actors, the nonfocal actor typically finds that it has a fixed role and identity in the ecosystem due to a lack of momentum. Under such conditions, its growth ambitions are likely unrealistic, making it rational for the nonfocal to put them on hold and go for tight coupling through unchanged user scoping and intense product interfacing, which, in turn, leads to growth that mirrors the growth of the platform ecosystem.

When there is a growing interest from end users and certain focal actors to commit to the complementor’s product, and the technical architecture is favorable (e.g., widespread adoption of 4G technologies), the nonfocal actor can capitalize on the platform ecosystem’s momentum by selectively decoupling from actors in it. For instance, the nonfocal actor might decide to actively pursue user scoping through the acquisition of one focal actor’s users while working to retain the support of other focal actors in the platform ecosystem. We refer to such decoupling as selective user scoping and selective product interfacing, because the nonfocal actor selectively competes with some focal actors in the ecosystem by attracting their users while ensuring that the support of other focal actors is retained. As a result, selective coupling is a decoupling strategy that seeks to accomplish stratified growth, as the nonfocal actor partitions the platform ecosystem to prioritize certain actors at the expense of others.

In situations where end users find the product innovation to be increasingly compelling in the mainstream market, the nonfocal actor can proactively decouple from the focal actors by establishing sizable network effects that allow the loss of support to be withstood. Under this condition, the nonfocal actor can pursue imitative user scoping to develop a user base and technological profile similar to that of the focal platforms, thereby directly competing for users. Simultaneously, it can enact opportunistic product interfacing to accelerate user acquisition by extending outbound connections with other nonfocal actors in the ecosystem. This competitive coupling is an attempt to accomplish enveloped growth, which describes how the nonfocal actor grows by serving and capturing the same market as focal actors.

When the product innovation provides essential complementary benefits to diverse applications at the service layer of the ecosystem’s technical architecture, and all ecosystem members recognize the platform value that stems from the product innovation, the nonfocal actor might be emboldened to develop broader user and technological profiles that go past the focal platforms through unrestricted user scoping. Opening boundary resources and inviting inbound connections from diverse nonfocal actors across markets creates unrestricted product interfacing, enabling the nonfocal actor to develop its own platform ecosystem populated by an array of complementors. Together, this represents a loose coupling strategy with the objective of accomplishing sovereign growth in the platform ecosystem.

Dynamics: The nonfocal actor takes a dynamic approach to coupling. Each time a dilemma is navigated, the nonfocal actor’s new position in the platform ecosystem serves as input for a new dilemma due to a change in the coupling with focal actors. As with any path-creating process (Garud & Karnøe, 2003), the nonfocal actor engages in mindful deviation, trying to break out of its embedded couplings. The change in couplings positively and negatively affects the nonfocal actor’s technical and user resources for pursuing further growth and thus informs its ability to deal with a subsequent, new complementor’s dilemma. If, at any point in time, the nonfocal actor is content with its place within the platform ecosystem and loses its growth ambitions, or if there is no ecosystem momentum and the complementor’s dilemma remains, the nonfocal actor will not develop into a focal platform.

A nonfocal actor cannot become a focal platform without navigating multiple subsequent, emergent complementor’s dilemmas over time. Simply put, the nonfocal actor needs to iteratively decide whether to pursue a path with increasing coupling with focal actors or to pursue a path that involves decoupling from focal actors. Over time, the ecosystem momentum develops dynamically, as stakeholder interests and the technical architecture change. Therefore, under certain conditions without sufficient momentum, periods of growth may be interrupted by periods of stagnation. Although ecosystem momentum is largely exogenous to individual actors, a nonfocal actor’s decoupling strategies influence stakeholder interest and technical architecture in its favor. For example, Douyin pushed algorithmic short-form video into China’s social networking ecosystem to make it a very popular new feature, thereby paving the way for its own future growth.

Focal actor: When does a nonfocal actor with growth ambitions become a focal actor? The progression from nonfocal to focal requires the nonfocal actor to become more and more decoupled from the focal actors of the platform ecosystem. Such a transition demands that the ecosystem momentum remains favorable to the nonfocal actor. Even though this combination of circumstances is likely to be rare since ecosystem momentum can easily become unfavorable for the nonfocal actor due to changes in stakeholder interest or the technical architecture, positive ecosystem momentum in combination with the right management and technical decisions will create a tipping point, after which growth is no longer dependent on the technical resources and user base of other focal actors in the ecosystem (i.e., technical and social couplings), making the nonfocal actor loosely coupled in the platform ecosystem.

## Implications

Our research contributes to the innovation stream of the platform ecosystem literature (Agarwal & Kapoor, 2023; Constantinides et al., 2018; Thatchenkery & Katila, 2023; Tiwana, 2015; Wang, 2021) in general and to existing views on nonfocal actors (Cutolo & Kenney, 2021; Eaton et al., 2015; Hurni et al., 2022; Kude & Huber, 2024) in particular. First, we conceptualize the complementor’s dilemma as a significant phenomenon confronted by a nonfocal actor pursuing its own growth ambitions in platform ecosystems. We thus speak to the growing body of literature that focuses on nonfocal actors in platform ecosystems, such as device manufacturers (Selander et al., 2013) and application developers (Benlian et al., 2015; Qiu et al., 2017; Soh & Grover, 2020). We build on the increasing recognition that the complementor, as a nonfocal actor, is not entirely helpless in its relation to focal actors (Cutolo & Kenney, 2021; Eaton et al., 2015; Hurni et al., 2022). The complementor’s dilemma captures the conundrum experienced by a nonfocal actor that is deliberating on the unfavorable alternatives of acting on growth ambitions at the risk of losing the support of focal actors needed for that growth or not acting on growth ambitions to maintain the support of focal actors. We demonstrate how this conundrum can manifest differently over time as a reflection of contextual conditions evolving with the platform ecosystem’s momentum and the changes in coupling between the nonfocal and focal actor.

Interestingly, alongside focal actors (Foerderer et al., 2019; Huber et al., 2017; Sarker et al., 2012; Wareham et al., 2014), nonfocal actors can change the interdependencies between actors in platform ecosystems. Unlike focal actors that have “domination power” in platform ecosystems (Hurni et al., 2022, p. 315), the actions of a nonfocal actor may address, but can hardly ever redress, the complementor’s dilemma since whatever direction the complementor takes may come back to bite it. In this regard, the complementor’s dilemma is always salient for a nonfocal actor with growth ambitions and is therefore remanifested in each new iteration of our process model. The recurrence of the complementor’s dilemma and the difficulty of overcoming it may explain why so few nonfocal actors grow to become focal actors in their own right (Hurni et al., 2022) and, indirectly, why platform ecosystems tend to evolve into oligopolistic structures (Eisenmann et al., 2006). In other words, our research does not suggest that a nonfocal actor will always be able to resolve the complementor’s dilemma. Even though the complementor may successfully navigate one iteration of the dilemma, evolving ecosystem conditions may cause the nonfocal actor to put its growth ambitions on hold.

Second, the process model contributes to existing views on complementor-platform relationships (Cutolo & Kenney, 2021; Eaton et al., 2015; Hurni et al., 2022) by proposing ways of navigating the complementor’s dilemma. In particular, informed by views on coupling across the platform ecosystem (Brunswicker et al., 2019; Jacobides et al., 2018; Tiwana et al., 2010), modularity (Baldwin & Clark, 1999; Brunswicker & Mukherjee, 2023; Schilling, 2000), and social psychology (Orton & Weick, 1990; Weick, 1976), the model zooms in on the interconnections between the nonfocal actor and the relevant focal actors in the platform ecosystem. It proposes four different coupling strategies (tight coupling, selective coupling, competitive coupling, and loose coupling) through which the nonfocal actor can attempt to navigate the complementor’s dilemma by enacting two mechanisms: user scoping and product interfacing. Each coupling strategy manifests a particular growth strategy, expressing the dynamics through which the nonfocal actor expresses its growth aspirations but with an eye on the risks versus benefits of doing so. Mirrored growth results from a tight coupling strategy, where the nonfocal actor limits its growth to a rate that reflects the growth of the platform ecosystem as a whole. Stratified growth results from a selective coupling strategy, where the nonfocal actor partitions the platform ecosystem with the intention of growing in a promising subset of the ecosystem. Enveloped growth results from a competitive coupling strategy, where the nonfocal actor grows by serving the same market as the focal actors and capturing value from it. Sovereign growth results from a loose coupling strategy, where the nonfocal actor is free to explore and extend the boundary of the platform ecosystem at will. Taken as a whole, the proposed coupling strategies through which a nonfocal actor can navigate the complementor’s dilemma with its associated growth strategies extend existing work on nonfocal actors in platform ecosystems. However, our model assumes multifinality; that is, following a particular growth strategy does not guarantee that the nonfocal actors will become focal platforms (e.g., Agarwal & Kapoor, 2023; Hurni et al., 2022; Kude & Huber, 2024). The growth of nonfocal actors may not play out as expected, as ecosystem momentum may be unfavorable due to unexpected technological developments or the dominant power of a focal platform. We encourage future research on the conditions under which a complementor is unable to turn into a focal platform; in other words, when nonfocal to focal growth is not possible.

It is important to note that to achieve any of the above growth strategies, user scoping and product interfacing are essential for a nonfocal actor facing the complementor’s dilemma. These mechanisms must be used strategically and repeatedly in various enactments. Repeated user scoping is essential for the nonfocal actor to gradually secure a larger stake in the user market over time. This involves user seeding through specific campaigns or targeting new user groups and extending product functionality by developing new features and mimicking those of focal actors. Additionally, a nonfocal actor can iteratively reshape its technical connections in the platform ecosystem through product interfacing. This involves altering inbound connections with the focal actor’s interfaces and introducing outbound connections to other nonfocal actors in the ecosystem. While focal actors also continuously scope users and change their product interfacing (e.g., Foerderer et al., 2018; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Shi et al., 2023), the timing and manner of these actions are crucial for a nonfocal actor. For instance, a nonfocal actor can only imitate one focal actor’s product functionalities if it is certain of another focal actor’s support. In this case, ecosystem momentum significantly influences the evolution of a complementor into a focal actor, and this is something over which the nonfocal actor, unlike the focal actor, has limited control.

Finally, our research responds to the call for cross-level analysis of platform ecosystems (Wang, 2021). Douyin’s firm-level engagement with the complementor’s dilemma is dependent on ecosystem-level momentum. Interestingly, our process research illustrates that changing conditions in terms of the growing significance of a category ecosystem (short-form video) can help the nonfocal actor evolve its role into one more loosely coupled with the focal actor. Extant studies look at intraecosystemic dynamics at the business level (Eaton et al., 2015; Huber et al., 2017; Hurni et al., 2022) and category level (Agarwal & Kapoor, 2023; Alaimo et al., 2020; Selander et al., 2013), but future research would benefit from focusing on interecosystemic dynamics (cf. Kude & Huber, 2024).

## Conclusion

Our study is not without limitations. First, we rely on the extreme case (Gerring, 2006) of Douyin in the social network ecosystem in China. While Douyin serves as a paradigmatic example of a nonfocal actor’s growth, we recognize that the interdependency structure of this ecosystem is less solidified than that of other platform ecosystems, such as iOS and Android. Future research could examine other ecosystem contexts to better understand the conditions under which a complementor may confront and navigate (successfully or not) the complementor’s dilemma. Second, it would be useful to design large-N studies to study the growth patterns of nonfocal actors to determine and test the efficacy of different strategies on the growth of complementors in platform ecosystems.

This paper advances our understanding of a core challenge faced by complementors during growth: how to navigate their growth ambitions against the dependency on focal actors. Our NFTF model conceptualizes the complementor’s dilemma and explains the process through which it can be navigated. Hence, our study provides insight into possible power shifts between nonfocal actors and focal actors in ecosystems, finding that the hegemony of platform ecosystems can sometimes be successfully challenged. We hope that our research stimulates further research in the emerging area of nonfocal actors in platform ecosystems.
