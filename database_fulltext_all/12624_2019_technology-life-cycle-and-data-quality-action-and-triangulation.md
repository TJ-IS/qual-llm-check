# Technology life cycle and data quality: Action and triangulation<sup>☆</sup>

Daniel E. O'Leary

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Technology life cycle Data quality Non-stationary data Hype cycle Data biases Data phase triangulation

## A B S T R A C T

Where a technology is its life cycle can make a diference in the data generated by or about adopting, using or implementing that technology. As a result, it is arguable that adoption, usage or implementation data generated early in a technology's life cycle is directly comparable to data generated later in the life cycle. In particular, comparisons of early and late data can result in a number of disparate results and limit replicability, because of biases in the data and non-stationary data. This paper suggests that it can be important for information systems researchers to disclose an estimate of the location in the technology's life cycle, as part of their analysis.

The data life cycle discussion is then extended to the notion of “data phase triangulation,” which is contrasted with “methodology triangulation” and “data (collection) triangulation.” In addition, we discuss the importance of being able to use the findings from life cycle-based research to “push” a technology from one phase to another phase.

## 1. Introduction

When enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems were first introduced in the early 1990's there were no established methodologies for implementing those systems and no best practices for using them. This resulted in a number of cost and time overruns, while not achieving their objectives [1]. In 1996 SAP, the leading ERP vendor introduced ASAP, Accelerated SAP, designed to speed implementation and control costs, facilitating implementation of SAP. Introduction of ASAP helped firms better control their ERP implementations. In addition, as ERP systems evolved, a number of capabilities, such as industry specific versions and business intelligence, were added to the systems. As a result, of these developments, it is arguable that data variables associated with the adoption, use and implementation of early ERP systems are comparable to later versions.

ERP systems are just one example of a technology where characteristics and analytics captured about technology implementations change as the technology moves through its life cycle and becomes better understood. Accordingly, comparison of characteristics about the adoption, use and implementation of a technology, say cost and time, are likely to be diferent early in the technology life cycle as compared to late in the life cycle. In particular, data describing the adoption, use and implementation of technologies can be non-stationary, potentially generating diferent findings, from similar events at diferent times and limiting replication of results.

## 1.1. Data quality

Recently, there have been key concerns about data quality and the ability to replicate research about information systems (IS). As noted in Marsden and Pingry [2].

“... there are major, persistent numerical data quality issues in IS academic research. These issues undermine the ability to replicate our research — a critical element of scientific investigation and analysis. In IS empirical and analytics research articles, the amount of space devoted to the details of data collection, validation, and/or quality pales in comparison to the space devoted to the evaluation and selection of relatively sophisticated model form(s) and estimation technique(s). Yet erudite modeling and estimation can yield no immediate value or be meaningfully replicated without high quality data inputs.”

## 1.2. Purpose of this paper

The purpose of this paper is to note some of the potential theoretical diferences in the data generated by and about a technology as it moves through its life cycle, primarily focusing on the biases and non-stationarities of data that relates to adoption, use or implementation as a technology moves through Gartner's hype cycle, the maturity curve and the adoption curve. As a result, this paper drills down on both the “when” and “where” collected of data quality. Specifically, as noted by Marsden and Pingry [2] “When refers to the time at which the data is collected” and “Where refers to the location (virtual or real) of the data collection.” In particular, this paper argues that the where or when a specific technology is in its life cycle provides important information about the replicability of information systems research. In particular, data gathered early in the life of a technology about its adoption, use and implementation are not likely to be directly comparable to data gathered late in the technology life cycle, limiting replicability. As a result, where data is collected in the life cycle is a core data concern. After making the case for the importance of life cycle for data, this paper then investigates some implications, focusing on data phase triangulation and potentially using the research to push the technology through other phases of the life cycle.

## 2. Hype cycle, adoption cycle and performance curve<sup>1</sup>

Gartner's hype cycle provides ([3], p. 1) “… an overview of relative maturity of technologies …” and “it characterizes the typical progression of an emerging technology from user and media overenthusiasm through a period of disillusionment to an eventual understanding of the technology's relevance and role in a market or domain”. The hype cycle captures the amount of discussion, the expectations and the knowledge about emerging technologies generally available over time in a dynamic model of technology evolution and use.

There are five basic phases associated with Gartner's Hype cycle as seen in Fig. 1 (e.g., [4]). The first phase is the “Technology Trigger” also referred to as the “Innovation Trigger” which occurs when there is a “break-through” or some event such as a public demonstration of the use of a technology or a product launch that initiates interest by the press or the industry. Next, the technology begins to near the “Peak of Inflated Expectations,” where expectations for the technology rise above the current capabilities of the technology. At this point, press about the technology is largely positive and often speculative. After the peak of inflated expectations is the trough of disillusionment. In the trough, people realize that the actual results will not meet the expectations, because of “… (p)roblems with performance, slower-than expected adoption or a failure to deliver financial returns in the time anticipated all lead to missed expectations, and disillusionment sets in”. Firms try the technology and find that results from actual use are different than the original speculation. In the “slope of enlightenment” organizations' and vendors' experimentation and work begins to generate a better understanding of the technology's applicability, risks and benefits. During this phase, vendors begin to provide commercial of the-shelf methodologies and tools designed to ease the implementation process. Finally, in the plateau of productivity the technology's realworld benefits are demonstrated and accepted. An increasing number of organizations feel comfortable with the reduced levels of risk, and the phase of rapid adoption begins.

Fig. 1. Hype cycle stages [5].

Some of the events that occur across the hype cycle indicate the potential for both bias and non-stationarity in the data. As seen in Fig. 2 there are clear diferences in technology features as a technology goes from first, to second to third generation products and services. Early versions of the technology are prototypes, and are not really working products. The first generation is likely to be higher in price and require customization to use in real world settings. Initial positive press turns into negative press and firms experience dificulty using the technology. In addition, there are clear diferences in the number of users, going from zero, in the beginning, to less than 5% as the technology moves into the slope of enlightenment to 20% as the technology enters the plateau of productivity, when the technology nears the peak of the maturity curve. At that 20% mark there often is a high growth phase for the technology, typically generating additional competitors and affecting costs of adoption.

There are at least two other curves of direct interest, in addition to the hype cycle, used to capture the flow of a technology through time as illustrated in Fig. 3: the maturity curve and the cumulative adoption curve. The maturity curve captures the improvement of a technology over time, while the adoption curve captures the extent of the adoption of the technology over time. At the intersection of the three curves, the technology achieves roughly a 20% adoption rate. As a result, those organizations before the intersection can be characterized as “early adopters,” while those after are “later adopters.”

Early and late adopting groups of firms likely have diferent characteristics consistent with their timing of the adoption of the particular technology. Firms involved with a technology early in the life cycle may be larger firms with available resources, struggling firms looking for technology salvation from a stressful situation, firms with a strategy dependent on a particular technology and other rationales. Fenn et al. [5] suggests that adopting firms are diferentiated across diferent variables, including, the innovation's value to the firm and how good the organization is at tolerating and managing risk. However, additional firm characteristics could include the extent to which the in novation is being embedded in the firm's processes, the firm's tech nology strategy, the industry, the competition, partners, regulation and other factors.

Thus, this suggests that there can be a fundamental diference between the data gathered from early and late adopters. Early adopters are characterized by Gartner in Fig. 4 as firms of Type A (Aggressive) or Type B, while those adopting at or after the 20% mark are characterized as Type C (Conservative) firms. The cumulative adoption S-curve and the maturity curve efectively “level of” shortly after roughly the 20% adoption point. This suggests that there is very rapid growth in adoption up to that point and that technology capabilities are relatively stable from that time forward. Stable capabilities are likely necessary for stable data.

It should be noted that the risks for each of the three strategies, A, B and C, are diferent. Since type A, B and C firms each adopt the technology at diferent times. Firms in categories A, B, or C likely face diferent adoption, use and implementation issues, with the greatest risk and costs, generally for type A firms and the least risk and costs for type C firms (except for lagging competitors), but the greatest potential strategic gain for Type A firms. This also suggests that adoption, im plementation and use data gathered at diferent points in the process will difer for each of those three groups. In addition, it suggests that there can be substantial diferences between the types of firms that are early in the adoption process as opposed to later in the adoption process that also implies a lack of stable data.

## 3. Data biases apparent from the hype curve

These difusion curves and this discussion illustrates some of the efects on data of the technology life cycle, broadly suggesting that the data about technology adoption, use and implementation is non-stationary over time and that there are certain biases in the data at different times. At the innovation trigger there is little if any empirical data available about firm use of the technology. At the innovation trigger and through the trough of disillusionment, there is largely individual firm case data about early adopters. In the peak of inflated expectations there are likely cases that are biased with positive expectations and positive sentiment. In the trough of disillusionment, the data are likely be biased by negative sentiment, as some firms literally find out what does not work when they begin to implement and use the technology. Although such case data can provide signals as to issues associated with use of the technology, at this point in the life cycle there is no data available that would allow the ability to provide broad sweeping generalizations about a technology – only early phase information is available. Instead the data is case study data and the tone of those cases depends on when they occurred.

Even as the technology moves into the slope of enlightenment, only 5% of the firms have adopted the technology. Thus, any study of firms' use of technology at that time would likely be using a biased sample of firms, those willing and interested in trying the technology before it is well-established. (Type A Firms). Further, as noted by Gartner, the Type A firms are more aggressive. It is only when the technology enters the plateau of productivity that roughly 20% of the firms would be using the technology, and it is arguable if that first 20% also is a biased sample from which to investigate the adoption and use of the tech nology, since that 20% would be largely Type A and B firms

4. Impact of life cycles on research methodologies and the data they generate

Some methodologies and the data that they use and generate, are likely to be more afected by location of the technology in the life cycle, potentially generating biased data. This section examines surveys, event studies and behavioral research, but could be extended to other methodologies.

## 4.1. Surveys

Since surveys typically efectively gather opinion, those surveys are particularly vulnerable to where a technology is in its life cycle. Data gathered during the peak of inflated expectations is likely to be of positive sentiment, while surveys gathered during the trough of dis illusionment can be negative. Opinions gathered from firms of Type C during the peak of inflated expectations are likely to be uninformed because they will not be implementing or using for some time. Opinions gathered from firms of type A during the period of the plateau of productivity are likely to capture knowledge gathered during the peak of inflated expectations. In addition, both sets of firms will have diferent perspectives on the risks of the technology.

Further, at some point in the life cycle, opinion does not matter, because actual results are being generated. As a result, it is arguable whether opinion surveys are even appropriate for some variables at diferent points in the life cycle (e.g., [6]).

## 4.2. Event studies

Event studies investigate some organizational behavior both before and after an event to see if there is an impact of the event on some dependent variable (e.g., [7]), such as technology adoption. Oftentimes in information systems research technology adoption is the independent variable and stock price is used as a dependent variable to determine impact. However, whereas early in the life cycle, data might suggest that value creation (abnormal returns) from adoption that is not necessarily the case latter in the life cycle when introduction of the technology might not bring the same value creating opportunities, or firms may be “penalized” for being a late adopter. For example, it is likely that in the beginning of the life cycle, the technology can provide a first mover advantage, whereas latter the adoption can simply mean that the adopter is keeping up with the competition or has lagged be hind. Further, as the stock market becomes more informed, it is likely to be able to make more informed decisions based on the information gathered about the success of other firms implementing the technology, afecting market price and assessment of alignment between adopting firms, the technology and the timing of that technology. Unfortunately, in the beginning of the technology life cycle that knowledge is limited.

## 4.3. Behavioral/experimental research

Behavioral research can be done across the life cycle of a tech nology. Experiments can be used to generate data about a range of technology uses. However, the design of the experiment and the resulting data that is generated can be limited by the existing capabilities of the technology and the experimenter's knowledge of the technology. As seen above in the discussion of the hype cycle, technology cap abilities change as the technology evolves. Accordingly, experiments generated at the beginning of the life cycle run the risk of being irrelevant as second and third generation products and capabilities are released.

## 5. Impact of life cycle on diferent variables

Diferent variables, such as adoption, use, benefit, implementation time and others, are likely to be more afected by life cycle than other variables. As seen in Fig. 4 above, the rate of adoption on the adoption curve varies through the early life cycle. Since usage depends on adoption, usage data is likely to be diferent in the beginning of the life cycle rather than later in the life cycle. At individual firms, users are not likely to be as familiar with the technology in the beginning, and there can be a need for substantially more education as compared to later in the life cycle. In addition, as noted in Fig. 2, additional features typically are added to technology as it moves through its life cycle, with those features making technology use easier, later in the life cycle. The expected benefit (e.g., stock price, costs or revenues) to an organization of adopting a technology is likely to difer as a technology moves through its life cycle and knowledge of the ability to receive benefits also is likely to evolve as new capabilities are added to the technology. Implementation likely is more dificult early in the life cycle, rather than later in the life cycle, as vendors and users gain more experience with the technology. Thus, implementation time and costs are likely to vary. As in the example at the start of the paper, SAP developed ASAP to facilitate implementation. By 2014 [8] ASAP was on its 8th version, providing more guidance to aid implementation of SAP.

## 6. Some other sources of non-stationarity in technology-based data

Finally, the data gathered about the adoption, use and implementation of technology in organizations can be limited by a number of factors, also potentially resulting in non-stationarity.

## 6.1. Technology features

Early in the hype curve, technologies do not have the many features that will ultimately be developed for and around them. As seen above in Fig. 3, when “climbing the slope” second generation capabilities and third generation out of the box capabilities are introduced. In each, such capabilities could make both implementation and use of the technology easier and cheaper, influencing a range of variables of interest.

## 6.2. Experience with technology — best practices

As the technology goes through these difusion curves, organizations' experience with the technology increases over time, and best practices are established and evolve [9, 21]. Those best practices emerge as diferent organizations use the technology and find out what works and what does not work and as the technology changes. Best practices typically evolve over time in concert with technology changes. At the beginning of the life cycle for a technology, there are no best practices directly available, whereas the technology moves into the slope of enlightenment and the plateau of productivity, best practices emerge and facilitate adoption, implementation and use. Implementing best practices allows organizations to capture value, while minimizing risks associated with the use and implementation of the technology.

## 6.3. Technology and processes

As noted by Hammer [10] with the introduction of new technolo gies, processes need to change to fully leverage the new technologies. Unfortunately, until firms have actually used a technology it can be dificult to understand how processes need to change, limiting adoption, negatively afecting usage and inhibiting implementation. In any case, after a technology has been around for some time, it is likely that firms are in a position to change their processes to facilitate technology use and implementation. As a result, use and implementation of a technology early in the difusion process is at a disadvantage particu larly when compared to later in the difusion process.

## 7. Triangulation — background

Researchers have developed research strategies aimed at generating method triangulation and data (collection) triangulation. This section of the paper briefly reviews key background aspects of these types of triangulation and the following section extends these concepts to “data phase triangulation.”

## 7.1. Method triangulation

Denzin [11] defined triangulation as “… the combination of methodologies in the study of the same phenomena.” As noted by Jick [12] and others, the triangulation metaphor is based on military strategy and navigation, whereby multiple reference points facilitate location of an object's position ([13], p. 273). The use of multiple methodologies can be traced back to Campbell and Fiske [14] who generated the notion of “multiple operationalism,” while arguing that multiple methods should be used in the validation process.

## 7.2. Validation vs. holistic understanding

Method triangulation has been proposed for two primary purposes, validation and to ensure “… a complete, holistic and contextual portrayal of the unit(s) under study” ([12], p. 603). On the one hand, having multiple methods provides assurance that the conclusions reached are appropriate. On the other hand, using multiple methods provides a more thorough analysis that is likely to bring out a richer and more robust set of findings.

## 7.3. Within methods vs. between methods

Denzen (1978, p. 301) called the situation of using multiple tech niques within a given method, the “within method” approach. From a statistical approach, this could include multiple regression approaches, say linear and generalized methods, to examine the same data or with a survey there could be multiple scales (e.g., [12])

Denzen (1978, p. 302) labeled “between (or across) methods” the setting where a two or more distinct methods are used, typically as part of the validation process, where those multiple methods provide similar findings and thus are found to be congruent. For information systems researchers this would involve using multiple methods to examine some aspect of the problem. As noted by Jick [12], “Multiple and independent measures, if they reach the same conclusions, provide more certain portrayal of the … phenomenon.”

## 7.4. Data (collection) triangulation

However, more than methods have been the focus of triangulation. Kaplan and Duchon ([15], p. 575) noted that there are advantages to data (collection) triangulation “Collecting diferent kinds of data by diferent methods from diferent sources provides a wider range of coverage that may result in a fuller picture of the unit under study. .. Moreover, using multiple methods increases the robustness of results because findings can be strengthened through triangulation - the crossvalidation achieved when diferent kinds and sources of data converge and are found congruent.”

## 7.5. Summary

Throughout the triangulation literature there seems to be one underlying assumption that it better to use multiple methods or capture multiple sets of data from multiple sources. As noted by Jicks [12], “The efectiveness of the triangulation rests on the premise that the weakness in each single method will be compensated by the counter balancing strengths of another.”

## 8. Data phase triangulation

This section generalizes the method and data collection triangulation to the notion of data phase triangulation. As with method triangulation, data phase triangulation can facilitate validation and the development of a more complete and robust view of a technology. We will say that there is a data phase triangulation if multiple sets of data are gathered at a specific phase in the life cycle or if multiple sets of data are gathered at diferent points in the life cycle, and that data is analyzed in the context of that life cycle to investigate some phenomenon.

## 8.1. Within data phase vs. between data phase

Researchers can triangulate on multiple sets of data within a single phase or between multiple phases. As an example, as seen in Fig. 5, triangulation within a given phase could contrast multiple corporate experiments from a single corporation or experiments from multiple corporations, say when the technology is in an emerging phase. For example, the data analyzed by O'Leary [16] derived from one experi ment from one corporation. That data was presented at a workshop where other corporations were discussing their use of the same technology, generating the potential for other data sets in that same phase. (See Fig. 5.)

Similarly, triangulation between phases could contrast data gathered from diferent points in the life cycle with each other. This could be done by a researcher who has access to data across the life cycle, by analysis of research from multiple researchers, who examined the technology at diferent points in time, by an analysis of research performed in earlier studies or by an organization with access to benchmarking data on some set of analytics. An illustration is in Fig. 6.

Although there appear to be a number of advantages, unfortunately, there are some potential limitations of data phase triangulation in either setting. The data available may vary by the data format and even the available variables might vary. Further, when comparing data from multiple researchers or diferent phases, the research questions may difer resulting in heterogeneous research and data generating additional data diferences, making further comparative analysis dificult or impossible.

## 8.2. Validation vs. holistic understanding

Data phase triangulation provides data validation for a number of rationales. First, to the extent that there are expectations about the use of the technology, the data can be investigated to see that it matches expectations as a validation test, using either inter-organizational or intra-organizational comparisons. For example, expectations may come from gathering benchmarking data from other firms that may have already implemented the technology (inter-organization). Second, in some cases the data might be compared to what has happened with other technologies that the firm has implemented, to determine if the data changes are consistent with what has happened with other tech nologies (intra-organization). For organizations that seldom implement technologies, there may be limited information available to help expectations. This lack of data provides another real-world rationale for why companies that seldom implement technologies, have dificulties when they do decide to implement a new technology.

Data phase triangulation can help to facilitate a holistic under standing of the particular technology under consideration by better understanding how the technology functions across the life cycle. As a simple example, the number of users could be tracked over time and analyzed with respect to a set of related variables. As another example, the hype curve itself, suggests that on some dimensions, diferent technologies seem to follow the hype curve providing a holistic understanding of technology.

## 8.3. Data access and triangulation in research applications

This paper is not arguing that all researchers need to include data from multiple phases or multiple data from each phase, in each research investigation. Often, researchers only have access to one data set from one phase in the life cycle, and they are delighted to have access to that data. Thus, it would be a shame to miss the contribution of that analysis in the literature. However, there will be settings where researchers have data available from multiple locations in the life cycle and those should be leveraged. Further, multiple single data sets can be used to facilitate meta-research about a given technology. In settings where a single set of data is available, those findings can be contrasted with the rest of the literature to provide a richer picture of the phenomenon involved.

## 8.4. Integration of data phase triangulation

Data phase triangulation can be integrated with other forms of triangulation, such as method triangulation as summarized in Fig. 7, to merge multiple methods, multiple data sources and multiple locations in the technology's life cycle. It would appear that, if it is feasible, that research should try to align with method and data phase triangulation. As an example of the use of Fig. 7, O'Leary [16] used a single phase of data and multiple methods, so that paper is a method triangulation paper.

## 8.5. Propensity matching

One potential approach, in some settings, to mitigating the impact of life cycle is to create a matched sample using propensity analysis [17–19], with time as one of the variables as the basis of the matching, as a means of investigating causal relationships. As a recent example, in an analysis of analytic crowdsourcing contests, O'Leary [20] used the year of the contest as one of the variables on which the observations were matched.

## 9. Actions: meaningful and impactful research

One measure of research impact is whether the research helped drive a technology from one phase to another or helped technology mature from early phases to later phases. Design research is often seen in this light – building artifacts and using those artifacts to make a diference. However, other forms of information systems research also can push the technology along the life cycle. For example, empirical research could identify limitations in a technology or the use of the technology and propose approaches to mitigate those limitations. As an example, in an analysis of early life cycle phase data generated by a corporate experiment, O'Leary [16] found decreasing participation over time in the use of a prediction market. Such a finding suggests that unless approaches were generated to mitigate that phenomenon the technology might not progress the entire life cycle. Accordingly, O'Leary [16] presented some opportunities to mitigate the decreasing participation. That research could be extended by performing experiments to determine which approaches worked best, generating addition insights and potentially facilitating movement along the life cycle. In addition, further research could be done to understand the extent to which other markets had similar decreases in participation.

It is important that champions of particular technologies do not try and hide or ignore research that finds limitations. Instead, champions should seek out such research and find ways to mitigate limitations. In so doing, such research can provide the ability to move the technology along the life cycle.

## 10. “What” (technology) pushes through these life cycles

Up to this point, we have been largely focused on new and independent technologies coming into being and then pushing through the life cycle. However, it is likely that many other aspects of human endeavor involving technologies push through life cycles. For example, some large information systems projects take multiple years. In those projects there often is initial exuberance, followed by a realization of the dificulty of implementation as the engagement goes forward. As the engagement gains momentum, and more aspects of the software are configured, it is likely that the performance increases. As the system is rolled out, more and more users adopt the system, providing a scenario similar to that in Fig. 3, providing an opportunity to use this approach. This indicates that technology does not need to be new to apply the concepts used in this paper, even information technology engagements can go through similar life cycles. Further, rather than just individual technologies, correlated technologies could be examined.

## 11. Estimating life cycle location

A key implication of the life cycle impact is the importance of carefully documenting the system being analyzed and the context of its use at the time of the research. That information can be used to help assess the life cycle location.

Researchers can estimate where a technology is in its life cycle using multiple other sources of information. First, life cycles are provided by diferent information technology research firms, such as Gartner. Second, the type of data can be indicative of its location in the life cycle. For example, generally corporate experiments with technologies occur early in the life cycle. As a result, the source of data can provide important information. Third, measures of adoption, such as number of organizations using the software can provide estimates as to the location in the life cycle. Fourth, management and others might be asked so that there is information relating to the “perceived” life cycle location. Finally, as above, all of this information can be used to triangulate the location.

## 12. Summary, contributions and extensions

This paper has argued that “where” a technology is in its life cycle or “when” the technology is investigated, can afect data gathered about the technology use and implementation. Data can be missing, data can be biased, and in some locations on the life cycle there can be a positive and a negative sentiment associated with the data. Data can be biased toward aggressive firms or data can be biased toward conservative firms.

In addition, characteristics of technology and their implementation can result in non-stationary data. Technology features evolve over time as a technology matures. There typically are no best practices for a technology at the beginning of its life cycle, but best practices emerge as organizations gain experience. Technologies and processes are in evitably linked afecting how the technology is implemented and the extent and quality of the impact. The introduction of new technologies generally requires the reengineering of processes to gain the most from technologies.

## Contributions

This paper has used technology difusion curves to generate rationales for why data about technology use and implementation is likely to be biased and non-stationary. Those biases and that non-stationarity can be an important reasons why research based on data early in the technology difusion life cycle may not be consistent with data generated later in the life cycle. As a result, the discussion in this paper suggests that researchers document the system and to the extent possible, disclose the relative location of their data in the technology's life cycle or disclose information about that location. In addition, this paper has extended the notion of triangulation to include “data phase triangulation”, extending notions of method triangulation and data (collection) triangulation.

## Extensions

There are a number of potential extensions to this extension. First, this paper examined the efects of the technology's life cycle on three methodologies (surveys, events and behavioral). Future research could examine the efects on additional methodologies. Second, additional variables beyond adoption, usage and implementation could be investigated. Third, additional sources of non-stationarities could be analyzed. Fourth, research on “sample mixing” from diferent portions of the life cycle, say through the use of propensity analysis, needs to be investigated. Fifth, since some data biases emerge because of diferent types of firms adopting technologies at diferent times, more analysis could be directed to those firm characteristics. Sixth, the concepts in this paper can be extended to other emerging technologies, such as blockchain [22]. Seventh, the data discussion in this paper is not limited to numeric data, but can also include text [23].
