# Informing strategic IS change: Towards a ‘meta-learning’ framework

John Pillay <sup>a</sup>, Ray Hackney <sup>b,</sup>⇑, Ashley Braganza <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Cranfield University, UK <sup>b</sup> Brunel University, UK

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 20 February 2010 Received in revised form 25 November 2011 Accepted 16 December 2011 Available online 9 January 2012

Keywords: Change theory Experiential learning Meta-learning framework Practitioner guidance process IS change

## a b s t r a c t

This paper combines theoretical models on organisational and Information Systems (ISs) change with the experiential theorising of practitioners from a case study site. Contemporary strands of IS and organisational change literature were reviewed, grouping common themes into planning, leadership, learning and culture approaches. This theoretical basis was extended through a case analysis of an IS organisation in the banking industry which had undergone wide ranging and successful strategic change. Twenty in-depth interviews were conducted with senior experienced change practitioners from across the organisation. Three propositions on IS and organisational change theory were developed from the finding for this research. Firstly, IS change is a subset of organisational change which is frequently noted and argued through existing academic or ‘espoused’ change theories. Secondly, whether successful or unsuccessful, strategic change is in itself a rich learning experience which may be leveraged through experience, intuition and self-reflexion. Thirdly, managers develop their own ‘theories in use’ of change based on experiential learning and apply them in a highly context specific ‘informed’ way. The main contribution of the research is a ‘meta-learning’ framework for informing IS strategic change which suggests valuable theoretical insights, a practitioner implementation process and proposals for further research directions and limitations.

## 1. Introduction

Although difficult to definitively measure, the failure rate of Information Systems (ISs) change initiatives, is almost universally regarded as extremely high, and has been variously estimated at between 40% and 70% of all projects undertaken (Braganza and Möllenkramer, 2002; Royal Academy of Engineering and British Computer Society, 2004). Given the enormous costs and wasted effort, this level of failure is unacceptable to both the sponsors and recipients of change. This research is focused on investigating ways in which we can develop more successful and better informed methods of delivering strategic change. In this paper we note that superior change outcomes can be derived through integrating change theory with practical experience. We also argue for a self-reflexive mode of change management, based on ‘‘meta-learning’’ using past experiences and context sensitivity applied to the specific change environment (Argyris and Schon, 1978; Biggs, 1985; Losada, 1999).

Our research found limited integration between IS and organisational change theory which, although surprising, highlights the opportunity to bring these literatures together (Hackney et al., 2004; Heath and Heath, 2008). This research was further developed through a case study using data collected from experienced change practitioners from an IS organisation which has undergone significant strategic change. Through our consequent theoretical insights and empirical findings we propose a novel ‘meta-learning’ change management framework, which integrates change theory with change practice, and provides a basis for exploiting strategic IS initiatives.

## 2. Literature review

The IS literature has long recognised the need to embrace change, and to develop a holistic approach to IS change management. This has led to a variety of approaches such as ‘systems’ theory (Checkland, 1981; Stowell et al., 1997), structuration theory (Orlikowski and Robey, 1991; DeSanctis and Poole, 1994), and ‘‘social’’ theory (Walsham, 1993; Orlikowski, 1996; Walsham and Sahay, 1999; Balagun, 2006). There have also been attempts to explicitly integrate the social and the technical into so called ‘socio-technical’ theories (Mumford, 2003; Lyytinen and Newman, 2008). Theorists have also sought to isolate critical issues in IS change management (Brooks. 1987: Connell and Shafer. 1989: Willcocks and Lester. 1999), and there have been attempts to describe the process of IS change (Abdel-Hamid and Madnick, 1990; Robey and Newman, 1996). Other theorists have attempted to codify change by developing IS change methodologies through attention to organisational processes and culture (Iivari and Huisman, 2007; Hackney et al., 2008; Franken, 2009).

However, alongside these discussions in the IS domain, mainstream organisational change management theory has also developed rapidly and has created its own traditions and theoretical directions (Mellahi and Sminia, 2009). As a result, change management theory and IS change theory are now quite disconnected, and we believe that this is a lost opportunity. It is important to learn from the wide body of organisational change literature and how it can inform the management practices undertaken by IS analysts. Our research also focuses on the self-reflexive ‘meta’ learning processes that successful organisations (Argyris & Schon), teams (Losada, 1999) and individual practitioners (Biggs, 1985) use to improve performance over time. The concept of meta-learning is historically well noted in the literature through the process by which learners become aware and increasingly in control of habits of perception, inquiry, learning and growth that they have internalised (Hare, 1952; Maudsley, 1979; van Someren, 2000; Davenport and Harris, 2007). The essence of the approach is synthesised through manager assumptions, structures, change processes and facilitation. Consequently, the value of the meta-learning concept to our research is critically apparent. As evidenced, respondents determined their own intellectual experiential learning, noted as, ‘The insight gained through the conscious or unconscious internalisation of our own or observed experiences which build upon our past experiences of knowledge’ (Beard and Wilson, 2002, p 16), as a fundamental contributor to decision making and enactment. In this paper, we contend that this combination of experience and practice, when harnessed well, leads to more successful informed IS change outcomes.

## 3. Change theories in use: thematic groupings

The literature on organisational change is vast and growing. The practicing change manager now faces a bewildering array of diverse perspectives, offering advice on how to manage change (Bennis et al., 1961; Van de Ven and Poole, 1995; Kotter, 1996; Senge et al., 2002). Ranging from Sun Tzu’s The Art of War written two and a half thousand years ago (Sun Tzu, trans. Wing, 2000) to recent articles which note the strategic ‘frontiers’ of change management (Mellahi and Sminia, 2009).

There have been various attempts to assemble and categorise change management theory into common unifying themes or frameworks (Bennis et al., 1961; Van de Ven and Poole, 1995; Mintzberg et al., 1998; Caldwell, 2006). Each of these reviews takes a different approach to classifying and ordering the literature. Bennis et al. (1961), for example, focus on types of planned and unplanned change and the relative power relationships therein. Whilst Van de Ven and Poole (1995) in their influential study focus on the way in which change develops and unfolds. Mintzberg et al. (1998) develop their view of stra tegic change through analysing the different decision-making processes at play, and divide their typology into various schools based on the dominant strategic process. Differently again, Caldwell (2006) has proposed a fourfold framework based on the underlying discourses of the various change theories: Rationalist, Dispersalist, Contextualist or Constructionist. This framework focuses on the underlying philosophical roots and traditions of change theories. Each of these frameworks have their own merits and insights. However they also have shortcoming in terms of their intrinsic biases and in some cases are highly theoretical and lack some of the practicality required for implementation. Table 1 summarises the components of these four influential change theory frameworks and sets out some of the main problems with their theoretical basis.

In examining the literatures on organisational change theory and IS change theory it is apparent that there are recurrent philosophical and managerial themes. Since there has hitherto been no attempt to bring these literatures together, we have used these commonalities to develop an overarching ‘meta-learning’ framework of change, which initially we classify into four main groupings, i.e.;

1. Planning: change as a process of formal planning and analytical direction setting. Planned change incorporates the positivistic biases of many organisational and IS theorists, and represents the attempt to organise and control the environment and process of change (Bennis et al., 1961: Abdel-Hamid and Madnick. 1990: Robey and Newman. 1996).

2. Leadership: change as an outcome of management focus and drive, In this mode of change, the role of top management and leadership within the organisation is prioritised over other factors and is the main subject of investigation (Peters and Waterman, 1991; Kotter, 1996, 1998; Willcocks and Sykes, 2000).

## 4. Planning based change

At one stage in change theory, developing a planned approach to change was the dominant objective of change management (Bennis et al., 1961) and this led to a number of mechanistic responses to change practice. Planning literature focuses on change as rational, essentially positivistic, and as part of a formal management process. This approach has been interpreted in multiple ways. Lewin (1948) is often cited as the forefather of the planned/rationalistic approach. Lewin developed a theory of force field analysis, which held that group behaviour is typically held in equilibrium by equal and opposite forces.

Using this theory he proposed a three stage model of change: unfreeze, movement, refreeze. The three stage model was very influential in developing planned change approaches, and was popularised by many theorists from the 1960s onwards (Steiner, 1969; Cyert and March, 1992). More recently the planning theme can be seen in change tools, techniques and process based ‘best practice’ methods widely used in organisations (Worley and Feyerherm, 2003). The distinctive feature of these more modern change approaches is their emphasis on planned interventions (often driven by consultants). The IS perspective of Planning based change is found in methods such as PRINCE 2 and SSADM (Siau and Tan, 2005). These methodologies offer detailed prescriptions of the steps that practitioners need to take, documents that have to be completed and personnel that should be involved in a strategic IS change programme. Fundamentally, the dominant logic is that change can be controlled through the use of prescribed approach focused on deliverables, formal roles, stage gates and oversight review bodies.

Although popular, and widely adopted, the Planning literature has also been highly criticised (Pettigrew, 1985; Pettigrew et al., 2001). Planning, which is founded on a belief in objective rationality, has been criticised for promoting false certainty about future events. In this respect, and despite efforts to suggest otherwise, planning techniques may be seen as subjective processes, masked by a sense of spurious objectivity. Mintzberg (1989) calls this ‘the soft underbelly of hard data’. Planning is also criticised for promoting inflexibility and lack of responsiveness (Duck, 1998), and of being over-simplistic (Johnson and Scholes, 1999). Researchers in the field of IS also criticise positivistic modes of change by arguing that the plans developed invariably focus on technology deployment and play down the cultural and organisational changes that are required for successful implementation (Willcocks and Sykes, 2000).

## 5. Leadership based change

The Leadership approach puts the role of top management at the centre of strategic change, and focuses strongly on the style, personality and actions of senior leaders (Conner, 1998; Denning 2007). The concept of leadership has proved to be very popular within business literature. Prominent amongst these theorists is John Kotter (1996, 1998) who places great emphasis on the role of senior leadership to create a guiding vision during periods of organisational change. Other significant developments in the Leadership approach to change include Adair’s (2003) focus on Action Centred Leadership, Nadler and Shaw’s (1995) view of leadership as a change ‘competency’, and the investigation of ‘transformational’ leadership (Bass, 1990; Greenwood and Hinings, 1996).

The IS literature also identifies leadership as a key factor in the success or failure of IS programmes (Roepke et al., 2000; Shore, 2005). For some IS theorists this has led to a specific focus on the role of the CIO and their strategic importance to organisations (Polansky et al., 2004). Argyris (1997) made the study of leadership more broadly in an IS context and in opposition to the planned approaches previously described and criticised IS leaders for taking an overly rational approach to implementation programmes. He classified leadership styles into three types: charismatic, task oriented and relationship oriented. Charismatic leaders exude confidence, present their vision of the future clearly, set goals followers can achieve, show that they have confidence in the followers and build unity and identify among their followers. Task oriented leaders focus on planning and control, resource allocation and delegate responsibilities to meet pre-defined milestones. Relationship oriented leaders show compassion, respect and are natural communicators.

As with the Planning tradition, Leadership based change theory has its critics. In some cases the visioning process is seen as detached from reality, ‘blue sky thinking’ or ‘head in the clouds’. Stacey (1992) notes: ‘the advice to form a vision is neither concrete enough to be useful, nor is it possible when the future is unknowable’. Correspondingly, top-down change initiatives (Kotter, 1998) are sometimes criticised as one of the ways that senior management can in fact disconnect itself from the organisation, rather than lead it through times of major change (Beer et al., 1990). Leadership can be seen as galvanising and energising, but to others it can also be seen as pathological, egotistical and de-motivating (Mintzberg et al., 1998). Mintzberg notes that the problem with leadership is that there is just ‘too much’ of it (2004) and raised the concern that business literature has tended to develop a kind of ‘cult’ of leadership by creating heroic examples such as Jack Welch, Lee Iacocca, or Steve Jobs. Rather than being an asset, this idea of the outstanding leader is instead regarded as a risky proposition, where an organisation can be left ‘hanging on the health and whims of one individual’ (Mintzberg, 1989). Mintzberg (2004) argues that rather than having more leadership: organisations need ‘just enough leadership’ (Mintzberg 2004, p. 22).

## 6. Learning based change

Learning proposes a highly inclusive approach to change through individual and collective processes of learning. Theories on how managers learn and adapt have long been the subject of organisational change research (Lindblom, 1959; Cyert and March. 1963). However, interest in learning based approaches to change dramatically surged with the concept of the learn: ing organisation’ proposed by Senge (1990). This view had a number of theoretical antecedents and predecessors. Senge (1990) drew extensively on Argyris and Schon’s concepts of ‘single’ and ‘double-loop’ learning, where deeper insights into organisational motivations and patterns are explored to open up new possibilities for change. A contributory model was also proposed by Weick (2001) who developed the notion of organisational ‘sense making’ as a key factor in developing change. In a similar vein, Nonaka and Takeuchi (1995) promoted change through knowledge creation processes, first initiated at an individual level and then spiralling up to the organisational level. In an associated development, Hamel and Prahalad (1994)

proposed that organisational ‘stretch and leverage’ was required to create successful change. In this formulation, a collective awareness of the organisation’s core competencies is used to set appropriate goals for the organisation as a whole. Mumford (2003) and Lyytinen and Newman (2008) also promulgate highly decentralised modes of change, through learning about and understanding the organisation.

Although learning-based change has enjoyed increasing popularity, particularly in the last two decades, there have also attracted a number of criticisms. Some critics see the Learning School as confusing or sidestepping the role of planning, which can lead to unnecessary or wasted resources. Historically, Lindblom (1959) described incremental management approaches as the science of ‘muddling through’. At their most extreme, some critics regard the vogue for the Learning School as ‘anti-strategic’, ‘disjointed’ and ‘irrational’ (Hayes and Jaikumar, 1988). A related criticism is that learning approaches to change results in a lack of focus or ‘strategic drift’ (Johnson, 1987). Critics also regard Learning based change approaches and the idea of the learning organisation as overly conceptual and lacking in substance (Thompson, 2003). Furthermore, learning is sometimes criticised as encouraging introspection and passivity. For example, in his critique of learning and using the defeat of the Roman army by Hannibal as an example, quips: ‘apparently a suitable strategy for the superior Roman army failed to ‘emerge’ as the battle wore on’. Consequently, the challenge of IS change research is to provide a mechanism to capture managerial approaches to learning which are essentially complex, individual and context specific.

## 7. Culture based change

Culture based change focuses on harnessing power dynamics and social interactions within the organisation. This change approach emphasises the role of influence, agendas, politics, negotiation, and interest groups both within the organisation and also externally (Pettigrew, 1985; Deal and Kennedy, 2000; Buchanan and Badham, 2002). Machiavelli, 1988) is sometimes referred to in this context, and has remained a source of inspiration for many change management theorists focused on the ‘darker’ side of change (Jennings, 1971; Roberts, 1995; Zugbach, 1995). With less self-interested intent, Macmillan and Guth (1985) also developed power based principles into a theory of the political strategies required to successfully ‘seed’ change. Robbins and Finley (1997) compared these ideas with concepts of stakeholder management and psychological profiling to determine ‘change types’ and devise ‘pull’ or ‘push’ change strategies (Braganza and Lambert, 2000). Culture is another factor that features prominently in the ‘excellence’ work of Peters and Waterman (1991), and has been widely debated in the change management literature

Faced with frequent, ‘user-driven’ failure, the social and cultural dimension of change has been of much interest in the IS literature, with analysts drawing heavily on social theory to develop highly context aware modes of change theory (Walsham, 1993; Orlikowski, 1996; Walsham and Sahay, 1999; Balagun, 2006). Balogun and Hope Hailey (2004) also anchor their view of change in culture and use the analogy of a car engine, noting ‘organisations are composed of a number of interconnected parts or subsystems, and are most effective when the major components are in alignment with each other’. These theoretical perspectives have been influential in the IS literature, in part at least due to their strong opposition to the planned approaches previously described.

Aspects of the Culture grouping of change theory are among the most controversial of the literatures described (Jermier et al., 1994). Buchanan and Badham (2002) note that many socio-political change strategies are primarily concerned with self-advancement and not organisational change in the broader context. The concern here is that personal ambition can effectively override normal change processes and result in wasted effort and distorted priorities. Also, whilst few would deny the role of culture in change, critics note that the language of culture change can actually slow the process down, by providing managers with ‘a ready-made vocabulary by which to justify the status quo’ (Mintzberg et al., 1998). There is also the more general problem of translating theory into practice. Whilst interesting insights are generated by the Culture change grouping, there are difficulties in turning these insights into actions to drive forward the strategic change (Buchanan and Badham 2002; Franken, 2009).

## 8. Key ‘espoused’ change management themes

Our review of the change literature demonstrates the wide range of management strategies and approaches available (Mellahi and Sminia, 2009). It is clear that there is no one integrated theory of change. Indeed, the Planned, Leadership, Learning and Culture based views of change are frequently in opposition to each other. However, it is not our objective to offer a unified view of change theory. Instead we propose that each of the four change perspectives has a contribution to make to our understanding of change and is worthy of further investigation in both IS and organisational change contexts. The major contributions and criticisms of the four thematic change groupings from the literature review are summarised in Table 2.

Some conclusions are offered from this review of the change literature. Firstly, although these change theories represent alternative perspectives, organisational and IS change theory actually overlap and interrelate in multiple ways. This is despite the fact that they derive from quite different theoretical and philosophical traditions. Secondly, as noted, given the diversity of approaches it also seems unlikely that there will be a single ‘silver bullet’ model, framework or approach to successful change management. This is also perhaps undesirable given the view that change theory needs to be highly context specific and situational in nature (Pettigrew et al., 2001; Ngosi and Braganza, 2009). Finally, despite commonalities between the IS and organisational change literature, there have been limited attempts to consciously exploit organisational change theory in the IS change management field.

It is apparent, particularly from the theoretical analysis, that additional features may shape organisational change at Technology Infrastructure. The objective is to combine these overarching theoretical features with a substantive case analysis to provide valuable insights for successful IS change management. It is argued that ‘espoused’ theories may be augmented through the ‘theories in use’ from managers themselves particularly with respect to their experiential learning.

## 9. Empirical investigation: transforming IS services in a UK bank

We adopt a case study methodology in examining a major change in a UK bank (the Group). Three main objectives motivated the empirical investigation. Firstly, we sought to align the theoretical groupings described above with change management implementation, and to assess their explanatory potential. Secondly, we also sought to understand the key factors underpinning successful and unsuccessful IS and strategic organisational change from the standpoint of senior practicing managers. Thirdly, we wanted to examine managers’ attitudes and behaviours to change theory, and to investigate whether they explicitly adopted such approaches in practice. These three objectives framed the qualitative data collection and interpretative analysis deployed.

Given that the research required insight into personal reflections on change, it was believed that a broadly applied multisite case study would offer less than a deep investigation of a single, well formed, case. Therefore, the main criterion for the case study site was to find an organisation that had successfully undergone extensive, strategic organisational and IS change, and provided good access to well informed respondents. In addition, the strategic change needed to have run its full cycle and the success of the change needed to be measureable quantitatively as well as qualitatively.

The case research site selected was the IS function within a UK based international banking group. This is one of the largest banks in the world, and in 2011 the Group employed more than 100,000 staff worldwide. The bank was structured into several business clusters organised into Strategic Business Units (SBUs) and Shared Service Units (SSUs). SBUs were businesses tasked with creating profit for the group centre, whilst SSUs were internal businesses focussed on supplying support services to SBUs throughout the Group. The IS shared services unit (‘‘Technology Infrastructure’’, name also withheld), was the largest SSU created within the bank, and was the primary focus of the case study. At its inception, Technology Infrastructure employed approximately 6000 people and provided IS and operations services to businesses across the Group. It managed a total operational budget of approximately £1bn, which included various large sourcing contracts with major IS and operations suppliers.

Technology Infrastructure developed five interlinked strategic priorities built around cost reduction, sourcing, consolidation, commerciality and services. Over a 5 year period, Technology Infrastructure made significant progress towards delivering against these strategic priorities. The changes across Technology Infrastructure were widely regarded as successful, and resulted in a much larger scope of operations, simplified IS infrastructure, improved services, improved customer satisfaction and improved overall Group contribution. The case study investigation focussed on the lessons learnt by the stakeholders directly involved in this change and the factors affecting the ultimate success of the change programme.

## 10. Research methodology and design

The research methodology targeted the gathering of perspectives on the changes at Technology Infrastructure, and more general managerial reflections on the change process. Given the depth of individual discussions required, and the exploratory nature of the investigation, a semi-structured interview approach was adopted (Yin, 1994; Golden-Biddle and Locke, 1997). A rigorous set of evaluation criteria was developed to ensure that appropriate stakeholders were identified for interview selection within the organisation. Interviewees were required to have played a major role in the changes at Technology Infrastructure, and they also had to have wider change management experience. Proposed respondents were formally vetted for the appropriateness of their contribution and for the reliability of their account. They needed to have been part of the transformation throughout its various stages (a 5 year period), and to have been part of other major change initiatives within the bank or externally. The interviewees had to have witnessed and engaged with the changes first hand. In addition to inter nal employees, consultants and contractors were also considered as potential respondents, but only if they had met the foregoing criteria (Hammersley and Atkinson, 1993). These were significant and important qualifying requirements which considerably limited the number of relevant interviewees from the 6000 employees of Technology Infrastructure. Given the strategic nature of many of these discussions, it also tended to lead to the targeting of senior managers within Technology Infrastructure.

Interviews were conducted on site in Technology Infrastructure offices in the North West and London, UK. A pilot phase was undertaken before the launch of the main body of interviews in which a sample of three managers were interviewed. This pilot was used to refine the semi-structured interview schedule (in particular the probes), and to further identify the most appropriate interviewees. In total twenty interviews were then conducted which formed a cross section of change managers across Technology Infrastructure. Interviewees predominantly fell into three main groupings:

\- Senior executives: Technology Infrastructure executive board members, including the Group CIO (six interviews). There had been some turnover in executives at this level, but much of the senior leadership remained intact.

\- Programme managers and other prominent change managers: managers directly involved in the changes at Technology Infrastructure, and with proven expertise of managing strategic change in other contexts (eight interviews). These managers were specifically involved in leading major parts of the change.

\- Consultants: non-group employees with extensive experience of strategic change both within Technology Infrastructure and other large organisations. All consultants had worked on assignment at the Group throughout the major change period (six interviews).

As noted, all 20 interviewees were selected for their breadth of strategic change management experience, and this was well represented. The final group of selected interviewees had significant change management experience encompassing such areas as mergers and acquisitions, sourcing, organisational design, restructuring, process re-engineering, enterprisewide technology integration, strategic cost reduction initiatives, branding changes, infrastructure renewal, bankruptcy, and creation and disposal of new products, services, and businesses. Interviewees also came from a diverse background, many having experience from a wide variety of large organisations and multiple industries, with some managing change at a global level. Some interviewees had up to 25 years service within the Group, and had experience of running many strategic change projects across the organisation.

Data collection was carried out at the research site for a 4 month period during 2009. One of the researchers was an employee of the Group at the time of data collection (as a programme manager), and was familiar with the structure of the company and the context of the changes that had occurred. Interviews were scheduled to last 2 h. The semi-structured interview format was divided into three sections.

1. Introduction, research objectives and confidentiality: the objectives of the research were explained to interviewees. Permission to tape the interviews was also requested at this stage.

2. Personal strategic change experiences: in this section interviewees were invited to describe their own experiences of strategic change within Technology Infrastructure and more generally. This included discussion on what had worked well and what could have been improved upon. We also invited comment on whether IS change differed materially from organisational change in their experience.

3. Exploration of methods and frameworks: having focused on personal experiences, interview questions then moved to discussion on the use of strategic change management methods used by the interviewee. This included probes on the use of change theories, models and frameworks

In addition, data from the interviews was triangulated with other sources including internal reports, steering committee meeting minutes and change programme information which were accumulated throughout the research period. This was collated into an information archive of tapes and documentation. The taped interviews were transcribed in full, and data collated was then analysed thematically (Miles and Huberman, 1994). A first pass of interviewee data and documentation was coded into key words and topics. These themes were then analysed and interpreted against the change theory groupings described in the literature review and conclusions were drawn from the findings.

## 11. Case analysis findings

During the course of the data collection process, it became apparent that respondents were highly engaged in the research area, and were keen to share their change experiences and views. Many of the interviews overran in terms of time, and many of the respondents were interested in the outcomes of the research. All interviewees were of the view that the management of strategic change could be improved both within the research site and in other organisations. Respondents noted the topic was interesting and one that people return to frequently. It was clear that managers always seemed to be dissatisfied with what they read and what they actually experienced.

The relative success of failure or strategic change was discussed, and respondents tended to agree with the view that ‘most change programmes are neither complete successes nor abject failures’ (Binney and Williams, 1997). This tended to reinforce the view that strategic change was an exceptionally hard and complex activity to undertake. Only one interviewee could recount an example of a change program that they regarded as being entirely unsuccessful, with most indicating that change was always a learning experience. A consultant paid tribute to the long running efforts to improve change management in the Group and Technology Infrastructure noting that people were committed to change and opportunities to im prove processes.

However, there was a general view that there was a reluctance to badge any change as a failure at Technology Infrastructure and in other organisations, and that this was an industry-wide issue. The view was expressed that exposing perceived failure is counterproductive and the experience gained should be exploited. Interviewees generally regarded failed change as an opportunity to create learning, but this was not always realised. This related to a general theme from interviewees that, although taking pride in the success of the changes at Technology Infrastructure, interviewees also felt that there was still much room for improvement. Feedback from the interviewees fitted well into the change theory groupings previously identified.

## 12. Planning based change

Planning approaches to change were discussed with interviewees. In particular the question of ‘why’ change at all? The role of planning was determined as an important factor in this respect. Various initiatives were outlined including the setting up of ‘governance gates’ to ensure appropriate and justified expenditure. However, the role of planning was also regarded as part of change, but not the change itself, as noted by an Executive.

Other dimensions of planning described included the funding of change, objective setting and milestone tracking. Interviewees also commented on strategic planning processes within the Group, and generally felt that this was an area for improvement. Specifically, the budget process was high on each manager agenda. Strategic planning was generally regarded as part of budget allocation processes rather than as something distinctive about the delivery of change itself, and was a typically viewed as a bureaucratic mechanism and a ‘necessary evil’.

## 13. Leadership based change

The topic of leadership was extensively discussed. All interviewees emphasised the role of leadership during times of change. Managers commented about the importance of leadership in Technology Infrastructure and other organisations. Lack of leadership was frequently cited as the reason for failed change programmes. However this support was qualified with some suspicion of the kinds of leadership models proposed by key authors such as Adair (2003) and Kotter (1998), i.e., ‘. . .. if you have a visionary leader that is so visionary that nobody else understands then you have a problem’. Interviewees associated strong leadership with the set-up phase of the strategic change agenda at Technology Infrastructure, and then with the ongoing direction and sponsorship of the change. This involved giving people ideas on what to expect both of themselves and the process. Respondents also focused on the practical dimension of leadership. Managers and consultants in particular emphasised the need for leaders to be consistent and to lead by example.

These groups also emphasised the need for leadership to be visible. All interviewees gave views on what they regarded as exemplary or poor leadership behaviour. Leaders should give direction, take risks and purvey confidence. Many interviewees used examples of influential leaders that they had witnessed in the past as critical within strategic change. The notion of a ‘personal crusade’ was outlined where a leader had obvious charm and charisma. Despite the success of change in Technology Infrastructure and the Group, interviewees also had many suggestions on areas for improvement in terms of leadership within the Group. There were many comments on the contrast between change leadership in the Group and in Technology Infrastructure. Leaders in the organisation were regarded as too inaccessible through hierarchical and status. It was noted that this makes it very hard to build relationships, ‘because it is relationships that create change’.

## 14. Learning based change

All respondents commented on change as a key opportunity to learn. However, many interviewees were uncomfortable with the term ‘learning’ and the ‘learning organisation’. Some time was spent in almost all interviews discussing different interpretations of what learning actually meant in the context of change. However, even though most interviewees expressed some dissatisfaction with the ‘learning’ terminology, all interviewees emphasised the importance of learning in the process of strategic change. It was clearly important that people have an opportunity to constantly learning about issues and the organisation should recognise and take account of these opportunities.

Although interviewees were often sceptical about the theoretical side of organisational learning, they all tended to regard learning as a key aspect of the change. In particular managers and executives were interested in developing ways of aggregating collective learning. In this respect learning was quite strongly associated with knowledge management processes in that managers did not general discuss their experiences. This was claimed to result in repeating frequently made mistakes and regarded the potential for learning as a lost opportunity within the Group unless expertise is harnessed. In many interviews there was also discussion about the pace of learning in the Group and other organisations. Learning was regarded as an important aspect of the strategic change process, but not a particular area of strength in the Group, or something that had been focused on during the changes at Technology Infrastructure.

## 15. Culture based change

The characteristics of Culture based approaches (stakeholder management, organisational politics, and organisational paradigms) were discussed with interviewees. This area of change prompted the most vigorous debate amongst interviewees. Recognising the culture of the organisation and attempting to harness it within the change was universally regarded as important. This again involved discussing the ‘ground’ for change which would enable a fertilisation of the processes toward a sustainable outcome.

There was a recurrent theme around how culture can work for or against the change, and this manifested itself through various forms of support or resistance. Another senior manager commented that she assessed culture and attitudes through listening to the implicit dialogue between different parties within the organisation. It was felt important to listen to the ‘conversations’ within the Group and the apparent tensions between business and IS objectives.

Although acknowledging its importance, many interviewees were uncomfortable with aspects of Culture based change and were unsure about its application within the Group. Much discussion focused on the culture of the organisation itself and stakeholder management systems, which were widely used in the organisation. The complexity and multiplicity of stakeholder challenges of strategic change was emphasised. The view was expressed that, ‘too many people have the vote and that stakeholder management is actually an art form. A key issue described by many interviewees was the amount of ‘power brokers’ to keep ‘on-side’ and updated. Many interviewees expressed dislike of the stakeholder approaches and tools prevalent in the Group and other organisations.

## 16. Case analysis emergent key themes

Following an interpretive analysis of the interview data it is useful to identify the key themes arising from the empirica investigation – summarised thematically in Table 3.

In terms of IS change as a specific category of organisational change, it was clear that interviewees did not recognise any material difference. A common view recalls the old adage about no technology problems but only people problems. For example, in describing one of the larger IS change programs at Technology Infrastructure that had been difficult to deliver. There was a need for better communication and input from users of the systems. Most importantly there was little support for the idea that IS change should be handled any differently to any other type of organisational change within Technology Infrastructure or the Group.

The most persuasive aspect of the responses is the significant and repetitive attempts for critical thinking, self-reflection and learning in the process of change management challenges. These are consistently noted throughout all of the four thematic groupings and clearly represented in the perceived contributions and concerns identified (Table 3). It is apparent that attention should be given to approaches and support for ‘informed’ managerial cognitions in this respect which may enable more successful and purposeful IS change management strategies. This view is to argue for a perspective on human behaviour which is essentially existentialist which forms a ‘meta-learning’ feature of goal orientated change management practice.

## 17. Meta-learning framework

Adapting the concept of meta-learning previously described (Biggs, 1985; Davenport and Harris, 2007) and integrating our findings, we offer a framework derived from the theoretical and empirical investigation. Fig. 1 adopts three interrelated sections, which combine all the main topics described in this paper.

The notion of experiential theorising is the culmination of, and is central to, the argument within our research as it is evident through the comments and behaviours of the managers involved. Essentially, the frequently identified process (Kolb, 1984) of ‘active experimentation’, ‘concrete experience’, ‘reflective observations’ and ‘abstract conceptualisation’ were all enacted in some form during the empirical investigation within Technological Infrastructure.

In this way managers consistently applied a practical ‘sense making’ approach to enable, what was believed to be, more appropriate decision making within the context of strategic change. Consequently, ideas and their implementation were based upon both personal and contextual experiences. Our research demonstrates, however, the added dimension of their consideration and formative adoption of ‘change theories’ in the process. This is therefore an important aspect of applied strategic IS conceptualisations as managers represented their ‘informed’ view of change. The respondents in our study were, as noted, very senior and were able and willing to be actively communicate their ‘experiences’ through adaptation and reflectivity (Waugh and Fredrickson, 2006). It is highly probable that middle/junior managers would not be afforded these opportunities and thus play a minimal role in change management decisions. It is also clear that the dominance of executives in this respect is a consequence of their abilities, knowledge and pro-activity for experimental theorising.

We contend, therefore that it is critical that IS change is developed through both theoretical constructs and experiential learning, and not exclusively one or the other. Fig. 2 illustrates these applied concepts, derived from the literature and empirical theorising offered by the case study interviewees. The process represents a useful practitioner guide to formulating and implementing the various change management phases. Kolb (1984) ‘theory of experiential learning’ is presented and reformulated which attempts to capture collective cognition of the change managers involved. The four phase process is proposed to enable an ‘informed’ view of the previously presented thematic grouping (planning, leadership, learning, culture).

Fig. 2 also highlights the notion of existentialism portrayed through a focus upon enabled action (enactment) rather than an ‘abstract set of theoretical truths’ (Flynn, 2006). Managers within Technology Infrastructure frequently noted their own ‘theories in use’ (based upon experiences) as opposed to ‘espoused theories’ within existing academic viewpoints. Fig. 2, therefore, represents in this respect an opportunity for managers to develop and, most importantly, to apply collective critical thinking and effective decision approaches to IS change management issues. Following Karjalainen and Siponen (2011) the process should, involve learners’ concrete experiences, engage in reflective observation, support formulation of abstract concepts, and, enable active experimentation. These approaches extend far beyond existing norms, routines and challenges often associated with prescriptive organisational transformation (espoused theories) and suggest a focus towards (Phase 4) formulating a culture derived through, as noted, experimentation (Kirkpatrick, 2006). Here, managers agree to actively support the transfer of knowledge, experience and behaviours to enable successful IS change.

Furthermore, using the four change groupings to frame the concepts uncovered in the empirical investigation, an implementation approach emerges, which may be enacted through each experiential learning phase, specific to the changes within Technology Infrastructure.

\- Formal planning was regarded as a base requirement for the changes at Technology Infrastructure. Individual tasks and tactics turned into group level processes and methods, and these were supported by appropriate engagement and supporting organisational structures. Where possible plans were made explicit, they were placed within an overall strategic vision; this helped to set deadlines and synthesise the change (enact Phase 1).

Similarly leadership operated at individual, team and corporate levels. In this case personal leadership drive was matched by empowerment at a team level and top level sponsorship at a corporate level. Leadership sought to be context aware and was not simply top-down driven. Creativity and reward for innovation at lower levels in the organisation were encouraged. Leadership was not always in balance with the change, and issues with accessibility to senior management were noted (enact Phase 2).

\- From a learning perspective, individual insights were shared at a group level to provide shared context and understanding of the change. This enabled the organisation to adapt its approach to changing situations and events. Management provided opportunities for learning through collective events, although it was recognised that improved knowledge transfe mechanisms would have made the change more successful (enact Phase 3).

\- In addition culture was harnessed to create momentum. Management used various methods to influence people at a team level and build coalitions committed to the change. There were efforts to make the governance of the change inclusive, and there were conscious attempts to develop a sense of professionalism about the change through regular bulletins focused on progress, metrics and validation of positive change behaviour. Culture was also recognised as problematic at times, and not always supportive of the change (enact Phase 4).

It is evident that the managers within Technological Infrastructure adopted a fervent and applied view of ‘learning’ where they attempted to translate change management decision making into action. As senior executives they clearly had the qualities and motivation to facilitate initiatives which were self-fulfilling, self motivating and self-evaluating. The perspective developed within our research addresses and augments these issues where the managers involved formulated new ways of informed thinking.

The importance of role experience is a critical factor which should be nurtured and encouraged within change management approaches. The notion of ‘experiential learning’ is not frequently emphasised and is evidently critical for gaining new knowledge. An informed strategy in this respect involves the participation of the managers individually, within teams and across the organisation as a whole. Success in strategic IS change projects will depend upon managers identifying imaginative and absorbing issues requiring complex intuitive learning experiences.

## 18. Discussion

The foregoing theoretical and empirical investigation demonstrates a wide diversity of perspectives on change theory and management methods. As noted, change theory has a long history of attempting to understand change management processes, and correspondingly the empirical research shows a multiplicity of working theories and heuristics used by practicing managers. The case analysis demonstrated the value that managers associate with taking theory, from whatever source, and putting it into action (enactment). This intersection between theory and practice is of primary concern of our research which has been extensively developed. For example, on the question of whether managers used strategic change models and frameworks, one consultant responded, ‘I read the books, but a lot of it seems pretty obvious to me’.

It is clear that, whilst most respondents were familiar with academic research, they did not regard their own experiences as any less valid in terms of theory application. Experiential theorising, or developing working ‘‘theories in use’’ (Argyris and Schon, 1978; Huff, 2008) was clearly important to managers at Technology Infrastructure and this manifested itself in a variety of behaviours. This is an important and novel insight into managerial action as the evidence is unequivocal that respondents consider their ‘own’ theories of change as highly applicable. Consequently, as noted, we describe a process for practitioner efforts (Fig. 2) in this respect where manages may be able to engage and exploit approaches to the management of IS change more successfully.

In some cases respondents were reaching for metaphors to propose ways of approaching change management problems (deductive theorising), generalising from their experiences (inductive theorising), or explaining how they revised their thinking having initially made inappropriate judgements (abductive theorising). It is evident from our study that practitioners and theorists alike need to find their own ways of leveraging expertise, and methods of adapting change theory for specific purposes.

We further argue that our study provides novel insights into the field of IS change. It has shown that practitioners do not simply adopt academic ‘espoused theories’. Instead, they apply their prior, personal experiences and formulate their own concepts of change (theories in use). Successful change management strategies demonstrate persuasive context awareness, which should be used to identify and deepen existing academic change management constructs. In this respect it is useful to highlight four significant areas for further research.

Research Direction 1: Under leadership it is noted that the respondents raised concerns about charismatic and visionary leaders (and equally insightful is the perceived gap between the visionary leader and colleagues). This view is consistent with Mintzberg’s work on leadership (Mintzberg, 1989, 2004), but has not been thoroughly considered in IS change contexts. Similarly, we note the concern of managing stakeholders in the section on culture. In many cases, IS projects have to deal with a multitude of views, and so does the presence of multiple stakeholders mean that we need stronger leadership to bring them together?

Research Direction 2: The field of IS change would also benefit significantly from in-depth qualitative studies that examine the effects of issues such as politics and employee engagement to change. IS change projects require organisations to relinquish old knowledge and to absorb new working practices. These factors may raise managers’ levels of concerns and anxieties. Further studies could pose questions such as; why does this fear exist, what does this tell us regarding the composition of change management teams and what can we infer about the selection of managers for IS change?

Research Direction 3: Additional more qualitative constructs could usefully be determined for example from attention to ‘design science’ (Hevner, 2007) or ‘institutional theory’ (Campbell, 2010). Here, managerial objectives are truly goal orientated to produce ‘theoretically informed guidance’ which set a fundamental direction for, and characteristics of, IS change.

Research Direction 4: Most critically, following Karjalainen and Siponen (2011), research directions could usefully develop insights from experiential learning processes relating to ‘critical-level principles’ and ‘evidence-based intuition’ captured through methodologies which may validate successful IS change management approaches. How can we operationalise ‘experiential learning’ to exploit natural managerial behaviours when confronted with complex change management issues? The opportunity should also be taken to apply existing ‘espoused’ theoretical constructs to augment existentialist perspectives at a more practical level. Essentially, there is an important need to understand how learning is adopted and implemented throughout an organisation noting contextual and cultural features.

We also acknowledge certain limitations from our research noting that whether successful or unsuccessful, undertaking strategic change is in itself a rich learning experience. The organisation is a site of continuous change, and in order to be effective, a context-specific understanding of how change impacts upon the organisation is needed. We suggest that the added value of such a change strategy is to recognise the issues relating to the practice of IS change through aspects of Planning, Leadership, Learning and Culture. These are our attempts at ‘analysis’, ‘explanation’, ‘prediction’, etc. Consequently, our application of ‘meta-learning’, whilst recognising fervent managerial intuition and action, may not fully capture the extent of individual maturity and experience in the process.

Change managers have a portfolio of lessons, stories and a diverse range of experiences to draw from, and this is part of the foundation of good ‘informed’ change management. Furthermore, since there is unlikely to be a single successful approach, to be authentic and effective, managers should be encouraged to develop their own ‘theories in use’ of change and methods. Over time these may evolve into a personalised and organisation specific existentialist approach. To capture this context sensitivity is challenging and recognises that the organisation site is a dynamic entity and only the most basic forms of organisational change can be reduced to a formula or codified into a prescriptive process (Fig. 2). Consequently, given these limitations, our research has at least attempted a novel approach to investigate the cross section between organisational and IS change through empirical insights and offered an extended overall ‘meta-learning’ framework to consider and co-ordinate the complexity and multiplicity of the behavioural factors involved.

This leads to the main contributions emerging from our research. Firstly, there are the change models and theories (espoused) offered by the literature, and in common with our survey these are divided into four thematic approaches:

planning, leadership, learning and culture. Secondly, there are also experiential theories operating in the organisation identified through the case analysis. These are actually the cognitive (thought) processes of the organisation’s most successful change practitioners. The challenge is to bring this expert ‘know-how’ to the fore where there are ways of organising based on the context within the organisation. It is argued that these dimensions are combined together to provide an ‘informed’ approach to IS change. This would be one in which there is enough contextual awareness to recognise the key success factors to emphasise, and understanding of, the inherent risks to avoid within that specific organisation setting. Consequently, our ‘meta-learning’ framework is offered as a way of supporting managers in their attempts to integrate their own change management experiences within current ‘espoused’ generic change theory. This level of theoretical consideration (meta learning) attempts to capture the intuitive nature of how respondents adopted an ‘informed’ IS change management strategy.

## 19. Conclusion

In this research we have explored the literature on organisational change management and IS change and found opportunities to bring these elements together. Furthermore, the input of the experiential theorising of change practitioners in the field reinforces the need to seek agile and context-rich approaches to change management in both IS and organisational environments. This supports the view that IS change, as a subset of organisational change, is a dynamic, multi-dimensional and complex phenomenon that does not lend itself to theoretical reductionism. It is therefore apparent that research efforts to formulate a generic theory of change may be inappropriate. A more insightful approach would be to focus on pursuing selfreflexive, context specific IS change which is informed through a ‘meta-learning’ perspective.
