## A B S T R A C T

Despite several calls for considering industry in the IS value research, few studies investigated the relationship between bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of IT and industries. This study extends Chae et al. (2014) by investigating how industry in<sup>fl</sup>uences the relationship between IT capability and business performance. Unexpectedly, it <sup>fi</sup>nds that the control group in the industries in which the "transform" IT strategic role dominates showed superior performance than the IT leader. Also, the IT leaders in the "automate" IT strategic industry did not exhibit better business performance than the control <sup>fi</sup>rms. We discuss the implications of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 1. Introduction

It is estimated that <sup>fi</sup>rms spend globally over \$3.8 trillion in IT in 2015 and the IT investment is expected to grow continuously over the next few years [1]. With the enormous and increasing IT spending, it is crucial to understand and manage if and how IT investment is translated into improved <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Numerous studies have looked into this important and challenging question about the value of IT investment [2–12]. Among these studies, Chae et al. [2] calls for a particular attention. They updated highly cited Bharadwaj’s [13] study with latest data from the 2000s to see if the positive connection between IT capability and business performance measured by pro<sup>fi</sup>t and cost ratios still held true. They compared 296 pairs of IT leaders and carefully matched control <sup>fi</sup>rms from 2001 to 2007 to see if the two groups di<sup>f</sup>er in their performance. Contrary to the earlier studies, they found no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bearing of IT capability on business performance.

As to the causes and reasons why Chae et al. [2] derived at the contradicting result, we may speculate two possible avenues to explain. First, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings may re<sup>fl</sup>ect the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes that took place in IT and business during the time period that spans between the studies. For example, the advent and prevalence of the Internet and continuous and dramatic reduction in the cost of IT resources have made IT more standardized and easily accessible. As a result, it has become more di<sup>fi</sup>cult for IT leaders to achieve and sustain superior performance through superior IT capability. The diminishing return of IT, known as the productivity paradox [14], may also have contributed to the disappearance of the connection between IT capability and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. The second line of reasoning as to why the new study did not detect the connection between IT capability and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance is inherent to the research itself. The design and method of the research somehow may have obscured the underlying presence of the connection. Chae et al. [2] acknowledged some of such factors that may have diluted the outcome, including the size of a <sup>fi</sup>rm and the industry in which it operates. In fact, Chae et al. [2] and previous studies never discriminate industry in constructing and analyzing the sample, while it is widely noted that IT plays di<sup>f</sup>erent roles for di<sup>f</sup>erent industries [15,9,10]. Thus, it is the focus of this study to see if and how industry a<sup>f</sup>ects the connection between IT capability and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance.

Industry in<sup>fl</sup>uences not only a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s research and development (R& D), advertising, but also its IT budget, resources, and capabilities [16–18], and it is critical to align a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT resources and capability with the competitive environment that surrounds the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Indeed, the characteristics of the industry in which a <sup>fi</sup>rm competes often dictate the type of IT required, the way it is applied, and the extent of value generated and retained [19]. A <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT investment on resources and capability pays o<sup>f</sup> when it is properly aligned with the speci<sup>fi</sup>c environment in which the <sup>fi</sup>rm operates and competes [20,21]. However, it is unclear how and why the strategic <sup>fi</sup>t between IT capability and industry varies and no concrete theoretical framework exists to explain such di<sup>f</sup>erence [5,9,10,22]. Therefore, we decided to explore the presence and the role of industry in the relationship between IT capability and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance.

IS researchers have used three di<sup>f</sup>erent approaches to understand the e<sup>f</sup>ect of industry on IT: (1) Measuring it using dynamism, muni<sup>fi</sup>- cence, and complexity of industry, (2) understanding it from the perspective of IT intensity of industry, and (3) applying a construct called the strategic role of IT in industry. Table 1 summarizes the three approaches with the list of studies conducted following each approach and the advantages and disadvantages of each approach.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst approach, the most popular and drawn from the strategy literature [23], is to investigate the industry environment in terms of dynamism (i.e., turbulence, volatility, or demand uncertainty), muni-<sup>fi</sup>cence (i.e., fast or slow growing), and complexity (i.e., industry concentration, the number of competitors) [10,25,36,37,21,38,12,40]. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, researchers examined how these three industry characteristics in<sup>fl</sup>uence IT capability [21], e<sup>fi</sup>ciency [39], IT outsourcing decisions [36], vertical integration [37], the relationship be tween IT assets and operational e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and innovation [40], the productivity impact of IT capital [25], the volatility of <sup>fi</sup>rm perfor mance [24], and the link between agility and performance [38].

The second method is to use the intensity of information [33] and the intensity of IT [26,41] of an industry. It is reasoned that when products and services sold in an industry are information intensive, the industry is more likely to invest in IT and become an IT-intensive in dustry. Researchers have studied how the level of IT intensity in an industry in<sup>fl</sup>uences productivity [26,41], IT investment and its lag ef fect [42], IT outsourcing [43], and IT spillovers [44].

While these two approaches are useful to show general aspects of the business environment in which an industry operates, they have a couple of shortcomings [40]. First, the two methods fail to recognize the fact that the industry environment is not static and exogenous but <sup>fl</sup>uid and endogenous. That is, they assume that the industry environment is <sup>fi</sup>xed and stable and they do not explain why an industry be comes more volatile (dynamic), fast-or-slow growing (muni<sup>fi</sup>cent), complex with more competitors, or IT intensive. In fact, changes in an industry are frequently caused and in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by information and communication technologies including the Internet, mobile computing, o<sup>f</sup>shoring outsourcing, cloud computing, big data, and enterprise ap plications such as ERP, CRM, and SCM. Many industries, especially non manufacturing industries such as trucking, food retailing, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial services, have undergone fundamental change as a result of adopting advanced information technologies (IT) [27].

Especially, the Internet has dropped entry barriers in many in dustries and turned a stable and slow-growing industry into a dynamic and fast-growing one. Also, a concentrated industry with relatively few participants becomes complex with newer contestants from other in dustries or other markets. For instance, prior to the Internet, the music and the book industries were slow growing with stable sales, low IT intensity, and little competitive pressure because a small number of major music studios and bookstore chains dominated these industries. However, the advent of the Internet dramatically changed the landscape of the music and the book industries. New players like Amazon.com and iTune have disrupted the distribution channels, suppliers, buyers, and competition itself [34], making these industries to abruptly become more volatile, fast growing, complex, and IT dependent.

In addition, the <sup>fi</sup>rst two approaches do not take di<sup>f</sup>erent industries and their distinctive aspects into consideration [40], providing little help for managers who make IT investment decisions based on the in dustry they belong to. The degree of importance of IT and the extent to which IT brings structural changes vary from industry to industry, ultimately in<sup>fl</sup>uencing IT capability and IT investment decision [45–48]. Managers make decisions in particular contexts, considering the nature of the interdependence between <sup>fi</sup>rms and industries [49,50]. The various industry characteristics impact the social norms about what IT resources to invest in as well as the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of IT investment. Thus, managers’ decisions on IT re<sup>fl</sup>ect the social and cognitive in<sup>fl</sup>uences of the industry setting in which their <sup>fi</sup>rms are located [15].

Less prescriptive to managers [51], the previous two methods assume that IT capability is always important and recommend <sup>fi</sup>rms diligently build it, but that may not be the case in certain circumstances. Particularly, as the cost of IT tumbles and fast changes in IT pose a risk of having obsolete technologies, it becomes important to pick whether <sup>fi</sup>rms should be proactive or reactive in IT investments [22,52]. If IT is imperative for the industry, <sup>fi</sup>rms in the industry should aggressively cultivate their IT capability to successfully compete in the market. In the opposite case, however, they should defer IT investments until costs drop and latest technologies and systems become more stable. Thus, this calls for a better understanding of the environments where superior IT capability bestows most bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

The third method utilizes a construct called the strategic role of IT in industry [53,54]. It categorizes the role of IT for an industry as “Automate,” “Informate up or down,” or “Transform.” These categories are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as follows [55]:

• Automate: Replace human labor by automating business processes.

• Informate Up/Down: Provide data/information to empower management and employees.

Transform: Fundamentally change business and industry processes and relationships.

The construct of the strategic role of IT for an industry has been used to understand the impact of IT on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s business activities and competitive strategies [56], the stock price reaction to announcements of newly created CIO positions [55], and of major IT investments [57].

However, there is no study that examines how this construct in-<sup>fl</sup>uences the relationship between IT capability and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance despite the prevailing notion that the importance of IT capability varies from industry to industry. For instance, in certain industries like the media, IT plays a critical role and even induces substantial structural changes in the industry. In such industry where IT can transform the industry itself, having superior IT capability is crucial for the <sup>fi</sup>rms operating in the industry. Thus, IT capability is a strategic asset and IT investment is a strategic decision. On the other hand, in other industries such as consumer retail or utilities, the role of IT is rather supplementary to either provide information to management or automate business operations. Thus, having superior IT capability may not be as important as other crucial resources. Applying the construct of the strategic role of IT in industry is valuable to understand not only how IT in<sup>fl</sup>uences the industry environment but also how IT capability is used di<sup>f</sup>erently across industries.

We adopted the aforementioned construct of the strategic role of IT in industry to further explore the relationship between IT capability and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. We believe that it can provide insights into the connection between unique industry environments and IT capability. In addition, the level of investment in IT is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant decision [58] as managers need to balance between the <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk of investing and the competitive risk of not investing during challenging times [59] and determine an appropriate IT asset portfolio. Findings from this study can help IT managers make intelligent investment decisions on IT depending on the business environment of the industry in which the <sup>fi</sup>rm operates. In the remainder of this paper, we present the review of relevant literature, set forth our hypotheses, and report the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the study. In the <sup>fi</sup>nal section, we discuss the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and the implication and limitations of the study.

## 2. Strategic role of IT in industry construct, and hypotheses

## 2.1. The strategic role of IT in industry

It is known that the extent to which IT impacts business and induces structural changes varies from industry to industry [25]. Industries di<sup>f</sup>er in the way they use IT and expected return from the use of IT [60]. There is considerable heterogeneity in the payo<sup>f</sup>s across <sup>fi</sup>rms and industries. IS researchers have used the strategic role of IT in industry construct to capture the characteristics and the context of industry in studying IT practices of <sup>fi</sup>rms [57,54]. Schein [53] provided the foundation for the strategic role of IT research by identifying three major categories of strategic IT roles: (1) Automate: replace human labor by automating business processes, (2) Informate up/down: provide data/information to improve management and employees, and (3) Transform: fundamentally change traditional ways of doing business by rede<sup>fi</sup>ning business processes and relationships.

Although this classi<sup>fi</sup>cation was originally created to understand the strategic role of IT applications at individual <sup>fi</sup>rms, it was later expanded to examine the strategic role of IT at the industry level [55]. Transform industries refer to those industries whose business processes are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly changed by IT and include such industries as media, banking, airlines, and advertising. Informate up/down industries are those whose business processes can be improved by information from IT and include many traditional manufacturing businesses. Automate in dustries refer to the industries in which the main role of IT is to automate business processes, such as railroad and utility industries. While IT-induced structural changes occur most frequently in the Transform industry, it is noted that they can also occur in the Informate and the Automate industries [57].

Table 2 lists industries as classi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Chatterjee et al. [55] for the strategic role of IT during the periods of 1987–1994 and 1995–1998. From 1987 to 1998, many industries moved from Automate to Informate Up/Down, from Informate Up/Down to Transform. During the period of 1995–1998, the majority of industries belong to Informate Up/Down. With the developments and events that took place in IT after these periods such as the Internet, the dotcom bubble, and mobile and cloud computing, it is necessary to update this classi<sup>fi</sup>cation to re<sup>fl</sup>ect these new developments in IT before we apply the construct to a new study.

## 2.2. IT capability and firm performance in industries where the strategic role of IT is transform (“Transform” industry)

Information technology capability is an organization’s ability to generate business value using its IT assets and know-how. IT capability of a <sup>fi</sup>rm comprises IT infrastructure, human IT resources such as technical and managerial IT skills, and IT-enabled intangibles including knowledge assets, customer orientation, and synergy [13,2]. Companies can improve their business performance by leveraging their IT capability to increase revenues, reduce costs, or both.

The role of IT in some industries is to transform traditional ways of doing business by rede<sup>fi</sup>ning business processes and relationships. Such industries undergo dramatic changes due to new developments in IT including the Internet, enterprise applications, and mobile computing. Especially, IT changes the nature of the product itself [61]. In some cases, information becomes an important part of the good or service. In other cases, the product itself becomes digital (e.g., music CDs, digital videos, online journals). Such changes to the nature of the product can lead to changes in the basis of competition among <sup>fi</sup>rms and changes in industry structure [62].

Moreover, the Internet accelerates industry transformation as it lowers entry barriers by reducing the size of capital required for IT investments [34]. The lowered entry barrier in turn intensi<sup>fi</sup>es competition as it increases not only the number of new players in the market but also the rivalry among <sup>fi</sup>rms because the Internet improves information and price transparency via search engines, empowered customers, along with negligible reproduction cost of digital products [34]. The <sup>fi</sup>rms in the media industry, for instance, are susceptible to new threats and opportunities created by the Internet and mobile computing. With highly information-intensive products and services, movie or music producers are experiencing <sup>fi</sup>erce competition by new entrants like YouTube, Net<sup>fl</sup>ix, Amazon, Facebook, or computer manufacturing companies like Apple. Savvy customers empowered by the Internet, search engines, and social-networking tools also a<sup>f</sup>ect those producers.

Consequently, in the Transform industry, managers face an arduous challenge to make strategic decisions about IT capability. IT capability decisions often accompany various risks resulting from incorrect identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and deployment of resources and capabilities, unreliable bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from using it, irrecoverable deployment costs, and di<sup>fi</sup>cult implementation processes. Their struggle is intensi<sup>fi</sup>ed by ferocious and rapidly changing business environment. A key issue thus is how a <sup>fi</sup>rm should align its IT capability in the Transform industry and understand when and how to use IT capability to attain competitive advantages. However, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the few studies that have examined the role of industry environment in the relationship between IT and performance have been inconclusive [52]. There are three di<sup>f</sup>erent angles about how IT capability in<sup>fl</sup>uences <sup>fi</sup>rm performance in the Transform industry: positive, neutral, and negative.

First, some people contend that a <sup>fi</sup>rm with superior IT capability can have better performance in the Transform industry because a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT capability enables swift response to changing market conditions [63]. That is, in the Transform industry, a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s response speed is vital, and to meet the demands and pressure of the rapidly evolving environments, a <sup>fi</sup>rm needs information processing ability. Having these “sense and respond” capabilities will help the <sup>fi</sup>rm enhance its agility [64], leading to the surge in the number of new products, innovations, or patents [30,40]. Those new products result in positive business performance.

On the other hand, in the Transform industry, since most <sup>fi</sup>rms are well aware of the importance of rapidly developing and simultaneously exercising IT capability, they aggressively chase IT innovations and are more likely to imitate others or follow fads, neutralizing the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of having superior IT capability. That is, new strategies and business models will di<sup>f</sup>use and spread quickly and dissipate any advantage that accrues to IT leader <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior IT capability. IT leaders require experience and time to learn and internalize new IT, a foundation for path dependency and barriers to the erosion of competitive advantages [31]. However, IT leaders experiencing the same time press as their competitors are subjected to diminishing returns. Particularly, contemporary organizations in the 2000s rely on highly standardized and homogeneous information systems because of the rapid adoption of ERP and web technologies [34]. Such standardization enables a <sup>fi</sup>rm to counter and even outdo its competitor’s IT capability [2].

Last, some believe that superior IT capability can negatively impact a <sup>fi</sup>rm performance because of the rigidity of legacy systems and the duality of technology. That is, IT can enable business agility, but it can also limit a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s options to change as the enabling technology may become obsolete and in<sup>fl</sup>exible over time, thereby causing lock-in rigidity [65,66]. Particularly, in<sup>fl</sup>exible legacy IT systems or tightly in tertwined enterprise systems can result in rigid IT architectures and constrain agility in the face of unanticipated fast changes [67]. With those in<sup>fl</sup>exible legacy systems, IT leaders in the Transform industry may experience di<sup>fi</sup>culty in adjusting in the volatile business environments [68]. In addition, according to the structuration model of technology, the duality of technology simultaneously enables and restrains human actions, resulting in unexpected consequences of constraining <sup>fi</sup>rms when change is necessary [32,29]. Thus, in the Trans form industry, IT capability can make <sup>fi</sup>rms vulnerable to adapt as the existing legacy IT base of <sup>fi</sup>rms would increase their switching costs of adopting new technologies [49], which can be negatively related to <sup>fi</sup>rm performance [69].

With no conclusive evidence to support any particular view in the literature, we propose the following hypothesis.

H1: There is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence in pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs between the <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior IT capability and the control companies in the Transform industry.

2.3. IT capability and firm performance in industries where the strategic role of IT is informate up/down (“Informate” industry)

The Informate industry includes the industries in which IT strategic role is to improve business processes with better information <sup>fl</sup>ow, and many traditional manufacturing <sup>fi</sup>rms belong there. Just the same as the Transform industry, there are contrasting perspectives about how IT capability in<sup>fl</sup>uences <sup>fi</sup>rm performance in the Informate industry: positive, neutral, and negative.

IT leaders in the Informate industry can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from e<sup>fi</sup>ciency gain, competitors’ slow response as well as complementary resources such as <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT infrastructure, business process redesign and organizational structure, and learning curve. Especially, although information systems in the Informate industry are also tightly connected like the ones in the Transform industry, it is less vulnerable to disruptions because the operations do not much experience rapid changes. That relatively stable environment provides a favorable condition for IT leaders to pursue IT innovation and build up IT capability.

On the other hand, as <sup>fi</sup>rms try to achieve e<sup>fi</sup>ciency gain through IT capability, they integrate systems with suppliers and customers. Particularly, many manufacturing <sup>fi</sup>rms tightly intertwined enterprise systems such as ERP, SCM, and CRM during the 1990s and 2000s, and those systems can constrain <sup>fi</sup>rms from adjusting their business processes. Enterprise systems can hamper changes due to the tight integration of the processes, which makes it di<sup>fi</sup>cult for <sup>fi</sup>rms to customize or upgrade systems [70]. A recent study using data obtained from 309 Canadian small- and medium-sized manufacturing companies reveals. In the study, the authors examined whether IT integration through applications such as ERP, manufacturing resource planning, and electronic data interchange improves a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s innovative capability in terms of growth and productivity. They found that IT integration did not improve the innovation capability for growth. Even worse, IT in tegration has a negative e<sup>f</sup>ect on productivity [71].

In addition, accelerated IT commoditization in the 2000s can make the advantages of IT capability in the Informate industry as short-lived as the ones in the Transform industry, leveling every <sup>fi</sup>rm in the industry. Indeed, IT leaders become di<sup>fi</sup>cult to maintain their competitive edge as rapid changes in the Informate industry neutralize and obsolete their competitive advantages, allowing faster response from competitors. The impact of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s superior IT capability maximizes when the adoption of IT is slow in the industry [28]. However, the cutting-edge IT capability is readily available to all, so any advantage from IT can be quickly neutralized and transitory. The ever-decreasing price of IT in the 2000s has made IT innovations more a<sup>f</sup>ordable and available, so following companies buy an o<sup>f</sup>-the-shelf, state-of-the-art application for a fraction of the cost that IT leader <sup>fi</sup>rms would pay to develop it.

In sum, as with the Transform industry, there is no conclusive evidence to support any particular view as to the role of IT capability in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, so we propose the following hypothesis:

H2: There is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence in pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs between the <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior IT capability and the control companies in the Informate industry.

2.4. IT capability and firm performance in industries where the strategic role of IT is automate (“Automate” industry)

The industries where the “automate” IT strategic role dominates include heavy manufacturing, metal production, and electric utility. Such industries require signi<sup>fi</sup>cant investment capital, creating high barrier entry, and sell homogeneous commodity-type products like electricity. The <sup>fi</sup>rms in the Automate industry tend to experience low competitive pressure from competitors and enjoy high pro<sup>fi</sup>ts due to high barrier entry and low product di<sup>f</sup>erentiation [12]. They have greater control over inputs and price, less likely to be concerned with e<sup>fi</sup>ciency [72], resulting in diminished IT e<sup>fi</sup>ciency [51].

The main role of IT in the Automate industry is to replace human labor by automating business processes, and although IT can be a substitute for human labor and ordinary capital, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are quite limited [25]. In the Automate industry, the capital often takes the form, such as a stamping press and smelters, so there is likely no suitable substitute [51]. In addition, increased capital investment is likely to take resources away from complementary resource investments that are necessary with IT investments [72]. IT capability is not considered as an important competitive advantage [33]. Further, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of superior IT capability in the Automate industry may be subdued because the products like electricity are a commodity, so <sup>fi</sup>rms are di<sup>fi</sup>cult to be di<sup>f</sup>erentiated with better customer services powered by IT capability. Similarly, IT outsourcing to achieve e<sup>fi</sup>ciency is seldom observed in this industry [36].

As a result, <sup>fi</sup>rms in such industry are conservative, underinvest IT due to resource demands of ordinary capital [51], and do not have a strong desire to apply IT for innovations [44]. Even worse, increased capital intensity makes it more di<sup>fi</sup>cult for new <sup>fi</sup>rms to enter the market when new <sup>fi</sup>rm entry is a key to e<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains through com petition. In sum, the Automate industry is low IT intensive, and <sup>fi</sup>rms receive limited bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from their IT capability given the nature of products and industry [41]. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis.

H3: There is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence in pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs between the <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior IT capability and the control companies in the Automate industry.

## 3. Methods

We used the same “matched sample comparison group” method used in previous studies on IT capability and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance to test the proposed hypotheses. This method selects a treatment sample and a control sample and compares the levels of interest variables between the two groups. In this study, the treatment sample is the <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior IT capability, and the control sample is the <sup>fi</sup>rms matched to the treatment sample of similar size and industry. Using this method, we examined whether IT leaders show competitive advantages in dif ferent industry settings measured by the role of IT in industry.

## 3.1. Selection of IT leaders and control firms

For IS researchers interested in identifying IT leaders with superior IT capability, they have used the Information Week (IW) 500 to select IT leaders. Information Week has chosen 500 companies as leaders in business technology innovation since 1989. The selection criteria for the list have constantly altered to re<sup>fl</sup>ect business and technological changes and to <sup>fi</sup>ne-tune its benchmarking power. For instance, to be selected for IW 500 from 2001 to 2004, companies had to submit quantitative data such as IT budgets as well as an essay that describes how innovatively they use IT. Once the surveys were evaluated, weighted, and combined to a composite score, IW editors ranked the companies based on each composite score. Using IW 500 o<sup>f</sup>ers many advantages in terms of a high response rate or the quality of the respondents [13]. IW 500 is considered as a reliable barometer for <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT capability and has been used in prior academic studies [13,2,73,21].

Firms considered to have superior IT capability were chosen from the Information Week 500 listing published from 2001 to 2004. That is, the IW 500 list was used as a proxy for measuring IT capability. To select the <sup>fi</sup>rms with enduring IT capability, the IT leader sample was restricted to <sup>fi</sup>rms listed in the IW 500 listing at least two of the four years. This yielded a list of 561 <sup>fi</sup>rms. Once IT leaders were selected, a matching set of control <sup>fi</sup>rms was selected from Standard & Poor s Compustat database that provides <sup>fi</sup>nancial, statistical, and market information on active and inactive global companies. From the set of potential control <sup>fi</sup>rms in the same industry as the IT leaders, the matching control companies were chosen with the closest <sup>fi</sup>ve-year average sales level to the IT leaders. The main goal of this study is to understand Chae et al. [2] contrary <sup>fi</sup>ndings, and we followed the same two-step procedures that they used. In step 1, for each IT leader, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed all <sup>fi</sup>rms that have the same four-digit Standard Industrial Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (SIC) code as that of the IT leader. Established in the United States in 1937, SIC is a system for classifying industries by using a four-digit code. The <sup>fi</sup>rst two digits of the code represent the major industry sector in which a business operates. The third and fourth digits describe the sub-classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the business group and specialization, respectively.

Among a set of potential control <sup>fi</sup>rms, we chose a control <sup>fi</sup>rm whose <sup>fi</sup>ve-year average sales of prior <sup>fi</sup>ve years are within 70–130% of the leader <sup>fi</sup>rm. That is, the control companies should be in the same industry as the IT leaders, and their average sales from 1996 to 2000 must be 70–130% of IT leaders’ average sales from 1996 to 2000. In the second step, if there are no matching <sup>fi</sup>rms in step 1, the control <sup>fi</sup>rms are found using a two- or three-digit SIC code. The process yielded the 296 pairs of IT leaders and comparable control <sup>fi</sup>rms.

A researcher who examines accounting measures must use control groups to minimize unexpected results caused by di<sup>f</sup>erent industry and <sup>fi</sup>rm size. Using the control <sup>fi</sup>rm in the same industry and of similar size and performance is important for several reasons. First, operating performance varies widely by industry and <sup>fi</sup>rm size. By using the matching samples, the variance of performance in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the dif ference in industry and <sup>fi</sup>rm size can be minimized. In addition, the literature in accounting suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rm size and industry type are strong predictors of the choice of accounting methods and process used to compute costs such as depreciation and amortization [13]. Thus, the impact of variance in accounting methods can be controlled, and the direct comparison of pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and cost ratios becomes more reliable. Finally, having control <sup>fi</sup>rms matching on prior performance is crucial to obtain well-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed and powerful test statistics without matching on prior performance, the observed abnormal performance is unclear whether it is a result of mean reversion or the event under investigation [74]. That is, an IT leader’s superior performance can be subjective by its prior performance, industry, or economy, so using a control group as a benchmark ensures to control any in<sup>fl</sup>uences related to a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s superior performance. Although a <sup>fi</sup>rm may show superior business performance, it is uncertain whether that positive performance is a result of competitive advantages unless the performance of the <sup>fi</sup>rm is compared with those of similar competitors in the same industry. The <sup>fi</sup>rm that shows great performance in isolation may lag behind major competitors on key performance measures. Using benchmarks to con trol normal changes in performance is a basic and minimum require ment in assessing performance e<sup>f</sup>ects of IT capability [74].

A <sup>fi</sup>rm’s size can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT budget, so the two groups were compared to ensure that there are no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant dif ferences in <sup>fi</sup>rm size [13]. Commonly used measures such as sales, total assets, and the number of employees were used. Means test (t-test) indicates no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences in average sales, assets, and the number of employees between the IT leader group and control group.

## 3.2. Categorizations of the strategic role of IT in industry

In order to categorize the <sup>fi</sup>rms in the study according to the IT strategic role, we used the same method developed by Chatterjee et al. [55]. Following their method, we created the instrument (provided as Appendix A) to access the strategic role of IT. The instrument was sent to <sup>fi</sup>ve judges, who all have extensive experience in researching, teaching, and practicing IT. The categorization process went through two phases for a total of 63 industries. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, we asked each judge to evaluate the strategic role of industry for the original 42 industries that Chatterjee et al. [55] categorized for the time periods 2001 through 2004 and for the time periods 2005 through 2007. In the second phase, which was performed one month later on, they evaluated additional 21 industries not categorized in Chatterjee et al. [55] but covered in Chae et al. [2] for those two periods.

It is possible that each judge can have di<sup>f</sup>erent opinion about the strategic role of IT in industry, so inter-rater reliability was used to ensure reliability of categorizations. Inter-rater reliability (kappa value) was 0.85 for the time periods 2001 through 2004 and 0.81 for the time periods 2005 through 2007. According to Fleiss [75], kappa values lower than 0.40 can be interpreted as poor, between 0.41 and 0.75 as fair, and above 0.75 as excellent agreement. Therefore, our score of inter-rater reliability is regarded as acceptable.

After identifying the strategic role of IT for all industries, we categorized where the 290 pairs of IT leaders and control <sup>fi</sup>rms belong to one of the three groups: “Transform,” “Informate,” and “Automate.” We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that 68 pairs belong to the “Transform” role of IT in industry during 2001–2004, and the number increased to 69 for 2005–2007 as the hotel industry became Transform from Informate. 201 pairs are categorized to the “Informate” from 2001 to 2004, and 215 pairs from 2005 to 2007 as several industries such as the construction changed from Automate to Informate during that time period. 21 pairs belong to the “Automate” during 2001–2004, and 6 pairs for 2005–2007. ## 3.3. Business performance

Aligned with prior studies, this study uses eight variables to mea sure pro<sup>fi</sup>t and cost ratios. As for the pro<sup>fi</sup>t ratios, the following <sup>fi</sup>ve variables are measured: (1) return on assets (ROAs), (2) return on sales (ROSs), (3) operating income to assets (OI/A), (4) operating income to sales (OI/S), and (5) operating income to employees (OI/E). The operating income was not clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned in Bharadwaj’s [13] study, so we used earnings before interest payment and taxes (EBIT) as the operating income. Three cost-related ratios were also compared: (1) total operating expenses to sales (OEXP/S), (2) cost of goods sold to sales (COGS/ S), and (3) selling and general administrative expenses to sales (SG&A/ S). As in Bharadwaj [13], operating expenses are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the sum of COGS and SG&A.

## 3.4. Hypotheses tests

This study examines whether the <sup>fi</sup>rms with high IT capability show di<sup>f</sup>erent business performance from the matching control <sup>fi</sup>rms depending on the industry they operate in. One way to test the hypotheses is to compare the mean value of variables for the IT leader and the control samples using a standard t-test. However, since the sample is not normally distributed, a non-parametric test, the Wilcoxon signedrank test, was used.

## 4. Results

For the Transform industry, we hypothesized performance di<sup>f</sup>erence between the IT leaders and the control <sup>fi</sup>rms, and the results indicate the presence of a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence between the two groups (see Tables 3 and 4). While the presence of such di<sup>f</sup>erence was not unexpected, it was surprising to learn that IT leader group showed poorer performance than the control group. For the <sup>fi</sup>ve pro<sup>fi</sup>t-related <sup>fi</sup>nancial measures, the IT leader <sup>fi</sup>rms did not show any better performance than the control <sup>fi</sup>rms. Rather, the IT leader <sup>fi</sup>rms exhibited statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative results measured by ROS, OI/S, and OI/ E than the control <sup>fi</sup>rms. Even worse, IT leaders’ poor business performance had worsened as time went. The control group showed higher ROS and OI/S for the years 2005–2007, OI/E for two of the three years, ROA and OI/A for one of the three years.

Although the IT leaders showed substantially lower raw-material costs measured by COG/S than the control <sup>fi</sup>rms during the period of 2001–2004, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts were o<sup>f</sup>set by higher administrative expenses measured by SGA/S, so overall operating expenses showed no di<sup>f</sup>erences between the IT leader group and the control group in the years 2002 and 2003. However, the IT leaders’ lower raw-material cost advantages did not last long while they incurred considerably higher administrative costs and operating expenses. The IT leader group had signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher operating expenses than the control group during the period of 2004–2007. In sum, the control group performed better than the IT group in the Transform industry.

The second hypothesis was to examine how IT capability plays a role in the Informate industry (see Tables 5 and 6), and the results showed no performance di<sup>f</sup>erence between the IT leader group and the control group. The IT leader <sup>fi</sup>rms showed higher ROA in the year 2004 and OI/E in 2001 and lower COG/S in 2007 and they had consistently performed better, but the di<sup>f</sup>erences were not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant.

The Automate industry consists of such <sup>fi</sup>rms as railroads and utilities. These businesses normally require substantial capital investment that increases the entry barrier for other potential competitors. Thus, the role of IT in such industry is not as strategically important as in Transform and Informate industries [12]. We hypothesized that there were no di<sup>f</sup>erences in <sup>fi</sup>rm performance between the IT leaders and the control <sup>fi</sup>rms, and the data supported the hypothesis as shown in Table 7.

## 5. Discussions and implications

## 5.1. IT capability in the transform industry

One of the most intriguing and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study is that the IT leader group in the Transform industry did not exhibit pro<sup>fi</sup>t and cost advantages over the control group. Surprisingly, it was the control group that showed better performance. While the positive role of IT capability on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance may have been the most widely accepted view, the literature provides other possible views that may explain the unexpected <sup>fi</sup>nding. Thus, we present and discuss these possible views – positive, neutral, and negative – of IT capability on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance to understand the counter-intuitive <sup>fi</sup>nding in a proper perspective.

It is generally believed that IT can empower <sup>fi</sup>rms with such capabilities as environment scanning and customer services and enable them to perform and compete better in the uncertain market and environment [76,77,21,38]. While superior IT capability is important for all types of industries, it is viewed particularly essential for the Trans form industry as it can play a critical role for competitiveness. In the Transform industry, <sup>fi</sup>rms often have to deal with the uncertainty in the decision processes as conventional rules and assumptions are challenged and disrupted. IT can help a <sup>fi</sup>rm cope with such environmental uncertainty by allowing it (1) to discover and anticipate changes through competitive intelligence gathering and analysis [78,35], (2) to counter unfavorable changes in the market with <sup>fl</sup>exible product offerings, and (3) to recon<sup>fi</sup>gure the operational processes required to produce and distribute new products and services, thereby di<sup>f</sup>erentiating itself from its competitors [79,80].

In addition, IT capability allows the <sup>fi</sup>rm to counteract the unfavorable environment by initiating innovative and competitive actions to re<sup>fl</sup>ect new business models [81]. Firms in the industries undergoing IT-driven changes would have a heightened need for superior IT capability to initiate new business models and execute the subsequent business strategies, resulting in improved <sup>fi</sup>nancial outcomes. Indeed, automated processes and data repository in information systems may allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to leverage its IT capability to launch future competitive moves and initiatives [81]. Although IT-based competitive edges are getting shorter-lived, it is still possible for IT leaders to enjoy a sustained competitiveness by o<sup>f</sup>ering new products and services by creatively taking advantage of innovative technologies [46,82].

An alternative view of the impact of IT on performance suggests that any competitive advantage from the superior use of IT in the Transform industry tends to be short-lived and easily neutralized [10]. The highly competitive market may pressure all <sup>fi</sup>rms in the Transform industry to adopt IT to stay competitive in the market [44], turning IT into a necessity for survival rather than a competitive resource [83,2]. This view may be understood as a manifestation of the productivity paradox, particularly pronounced in the Transform industry than other industries [73,10].

Finally, it may sound counter-intuitive that IT can negatively a<sup>f</sup>ect <sup>fi</sup>rm performance but the <sup>fi</sup>nding of this study appears to support this contention. Some researchers have long recognized the importance of organizational agility in the turbulent business environment and IT may hinder <sup>fi</sup>rms to respond promptly to the changes taking place in the market [77]. As IT such as the Internet brought changes to the market in the 2000s, <sup>fi</sup>rms were required to be agile to react, respond, and reposition themselves in the changing business landscape. Firms competing in the Transform industry would require an even higher level of agility as they compete in the most volatile and competitive market condition [38]. Thus, a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT capability, along with <sup>fl</sup>exible IT infrastructure, must be able to adapt to the new market conditions and initiate new business models and execute the subsequent business strategies as they become necessary [81].

While new business models call for constant and dynamic adjustments in response to the changes in the market, the IS systems may not be nimble enough to react to the new requirements [67]. That makes <sup>fi</sup>rms di<sup>fi</sup>cult and slow to adapt to changes, especially initiated by disruptive and new technologies like the Internet. IT can be con straining and in<sup>fl</sup>exible, “like cement, highly <sup>fl</sup>exible in the beginning, but rigid later” [84], p. 16. Core capabilities can become core rigidities [95].

Firms develop business processes carefully aligned with business strategies as well as information systems. At that time, their value chains, business processes, and information systems are all aligned. As their information systems, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally enterprise-wide IS, are deeply involved in handling a wide range of business processes, the costs and risks involved in changing them dramatically increase. That is, information systems become tightly coupled with its strategy and struc ture and remain relatively stable. Enterprise information systems seamlessly integrate all aspects of business, making the systems interdependent, tightly coupled, and replete with dense webs of causal connections among applications. Such systems can be vulnerable to unexpected changes to one of their parts, leading to abrupt and un expected degradation of systems performance.

## 6. Future study and limitations

In this study, we proposed to understand the role of industry in the connection between IT capability and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance through the perspective of IT strategic role. We found that the impact of IT capability on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance varies by industry types. The results raise interesting issues in the Transform industry as managers face a perplexing dilemma about IT investment, and future studies are required to address these questions:

1. Which IT strategy should a <sup>fi</sup>rm choose?

2. Does IT capability provide the <sup>fi</sup>rst mover a strategic advantage or disadvantage?

3. How do enterprise systems impact organizational agility?

Leidner et al. [22] proposed that <sup>fi</sup>rms are likely to have one of the three types of IT strategy – (1) IT Innovator, (2) IT Conservative, and (3) IT Undecided – and argued that <sup>fi</sup>rms should align their IS strategy with the environment. Then, which strategy should <sup>fi</sup>rms in the Transform industry choose? A conventional wisdom suggests that an IT Innovator should excel in the Transform industry, but our <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that this may not be necessarily the case. Nevertheless, there is little empirical research on this issue to provide a conclusive direction. Further research is needed to better understand how IT Innovators or Conservatives face such issues as rigidity, duality of technology, or competence trap in the Transform industry.

Another potential future research agenda is to explore whether early investment in IT capability generates <sup>fi</sup>rst-mover advantages in the Transform industry. Decisions about when to invest is an important issue to managers as well as what to invest in. In certain industries where a <sup>fi</sup>rst mover confers brand loyalty, technological leadership, preemption of scare assets, and exploitation of buyer switching cost [91], early timing can be crucial. On the other hand, later movers can reap the bene<sup>fi</sup>t from newer and more e<sup>fi</sup>cient technologies while earlier movers are either stuck with older technologies or must pay to rebuild their technologies [92]. Understanding of early-mover advantages or disadvantages will o<sup>f</sup>er a useful guideline to managers about investment timing.

Discussing the results, we speculate the organizational agility or the lack of it may be a reason for IT leaders’ poor performance. We need to better understand whether popular enterprise systems provide agility or impose rigidity in the Transform industry. That is, do they make <sup>fi</sup>rms <sup>fl</sup>exible enough to launch new business models as promised or limit them to initiate new products? Investigating cases like Dell that failed in the smartphone market may provide some insights as its once successful just-in-time enterprise systems may have become a cause for the failure [89].

We acknowledge that this study has several limitations. Although the use of the method of IT strategic role for understanding business environments and classifying industries is considered superior to other methods such as dynamism, muni<sup>fi</sup>cence, complexity, or IT intensity, this is still simplistic and retrospective. In the knowledge economy, as IT becomes more vital and pervasive in every business activity, using only three categories such as Automate, Informate, and Transform may not be adequate enough to capture delicate attributes and importance of IT in the industry context. Instead of the relatively basic trichotomy, a more comprehensive classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of IT strategic role is desired so that managers can better determine their IT investment needs and align their IT capability with the environment. Another limitation is that the industry categorization scheme is retrospective, rather than progressive, based on historical archival data. That is, we asked expert judges to indicate IT strategic role within an industry in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c time period using the Automate, Informate, or Transform categories. After that, the level of IT-driven Transformation was computed and assigned to each industry. However, as IT rapidly changes each year, this retrospective method doesn’t provide up-to-date information about current IT strategic role, so managers are di<sup>fi</sup>cult to decide IT investment based on the industry they belong to and the present stra tegic IT role.

## 7. Conclusion

This study with the main goal to examine why Chae et al. [2] found the results contrary to earlier studies that supported the connection between IT capability and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance makes several important contributions to the research as well as practitioner communities. We speculated and con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the role of industry as a possible culprit as IT plays di<sup>f</sup>erent roles and carries varying levels of impact on business depending on the industry in which <sup>fi</sup>rms operate. As expected, the study supported the conventional contention that IT can be a di<sup>f</sup>erentiating factor for <sup>fi</sup>rm performance in the “Transform” industry where IT can fundamentally change business and industry processes and relationships. This <sup>fi</sup>nding reminds researchers and practitioners of the important role of industry in researching and managing IT capability and calls for increased attention to the environment in which IT is adopted and deployed.

Furthermore, the study provides an insight into an unexpected and yet plausible dynamics between IT capability and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Contrary to the generally accepted belief that <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior IT should produce superior performance, the study suggests that IT can have a negative impact on business performance. While further studies are required to better understand this unforeseen <sup>fi</sup>nding, it provides a glimpse of the mechanism and dynamics of IT capability and performance much more complex and <sup>fl</sup>uid than we have estimated. We believe that this <sup>fi</sup>nding calls for more comprehensive and rigorous research into the role of IT in a broader context of corporate strategies.

Thus, IS researchers need to generate “macro” theories of IT-enabled transformations in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts [93]. Understanding IT’s heterogeneous impact across di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts helps identify some of the mechanisms through which IT in<sup>fl</sup>uences <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Also, it can improve the targeting of scarce corporate resources when making IT-related investments. Managers can better decide IT investment by being aware of di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts [49]. Further, there is a need for a balance between the <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk of investing and the competitive risk of not or late investing [59], and these simultaneous contradictions are relevant in the process of developing a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT capability and can intensify the dilemma in IT innovation decision making.

Finally, this study is valuable in that we updated how the IT strategic role in industry changes in the 2000s and applied it to understand the impact of IT capability on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. This is an important addition to the IT value literature because two research streams on IT value (industry level and <sup>fi</sup>rm level) are simultaneously considered and integrated. Managers often use industry norms as a basis for their decisions, but have to adapt those norms to the environments of their speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>rm. Better understanding industry can potentially improve their IT decision.
