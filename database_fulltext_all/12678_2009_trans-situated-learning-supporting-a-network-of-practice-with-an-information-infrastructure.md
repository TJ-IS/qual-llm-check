# Trans-Situated Learning: Supporting a Network of Practice with an Information Infrastructure

Emmanuelle Vaast

School of Business, Long Island University, Brooklyn, New York 11201, emmanuelle.vaast@liu.edu

Geoff Walsham

Judge Business School, University of Cambridge, Cambridge CB2 1AG, United Kingdom, g.walsham@jbs.cam.ac.uk

his paper investigates the practice-based learning dynamics that emerge among peers who share occupa tional practices but do not necessarily work with each other or even know each other because of geographical or organizational distance. To do so, it draws on the literatures on situated learning, networks of practice, and information infrastructures, and on insights from a longitudinal case study of the implementation of a Web based information system used by people working in the field of environmental health. The system was deeply involved in the transformations of local practices as well as relationships between peers. Based on a dialogue between existing literatures and observations from the case study, this research extends the practice-based perspective on learning to the computer-mediated context of a network of practice. To that effect, it proposes a model of what we call trans-situated learning that is supported by the local universality of an information infrastructure whose use becomes embedded with other infrastructures.

## 1. Introduction

The ability of people and organizations to thrive in today’s knowledge economy relies on their learning abilities (Alavi and Leidner 2001, Grant 1996, Kogut and Zander 1996). In particular, practice-based learning is a key foundation for the building of individual and organizational competitive advantage (Spender 1996, Tsoukas 1996). Academics and practitioners have thus been increasingly interested in understanding and stimulating the learning processes that emerge from activity and experience (Bourdieu 1972, 1990; Gherardi 2000, 2001; Lave and Wenger 1991):

Thinking of learning through participation in a practice enables us to focus on the fact that, in everyday practices, learning takes place in the flow of experience, with or without or awareness of it (Gherardi 2000, p. 214).

In this regard, the increasing use of Web-based systems has generated new ways for people to get information and to communicate with others based on joint interests and experiences, not relying solely on the proximity of their immediate social circles (Goodman and Darr 1998, Schultze and Leidner 2002). Online forums, knowledge management systems (KMS), databases, etc., stimulate the emergence of “global villages” whose members engage with each other related to various topics, including video games, illnesses, hobbies, or occupational practices (Cummings et al. 2002, Finholt et al. 2002, Rheingold 2000, Wellman et al. 1996). Web-based interaction seems particularly propitious to the acquisition and generation of new knowledge and competences based on similar practices and experiences, and beyond the confines of shared locations or of institutionalized educational settings.

Yet, practice-based learning is elusive (Contu and Willmott 2000, Yanow 2006) and, in particular, there remains at least one domain where the potential for practice-based learning is not well understood (Duguid 2006). This domain encompasses people who are not necessarily colocated and do not necessarily share an institutional affiliation, but who nonetheless come together through electronically mediated means to interact on issues related to their practices (Brown and Duguid 2001, McLure Wasko and Faraj 2005). So far, the existing knowledge management (KM) literature has focused most of its efforts on understanding how learning can happen in situated contexts or across boundaries. On one hand, situated learning (Brown and Duguid 1991, Lave and Wenger 1991) is characterized by rich exchanges of know-how and the often tacit knowledge that comes from people doing things together or communicating face to face at least occasionally (Orr 1990). Such contexts usually do not require and can even be disrupted by the implementation of sophisticated information systems (IS) (Carlile 2004, Hsiao et al. 2006, Nidumolu et al. 2001). On the other hand, the implementation and use of information infrastructures such as integrated information systems aims, among other things, to unleash learning potential across functional and geographical boundaries (Boudreau and Robey 2005, Ciborra 2000c, Rolland and Monteiro 2002). Information infrastructures correspond to sociotechnical systems that provide global resources and help connect multiple smaller entities (Star and Ruhleder 1996). Many of the challenges related to the implementation of such information infrastructures come from them having to support the practices of people separated by multiple boundaries (i.e., functional, geographical, or organizational) (Newell et al. 2001, Sole and Edmondson 2002). Less is known of the implementation and use of information infrastructures aimed at supporting the practices of people who share occupational interests but who are not working together and who cannot frequently interact face to face. They may not even be aware of their having common interests and practices. However, in today’s distributed and globalized work environments, such contexts are increasingly common.

Our paper examines the practice-based learning processes that emerge in such environments and investigates how an information infrastructure can support them. It aims to extend the literature on situated learning and to build on the literature on information infrastructures to better understand these learning dynamics and their reliance on the use of information systems.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We first discuss the relevant literatures on situated learning, networks of practice, and information infrastructures. The methods section then presents the field setting and the interpretive methods adopted for our research. A case study of implementation and use of an information system by participants in the specific practice of environmental health in public administration is then described and analyzed. The subsequent section proposes a model of trans-situated learning, i.e., practice-based but not quite situated learning. It then analyzes how trans-situated learning processes rely on the local universality (Timmermans and Berg 1997) of an information infrastructure and on the emergent embeddedness of its use with other infrastructures. The conclusion summarizes the main contribution of this paper, which is to extend the traditional situated learning model to broader computer-mediated contexts. The conclusion also suggests the need for further developments of this model of trans-situated learning to explore current sociotechnological trends (e.g., social networking) that increasingly affect the way people work and relate to one another.

## 2. Situated Learning, Networks of Practice, and Information Infrastructures

2.1. A Canonical View of Situated Learning Most empirical accounts of practice-based learning are actually accounts of “situated” learning (Lave 1988, Lave and Wenger 1991). Situated learning corresponds to the processes through which people acquire new knowledge and skills on the job as they work and interact with their closest colleagues (Brown and Duguid 1991, Orr 1990). Situated learning is embedded in the specificities of the context of work and this embeddedness provides resources for people to learn new practices (Suchman 1987, Tyre and Von Hippel 1997). Situated learning is especially well-suited to the transmission and development of tacit knowledge: As they learn a set of new skills, people acquire knowledge that cannot entirely be articulated and that is deeply ingrained into their practices and their context of work (Carlile 2004, Edmondson 2002). This tacit dimension makes it difficult to transfer such knowledge beyond its context of origin (Carlile 2004, Cook and Brown 1999, Duguid 2005, Szulanski 1996). The process through which situated learning emerges is that of “legitimate peripheral participation” (LPP) (Lave and Wenger 1991). In the apprenticeshiplike process of LPP, newcomers to a practice are at first only peripherally engaged in the practice at hand and then, gradually, learn tricks of the trade from oldtimers and become more fully engaged in the practice.

Situated learning is thus deeply social and contextualized. Lave and Wenger (1991) introduced the expression “communities of practice” (CoPs) as an analytical device to describe the bounded groupings in which LPP arises spontaneously. CoPs correspond to small groups of people who engage regularly in similar practices and have frequent occasions to interact with each other (Brown and Duguid 1991; Edmondson 2002; Wenger 1998, 2000). Learning in CoPs arises from newcomers who engage in processes of LPP and bring their own perspective and practices to the CoP (Lave and Wenger 1991).

Three aspects of this canonical view of situated learning are worth noting because they affect how practice-based learning emerges in CoPs. First, members of a CoP engage in similar but not necessarily identical practices. For instance, the copy machine repairers studied by Orr (1990) all diagnosed and fixed machines but no two repairs were exactly identical. Moreover, as members of CoPs develop a joint history of working together, a division of tasks and differentiation of expertise emerges (Wenger 1990). Second, situated learning is deeply embedded in a shared context of work (Suchman 1987, Von Hippel 1994), but members of a CoP may not always be colocated. Orr’s (1996) copier repairers worked most of the time at clients’ locations, where the machines were. However, they met regularly, discussed the issues they encountered, and exchanged “war stories” of how they had solved challenging problems. Hence, situated learning does not seem to necessarily require colocation of members of a CoP. However, it relies on at least occasional direct face-to-face exchanges that are essential for the sharing of experiences and for the emergence of a joint identification with the community (Sole and Huysman 2002). Third, the situated learning literature has insisted on the delicate nature of these processes (Contu and Willmott 2000, Gherardi 2000) and on the idea that situated learning emerges de facto from practices (Lave and Wenger 1991, Orlikowski 1996). Therefore, managerial efforts to stimulate such processes through the implementation of KMS, in particular, can backfire if these KMSs are implemented without sufficient regard for the local conditions of practices (Hsiao et al. 2006, Nidumolu et al. 2001, Sole and Edmondson 2002).

## 2.2. Beyond Situated Learning: Networks of Practice

Situated learning theory powerfully captures some of the ways people learn from practice (Duguid 2006). CoPs have also increasingly been considered by academics and practitioners alike as precious entities to stimulate practice-based learning processes. Yet, CoPs do not always generate appropriate situated learning, and the model of situated learning itself does not account for all processes of practice-based learning.

First, there are potential drawbacks to CoPs that can limit their learning potential (Roberts 2006). Conflictual and disruptive power relations within CoPs and between their members and a wider institutional context can limit the learning that actually takes place in CoPs (Contu and Willmott 2003, Fox 2000). In particular, exceedingly unbalanced relationships between “old timers” and newcomers can restrict the learning of all parties (Lave 1988). Moreover, the small size and the intensity of exchanges among its members may make a “closed system” out of the CoP (Vaast 2003). Such an internally focused CoP would not easily welcome new members or new perspectives, which would restrain its epistemic horizon (Swan et al. 2002, Thompson 2005). Its members may then encounter difficulties in developing competencies suited to new and unusual issues.

Second, the traditional model of situated learning cannot account for all processes of practice-based learning. For instance, the idea of “constellations of (communities of) practices” (Gherardi and Nicolini 2000) illustrates people who interact frequently and are engaged in a common enterprise while coming from different practices (e.g., in a joint project). There are also many environments in which people are not necessarily collocated but are engaged in practices that share a certain degree of similarity. Such groupings could not rigorously be labeled CoPs as this would excessively broaden the notion. Brown and Duguid (2001) introduced the concept of a “network of practice” (NoP) to describe these groupings. Members of an NoP do not necessarily work with each other and may never have met face to face. Yet, they share a common foundation of work practices and may be interested in similar types of issues (Brown and Duguid 2001).

Contrasted with a CoP, members of an NoP have a lesser degree of similarity in their practices and are not necessarily aware of each other. For instance, lawyers employed by the same corporate law firm but who work for different clients in geographically scattered offices develop practices that are partly similar and partly different according to the idiosyncrasies of their local contexts and clients. Arguably, however, regardless of their organizational affiliation, lawyers have a higher degree of similarity in their practices if they are specialized in a specific realm of law (say, intellectual property) rather than in different domains of law (e.g., intellectual property versus criminal). The degree of similarities and differences in practices among members of an NoP is largely a matter of degree of detail in the observations of practices. At the extreme, no two CoPs develop strictly identical practices, just as no two members of the same CoP do exactly the same thing. Yet, members of an NoP who share a sufficient degree of similarity in their practices could benefit from communicating with each other and from exchanging ideas on their practices (Duguid 2003, 2005). Sharing a certain degree of similarity in practices makes it possible for members of an NoP to understand others’ concerns and, given a suitable infrastructure, to interact on practice-related issues (Duguid 2006). Because of their members’ related practices, according to Brown and Duguid (2001), knowledge “flows” laterally along NoPs.

However, the learning processes that may emerge in NoPs are not yet well-understood. They cannot rigorously be characterized as “situated” learning processes because the apprenticeship-like processes of LPP cannot easily emerge in big and loose entities such as NoPs (Duguid 2005). The literature so far has examined the processes through which information systems may be implemented to stimulate exchanges across CoPs and throughout an NoP (Pan and Leidner 2003, Vaast 2004). It has also described cases of such initiatives and their varying degrees of success (Bobrow and Whalen 2002, Constant et al. 1996, Goodman and Darr 1998, McLure Wasko and Faraj 2005). “Success” in these studies has, however, been related to evidence of contributions to the information system rather than to evidence of enhanced practice-based learning processes, which involve the acquisition of new skills and competences translated into work practices. Therefore, little is known about whether practice-based learning emerges from an NoP and, if such learning is possible, what it involves and what role the implementation and use of IS may play in its emergence.

## 2.3. Information Infrastructures for Networks of Practice

Contrasted with the model of situated learning that emerges in CoPs, practice-based learning in NoPs poses a challenge; practitioners share a certain degree of similarity in their practices without necessarily sharing material contexts and specific situations. For practice-based learning to emerge, members of an NoP should be able to exchange resources and experiences beyond their local CoP, access the experiences of peers whom they may not know of, and, from these exchanges, develop new expertise and knowledge that would be useful for their own practice and for that of their peers. For such local and global exchanges and dynamics of practice to be possible, the NoP needs a supportive information infrastructure.

In the current literature, the notion of information infrastructure is very often used to describe the implementation of integrated applications such as enterprise resource planning systems (ERPs) and the problems involved in succession management, side effects, and interconnectivity throughout the functions of the enterprise (e.g., Boudreau and Robey 2005). At its core, though, the notion of an information infrastructure corresponds to the idea of shared global resources that shape and are shaped by practices and connections among local entities (Rolland and Monteiro 2002, Star and Ruhleder 1996). It is in this fundamental sense that we consider the concept of information infrastructure in our paper to be of relevance to understanding the dynamics of NoPs.

Historically, infrastructures have relied on a heavy material foundation. Railroads constitute the archetypal example of material infrastructure that shaped work and economic development and allowed for the greater circulation of goods and people. Their development fostered the connection of multiple locales and strongly stimulated economic development (Chandler 1962). The material infrastructure of organizations is characterized by the physical headquarters and all the material resources that support the work being accomplished. NoPs do not usually benefit from a strong material infrastructure. Because NoPs do not necessarily correspond to formal entities from the organizational chart, and because only a few occupations actually become heavily regulated and supported professions (Van Maanen and Barley 1984), members of NoPs often lack the material resources to exchange practice-related information and get to know each others’ practices across geographical and organizational distance (Duguid 2005).

Today, material infrastructures have been complemented by information infrastructures whose IS foundation can help connect people across multiple boundaries, be they temporal, geographical, functional, or organizational (Bieber et al. 2002, Ciborra 2000b, Huysman and Wulf 2005, Kayworth and Sambamurthy 2000, Von Krogh 2002). Information infrastructures provide resources that condition local practices and generate an overall coherence to the organizations they support beyond the specificities of their local entities (Rolland and Monteiro 2002, Star and Ruhleder 1996). Information infrastructures require the emergence of a delicate balance between the global standards that are needed for systems to interconnect and for coherence to be secured, and attention to local idiosyncrasies that condition whether the infrastructure will be used (Robey et al. 2002, Rolland and Monteiro 2002). Consistent with a practice perspective, information infrastructures are conditioned by and dependent on practices and relationships among people and entities, just as they condition practices and relationships, in a mutually constituted and dynamic fashion (Boudreau and Robey 2005, Star and Ruhleder 1996).

Empirical evidence about the implementation of the IS foundation of information infrastructures reveals that the articulation between local and global is difficult to achieve (Ciborra 2000a). Most of these implementations are conducted in a “top down” manner, but the imposition of global standards may be unfit for local needs and practices (Newell et al. 2001). On the other hand, if the IS foundation of the information infrastructure is only constituted by the sum of local initiatives in a “bottom up” manner, then there may not be sufficient coherence and continuity for the benefits of a global information infrastructure to emerge (Rolland and Monteiro 2002). These studies therefore reveal that the way in which the IS foundation of information infrastructures is implemented holds significant consequences for the emergence of articulation between local practices and global context (Boudreau and Robey 2005, Robey et al. 2002).

Findings from this literature, however, only partly apply to the context of NoPs. Most of the existing literature relates experiences in which information infrastructures are to be used across multiple boundaries. In particular, functional boundaries create important discontinuities in practices that strongly impact the implementation and use of an information infrastructure (Newell et al. 2001). For instance, enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems aim to integrate the systems of multiple functions of the organizations, from finance to operations and human resources. The situation of NoPs is different because members of an NoP share similar practices but usually lack the resources that come from being part of the official organizational structure of the organization (Brown and Duguid 2001). There is, therefore, a need to investigate how, in the context of an NoP, the IS foundation of an information infrastructure could be implemented and how it could stimulate practicebased learning processes. Our research tackles these issues directly.

For this purpose, this paper analyzes observations from a longitudinal case study to characterize practice-based learning processes in an NoP and examine how an IS may become the foundation of an information infrastructure supporting such learning processes. The following two sections present the field settings, methods, and observations from the case study. The rest of the paper interprets these observations in relation to relevant existing literatures to extend the practice-based model of learning to the context of an NoP and examine the role of an information infrastructure in these processes.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Field Setting

The case study relates the work practices of participants in the environmental health (EH) field within the French Public Health Administration (FPHA) and the implementation, management, and use of an IS dedicated to EH issues. More details about the field setting can be found in [reference suppressed]. EH activities deal with the enforcement of EH law and concern, for instance, air or water pollution, industrial risk, or safety procedures for healthcare organizations. The role of EH agents is to inform local authorities and various stakeholders (e.g., citizens, firms, unions, consumer associations) of new regulations. They also control the actual enforcement of the law. Moreover, EH agents deal with emergency crises such as local outbreaks of meningitis or salmonella. In such cases, EH agents have to inform the public, deal with the origin of the outbreak, and make sure that proper care is provided.

At the time of the study, about 1,500 FPHA employees worked in EH. They were dispersed in around 100 local teams of about 15 members. Each team was composed of EH technicians (about 10) and EH engineers (about 5). One EH engineer was usually the head of each team. Each local team dealt with two main types of tasks: routine-based tasks and emergency crises. Routine tasks required that agents keep up to date with the law (e.g., controlling the pollution level of city swimming pools to comply with changing standards). Emergency crises involved abnormal events and required immediate responses (e.g., pollution peaks in specific regions).

Local EH teams could be viewed as CoPs and, taken together, all EH members and their practices could be viewed as an NoP. EH engineers and technicians worked together on activities that were all related to environmental health such as air pollution, water quality, or public health education. Moreover, most EH members graduated from the National School of Public Health: All engineers had a master’s degree from this school while some technicians earned a bachelor’s degree. The similarity of their formal training gave EH members a common professional knowledge, a sense of what were good EH practices, and a level of professionalism. It also initiated a common history among EH members.

## 3.2. An Interpretive Methodology

The EH case was investigated and analyzed following the interpretive tradition of field studies in information systems (Klein and Myers 1999, Walsham 1993).

Rather than aiming at investigating objectively specific contexts, this stance calls for a better understanding of how participants in a situation interpret their context and act consequently (Berger and Luckmann 1966). It also recognizes that researchers subjectively construct their interpretation of observations according to their own situations, interests, and constraints (Burrell and Morgan 1979, Pawlowski and Robey 2004).

Interpreting the case study from a practice-based learning perspective posed three key methodological challenges. First, we had to be able to develop an understanding of what participants in the practice actually did at work: how they used their information systems as well as how they made sense of these practices. Second, observations had to account for the relationships between what happened locally in specific contexts and what happened throughout the NoP.<sup>1</sup> Third, our observations had to give us a sense of how, over time, changes occurred in the information system, in its use by employees, and in the employees work practices.

To respond to the first methodological challenge, the field researcher did some in-depth, semistructured interviews of EH members and also spent some time in local teams and observed actual practices. She got familiar with the physical and social environment in which participants in the practice worked and observed the ways in which they dealt with their tasks. She also observed EH members interacting with the system in the course of their activities. Observation notes were analyzed separately as background for and contrast with the analysis of interview transcripts. During interviews, respondents described their work environment, job, work practices, and use of the system. They also discussed relations with their peers and the potential changes they had experienced in their job. Interviews were recorded and transcribed. Table 1 details key characteristics of the respondents.

Regarding the second methodological challenge, to relate what happened locally and globally, the field researcher combined her direct observations at the local level with interviews with members of the team in charge of the management of the system as well as of the central headquarters of the FPHA.

For interviews of members of local teams, the field researcher visited different teams to get a sense of their local practices. Also, contrasting the perspectives of people who occupied different positions within the NoP as well as outside of it (i.e., members of the headquarters) gave the proximity and distance needed to combine local and global levels of observation. For each of the stages of data collection (in 2000, 2003, and 2005), interviews continued until saturation was reached, that is, until the additional insights provided by additional interviews were deemed insignificant, as in Pawlowski and Robey (2004). In addition to her observation of local practices, the field researcher also gained access to the EH system.

Access to the system was maintained after the periods of direct observations and interviews, which also helped tackle the third methodological challenge of this research, the need for dynamic observations. In addition, having three stages of interviews was useful to get a sense of how members of the NoP experienced changes in their job, in their environment, and in the system. For the second and third stage of interviews, the field researcher interviewed some of the respondents from the previous stage but, because of organizational turnover and to make the most of the broad access to practices, she also interviewed new respondents. Between periods of formal interviews, contacts were maintained with respondents as well as with the team that managed the EH system through frequent e-mails and regular quarterly phone conversations.

With respect to data analysis, we engaged in the constant confrontation of our empirical evidence (interview transcripts, notes, IS browsing, logs, etc.) with our evolving framework and with the existing literature in the field. More specifically, this research strove to follow Klein and Myers’ (1999) “principles for conducting and evaluating interpretive field studies in information systems.” In particular, the “hermeneutical process” principle that acknowledges that all human understanding is built by a constant consideration of the interdependent meaning of the parts and the whole that they form was especially crucial for this research. To build an understanding of the whole dynamics of the NoP, we combined our observations at different levels (microobservations of practices, observations of social dynamics, and interviews of members of various entities) and we looked for evidence of relationships between the parts and the whole. Another example is the principle of “suspicion.” We developed constant doubt about our observations by contrasting the perspectives of different participants and stakeholders in the NoP.

## 4. Case Study

This section presents the context and practices of people working in the EH field of the FPHA. It shows how the implementation of a computer-based information system dedicated to the EH field changed the conditions of practices and the relations among participants in the EH practice.

## 4.1. Early History

As mentioned above, all EH members graduated from the National School of Public Health; they therefore all benefited from similar training and socialized early with some of their graduating peers. However, after graduation, most of the socialization and sense of common history among EH members took place at the level of local EH teams through common experiences and direct exchanges. Indeed, local teams were located throughout France and members of different teams did not have many occasions to discuss work-related issues or develop joint practices with distant peers.

In the late 1980s and early 1990s, some EH members started complaining that they did not have sufficient resources to get informed quickly about changes in important aspects of their work, such as changes in the law. EH members asked the general manager in charge of EH in the central headquarters of the FPHA for a paper-based magazine to publish EH-related information and experiences, regardless of their geographical location. The general manager did not initially provide financial resources for the creation of such a publication because the EH occupation was not a priority for the FPHA.

In the mid-1990s, however, André, an EH engineer who managed a local EH team and had worked in EH for the last 15 years, created a Web-based IS dedicated to EH issues. André did not originally receive any funding from the central headquarters to launch his initiative, but he worked initially with a small team of interns from the National School of Public Health. The team called itself the “network coordination unit” (NCU), and the IS was called the “environmental health exchange network” (EHEN). The EHEN became available in 1997 to all agents working in the FPHA who had intranet access but, with few exceptions, only EH members actually used the EHEN. At that time, as well, most EH members started to get a professional e-mail address. There was no previous history of systematic IT use for work by EH members.

## 4.2. The EHEN and Its Management

The NCU had initiated, launched, and continued to manage the EHEN from a local EH team. The EHEN soon became one of the most frequently accessed systems of the FPHA. The success of the EHEN allowed André to quit his original position as manager of a local team to become, officially, manager of the NCU. André still worked in the offices of the local team he used to manage, but he obtained some resources from the central headquarters to develop his initiative, which allowed him to hire two and then three EH members who had undergone information technology training.

Even though central management had granted official recognition and some financial and technical resources to manage the EHEN, its commitment to offering technological resources to the EH profession was not strong. In particular, for the first two to three years of the EHEN, members of many local EH teams worked on desktops that did not have Internet access and therefore could not browse the EHEN from their workstations. Instead, they could only access the EHEN through a shared desktop with Internet access, which limited their use of the EHEN.

Members of the NCU often visited the central headquarters of the FPHA. André, in particular, met with central managers about once a month to explain the purpose and interests of the EHEN. In his words:

You have to tell them [managers at the central headquarters], again and again, what the EHEN is and you have to show them how useful it is for EH members. You have to constantly show them that it is good for everyone in the [FPHA], if you want to continue getting funding for it.

These visits were useful to obtain more resources to manage the EHEN. In particular, the NCU was instrumental in the gradual provision of most local EH teams with browser-equipped desktops.

The grassroots origin of the EHEN assured its visibility among EH members and in the FPHA. EH members were aware of the EHEN system, and they knew that the system originated from a local initiative rather than from the central headquarters. André had publicized the initiative through the social network of peers he had developed over his 15-year tenure as an EH engineer and then manager. The EHEN became very popular; its usage logs were higher than other FPHA systems, even higher than the ones that represented big departments of the FPHA (e.g., the Department of Hospital Administration).

## 4.3. Contents and Uses of the EHEN

The EHEN provided an updated inventory of legal information related to the EH field. It also offered various official documentation and procedures. The most popular folder soon became the “best practices” one, where EH members narrated experiences on various issues (e.g., how to respond to outbreaks of salmonella in public schools). By 2003, more than 6,000 best practice entries had been put on line, providing a database of very concrete experiences useful to EH members. Table 2 summarizes the main contents of the EHEN.

Many EH members said that as they contributed to the EHEN, they wanted to become known by others beyond their local teams and to be viewed as potential experts in their field. ## 4.4. Gradual Adoption of the EHEN

It took a few years for most EH members to adopt the EHEN. Even after most local teams had become equipped with personal desktops (around 2,000), members of local EH teams did not automatically become homogeneous in their use and contributions to the EHEN. Some EH members were more proficient than others, as the following quote suggests:

I am not such a big user of the EHEN, because I never seem to find what I am looking for. I do not know why exactly. My colleagues tell me that it is a question of logic, of organization (- - -). I guess I do not have this logic to find information. My colleagues, yes, they do. There is especially one of them. He always helps me; he shows me how to use the EHEN.

The colleague mentioned in the previous quote was a newcomer in the respondent’s local team who had recently graduated from the National School of Public Health, while the respondent had been working in the local team for several years already. Overall, most respondents (newcomers and old timers in the EH practice) considered that newcomers tended to use the EHEN more often and with more proficiency than old timers and that old timers often needed help from newcomers on how to use the EHEN. Some old timers also considered that they did not need the EHEN to do their regular tasks and complained that the EHEN made it “too easy” for the newcomers and changed the way work was done.

## 4.5. Becoming a Participant in the EH Practice

Most new EH members had developed a habit of using the EHEN during their training period, when they first learned about the EH field. They continued using it after they started working in a local EH team. An EH member who started working in 2004 noted:

I use the EHEN all the time. It is like a second nature to me. I cannot imagine how we [EH members] used to do without it.

Access to and use of the EHEN during the formal EH training phase contributed to making the practices related to the EHEN “second nature” for new EH members. The EHEN became available to all students of the National School of Public Health one year after its implementation. They relied on the use of the EHEN during their formal training and this contributed to the subsequent integration of the use of the system in new graduates’ regular work practices. The EHEN provided an experiential database at a time when, as students or newcomers in a local team, EH agents had not yet become fully engaged in professional practices and their knowledge of their field remained mostly academic. EH members who had gotten used to accessing the EHEN when introduced to a new topic at the training school continued browsing the EHEN regularly after graduation, as an employee explained.

What I am looking for on the EHEN depends upon the circumstances I am in (- - -). Sometimes, it [browsing the EHEN] can help for more complicated things, on specific issues on which we do not know much yet or for which we still have to experiment at the local level. The EHEN is an excellent source of inspiration in such cases.

These newcomers relied on the EHEN to work and they talked about it with their local colleagues. This word-of-mouth dissemination greatly influenced the overall uptake in use of the EHEN.

## 4.6. Boosting the EH NoP

The NCU had named the system the “environmental health exchange network” and soon most EH agents talked about the EHEN to refer indistinguishably to the system and to all members of the EH practice, regardless of their location. An EH member noted:

Before the EHEN, the concept of a network of exchange of experiences existed, but in an informal and limited way. We knew a few people, and we knew that some of them were specialists in field so and so. But it was so time-consuming to figure out who would give you the best advice on a specific topic. And sometimes, we just did not find them, because we did not know enough people. Given our professional field, complex and constantly changing, it is good to have the EHEN and to know what the others are doing and to be able to refer to them, wherever they are.

This quote suggests that the availability and use of the EHEN strengthened EH members’ feeling that they participated in similar practices and that they were related to other EH members beyond geographical distance. This quote also suggests that EH members appreciated that the EHEN offered them the opportunity to find peers who could help them deal with potentially any type of issue they faced. EH agents considered that the EHEN broadened their network of potential relations. The following quote also illustrates this idea:

Thanks to the EHEN, I found somebody who was extremely competent in food poisoning. I found him thanks to the tools that he had put online, and I contacted him. The EHEN is good for this, especially since we do not have any general index of competences in EH and since we are in dispersed services with many people who have various knowledge and experience, dispersed throughout the country.

## Another EH member commented:

The intranet and the e-mail have changed the way I work. Today, I feel much closer and more related to my colleagues from other regions of the country. I have access to more experiences from colleagues from other local services. To me, there is a much greater solidarity among our teams. Also, accessing information and other things improves the practices of our team.

This EH member considered that the broadening of his network of relations with EH peers transformed (and, in his view, “improved”) local practices.

## 4.7. Changing EH Practices and the EHEN

The EHEN also stimulated changes in the practices of local teams. For instance, traditionally, an important activity of EH teams was to update the information base on EH law and regulations. With the EHEN, the NCU searched for news on EH law, sent newsletters via e-mails, and posted the changes on the EHEN. There was no need any more for members of each local CoP to look for the same information. A new routine performed by members of the NCU replaced the multiplicity of practices of local teams. An EH agent noted:

What is very good, and important, in the EHEN, is this idea of sharing things. See, for instance, the Official Bulletin. Well, it has been a long time since I have had to read it, because I know that our legal news is going to be on the e-mail and the intranet (- - -). There is no need to reinvent the wheel.

This example illustrates that local teams increasingly and routinely relied on global resources. It suggests an increasing complementarity between what was done by members of the NCU and by local teams to avoid mere replication of practices.

The gradually generalized use of the EHEN also contributed to changes in the regional meetings that took place periodically among peers from different local teams in charge of similar issues (e.g., swimming pools, asbestos, etc.). More and more of these meetings were prepared and organized around documents that were published on the EHEN. After these meetings, minutes and other documents were often posted on the EHEN and electronic discussions were triggered.

Furthermore, as noted above, the EHEN made it possible for EH members to extend their own personal network of relations according to the issues they dealt with. New circles of EH members who had experienced similar issues sometimes formed. Informal initiatives started taking place among such EH members who had often met online through their mutual interest in a common topic. The NCU provided online resources to encourage these initiatives and new rubrics of the EHEN reflected these resources, such as e-mail lists, shared agendas, and shared folders. A member of the NCU commented:

There are so many topical issues we have to deal with in EH, from mad cow disease to summertime pollution level peaks or urban noise pollution - - - With IT, agents from everywhere are coming together to exchange ideas. They usually meet around a best practice put online. They start to exchange ideas via e-mail, and then they organize phone conferences. Once they have advanced their reflection and experiences on this issue, they contribute to the EHEN as well.

## 5. Trans-Situated Learning and Its Information Infrastructure

This section interprets observations from the EH case in relation to concepts of practice-based learning, NoPs, and information infrastructures. This discussion enables us to formulate a model of what we call “trans-situated” learning supported by an information infrastructure. We label as trans-situated the processes that emerge from the sharing of accounts of related practices without sharing the same context of work, in contrast to the traditional model of situated learning. Building on the literatures on information infrastructures and standardization (Bowker and Star 1999, Star and Ruhleder 1996, Timmermans and Berg 1997), we also consider the conditions in which an information infrastructure emerges and supports these transsituated learning processes. Figure 1 summarizes our model. The following paragraphs describe its elements in further detail. ## 5.1. Trans-Situated Learning Processes

Trans-situated learning refers to practice-based learning dynamics that are not limited by the bounded context of CoPs, as in the canonical descriptions of situated learning. Such learning processes may emerge throughout NoPs, i.e., among people who share a certain degree of similarity in their practices but who lack the ability to interact directly with each other on a regular basis. Based on evidence from the case study, we identify two interdependent learning processes: one of opening up of local CoPs and one of increasing proximity throughout the NoP.

5.1.1. Opening Up the Dynamics of CoPs. As described earlier, situated learning in CoPs comes from the processes of legitimate peripheral participation (LPP) through which newcomers to a practice learn their trade by becoming gradually more fully engaged in it and by interacting with old timers. The literature has pointed to two main tendencies of CoPs that limit their learning potential: Their members are prone to power struggles that can impair their ability to change, and they can become closed, which restricts their epistemic horizon. Our case study suggests that the availability of an information infrastructure may help widen their horizon and expand the resources available for local practitioners, which also affects processes of LPP.

Newcomers to CoPs lack actual experiences of work, but they are often more familiar with using information systems in ways that benefit their practices. In the case study, it was quite common for newcomers (who had used the system during their training) to teach old timers how to use the system, while old timers mentored newcomers on the routines of local CoPs. Dynamics of this kind stimulate the adoption of an IS but they also affect the conditions of centrality and peripherality in CoPs. Some old timers and newcomers experience a trade-off between experience on the job and experience using the system. The availability of the information infrastructure thus changes what is knowledgeability and expertise in the practice at the risk of making the expertise of some old timers appear outdated at times.

Overall, however, the relationships between old timers and newcomers in our case supported the gradual adoption of the computer-based system into the regular course of work practices, and therefore opened up local CoPs to the practices of other CoPs. This opening of CoPs leads members to expand their “epistemic horizon” as accounts of practices circulate, and allows participants in the practice to relate their situations and practices to that of other practitioners beyond their immediate circle of socialization. Such availability leads to local adjustments and reinventions of practices based on the reflexive use of information gathered from the information infrastructure according to the local situations at hand.

5.1.2. Increasing Proximity in an NoP. The increasing access to other CoPs’ practices has an overall effect on the NoP by extending the sense of proximity among participants in the practice. This sense of proximity relies on an increased ability to share resources and engage with peers throughout the NoP, regardless of location.

For resources shared throughout an NoP to alter practices, they have to be relevant, reliable, and useful. The relevance of such resources in our case study was achieved by having an IS whose contents were solely dedicated to EH practice. Their reliability and usefulness are more complex issues. In an NoP with no institutional recognition, contributions to the IS are most likely to be voluntary, with no extrinsic reward for contributions and with no systematic quality control of contributions. Such a context seems a priori unlikely to stimulate good contributions. However, the case study revealed the significance of contributors’ self-selection as a spontaneous quality control mechanism. The KM literature has shown that part of what motivates people to contribute to a KMS is their willingness to enhance their reputation (Kankanhalli et al. 2005, McLure Wasko and Faraj 2005). The case study showed that in the context of an NoP whose members are all familiar with particular practices, such willingness can work as a self-selection mechanism: Contributors spontaneously filter their own potential contributions to avoid demonstrating what they fear may be considered trivial or inaccurate knowledge. Quality control is exerted by contributors as they seek to establish themselves as experts in their practice.

The shared resources that are produced by selfappointed authorities in particular practices constitute the foundation for a “shared repertoire” available not only in particular CoPs but throughout the NoP. Such a shared repertoire provides a guide for action in different situations: It helps transfer “best practices” across CoPs and it provides a repository for the language of the practice. Being familiar with this language is a condition of participation in practice, so these shared resources both affect practices and support a sense of membership in the NoP, hence, further stimulating use of the IS when members are facing new or unusual problems.

The sharing of resources related to practices can also trigger new communications among participants in the practice who would have previously lacked the opportunity to interact. The literature on situated learning has shown the importance of members of CoPs interacting face to face, if only occasionally, to discuss their practices (e.g., Orr 1990). In an NoP, the sharing of electronic resources creates an awareness of other participants in the practice and offers a means of making new contacts among participants involved in some specific aspects of the practice. As seen in the case study, these contacts may be initially established through e-mail but they may also lead to further encounters and richer exchanges in telephone or face-to-face meetings. As they communicate through various means on a regular basis, new informal groups create solutions to deal with specific aspects of a practice. Moreover, some members of these groups assume the position of experts. Hence, the increased sense of proximity in the NoP can prompt the emergence of new CoPs and their own learning dynamics.

## 5.2. The Emergence of an

## Information Infrastructure

The two processes described above, of opening up the dynamics of CoPs and of increasing proximity in NoPs, were distinguished mostly for analytical purposes but they are deeply interdependent in practice. In what follows, we build on the literatures on information infrastructures and emerging standards to understand how these processes of trans-situated learning are supported by the emergence of an information infrastructure for the NoP and condition its emergence.

5.2.1. The Local Universality of an Information Infrastructure. It would seem less problematic to use an IS to share accounts of experiences and practicerelated information across communities of related practices, i.e., in an NoP, than across communities of widely differing practices; the “boundary objects” linking these CoPs are not built on an initial vacuum of joint practices (Levina and Vaast 2005, Star and Griesemer 1989). Yet, common practice-related interests do not necessarily guarantee that global resources will be shared throughout the NoP and used in local practices. To become part of an emerging information infrastructure supporting the learning dynamics of an NoP, an IS has to become part of local practices while also being general enough to provide global resources. In this sense, resources shared throughout an NoP, through the use of an IS, have to be universal (i.e., the same for anyone in the NoP), but they also have to become appropriated and used locally according to the specifics of the challenges faced by members of local CoPs in a way that echoes Timmermans and Berg’s (1997) notion of “local universality.” Timmermans and Berg (1997) introduced this notion to discuss standardization of medical practices, but we believe that it applies especially well to the delicate context of implementation and use of an information infrastructure.

The way technology is implemented and becomes used greatly affects whether it can bring this local universality to the information infrastructure (Bowker and Star 1999). Two archetypal caveats of implementation of technology-based infrastructural systems are that they impose global contents that bear insufficient relation to local needs (especially when the implementation process is “top down”) or they fail to provide global resources and keep the local entities separate (which can happen when the implementation process is mostly “bottom up”) (Ciborra 2000c, Newell et al. 2001, Rolland and Monteiro 2002).

In the context of an NoP, balancing the implementation process between local and global and top down and bottom up is made more or less problematic according to the strength of the institutional representation of the NoP and the closeness of links among members of the NoP. In the case of the EHEN, the EH NoP originally had a weak institutional representation (illustrated in the lack of resources initially devoted to the EHEN initiative), but members of the EH NoP were relatively tightly knit: they all graduated from the same school and therefore received highly similar training and all worked for the same organization. Such an auspicious situation made it possible to have an implementation process that struck a balance between the local and the global; the top down and the bottom up. Thus, observations from the case allow us to suggest some key conditions for the emergence of local universality of an information infrastructure: a certain degree of similarity in practices, an initial similar organizational or occupational affiliation, and an information infrastructure grounded in practices.

5.2.2. The Embeddedness of Infrastructures. Star and Ruhleder (1996) noted that a defining dimension of information infrastructures is that they are embedded with other infrastructures. Our observations corroborate this statement and show that the local universality of the information infrastructure encouraged its embeddedness with other infrastructures of relevance to the NoP.

The significance of such embeddedness can be illustrated by the earlier years reported in the case study. Because of a lack of institutional resources for the

NoP, the system was not at first available on the desktops of all EH members. To use the system, they had to interrupt their regular activities to go to a shared desktop. This created a discontinuity in practices because of a gap between the system and the regular material infrastructure of work. On the other hand, over time the system became available at the training school (training infrastructure) and most desktops became equipped with Web browsers (material infrastructure of work), which triggered the embeddedness of the information infrastructure with other infrastructures of relevance to the practice.

Such embeddedness further transformed the conditions of practice. This was illustrated as the system became available during the training years of future participants in the EH practice. The original conception of situated learning offers a critical view of training as too often disconnected from actual practices (Lave 1988, Lave and Wenger 1991). In the present case study, though, the availability of the system contributed to bridging the gap between training and practice: It provided students with accounts of the “real world” practices that awaited them. It also generated in students a habit of using the system when, after graduation, they encountered difficult issues at work. The embeddedness of the training infrastructure and the information infrastructure of the NoP thus stimulated graduates in continuing to use the system and created a deep association between such uses and work practices. This research therefore reveals a dynamic reciprocal relationship between practices, the information infrastructure of the NoP, and its embeddedness with other supporting infrastructures. The emergent embeddedness of supporting infrastructures stimulated the adoption of the information infrastructure of the NoP. In turn, the wider adoption of the NoP information infrastructure further reinforced its embeddedness with other infrastructures of work, which then affected practices and learning dynamics.

## 6. Conclusion

The research reported in this paper investigated the practice-based learning processes that can emerge as people who work on related issues but do not share an actual context of work can communicate and exchange accounts of experiences through an information system. Because these people are separated by geographical, organizational, or temporal distance, their practices have a lesser degree of similarity than they would have in the canonical model of situated learning. Based on the examination of a case study and the existing literatures on practices, situated learning, and information infrastructures, we have proposed a model of trans-situated learning that extends the practice-based learning model to the computermediated context of a NoP.

As our research relied on the interpretation of a single in-depth case study, it did not seek statistical generalizability but, rather, aimed to generalize from empirical interpretations to conceptual statements (Lee and Baskerville 2003, Walsham 1995). In this regard, the main theoretical contribution of this paper is to propose a model of practice-based learning that does not rely solely on the stringent conditions of situated learning. Such a model is especially relevant given the conditions of today’s work environment, characterized by simultaneous trends of pressure on individual and community competences, increasing distribution of work, and heightened use of electronic media for communication and information storage. In addition to the main contribution, our paper also speaks to the literature on the role of IS as information infrastructures because it discusses the delicate conditions of emergence of the local universality of an information infrastructure and its embeddedness with other infrastructures supporting these trans-situated learning processes (Ciborra 2000b, Rolland and Monteiro 2002, Timmermans and Berg 1997).

The proposed model of trans-situated learning and its support by an information infrastructure certainly does not exhaust the complexity of learning processes in NoPs. Our research provides only one contribution toward extending the practice-based literature to more varied contexts of work and practices, and we would welcome further examinations of these processes. In particular, we are aware of the specifics of the context of our case study as the members of the EH NoP benefited from favorable conditions that made their relations quite tightly knit (e.g., they all graduated from the same institution and worked for the same organization). Future research could investigate how this model of trans-situated learning holds when the members of an NoP are not as tightly knit and when, arguably, practices vary more across CoPs.

In addition, the concept of trans-situated learning could provide a useful lens to help in understanding important trends such as open source development (Lee and Cole 2003, Von Krogh 2002), social networking, and collaborations through Wiki applications. In particular, our model suggests specific processes related to participation dynamics, access to others’ practices, resource sharing, and engagement with peers that may be of high relevance to the social learning dynamics of these new contexts. Further research could usefully refine the proposed model to understand how learning emerges in these computer-mediated environments, where participants jointly build their information infrastructure while interacting and developing their competences.
