# A hedonic study of network effects in the market for word processing software

Sujoy Chakravarty<sup>a,\*</sup>, Kutsal Dogan<sup>a,\*</sup>, Nels Tomlinson<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, 2601 N. Floyd Road, SM 33, Richardson, TX 7508, USA <sup>b</sup>Alaska Department of Labor and Workforce Development, Research and Analysis Section, Juneau, AK 99802, USA

Available online 17 May 2005

## Abstract

We investigate the effects of various quality attributes and network-specific features on the price of word processing software using a hedonic price framework in order to assess the effect of the network variable on the quality-adjusted price. We find a positive effect of network externalities on price in this market that is similar to markets for spreadsheet and web server software as shown previously (Brynjolfsson and Kemerer [E. Brynjolfsson, and C. Kemerer Network externalities in microcomputer software: an econometric analysis of the spreadsheet market, unpublished draft, MIT Sloan School of Management, (1997)]; Gallaugher and Wang [J.M. Gallaugher and Y. Wang, Understanding network effect in software markets: evidence from web server pricing, MIS Quarterly 26 (4), (Dec. 2002), 303–327]; Gandal [N. Gandal, Hedonic price indexes for spreadsheets and an empirical test for network externalities, Rand Journal of Economics 25 (1) (Spring 1994)]). As observed in other software pricing studies, we find strong brand effects among word processors. However, quality attributes do not significantly impact price in this market. This is contrary to the evidence from studies of spreadsheet software. Furthermore, in contrast to the evidence from spreadsheets, prices adjusted for quality show an upward trend over time in the market for word processing software.

## 1. Introduction

This paper examines the effect of an installed base of consumers on the pricing of DOS word processing software. These network effects are measured by using a hedonic pricing framework, which regresses product price on several product attributes, one of which is the installed base. Network externalities<sup>1</sup> arising out of a product or service are benefits provided to consumers due to the already existing pool of users of the commodity or a complimentary product. A market is said to exhibit network externalities when the value of owning a unit increases with each successive unit sold. A simple example of an <sup>b</sup>installed base<sup>Q</sup> of consumers providing benefit for later arriving consumers occurs in the use of electronic mail or telephones (Katz and Shapiro [26]).

A related topic that has received a lot of attention in recent years is that of switching costs. When a consumer makes a primary investment in a specific product, he creates economies of scope from the purchase of a compatible product or (especially) a repeat purchase of the same product. This also means he incurs a cost from switching to a different (or noncompatible) product and values compatibility, i.e., the ability to take advantage of the same investment between his purchases. Network effects arise out of the external benefits that arise between different consumer’s purchases, and thus compatibility with others’ purchases is required to take advantage of these economies.

The concepts of switching costs and network externalities are often interrelated. High switching costs may lead to a large installed base of users of a product, which in turn may lead to network effects for a potential adopter. This interrelatedness has produced some of the very important case studies in product adoption. The primary motivation for the empirical work that follows is from the literature in network effects, i.e., economies of scope that arise from compatibility with other adopters in the market.

In the examples of the telephone and e-mail, the benefits provided are almost all network related. In many other products, network benefits may be over and above the intrinsic utility provided by them. Software products conform to this category. For example, in Spreadsheets the network benefit of being able to share files with another user is an addition to the primary use of spreadsheets: to store and process a large amount of data. Network externalities may lead to a <sup>b</sup>lock in<sup>Q</sup>, where due to path dependence or <sup>b</sup>historical accident<sup>Q</sup> an inferior product may emerge as the market standard even though superior alternatives may be available (David [13]; Arthur [2–4]). Lopatka and Page [33] and Fudenberg and Tirole [18] assert that Microsoft has in fact used network externalities in order to become market leader in several software markets by writing application software which are compatible with (and thus tapping into the network of) the most used operating system in the world—Windows, also made by them. Though it is open to contention whether this is indeed a case of a lock in (i.e., Microsoft products are indeed inferior), it cannot be denied that computer markets provide us with some of the strongest examples of network effects. In March of 2004, an Antitrust ruling by the European Union found Microsoft guilty of monopolistic practices in bundling its Windows Media Player with its Windows operating system, thus not allowing access to its network programs written by other windows media software vendors such as Real Networks. The ruling carries with it a fine of \$613 million and reminds the world that network related market dominance is very much an issue that continues to concern policymakers and regulators [45]. David [13], Farrell et al. [16] and Tirole [42] postulate that consumers may have stuck to the QWERTY<sup>2</sup> keyboard even though more advanced keyboard designs were available, because of the high cost of relearning new keystrokes.<sup>3</sup>

Notions of path dependence and unstable adoption dynamics that form the genesis of the literature on network effects and switching costs make for far less autarkic decision-making than in conventional neoclassical economics. History matters in a big way in this <sup>b</sup>new economics<sup>Q</sup>, and this puts a greater weight on the institutions that make the initial choices. Day [14] studies the emergence of chaos or highly erratic fluctuations in economic growth processes that occur endogenously through the interplay of technology, preferences and behavioral rules, with exogenous interference of stochastic shocks. The growth paths in such situations are highly unstable, so that small perturbations in parameters or initial conditions can lead to large divergences in the trajectories observed. Arthur [2,3,4] develops the idea of path dependence and positive feedbacks as seen in economics and formalizes the ideas of historical accidents as modeled by stochastic processes that often cause markets to exhibit <sup>d</sup>tipping<sup>T</sup> in the direction of a certain locked in technologies or products. Gladwell [22] uses the same idea in his popular bestseller <sup>d</sup>The Tipping Point<sup>T</sup>, as do Von Burg [43] and Frank and Cook [17]. All of these works study the <sup>d</sup>winner-takeall<sup>T</sup> phenomena embodied by these markets that exhibit tipping, such as telephone services and operating systems.

Katz and Shapiro [26] note that network externalities can take various forms. A direct physical effect that increases the benefit to a user from an increase in the total number of people who buy the product<sup>4</sup> and indirect effects arising from the greater availability of complimentary products, or enhanced after sales service networks resulting from growth of the installed base. In their dynamic oligopoly framework, multiple fulfilled expectation equilibria occur which support the intuition that if consumers expect a seller to be dominant, they will pay more for his product and he will become dominant. This intuition is carried over to Katz and Shapiro [27] where property rights are deemed significant in the adoption of products in the presence of network externalities. Adoption equilibria are derived under different patterns of product <sup>b</sup>sponsorships<sup>Q5</sup>, some of which are inefficient and support the <sup>b</sup>lock-in<sup>Q</sup> argument of technology adoption. Katz and Shapiro [28] examine the introduction of a new product into a market with network externalities. They find that contrary to the belief that such markets display excessive inertia; under certain conditions there is a tendency to rush into new incompatible technologies. Choi and Thum [10] extend Katz and Shapiro [27] and analyze the relationship between market structure and the timing of technology in the presence of network externalities. They find that under conditions of both competition and monopoly, early adopters are impatient and tend to adopt even when it is socially suboptimal to do so. In the case of monopoly, this is further exacerbated as the consumers realize that the monopolist will ex-post appropriate that any surplus they may receive by waiting. In this case, it may be useful to consider licensing as a commitment device as explored in Farrell and Gallini [15] and Shepard [40]. Church and Gandal [11] address the issue of technology adoption in the presence of complimentary network externalities and show that when firms compete in price, the hardware technology that is adopted is often the one for which software development cost is minimum, even though it may be socially optimal to adopt the other technology.

A limitation of this literature is that even though there is a substantial amount of theoretical work, there has been very little empirical validation of the theory. Gandal [20] constructs hedonic price indices for spreadsheet software and concludes that the market has significant network effects, as consumers are willing to pay a premium for spreadsheets tha are compatible with the LOTUS file format. Saloner and Shepard [38] test for the existence of network externalities in the adoption of automated teller machines (ATMs) by banks. The larger the network of users of these machines the more a bank may gain by adopting this technology. Since the network size is unobservable, the number of branches of a bank is used as a proxy variable. The study indicates the presence of significant network externalities in this sector. Gandal [21] is a study on the effect of complimentary network externalities arising from the users of spreadsheet programs and database management systems, as users often need to exchange data files between the two. Brynjolfsson and Kemerer [6] extend Gandal [20] and directly measure the effect of an installed base of users on price of spreadsheet software in a hedonic regression model and conclude that the market exhibits significant network externalities. Gallaugher and Wang [19] show the existence of positive network externalities in web server pricing, a market that is based largely on open standards. Chakravarty [8a,b] examine the Katz and Shapiro [27] product adoption model in an experimental setting. The qualitative predictions of the theory are borne out in the analysis but the outcomes are not consistent with the point predictions of the theory.

This study contributes to the empirical validation of network externalities literature by examining the market for computer word processor software and testing for the presence of network externalities in this market. Accordingly, hedonic price indices are constructed using a set of product attributes and the installed base for a product.

Reports titled Word Processing Market Review & Forecast issued by the International Data Corporation (IDC) provide the price and quantity data. Expert reviews from Infoworld provide attribute data for the different word processors. Sections 2–4 describe the market for word processors and the hedonic methodology we employ and our conceptual model. Sections 5 and 6 discuss data sources, data collection and some difficulties that we encountered in conducting this analysis. Sections 7–9 describe the analysis, present some conclusions that we draw from our results and outline some future research that we may undertake in this area.

## 2. The market for word processing software

Word processing software like most other software products are subject to strong network effects due to the need for users to share files in the same format (or compatible format). There is a notion among researchers and the media that software prices are often more inflated than they should be [9]. They allege that software companies make use of their installed base, to extract a premium from their customers.<sup>6</sup> The marginal cost of an additional unit of software once the source code is written is very low. Thus over the years there has been a downward trend in software prices. However, in spite of this downward trend, observers feel that prices for certain software products tend to be relatively sticky due to the presence of captive networks of users who continue to use them in order to avoid losses in productivity by switching to cheaper products. This is especially true if the number of current users for these products is very large.

Lopatka and Page [33] identify network externalities as the main source of Microsoft’s monopoly power. Microsoft sells applications software in competition with independent software vendors. However, it also has a large installed base of customers for its operating system MS-DOS and it’s add on graphical user interface known as Windows. This, they assert, has allowed the software giant to tap into this network by making its applications software compatible primarily with Microsoft operating systems, thus adversely affecting market shares of the other independent software providers.

In 1986, Corel’s WordPerfect and Micropro’s WordStar were the market leaders and had a slightly higher market share than the other major programs: DisplayWrite (IBM), Microsoft Word (MSWord), MultiMate (Ashton-Tate) and Samna Word (Samna). With the introduction of the graphical user interface Windows 3.0 in 1989, and the shift away from DOS applications to those that worked on Windows, there was a regime shift and MSWord for Windows has dominated the marketplace ever since with Ami Professional (which was the renamed Samna Word for Windows after Lotus bought Samna in the early 1990s) showing good growth but never challenging the market leader MSWord. WordPerfect came up with a version of its product for Windows in 1991 but by then Microsoft had firmly established itself in the word processor market.

So why did MSWord win the word processor wars? It won due to a combination of reasons. Firstly, MSWord for Windows had more/better quality features built into it than its competitors, and its WYSIWYG<sup>7</sup> interface with the integration of the mouse was easier to use than the DOS-based approach, which used complicated combinations of the function keys on the keyboard (Lombardi [31,32]). Secondly, Microsoft bundled MSWord with its popular spreadsheet program EXCEL and its presentation builder PowerPoint in its Office Suite. This effectively widened the network of each program, as it was possible to import objects like tables, charts and graphics from one to another. Lastly, the Windows based word processors were not perfectly backwards compatible with their DOS era predecessors. In order to upgrade to them, consumers would have to sacrifice some functionalities of the older interfaces. Since this caused considerable loss in network benefits it may have weakened their resolve to stick to the same vendor. So at the time (late 1980s) when many users were rethinking adoptions, MSWord was for a long time the best all round product available to users.

## 3. The Hedonic Pricing framework

The basic idea in the Hedonic Pricing model is that goods are different bundles of attributes or characteristics, and the demand for a good is really a demand for all the attributes it contains. For most products that we use, the price paid is for the overall (bundle of attributes) good and it is almost impossible to gauge the influence of a certain product attribute on price. Hedonic Pricing allows us to separate out the degree of influence of each such attribute on price by regressing the market price on a number of product characteristics.

Griliches [23,24] and Rosen [37] provide the basic hedonic pricing model. Berndt et al. [5] construct quality-adjusted price indices for personal computers in the US and conclude that taking quality changes into account has significant impact on the pattern of price changes for PCs. Griliches [23] and Arguea et al. [1] apply the Hedonic framework to automobile industry data, i.e., they estimate the impact of quality variables like horsepower and open road miles per gallon on the market price. The Arguea et al. [1] study uses shadow prices from the hedonic price regression to estimate a demand– supply system of characteristics. It also tackles the issue of appropriateness of the linear form of the Hedonic equation by considering the Ramsey [34] RESET and the Sargan [39] tests for the linear versus a semilogarithmic specification. The Hedonic approach has been used on a wide range of products and services, both in the high tech industry as well as topics as diverse as marriage dowries [35] and apple prices and product quality characteristics in British Columbia [7].

In this study, we construct a Hedonic model to determine the effect on the price of word processors of three broad types of product characteristics.

1. Change in installed base or the network externality attributes of the ith product in period $t ~ ( N _ { i t } )$

2. Product features or attributes for the ith program in period $t \left( F _ { i t } \right)$

3. A time trend $( T _ { i t } ) _ { : }$ , which measures the effect of technological progress or other factors besides the network effect on quality-adjusted price. Year specific dummy variables may also be used in order to take this time trend into account.

$$
P _ {i t} = \phi (N _ {i t}, F _ {i t}, T _ {i t}, \varepsilon_ {i t})\tag{1}
$$

The error term $\left( \varepsilon _ { i t } \right)$ is assumed to be normally distributed, $N ( 0 , \sigma ^ { 2 } )$ . We consider a linear specification (as a robustness check we also consider a loglinear specification, which yields consistent results). We should expect to find that $N _ { i t }$ positively influences price, if indeed the market is characterized by network externalities. $F _ { i t }$ should positively affect price regardless of whether significant network externalities are present or not.

## 4. Conceptual model and hypotheses

We postulate that there are five sets of variables that affect the price of a DOS-based word processor product. These factors include the installed base of the product, strategic choices made by the vendor of the product, quality of the product, finally whether a Windows version of the product coexisted in the market from the same vendor. In addition to these four, the model also includes a time variable to account for changes in software prices over time and other factors that can be attributed to time.

As discussed earlier, the installed base of the product is expected to influence the price of the product positively. The larger the installed base the higher the price that vendor can charge. This hypothesis is consistent with that explored in other software studies like Chaudhury et al. [9], Gallaugher and Wang [19], Gandal [20,21] and Brynjolfsson and Kemerer [6]. Similar to installed base, quality attributes will also affect the price positively. Higher quality products will be priced higher as they are valued more highly by customers. If the same vendor had the Windows version of its product in the market, it would be expected that it would price its DOS product lower. This strategy of selling older software for cheaper may not just be to exhaust inventories but also to give people a chance to upgrade from an older version to a newer one rather than buying the newer version. The consumer pays less than what he/she would pay to buy the new Windows product and the vendor looks at a potential addition to his installed base.<sup>8</sup> Our three expectations are summarized by 1 hypotheses 2 hypotheses 3 given below:

Hypothesis 1. Installed base of a DOS-based word processor product influences the price of the product positively.

Hypothesis 2. Prices of DOS-based word processors increase as their quality is improved.

Hypothesis 3. If a Windows version of a DOS-based word processor is available in the market, the DOS word processor is priced lower.

The strategic preferences of software vendors are expected to influence the price of their word processors. We account for this using fixed effect intercepts corresponding to the different programs. However, our model does not suggest any direction for this effect. It is expected that different vendors will have differing pricing strategies. We model these differences without a prior. The Time variable is also modeled without a proposed direction. The average prices for most programs unadjusted for quality and network attributes show an initial rise followed by a leveling out and then a decline. These average prices are shown in Fig. 4 for the period 1987–1994.

## 5. Data

Our data set consists of price and quantity-shipped data on a number of proprietary word processors, and some data on the characteristics of these word processors that were gleaned primarily from the expert reviews in Infoworld.<sup>9</sup>

## 5.1. Word processors used in this study

We have used information on six major DOS word processors, for the period from 1987 to 1991.<sup>10</sup> They are Display Write (IBM), Microsoft WORD, Multimate (Ashton Tate), Samna Word, WordStar (Micropro) and Word Perfect. Their market shares for the sample years 1987 through 1994 are given in Fig. 1.

Though we have price and quantity data through 1994 (after which DOS word processors were not sold), we are not using the years 1992–1994 in our data analysis. During this period, all the vendors in our analysis had a Windows version of their product, which they were aggressively trying to position in the marketplace. The market for DOS products was also dwindling with the Windows interface growing rapidly, and a preliminary study of the data indicates that price may not be systematically related to product attributes during this regime shift and a hedonic framework may not be the appropriate way to investigate pricing behavior in this time frame. Fig. 2 shows the number of net adopters of the big three DOS programs from 1991 to 1994.

This number has been calculated by subtracting from the installed base of a software program in a given year, its installed base from 1 year ago. A negative number indicates that consumers have actually dropped out of the installed base; however, the reviews of program attributes remain as strong and in some cases unchanged from previous years. The price too does not vary consistently (some programs increase their average price, while others decrease their average price).<sup>11</sup> The packages analyzed in this study jointly account for over 95% of the DOS word processing market in each of the years. We have not included Windows (or Macintosh/OS2) based word processors for a number of reasons. First, including products that run on different operating platforms in the same regression is misleading, as adoption/pricing behavior may be markedly different in each regime.<sup>12</sup> Second, we do not have many observations for Mac/ OS2 programs in our data set (1986–1997). This would result in low error degrees of freedom for the hedonic analysis of the non-DOS packages done separately by platform. The availability of data (both market data and attribute data) on a substantial number of packages over several years prompted us to perform this analysis using DOS programs only. According to Liebowitz and Margolis [30], list prices do not include upgrade purchase prices, fail to account for units sold in software suites and the units sold to OEMs, which carry a far lower price. The average price, we have used in our research (as Liebowitz and Margolis [30] use in theirs), seems to be the best feasible estimator for a <sup>b</sup>market price<sup>Q</sup>. Fig. 4 graphs the average prices as obtained from dividing the total revenue by the number of units shipped.

From the quantity-shipped data, we construct the installed base for a program (which proxies for the degree of direct network externalities in this market) for each year, which is simply the total number of packages shipped for the preceding years in our database. Thus, since our price/quantity data begins in 1986, our analysis must begin with 1987, to give us a starting point for the installed base.

Since we begin by calling the 1986 sales <sup>b</sup>installed base<sup>Q</sup>, in the early years of our data set we are probably overestimating the installed base for products with smaller, growing market share, and underestimating the installed base for products with larger, stagnant market share and relatively small sales. A subtler and potentially more serious problem in calculating installed base as we do above (i.e., using quantities shipped) is that it does not separate upgrades from new purchases. All the companies appear to have sold their latest version to owners of their earlier versions for greatly reduced rates (list prices for an upgrade seems to typically have been about a quarter of the list price for a new purchase). Several of the word processors were sold as part of a computer-software bundle, as well as separately.

Finally, our installed base variable assumes that every copy of a package ever sold is still in use. For example, we are assuming that, in the later years, all copies of Display Write ever sold are still in use. This probably overstates its actual installed base, as some users of the program may have switched to another product or operating system. Thus the installed base, if accurately constructed, should contain only current users of a program.

The Installed Base Fraction (IBF) variable is constructed from the installed base variable. Installed Base Fraction is the share of the current installed base for a software program, expressed as a fraction of the total installed base. The IBF of program i in year j is given as

$$
\mathrm{IBF} _ {i, j} = \frac {\text { Installed   base } _ {i , j}}{\text { Total   installed   base } _ {j}}.
$$

The IBF variable addresses the problem of the aggregate installed base growing or shrinking over time. A spurt in the number of adopters of a certain software program may not provide any incentive on the part of a seller to increase price in the next period, if there is similar spurt in the growth in sales for all other vendors. Similarly, a drop in the number of units shipped may in some cases provide an incentive for a seller to raise his price if other software programs installed bases moved down at a faster rate. Thus, this variable, which tells us (approximately) what fraction of current users of word processors are using package i, provides a better proxy for the network effect than the pure number of users.

## 5.2.2. Other attribute data

We have nine quality attribute variables. We choose these particular attributes because they seem to be the most important quality variables according to the popular literature on word processing programs as supplied by trade periodicals like Infoworld and PC World. The attributes used are Basic Editing, Proofing Tools, Import/Export, Mail Merge, Macros, Layout, Documentation, Policies, and Technical. These are coded from 0 to 5, where 0 signifies that the feature is not present for the program and 5 signifies excellent. These represent the reviewer’s opinions on the relative merits of the various packages he reviewed during the year. Table 2 details these attribute variables and gives brief descriptions, whereas Table 3 gives summary statistics for each of them. ## 6. Difficulties

## 6.1. Price heteroskedasticity

The price data is quite heteroskedastic. The variance in price seems to peak around 1992. The standard deviation of price in that year is about double its standard deviation in 1987. Since this violates one of the fundamental assumptions of classical least squares regression, we use the reciprocals of the standard deviations of the dependent variable as weights in our estimation procedure.

## 6.2. Heterogeneity between brands

Each word processor has a very different median price, and a very different price history. Table 1 gives the median prices of the packages we work with. Over time, some increased their prices, some decreased their prices, and some showed no trend at all. See Fig. 4 for an illustration. Because of these different price levels, we needed to estimate separate intercepts for each word processor in a fixed effects regression model. We construct dummy variables for each brand of word processor for this purpose. See also Section 7 for further discussion on brand heterogeneity. We interact the time-trend variable with the brand dummies to provide a separate slope for each brand, but find that it does not change the results fundamentally. That more complex model is not reported here, since the added complexity does not add insight.

## 6.3. Multicollinearity

The quality attributes of the packages are all highly correlated. A program that scores highly in any one of the attributes is quite likely to score highly in most of the others as well. If we include all nine of the attribute variables in a regression, the condition number of the xVx design matrix is in the tens of thousands.<sup>14</sup> We turn to the principal components method to rectify this problem. This method calls for first finding the eigenvectors for the xVx matrix, then using them as weights to form linear combinations of the attributes. See Ripley and Venables [36]. The eigenvectors form an orthogonal basis for the attribute space. These may be used to construct <sup>b</sup>rotations<sup>Q</sup> (i.e., linear combinations) of the variables, which are orthogonal to one another. The k variables have k eigenvectors and k rotations, and thus we may construct up to k <sup>b</sup>synthetic<sup>Q</sup> variables, which do not suffer from multicollinearity.

This method is often used to reduce the dimensionality of a data set: rather than using all of the variables, it is common to work with only the rotations associated with the few largest eigenvalues. These contain most of the variation in the data, and thus most of the information. One disadvantage to this is that it is difficult (or impossible!) to interpret the coefficients on these synthetic variables. For more details regarding the principal components approach, see Johnson and Wichern [25]. As we are not as interested in the effects of individual quality attributes on price as we are with the effect of the IBF, we use the method of principal components and work with the rotation given by the first principal component only. This summarizes the majority of the information contained in the nine attribute variables in one variable, labeled first principal component, which we construct by using the first eigenvector of the matrix of attributes as weights to form a linear combination of the attributes.<sup>15</sup> Thus we are able to obtain a measure of variation in all the quality attributes without introducing multicollinearity.

We do not report the regressions with the unaggregated quality attributes. Our small sample size makes for very low error degrees of freedom and the high collinearity between the design variables results in making all coefficients statistically insignificant. The next section presents the specific forms of our estimating equations, our parameter estimates, and discusses our results in some detail.

## 7. Hedonic regression results

We estimated a set of models to find the best fit for the hedonic regression model given in Eq. (1). Models without brand specific dummies were studied initially but these models had low explanatory power and insignificant coefficients. These regressions have not been reported in this study. We then built the model with individual quality attribute variables in a linear specification with the installed base fraction and time variables. This linear model also included individual dummies for specific word processor vendors to account for fixed effects. As mentioned in an earlier section, this model suffered from low degrees of freedom and multicollinearity. Hence, the model resulted in mostly insignificant coefficient estimates. Much of the limited degrees of freedom we had were consumed by the individual attribute variables.

Second, we tried to group nine quality attributes into four subgroups<sup>16</sup> based on how related the attributes are to eliminate/reduce multicollinearity. Even though reducing the quality attributes to just four by aggregating some of the more obviously related ones eliminated some of the multicollinearity among these attributes, the resulting model had mostly insignificant coefficient estimates.

Then, we tried to use the first principal component as explained in Section 6.3 instead of individual attribute variables. Eq. (2) represents the model with the first principal component on quality attributes (FirstPC) that replaces the nine quality measures. Coefficients $a _ { 1 }$ through $a _ { 5 }$ are those for specific brand dummies $( a _ { 0 }$ represents Display-Write).<sup>17</sup> The variables IBF, WinVer (1 if a Windows version of the same program is on the market, 0 otherwise) and Time (the discrete time trend variable, 1987–1991) are the nonquality attributes for all versions of the hedonic price equation. The coefficient estimates and other test statistics results for the model given in Eq. (2) and the model given in Eq. (3) below are summarized in the first two columns of Table 4 for easy comparison.

$$
\begin{array}{r l} P _ {t} & = a _ {0} + a _ {1} \text {MSWord} + a _ {2} \text {MultiMate} + a _ {3} \text {Samna} \\ & + a _ {4} \text {WordStar} + a _ {5} \text {WordPerfect} + a _ {6} \text {FirstPC} \\ & + a _ {7} \text {IBF} + a _ {8} \text {WinVer} + a _ {9} \text {Time} + \varepsilon_ {t} \end{array}\tag{2}

We now have a model with no multicollinearity problem and satisfactorily significant coefficient estimates. For this regression, we see that the coefficient on IBF is positive and significant at the 5% level. Therefore, we find support for Hypothesis 1 in this data set. That is, the size of the network has a positive influence on price. Thus significant network effects characterize the market for DOS word processors, as they do for spreadsheets in the period 1987–1992 [21,6].

The dummies on WordStar and WordPerfect are significant at the 5% level and the dummy on Samna Word/Ami is significant at 10% level, indicating that their prices differ significantly from that of Display-Write, which is represented in the intercept. This means some of these programs are in distinct price/ attribute groups. We also find in this model that the coefficient on Time variable is positive and significant at the 5% level, indicating that the quality-adjusted price of word processing software is actually increasing over the years in our sample. Although the value of the coefficient for Time variable is relatively small in comparison to the value of the coefficient of IBF variable, even for a firm with 1% installed base (415.106-0.01=41.151 for the firm with 1% IBF versus 16.725 for Time), its effect on price is in contrast to the spreadsheet software market, where the quality-adjusted (real) price of programs declined.

The coefficient for the first principle component variable (FirstPC) is positive but insignificant indicating that quality attributes do not make a significant difference to the price set by a vendor of word processing software. This is an interesting result as it makes this market less quality driven than the market for spreadsheet programs analyzed by Gandal [21] and Brynjolfsson and Kemerer [6] and the market for web servers analyzed by Gallaugher and Wang [19]. According to their studies, non-network quality attributes are for most part significant determinants of the price prevailing in the market for spreadsheet software and in the market of web server software. In the DOS word processor market, quality attributes may not have had effect on prices. Based on the analysis in this study, we do not obtain support for the existence of a quality– price relationship as seen in conventional hedonic pricing studies. Even though this cannot be constituted as a proof, it provides support to the belief held by an expert<sup>18</sup> in the market, John V. Lombardi, that most of these word processors did pretty much the same thing. Even though they may have had some differential quality attributes, these attributes may not have been intrinsically valued by customers to a degree that would affect the pricing strategies of vendors, in comparison to the other factors that might affect the price. Admittedly, this is just a conjecture and cannot be tested with our current data set.

Finally, WinVer is negative but not significant in the model, indicating that the presence of a Windows version does not have a downward effect on the price of its DOS counterpart. Therefore, Hypothesis 3 cannot be confirmed with this model. In summary, with these estimates, we find support for Hypothesis 1. However, we fail to find support for Hypotheses 2 and 3. One reason for the failure for finding support for Hypothesis 3 would be the fact that these multiplatform firms, Microsoft, WordPerfect and WordStar, sold both DOS and Windows versions towards the end of the study period and also had large installed bases. Therefore, the installed base term may be picking the proposed effect of WinVer variable; a <sup>b</sup>product-line pricing<sup>Q</sup> or <sup>b</sup>exit-pricing<sup>Q</sup> phenomenon<sup>19</sup> in which a firm lowers the price of the previous generation product when a new generation is introduced into the marketplace. Furthermore, the installed base may affect the pricing strategy of multiplatform firms differently than that of single-platform firms. To test this and see if there is a difference between single-platform and multiplatform firms, we introduce an interaction term between WinVer and IBF variables.<sup>20</sup> The new model with the interaction variable is given in Eq. (3) below.

$$
\begin{array}{r l} P _ {t} & = a _ {0} + a _ {1} \text {MSWord} + a _ {2} \text {MultiMate} + a _ {3} \text {Samna} \\ & \quad + a _ {4} \text {WordStar} + a _ {5} \text {WordPerfect} + a _ {6} \text {FirstPC} \\ & \quad + a _ {7} \text {IBF} + a _ {8} \text {WinVer} + a _ {9} \text {Time} + a _ {1 0} \text {IBF} \\ & \quad \times \text {WinVer} + \varepsilon_ {t} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

The estimate for the model with interaction variable, given in Table 4, confirms the conjecture that the installed base term is picking up some of the effects of the windows variable. The inclusion of the interaction variable improved the statistical significance of individual coefficient estimates as well as improving the overall significance of the regression. This model has $R ^ { 2 }$ value of 0.87 (0.80 adjusted).

As discussed earlier, the values of coefficient estimates in this and the previous regression model cannot be interpreted as market prices directly as the values of the prices used in these regressions were deflated by the variances to eliminate/reduce heteroskedasticity. However, one can make ordinal judgments among different coefficients.

One important effect of the inclusion of the interaction variable is the ensuing increase in the value of the coefficient of the IBF variable. The coefficient increases almost 36%. This means the effect of network size for DOS word processor vendors on pricing strategy is more pronounced for single platform firms than it is for multiplatform firms. Multiplatform firms did exercise <sup>b</sup>exit-pricing<sup>Q</sup> and lowered their DOS processor prices as they started to introduce windows versions of their products. In the absence of the interaction variable, the effects of these cuts were picked up by the installed base variable.

Another result of the interaction variable is the observed increase in the significance levels of all of the coefficient estimates. Individual coefficient estimates do change somewhat but none of the estimates displays drastic value or sign alterations. Lastly, we check the robustness of these regression models given in Eqs. (2) and (3) by estimating them using semilogarithmic specification. The results of these models where the natural logarithm of deflated price values are used as the dependent variable are given in the last two columns of Table 4. Both these models result in improvement over their linear counterparts in terms significance levels and explanatory power. The $R ^ { 2 }$ value for the semilogarithmic model is 0.92 (adjusted $R ^ { 2 } { = } 0 . 8 7 )$ . The qualitative predictions of these logarithmic models are similar to that of the linear models.

## 8. Conclusions

The results from the hedonic fixed effects regressions presented in Section 7 illustrate that network size has a positive effect on price. This is in accordance with the finding of the prior network externalities literature. However, in the case of word processor programs quality attributes related to formatting, macros, import/export and technical policies do not significantly affect its price, in contrast to the other software markets analyzed in the literature like that for spreadsheet programs. One explanation for this may be the difference in the characteristics of users of these two software markets during the time frame for this study. While a spreadsheet program in the late 1990s could be thought of as more of a business tool, the same could not be said about the word processors of the same period. It may be safe to assume that word processors were more commonly used by relatively nontechnical users for personal writing needs. As these more casual consumers would be less likely to need/use all enhanced features of a software program, their purchase decisions might had been less affected by these features. This might have given the vendors of word processors more freedom in pricing their products without being constrained to reflect product quality. Incidentally, this could also lead to a stronger network effect and justify the increasing average prices in the period surveyed, even though DOS word processors were being phased out in favor of their Windows counterparts.

The regression results also hint that strong brand effects may characterize this market. Some software vendors’ prices differ significantly from others’, even after controlling for quality and nonquality attributes and adjusting for the upward trend in prices over time.

## 9. Looking ahead

This study is constrained by the lack of installed base data for the first 3 years of our study. This has given us no option but to use installed base data from later years and shipment data from earlier years to approximate the installed base. This is a serious shortcoming of this study. The period 1992–1994 was that of upheaval in this market, when a large proportion of consumers were shifting over from the DOS to the Windows platform. A regime shift of this sort tends to break down the hedonic relationship, as the motivations for price setting are often not systematically related to product features. A model may be estimated that investigates the effects of factors that are hypothesized to influence platform switch in the word processing software market. We feel that an important factor here would be the growth in the current base of users of the new (Windows) operating system. A high positive correlation between cross platform adoptions and the current growth of the new operating platform would indicate that consumers are forward looking and their purchases today reflect their desire to join the network of the market leader in the future. This would offer empirical support for Katz and Shapiro’s [26,27] theoretical models of technology adoption. Analyzing this regime shift may however be difficult on the market for DOS word processors, as we do not have many observations on products during the regime shift.

Finally, the word processor market, which is characterized by high number of packages, some with relatively short lifetimes, lends itself well to survival analysis. The semi-parametric Cox hazard regression model [12], which can be used to model the hazard rate as a function of several covariates may offer insights into the reasons why the market for DOS word processors, which was characterized by close to a dozen programs in the mid to late 1980s, had only three survivors by the mid 1990s: WordPerfect, MSWord and WordStar.
