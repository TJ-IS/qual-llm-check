# The dual role of penalty: The efects of IT outsourcing contract framing on knowledge-sharing willingness and commitment

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: IT outsourcing contract Service level agreement Bonus vs. penalty framing Knowledge-sharing willingness Commitment Experiment

## A B S T R A C T

Adding to the collective knowledge on how to craft efective IT outsourcing contracts, this study examines the efects of service level agreement (SLA) framing in terms of bonus versus penalty clauses on two important factors of outsourcing success: service providers' knowledge-sharing willingness and commitment (efort-related vs. afective). Results from an experiment with 198 IT professionals and novices show that penalty framing decreases knowledge-sharing willingness and affective commitment. while increasing effort-related commit: ment. Our experiment also controls for interaction efects between SLA framing and professional work experience. While we do not find disordinal interaction efects, we find some evidence that novices are afected more strongly by penalty contexts than more experienced IT professionals. Our results imply that the (bonus versus penalty) framing of SLAs can afect the success of IT outsourcing arrangements and that such framing decision require careful consideration of the specific outsourcing goals.

## 1. Introduction

IT outsourcing, or the act through which IT services are contracted to a third-party provider, continues to be a growing trend worldwide [1,2]. Since the late 1980s, the global IT outsourcing market has in creased more than thirty-fold to a \$344 billion market in 2013 with an estimated annual growth rate of 4.8% through to the end of 2018 [1,2]. However, “in spite of the continuous increase in IT outsourcing activities globally, a significant percentage of outsourcing deals are considered either a failure or sufered from serious problems” ([3], p. 859). In this context, existing research highlights the importance of “sound contracts” as a key success factor for IT outsourcing arrangements (e.g., [4–7]).

Prior literature on IT outsourcing contracts has accumulated a rich body of knowledge on the determinants of contract choices—with a particular focus on the contract type (e.g., fixed-price vs. time-and materials contracts) and duration—and the efects of such choices [6]. Despite these significant theoretical and practically relevant advances, two gaps in existing literature are particularly noteworthy: First, while service level agreements (SLAs) represent an integral component of an IT outsourcing contract, the design of such agreements has received surprisingly little attention in prior research: “Although [SLAs] are important for IT outsourcing management, appropriate mechanisms for constructing efective SLAs are still poorly understood, leading to inadequate or overcomplicated contracts that are inefective” ([8], p. 469). One key aspect of designing SLAs is their framing in terms of bonus or penalty clauses. While such clauses represent fundamental elements of SLAs [9–11] that are widely used in the IT outsourcing industry [12,13], the efects of bonus versus penalty clauses have been largely neglected in extant literature. For example, Gopal et al. [14] highlight that “research is required to understand other incentive-based contracts such as agreements with […] a reward or penalty for the vendor” (p. 1682).

Second and relatedly, prior studies have almost exclusively focused on the efects of IT outsourcing contracts on organizational outcomes such as IT outsourcing (project) success and profits (e.g., [14–16]). However, before having an efect at the organizational level, the design of IT outsourcing contracts (and SLAs in particular) will first influence the attitudes and behaviors of individual staf members (cf. [17–19]), especially when bonuses or penalties are associated with the contract. Examining contract-design efects at the individual level thus addresses an important research gap and enables the study of how diferent IT outsourcing staf members react to bonuses and penalty clauses, which ultimately may also help inform IS stafing decisions.

Against this backdrop, our study seeks to contribute to the collective knowledge on the design of IT outsourcing contracts by investigating the efects of SLA contract framing (bonus vs. penalty) on the attitudes and behaviors of individuals working in the organization of the service provider (SP). Regarding SP employees' reactions to SLAs being framed in terms of bonuses or penalties, the focus of our study lies on two key determinants of the quality of service delivery by the SP, namely (intrafirm) knowledge-sharing willingness and (inter-firm) commitment [11,20]. The former has been found to be an important success factor in knowledge-intensive industries such as the IT industry [21–23]. For example, the efective transformation of individual knowledge into organizational capabilities requires the sharing of knowledge among the members of a firm [24] and as such can help SPs to develop relevant capabilities, and ultimately to deliver high service quality to service recipients [25]. Commitment to inter-organizational relationships in cludes the willingness to be involved in the relationship through efortrelated commitment and the desire to continue a relationship through positive feelings associated with the partner [11,26,27]. Similar to knowledge sharing, SP commitment to the outsourcing relationship is seen as a critical factor for IT outsourcing success and is regularly used as the ultimate dependent variable in the study of SLAs [11].

Our study therefore addresses the following research question: How does the inclusion of penalty clauses (as opposed to bonus clauses) in SLA design afect SP employees' knowledge-sharing willingness and commitment? To study the efects of SLA design (in terms of bonuses vs. penalty clauses) on individual behaviors, we draw on prospect theory [28,29] that enables us to formulate theory-consistent expectations of the effects of contract framing. Our study also examines how the behavioral efects of SLA contract framing are moderated by individual diferences in professional work experience, which has been found to be a key determinant of organizational behavior [30–32].

To answer our research question, we conducted an experiment with 198 participants. A key advantage of using an experiment is that it enabled us to isolate SLA framing efects from other efects. For instance, the design of new contracts is often coupled with a change in management or leadership [33,34]. As such, contract efects are often hard to isolate from management efects [35–38]. Thus, our controlled experi mental approach allowed us to approach causality in a more directed way than in a less controlled setting. In conducting our experiment, we followed the recommendations by Jarvenpaa et al. [39] and Marsden and Pingry [40] to increase internal validity and ensure that the outcomes observed are a function of the manipulated SLA framing variable.

Our study contributes to extant literature in several ways. First, the study results extend Goo et al.'s [11] notion that SLAs should be considered as a tool for managing IT outsourcing relationships. Further, the results of our study also contribute to the literature studying the antecedents of knowledge sharing by extending the notion that incentives influence knowledge-sharing behaviors [18,31,41,42] and by demonstrating that the mere framing of incentives embedded in SLAs has an influence on such behaviors. Finally, our results show that including penalty clauses in SLAs reduces employees' afective commitment but increases their efort-related commitment. This dual role of penalty clauses highlights the complexity of designing SLAs, and thus con tributes to a deeper understanding of the mechanisms that help construct efective IT outsourcing agreements [8,14,16]. As such, our results suggest that SLA framing decisions (in terms of bonus versus penalty clauses) can afect the success of IT outsourcing arrangements and that such decisions require careful consideration of the specific outsourcing goals.

Our study is structured as follows: In the next section, we detail the research background and develop our hypotheses. We then describe our methodology and present the study results. We conclude by discussing the theoretical and practical implications of our study as well as the study limitations along with directions for future research.

## 2. Research background and hypotheses

## 2.1. Service level agreements and bonus versus penalty clauses

Service level agreements (SLAs) are inter-organizational contractual arrangements between a service provider (SP) and a service recipient (SR) [43,44]. They represent integral components of IT outsourcing contracts, in which a SP commits to provide certain IT services to a SR. SLAs have been described according to eleven contractual elements [8,11,43], which can be grouped into three characteristics (see Fig. 1). Foundation characteristics of SLAs specify the process ownership plan as well as the service level contents and objectives. Change characteristics include processes for managing changes and contingencies in future demands, for resolving anticipated conflicts of interest, for providing feedback, as well as for introducing improvements and innovations [45]. Governance characteristics capture the communication plan, measurement charter, conflict arbitration charter, and enforcement plan. While bonus clauses may relate to all three SLA characteristics (cf. [11]), penalty clauses typically relate to foundation and/or governance characteristics.<sup>1</sup> A potential reason for penalty clauses usually not being considered as a mechanism for controlling change [11,43] may be that change characteristics capture elements that are dificult to specify (e.g., innovation plan) and tend to be outside of a partner's control (cf. Bol & Smith [85]).

Through the design of SLAs, and in particular the inclusion of bonus and penalty clauses, the SR aspires to receive high service quality, and eventually to reap IT outsourcing benefits and improve the performance of its IT function [20]. In this regard, two key factors that determine the quality of outsourced IT services are the knowledge the SP possesses and the commitment the SP upholds into service delivery [11,20] (see Fig. 1). As such, in our study, we investigate how the inclusion of bonus versus penalty clauses into SLAs influences SP employees' knowledge sharing willingness as well as their commitment within the SP organization. Knowledge sharing is an important success factor for IT firms [21–23], since the efective transformation of individual into organizational knowledge requires the sharing of knowledge among the members of a firm [24]. Prior literature suggests that incentives, if set adequately, have the potential to promote knowledge-sharing behaviors [41,42]. On a related note, commitment to an IT outsourcing relationship is seen as one of the most important determinants of outsourcing success and is used as the ultimate dependent variable in the study of SLAs [11]. Commitment to inter-organizational relation ships includes the willingness to be involved in the relationship through the commitment of efort (efort-related commitment) and the desire to continue a relationship because of a positive attitude toward the partner (afective commitment) [11,26,27,48]. In the following, we develop our research hypotheses regarding the individual-level efects of SLA framing in terms of penalty clauses (as opposed to bonus clauses).

## 2.2. Knowledge-sharing willingness

Knowledge-sharing willingness denotes individuals' readiness to disclose knowledge to another party. In this study, we focus on employees' willingness to share knowledge within the SP organization. Such intra-firm knowledge sharing is found to be an important success factor for IT firms [21–23]. Knowledge is seen as the primary key to a firm's success and an efective knowledge base—as supported by effective intra-firm knowledge sharing—helps SPs provide high-quality services [49]. Yet, in knowledge work the “major challenge is to manage and support work that exploits knowledge to add value to the organisation” ([50], p. 290). Thus, it is important to have adequate controls (e.g., through SLA framing) in place to support intra-firm knowledge sharing among employees.

Individuals' willingness to share knowledge is influenced by a variety of factors: incentive structures, cooperation eficacy, level of group identity and personal responsibility, etc. (for a review see [51]). In this study, we focus on the role of incentives, which, if set adequately, can support intra-firm knowledge sharing [42]. However, incentives have not always been found to exert a positive influence on knowledge sharing [41]. For example, prior research shows that the efects of group rewards on knowledge-sharing willingness tend to be greater than those of individual rewards [42].

Individuals' knowledge-sharing behavior is likely to be influenced by cost/benefit considerations [18]. For instance, individuals who perceive associated costs to be lower than associated benefits are more likely to share their knowledge with colleagues. Personal costs can be as simple as additional time commitments required for knowledge sharing, or more complex such as concerns related to losing status as a subject matter expert. Benefits can include more tangible employee rewards or more intangible aspects such as fostering of collegiality, expectation of reciprocity, and satisfaction in establishing good work relationships.

Imagine, for example, an IT project manager who is faced with an urgent request by a colleague (currently working on a diferent project) to share her expertise. She knows that if she agrees to help out it will require significant time and may thus put at risk meeting some of the targets specified in an SLA in her project. This, in turn, may negatively afect her project's budget and her personal variable pay as specified in the SLA.<sup>2</sup>

Here, in line with prospect theory [28,29], we suggest that the framing of the consequences for not meeting the SLA targets in terms of a penalty clause (as opposed to a bonus clause) influences the IT project manager's cost/benefit considerations regarding knowledge-sharing behavior, and thus her willingness to share knowledge. Prospect theory models choice in form of an S-shaped utility function [28,29]. A critical feature of this curve is that it has a steeper slope in the loss domain than in the gain domain. As a result. changes in wealth in the loss domain afect individuals' subjective utility to a greater extent than changes in wealth in the gain domain. Thus, identical situations framed in terms of losses, or in terms of gains, can lead to diferent decisions because of individuals' loss aversion. More precisely, individuals can be expected to go to greater lengths to avoid a penalty than to achieve a bonus of an equivalent monetary amount. As such, in line with prospect theory [28,29], we suggest that framing the consequences for not meeting SLA targets in terms of bonuses versus penalties makes a diference to in dividuals' cost/benefit considerations, which in turn afects their willingness to share knowledge with colleagues.

In a nutshell, the perceived costs of the IT project manager increase when faced with a penalty as compared to a bonus because of loss aversion and there is more disutility associated with the change in wealth in the loss domain than in the gain domain. As a consequence, the manager is less likely to share her knowledge and expertise with her colleague if she faces a penalty instead of a bonus (as defined in the SLA). We thus suggest:

H1. SLA framing in terms of penalty clauses (as opposed to bonus clauses) decreases individuals' willingness to share knowledge within the service-provider organization.

## 2.3. Efort-related commitment

Capturing the level of work individuals are willing to expend, efortrelated commitment is a critical aspect of commitment to an IT outsourcing relationship [11,26,27]. For example, in prior research on software development projects has been shown to be positively associated with process maturity and ultimately with product quality [14,53,54].

In this regard, IT outsourcing clients can signal the importance of certain SLA targets by associating them with bonus or penalty clauses. Here, consistent with prospect theory [28,29], SP employees can be expected to be more committed to go to greater lengths, i.e., expend more efort, to avoid a penalty than to achieve a bonus of an equivalent monetary amount. As discussed above, this is because prospect theory's utility function has a steeper slope in the loss domain than in the gain domain. Thus, in the face of receiving a penalty (as compared to a bonus), individuals in the SP organization are more likely to go the ‘extra mile’ because of the anticipated negative consequences. Consequently, we suggest:

H2. SLA framing in terms of penalty clauses (as opposed to bonus clauses) increases service-provider employees' efort-related commitment to the outsourcing relationship.

## 2.4. Afective commitment

Afective commitment describes an emotional attachment to the outsourcing relationship “such that the strongly committed individual identifies with, is involved in, and enjoys membership” ([55], p. 2). In other words, afectively committed SP employees like being part of the relationship with the SR, which in turn increases their willingness to pursue the SR's goals and their desire to remain within this relationship [56]. SP employees' afective commitment thus represents another key aspect of commitment to an IT outsourcing relationship, especially when the role of the SP moves beyond a mere maintenance role toward a more strategic role [57]. Afective commitment can be seen as an important determinant of relationship quality, which can be described in terms of the level of trust and communication between the outsourcing partners [20,57] and which continues to be a focal research topic in the IT outsourcing literature [3,11,20,57,58]. Also, afective commitment is a key construct in the IT personnel literature (for an overview see [59]).

In our study, we focus on three aspects of afective commitment that have been suggested to be particularly relevant for individual performance: perceptions of fairness as well as feelings of enjoyment and success. For example, prior literature finds that perceptions of fairness have a positive efect on work performance [84], organizational citi zenship behavior [60], and organizational commitment ([61,62]; for a review see [63]). Similarly, positive afective states such as feelings of enjoyment and success have been associated with individuals' work productivity [64,65].

Relatedly, prior research suggests that, when employees are asked to choose between penalty-framed and bonus-framed contracts that are economically equivalent, they tend to prefer the latter [66]. Similar to above, this preference can be explained by prospect theory [28,29], which predicts that individuals perceive greater subjective (dis)utility due to changes in wealth that are perceived as losses than due to changes in wealth that are perceived as gains. Thus, in line with prospect theory, we expect that individuals perceive SLAs involving penalties as less fair than SLAs involving bonuses. Also, because of the negative SLA framing, we expect feelings of success and joy to be lower when individuals are being faced with penalties. On this basis, we propose:

H3. SLA framing in terms of penalty clauses (as opposed to bonus clauses) decreases service-provider employees' afective commitment to the outsourcing relationship.

In addition, our study controls for the extent to which behavioral efects of SLA contract framing are moderated by individual diferences in professional work experience, which is found to be a key determinant of organizational behavior [30–32]. The resulting research model is presented in Fig. 2.

## 3. Research method

We conducted an experiment employing a 2 × 2 between-participant full factorial design. The two independent variables are SLA framing and professional work experience. SLA framing was manipulated via two treatment conditions in the experimental instructions (bonus vs. penalty) and randomly assigned to participants. Professional experience was varied via participant recruitment (IT professionals vs. novices).

IT professionals were acquired via personal contacts and profes sional social networks. In particular, we contacted potential participants by e-mail or private message, and explained them the study context. To participate in our study, IT professionals had to have at least two years of project management experience in the IT industry. If the contact fulfilled the selection criterion and agreed to participate, we provided her/him with a link to the online scenario. In addition, we asked contacts to forward the study invitation to potentially interested colleagues (see also [67]). Participation for IT professionals was voluntary.

Students enrolled in IS courses from a large university represented novices. Students took part on a voluntary basis as well. They could choose to participate either in laboratory studies or in alternative tasks for receiving credit points. Before students decided to take part, the general terms of the study were explained to them. They then completed the study on computers in a behavioral laboratory of the university. Students who were invited to participate were considered ap propriate for the study at hand, since they had obtained training in information systems and business. They thus had a suficient understanding of the subject matter, despite their limited professional experience. Similar student samples have been used in other experimental information systems studies [68–72]. All responses were gathered in the second half of 2015.

## 3.2. Experimental scenario

We used a scenario-based experiment to test the hypotheses. The use of experiments allows for isolating hypothesized driving forces and for examining cause-efect relationships [73]. The scenario was adapted from Constant et al. [31] and Fehrenbacher [68]. All participants were asked to imagine a scenario in which they are a project manager at a vendor firm (SP) called ‘2020 Company’ operating in the IT industry. Participants were told that they will manage a project for an important client (SR), that the project has a duration of two or more years, and that the arrangements with the client have been made in a service level agreement (SLA). Explanations of the SLA were subsequently given. The explanations of the SLA contained the SLA framing treatment. More specifically, based on monthly targets specified in the SLA, it was stated: “If a target is not met, a penalty payment needs to be paid to the client. This directly afects the project's budget and your personal variable pay.” Or it was outlined: “If a target is met, a bonus payment is paid by the client. This directly afects the project's budget and your personal variable pay.” To check whether the participants had understood the scenario and the characteristics of the SLA, check questions for understanding were asked. Only if the participant answered all of them correctly, they could proceed.

Further, the scenario described that Dominique, a colleague working on a diferent project with a diferent client, has approached the participant (project manager) and asked her/him whether she/he can share her/his knowledge and expertise. It was further outlined that over the last years the participant had built up substantial knowledge and expertise about key processes and functions in her company. Further, although Dominique comes from a diferent department and i not in direct competition with the participant, the time spent sharing the expertise and knowledge would lead to a delay in the participant' project. Thus, the participant may not meet some SLA targets of her/his project, which would have a direct efect on bonus (or penalty) payments. Therefore, our scenario suggests potential costs to knowledge sharing.

## 3.3. Dependent variables

The experiment included three dependent variables: the IT project manager's knowledge-sharing willingness as well as her/his efort-related and afective commitment to the outsourcing relationship with the SR. The latter is consistent with Goo et al. [11] who define com mitment in terms of input, durability and consistency. Input involves the efort a party is willing to expend in an outsourcing relationship (i.e., efort-related commitment). Durability and consistency capture factors like positive afect of the relationship (i.e., afective commitment).

In line with Constant et al. [31] and Fehrenbacher [68], knowledge sharing willingness was captured via asking the participants on a scale from 1 (low willingness) to 7 (high willingness): “How willing are you to share your knowledge and expertise with your colleague?” This rating followed the direct question by Dominique, the colleague: “I have encountered some challenges on my project. Could you help me to meet these challenges?”

Efort-related commitment was measured with two questions: (1) “The penalty [bonus]-based variable component would make me work harder to serve the client.” (1 = Strongly Agree to 7 = Strongly Disagree); and (2) “The penalty [bonus]-based variable component would increase my commitment to deliver better service quality to the client.” (1 = Strongly Agree to 7 = Strongly Disagree). Afective commitment was measured with three questions: (1) “How fair do you perceive your penalty [bonus]-based variable component of the Service Level Agreement?” (1 = Unfair and 7 = Fair); (2) “If you meet all targets of your project, how strong would your feeling of enjoyment be when you do not receive a penalty [bonus]?” (1 = Very Low to 7 = Very High); and (3) “If you meet all targets of your project, how strong would your feeling of success be when you do not receive a penalty [bonus]?” (1 = Very Low to 7 = Very High). Both commitment variables are composite variables and showed adequate consistency (Cronbach's Alpha: efort-related commitment = 0.924; afective com mitment = 0.753).

## 5. Discussion

Designing an efective contract is an important step toward a successful IT outsourcing relationship. Here, to receive high-quality services, SRs seek to cooperate with SPs that possess a high level of expert knowledge and exhibit high commitment to the outsourcing relationship. Based on an experiment with 198 IT professionals and novices, we find evidence that SRs can influence the way in which SPs deal with knowledge as well as their level of commitment by means of SLA framing in terms of bonus and penalty clauses. The study results thus provide empirical support for our overarching argument that SLAs do not only serve as a mechanism for specifying foundation, change, and governance characteristics of an IT outsourcing arrangement [11], but can also be used by the SR to influence SP employees' behavior, and ultimately the quality and innovation capability of the outsourcing relationship. Consequently, penalty and bonus clauses should be used purposefully by client managers. As such, our study responds to a call by Gopal et al. [14] to study rewards and penalties in outsourcing arrangements from a vendor perspective. In this context, Benaroch et al. [46] show that the more complex the contracting transactions are the more extensive firms use safeguarding functions that include penalty clauses. This suggests that our study results are particularly helpful in better understanding complex contracting transactions.

In the following, we discuss key contributions of our study to the literature on IT outsourcing contracts and SLA design $[ 3 , 1 0 , 1 1 , 1 4 , 2 0 ]$ and the literature on factors associated with knowledge sharing $[ 1 8 , 3 1 , 4 1 ] _ { ; }$ , followed by a discussion of study limitations and associated avenues for future research.

## 5.1. Theoretical and practical contributions

First, research by Goo et al. [11] has furthered our understanding of SLAs in IT outsourcing relationships by examining different aspects of SLAs in an inclusive framework and highlighting the role diferent aspects of SLAs play for the management of successful outsourcing relationships. Their findings indicate that SLAs are more than just a way of measuring SP performance and can also be an efective tool in managing the quality of the client-vendor relationship. In this regard, the results of our study confirm and extend prior research findings by showing that the design of SLAs can signal additional and important information that influences SP employees' behaviors. Here, our study focuses on one central design aspect of SLAs, namely their framing in terms of penalty or bonus clauses. Specifically, we develop theory consistent expectations of diferential behavioral efects of bonus versus penalty clauses and provide empirical evidence for these efects. Our results thus extend Goo et al.'s [11] notion that well-developed SLAs “enable efective management of outsourcing engagements” (p. 120) by demonstrating individual-level efects of SLA framing decisions. This implies that the management of IT outsourcing contracts requires careful consideration of the ramifications of business-to-business con tracting on individual behaviors, including subsequent consequences for knowledge management (e.g., the level of knowledge sharing within the SP organization).

Second, our study contributes to prior research on the factors influencing individual knowledge sharing [18,31,41]. Specifically, the study results show that SLA framing in terms of penalty clauses (as opposed to bonus clauses) reduces individuals' willingness to share knowledge with their colleagues. Since IT outsourcing goes increasingly beyond a mere resource-acquisition perspective toward viewing outsourcing as collaborative and innovative relationships [43,77,78], it is important for both outsourcing partners to consider the efects of penalty clauses on knowledge behaviors, which likely shape innovation capability. After all, knowledge is seen as the primary key to a firm's success [49]. Therefore, managers need to be mindful that the appli cation of penalty clauses might inhibit a culture of openness and knowledge sharing. This is important because researchers and practitioners propose that a culture that values social interaction and sharing of ideas is important for knowledge management and business success [21–23].

The efects of SLA framing in terms of penalty versus bonus clauses on knowledge sharing is particularly interesting when considering the results of Kankanhalli et al.'s [18] study, which shows that pro-sharing norms established in an organization influence individual contribution to knowledge repositories. In particular, their study shows that prosharing norms, such as a norm of tolerance of mistakes and a norm of teamwork in organizations, moderate the relation between reciprocity considerations as an extrinsic motivation and usage of knowledge re positories. When pro-sharing norms are weak—but not when they are strong, reciprocity considerations influence the usage of knowledge repositories. As such Kankanhalli et al. [18] contend: “management can strengthen pro-sharing norms to reduce the necessity of reciprocity benefit for knowledge contributors” (p. 134). Thus, Kankanhalli et al. [18] demonstrate that organizations can exhibit influence on individual knowledge sharing behavior by establishing appropriate organizational norms. In their study, they suggest that such norms can be achieved through open ofice spaces and transfer of employees between departments, which can help organizations convey their expectations on how they would like their employees to work with each other. Our study thus extends Kankanhalli et al.'s [18] findings by showing that SR managers can influence individual knowledge sharing on the part of the SP through SLA design and framing. Thus, the framing of a contract in terms of bonus versus penalty can be used by management as a “way of conveying information about the expectations of the firm” ([79], p. 102), and thus to support the development of desired organizational norms.

Third, the results of our study show that penalty clauses in SLAs (as opposed to bonus clauses) increase efort-related commitment while decreasing afective commitment. Consistent with prospect theory, we find that penalty clauses are perceived to be less fair and that achieving targets to avoid a penalty is associated with less enjoyment and feeling of success. As such, penalty clauses seem to have inverse efects on commitment. While they drive the eforts SP employees are willing to expend, they are detrimental to the quality of the relationship between the SR and the SP due to decreased perceptions of fairness, enjoyment, and success. These results are particularly interesting in light of Bock et al.'s [41] findings that individuals' fairness perceptions—which are part of an organization's climate—have “a strong influence on the formation of subjective norms regarding knowledge sharing” (p. 99) as well as given Kankanhalli et al.'s [18] conclusion that “enjoyment in helping others is the most important motivator for [knowledge] contributors”. Further, our results also extend the findings of Gopal et al.' [14] study, which examines the efects of diferent contract types and explicitly calls for research into the efects of bonus versus penalty clauses. For example, Gopal et al. [14] find a positive relationship be tween vendor efort and the profitability of IT outsourcing projects. In this regard, our results suggest that the inclusion of penalty clauses in SLAs is one way to increase vendor efort. Yet, the results of our study also suggest that penalty framing has a negative influence on the af fective commitment of vendor employees, and thus may impair the quality of the outsourcing relationship [3,20].

In sum, for SR managers, the study results imply that, before installing penalty clauses, a careful evaluation of the present and targeted relationship with the SP is needed to balance potential impacts of penalty clauses. Ideally, an SR wants to ensure and maintain a high general commitment by the SP, since all types of commitment are im portant in achieving the best possible service quality [11]. Consequently, in the negotiation of SLAs, SR should be well aware of the opposing efects of penalty clauses and, given the results of our study, also consider the combination of penalty and bonus clauses in a single IT outsourcing contract.

## 5.2. Limitations and future research

The study results should be interpreted with several limitations in mind. Several of these limitations also represent promising avenues for future research. First, our study is limited to the extent that it does not examine the behavior of both sides of the SR-SP dyad. However, the focus on one side of this dyad is a common limitation that our study shares with many previous studies. Further, focusing on the SP per spective, our study complements prior research, which has tended to focus on the SR perspective only (e.g., [11,80]). Taken together, this provides rich opportunities for future research in examining efects of similar contract manipulations on the SR side, or on both the SR and the SP side in one and the same investigation. Second, while SLAs typically specify arrangements between a SR and a SP at the organizational leve [10], our study focuses on the individual level. Therefore, we would like to encourage future studies that combine individual and organi zational-level variables to infer implications for organizational design. Doing so can be a fruitful endeavour, as also shown by prior research [18,31,32,41,79]. For instance, Kankanhalli et al. [18] studies how organizational norms and self-eficacy influence usage of knowledge repositories. Third and relatedly, our study builds on the argument that IT outsourcing contracts will first influence the attitudes and behaviors of individual staff members (cf. [17–19]). especially when individuals' bonuses or penalties are associated with the contract. In this regard, we would like to acknowledge that other levels of study (e.g., team level) are equally important. For instance, team dynamics may play an im portant role in influencing the effects of bonus and penalty clauses (cf [81]). As such, this presents an interesting opportunity for future re search. Fourth, we acknowledge that our experiment takes two extremes by comparing the efects of “penalty only” vs. “bonus only” clauses. We do this to be able to isolate the effects of these two basic types of contract clauses. In practice, however, IT outsourcing contracts are likely to include penalty clauses, as well as bonus clauses. Yet, when a contract contains both penalty and bonus clauses, studying their effects and isolating them would be significantly more dificult, if not impossible. Thus, we leverage the possibility in controlled experiments to purposefully study the two extreme cases. Finally, future research should investigate the efects of other SLA factors on knowledge sharing and SP commitment. Given the importance of knowledge sharing for the success of IT outsourcing relationships [80,82], it becomes also important to understand factors influencing the level of knowledge sharing across organizational boundaries, as for instance explored in Jarvenpaa and Mao [83]. An interesting starting point for such research may be the study by Jarvenpaa and Staples [32], which finds simila rities between intra- and inter-organizational information sharing si: tuations regarding the formation of organizational information-own: ership beliefs.

## 6. Conclusion

The design of efective contracts represents a key success factor of IT outsourcing relationships. In this context, our study contributes to the collective knowledge on how to design IT outsourcing contracts by examining the individual-level efects of SLA framing (bonus vs. penalty) as well as by providing empirical support for the dual role of penalty clauses in outsourcing relationships. Specifically, based on an experiment with 198 participants, the study results show that the inclusion of penalty clauses in SLAs increases SP employees' efort-related commitment to the IT outsourcing relationship but decreases their knowledge-sharing willingness as well as their afective commitment to the relationship. Our study thus highlights that the design of IT outsourcing contracts, and in particular the framing of SLA clauses, have a direct efect on the attitudes and behaviors of individuals working in outsourcing arrangements. Further, ofering empirical evidence for the dual role of penalty clauses, our study highlights a major challenge associated with the design of efective IT outsourcing contracts as well as the importance of managers that are mindful of the opposing efects that the contract design may have on their workforce.
