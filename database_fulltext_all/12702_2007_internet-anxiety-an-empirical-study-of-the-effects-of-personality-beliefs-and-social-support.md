# Internet anxiety: An empirical study of the effects of personality, beliefs, and social support

Jason Bennett Thatcher <sup>a,\*</sup>, Misty L. Loughry <sup>b</sup>, Jaejoo Lim <sup>a</sup>, D. Harrison McKnight <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Management, College of Business and Behavioral Science, Clemson University, USA <sup>b</sup> Department of Management, Marketing, and Logistics, College of Business Administration, Georgia Southern University, USA <sup>c</sup> Department of Accounting and Information Systems, Eli Broad College of Business, Michigan State University, USA Received 26 October 2004; received in revised form 8 March 2006; accepted 12 November 2006

## Abstract

We examined sources of Internet anxiety; specifically modeling the ties from broad dispositional traits (computer anxiety, computer self-efficacy, and personal innovativeness with IT), beliefs about the work environment (about the adequacy of resources and trust in technology), and two forms of social support for IT (leader and peer support) to individuals’ anxiety about using Internet applications. We tested our model using respondents who participated in virtual teams during a 16-week period. Our findings suggested that Internet anxiety was affected both by the users’ personality and by beliefs that can be influenced by providing adequate resources to support the technology, encourage trust in technology, and working to assure users that leaders and peers are supportive of their using the technology. Our findings suggest that by providing appropriate resources and fostering a supportive environment, leaders could reduce Internet anxiety and thus influence the use of technology in the workplace in ways that benefit organizations.

Keywords: Computer anxiety; Computer self-efficacy; Internet anxiety; Personal innovativeness in information technology; Perceived resources; Social support; Trust in technology; Virtual teams

## 1. Introduction

The rapid development of new computer applications for the Internet has allowed firms to expand the numbers and types of Web-enabled services offered to consumers. As the number of Internet applications has increased, the technology has become more powerful. However, although many people have access to the Internet, some are reluctant to utilize many of its capabilities. Overcoming this reluctance has therefore become an important focus for organizations and researchers.

Many Internet users remain uncomfortable using Internet applications and instead use traditional methods to accomplish tasks that could be performed over the Internet. Many fear using the Internet for purchases. Because of this apprehension, consumers frequently use the Internet to gather information, but use other methods to complete the transaction [1]. However, organizations and individuals could benefit if individuals were more willing to perform transactions on the Internet.

Internet anxiety is the fear or apprehension that individuals experience when using the Internet [2]. It hinders individuals’ use of e-mail, instant messaging, or on-line databases. Because of its effect on behavior, it must be managed in order to create more opportunities for all to reap the benefits of the web. Hence, our research question was:

What are the sources of Internet anxiety?

## 2. Hypotheses

Anxiety refers to an unpleasant emotional state or condition characterized by tension, apprehension, and worry [3]. Individuals experience it when they perceive conditions in their environment to be threatening. General feelings of anxiety have been associated with broad work-related outcomes, such as stress and feelings of work overload [4], as well as beliefs about one’s ability [5].

Fig. 1 presents our model, which was developed to determine how personality characteristics, individual beliefs, and social support influence Internet anxiety.

## 2.1. Personality traits

Personality, demographic characteristics, and other individual aspects influence system users’ beliefs and behavior [6]. We examined three personality traits that should affect Internet anxiety: computer anxiety, computer self-efficacy, and personal innovativeness with IT.

## 2.1.1. Computer anxiety

Within IT, computer anxiety has been frequently studied. Computer anxiety is defined as an apprehension or fear of interacting with computers, irrespective of the existence of real danger [7]. It is assumed to result from general fears about using computers and thus is stable [8]. This contrasts with Internet anxiety, which is situation-based.

Internet anxiety is, of course, closely related to computer anxiety, but the concepts are distinct [9]. Internet anxiety is a feeling or emotion evoked by the use of web-enabled technologies. Empirical evidence suggests that computer anxiety and Internet anxiety are distinct; for example, in a study of e-mail, Brown et al. [10] found a 0.33 correlation between computermediated communication anxiety and computer anxiety.

The Internet may evoke anxiety because it requires users to learn new terminology and understand new applications that may seem strange to them [11]. In addition, the Internet may evoke emotions because it results in interaction with unknown situations or people. Furthermore, using the Internet presents risks, such as the potential for viruses, spyware, or invasions of user privacy. Hence, while computer anxiety reflects a lifetime of experience with computers [12], Internet anxiety reflects current encounters with IT involving the Internet.

Because individuals must use a computer to access the Internet, we believe that individuals who have greater computer anxiety will experience more Internet anxiety. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1a. Computer anxiety will be positively associated with Internet anxiety.

## 2.2. Computer self-efficacy

Self-efficacy considers individuals’ beliefs about their ability to perform specific tasks [13]. It ‘‘refers to beliefs in one’s capabilities to organize and execute the course of action required to produce given attainments’ [14] and is thought to result from past accomplishments, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and emotional arousal [15]. Individuals’ efficacy levels influence their ability to acquire skills, their choice of activities, and their willingness to continue in a course of action.

Within the domain of MIS, research suggests that individuals who have high computer self-efficacy are more likely to use IT [16,17] and to express more positive attitudes toward the Internet [18].

We expected that individuals with high computer self-efficacy would feel higher levels of mastery over computer applications and that they would report lower levels of Internet anxiety. Therefore,

H1b. Computer self-efficacy will be negatively associated with Internet anxiety.

## 2.3. Personal innovativeness with information technology (PIIT)

PIIT is ‘‘the willingness of an individual to try out any new information technology’’ [19]. Individuals who reported high levels of PIITwere more likely to take risks in experimenting with new technology [20]. Because of this tolerance to risk, highly innovative individuals are less likely to report computer anxiety [21].

Internet applications typically involve greater risks than stand-alone computer applications and involve new experiences, even for frequent Internet users. The Internet is characterized by a high level of uncertainty and a high level of anonymity in the virtual world. Furthermore, users have little or no control over the Internet or the responsiveness of the ISPs and content providers. Websites that users attempt to access may disappear with nothing to explain what is happening or when it will be resolved. Even when people use e-mail to communicate with known acquaintances, family, or friends, they may not know whether the recipient ever received a message.

Individuals may experience Internet anxiety because of the apparent unreliability of the Internet and because they perceive risks and vulnerability when using it. Individuals with higher PIIT should experience less anxiety because they are more comfortable with technology risks and changes. Hence, we propose:

H1c. Personal innovativeness with information technology will be negatively associated with Internet anxiety.

## 2.4. Individual beliefs

Beliefs are opinions that one holds to be true. Researchers in MIS have noted that beliefs are important for understanding individuals’ reactions to technology. Agarwal and Karhanna [22] found that the degree to which IT is compatible with the work environment influenced attitudes towards technology. In our study we examined one belief about the context in which the technology is used (perceptions of resources) and one belief about the technology itself (trust in technology).

## 2.4.1. Perceptions of resources

Resources, or facilitating conditions, are objective factors that make an act easy to perform and thus help people accomplish their tasks. Providing support to IS utilization is one way to affect both users’ perceptions of the adequacy of resources and their utilization of computers.

Access that works reliably, with reasonable speed, and that the user understands are resources that should play a major role in reducing Internet anxiety. Adequate training and time to learn are also likely to be important resources. Because adequate resources both help users to effectively and efficiently use technology and reduce barriers that make using the Internet difficult or frustrating, resources should reduce Internet anxiety.

H2. Users’ perceptions of higher levels of resources will be negatively associated with Internet anxiety.

## 2.4.2. Trust in technology

When people extend trust, they recognize that they do not have total control over the outcomes, but believe that the trusted party will not take advantage of the situation [23]. Hence, even when risk and uncertainty characterize a transaction, trust may exist.

An important reason why trust in technology has not been widely investigated is that many researchers have assumed that trusted parties have volitional control over their behavior [24]. However, others have argued that trust is not necessarily a feeling limited to relations between human beings, but that individuals can also feel trust toward objects and processes [25]; for example, individuals trust bridges that cross rivers and automated teller machines that access their bank accounts. Trust in technology, therefore, describes beliefs about how a technology will perform and a person’s willingness to rely on it.

Two facets of trust in technology are trusting beliefs and trusting intentions, which are closely related [26]. Trusting beliefs occur when individuals feel that the target of their trust is benevolent, competent, honest, and predictable. Trusting intentions occur when individuals are willing to depend on one another [27]. When technology users express both trusting beliefs and trusting intentions, they are comfortable using the technology to perform tasks [28] and are less likely to express apprehension when using it. This should be associated with lower levels of Internet anxiety.

Because the roots of Internet anxiety are in the fear of negative consequences of technology use, feelings of trust should diminish feelings of anxiety. Therefore, trust in technology should be associated with lower levels of Internet anxiety.

H3. Higher levels of trust in technology will be negatively associated with Internet anxiety.

## 2.5. Social support

Social information theory suggests that social support is important because it shapes users’ beliefs [29], including perceptions that they have sufficient resources to use technology and trust it.

## 2.5.1. Leader support

Leader support is a subset of what Igbaria et al. [30] called organizational support. In the context of IT, perceived leader support is defined as employees perceptions that leaders encourage the use of technology to accomplish their work.

Leaders can be supportive by providing reinforcement when a user has technical problems [31] and by showing they are committed to continue to use a technology. We believed that leader support would lower Internet anxiety, but that this would be mediated by users’ beliefs.

Support thus reduces Internet anxiety. Hence,

H4a. Leader support will have a positive effect on perceptions of the levels of resources, which will partially mediate the negative effects of leader support on Internet anxiety.

Leader support should also increase users’ trust in technology and reduce their mistrust of the systems. Also, leaders can provide a personal testimonial of the reliability and usefulness of the technology. This in turn, should reduce their Internet anxiety.

H4b. Leader support will have a positive effect on trust in technology, which will partially mediate the negative effects of leader support on Internet anxiety.

## 2.5.2. Peer support

Peers have important influence on one another by providing motivation for peers to want to learn to use the technology. The importance of peers has been demonstrated in many settings [32,33]. Internet anxiety is lessened when peers help one another learn by providing instructions, tips, or technical advice, and demonstrating particular Internet applications or mutually interesting Internet content. These, in turn, should reduce users’ Internet anxiety.

H5a. Peer support will have a positive effect on perceptions of the levels of resources, which will partially mediate the negative effects of peer support on Internet anxiety.

Similarly, peer support should increase users’ trust in technology, which should reduce users’ Internet anxiety.

H5b. Peer support will have a positive effect on trust in technology, which will partially mediate the negative effects of peer support on Internet anxiety.

Students were randomly assigned to work in virtual teams of three students, one student from each of the three sections, on semester-long projects that required the use of the Internet for research, e-mail, a proprietary Intranet application, and file transfer protocol. These applications were used to conduct group activities, such as scheduling, gathering information, exchanging and editing documents, brainstorming, and other communication that was critical to the success of the team projects. The teams were responsible for four projects, worth 50% of their course grade.

## 3.2. Measures

Measures were picked from validated questionnaires used in prior research when possible; e.g., for Internet and computer anxiety and perceived resource availability. Computer self-efficacy was measured using items derived from Johnson and Marakas [34]. Trust in technology was measured using items based on Ref. [35] using three trusting belief dimensions (competence, reliability, and predictability) and one trusting intention dimension (willingness to depend on technology). Leader Support and Peer Support were also measured using items from prior research. See Appendix A for the items and their weights. See Table 2 for item loadings by construct. Item weights and loadings were derived from PLS analysis described in the following sections.

## 4.1. Data analysis

We conducted a preliminary analysis to look for outliers and to assess the distribution of our sample but though there were no outliers, many item distributions were either skewed or showed kurtosis: the items were non-normally distributed. Therefore, we used Partial Least Squares technique to analyze the data.

To calculate reliability, we estimated each constructs’ internal consistency reliability. To examine explained variance, we calculated the average variance extracted (AVE), which measures the variance captured by the indicators relative to measurement error, and should be greater than 0.50 to justify using the construct [37]. Mixed support was found for the influence of social support on beliefs leading to Internet anxiety. First, we tested for the influence of social support on Internet anxiety. We found direct, negative effects from leader support and peer support to Internet anxiety. Next, we tested perceptions of resources and trust in technology’s influence on Internet anxiety. ## 5. Limitations

Before further discussing our results, it is important to consider the limitations of our work. The study was conducted using MIS students, but we believe that the teams had several parallels to virtual work teams in organizations. Team membership was randomly assigned and the students were in different sections. Therefore, group members were not acquaintances nor were they co-located. In addition, the semester-long projects on which they worked made up half of their grade, so the students had an incentive to take the assignments seriously. However, it is important to acknowledge that students differ from broader populations. Authors have suggested that students may be more innovative [39] and this may help to explain our findings that personal innovativeness in IT did not influence Internet anxiety. It is possible that there was a generally higher level of PIIT among the students than in the general population. Similarly, there may have been a generally high level of computer self-efficacy among the MIS students that also restricted the range of our data on this personality trait.

In addition, our results may be affected by common method variance because we collected the data from participants at the same time using the same survey. However, an inspection of the correlation of variables matrix shows that the relationships among the variables varied; thus common method variance was probably not responsible for our results [40]. Finally, our study involved a suite of Internet applications, not just one. Anxiety may be specific to the type of application, and the participants in our study might have experienced more anxiety about some applications than others, though we did not ask questions about specific applications.

## 6. Discussion

In this study we developed a model of Internet anxiety, which suggests that personality traits and individual beliefs directly affect Internet anxiety, and that social support affects Internet anxiety as mediated by individual beliefs. Our results showed support for some aspects of our model, but did not support all of our hypotheses.

Of our control variables, only computer anxiety was a significant predictor of Internet anxiety. Furthermore, much of the variance in Internet anxiety was explained by beliefs about the adequacy of resources available to support its use, and trust in the technology itself, leaving less variance to be explained by personality traits. From a practical standpoint, this is good news for organizations, because they can affect perceptions of resources and trust by providing reliable and useful technologies and giving users resources to support their use.

A second finding was that users’ perceptions of having adequate resources to enable the use of the technology reduced Internet anxiety. Organizations should therefore provide adequate resources, such as training. In addition, giving new users more time (to reduce pressure) and more attention from supervisors may reduce anxiety in a cost-effective way [41].

Our results showed that both leaders and peers had important influences on users’ beliefs, but that their roles were different. Leaders had significant influences on users’ perceptions of resources but our results showed that leaders did not significantly influence users’ trust in the technology. In contrast, peer support did not influence users’ beliefs about the adequacy of resources, but influenced users’ trust in technology.

Our results have practical ramifications. Leaders should work to make key resources available to all users, in order to reduce anxiety. In addition, they should work to make the broader environment in which the technology is used more comfortable for all users so that the peer influences help in building trust in the technology.

## 7. Conclusion

Our findings suggest that Internet anxiety is affected by users’ computer anxiety, which organizations cannot influence. However, it is also affected by beliefs that organizations can affect by providing adequate resources to support the technology, encouraging trust in technology, and working to assure users that leaders and peers are supportive of using the technology. Our findings suggest that, by providing resources and fostering a supportive environment, leaders can reduce Internet anxiety and may consequently influence technology use in their organizations.
