# A multi-group analysis of structural invariance: an illustration using the technology acceptance model

## Abstract

Selecting the appropriate mix of functional and/or interface characteristics to achieve user acceptance has proven to be a more challenging and difficult decision than expected. While numerous studies have shown that the technology acceptance model (TAM) is useful for predicting acceptance, estimates of its structural weights are not consistent across studies. Using initial exposure data from 742 users of office suite applications (i.e., spreadsheet, database, word processing, and graphics), our research illustrated the use of multi-group analysis of structural invariance (MASI) to test differences in structural weights across population subgroups for latent variables in TAM. We argue that, for large sample studies containing latent variables, MASI may be a more appropriate test of differences for structural weights/regression coefficients than analysis of covariance. The managerial implications of the results in setting functionality and interface goals and allocating resources to continued development efforts are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

More than half the cost of the development of complex software or IS is attributable to decisions made in the upstream stage, where many fuzzy ideas about functional and interface characteristics have to be converted into a requirements specification and design [37]. Effective software development requires the careful allocation of resources to work on functional and/or interface features. Adding functionality will improve user perceptions of the usefulness of the software and enhancing the user interface may make the software easier to use. However, selecting the appropriate mix of functional and/or interface characteristics to achieve user acceptance has proven to be difficult [30,35]. Davis [12] proposed measures of perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEU) as components of a technology acceptance model (TAM) [13] where intention to use (IU) was used as an indicator of user acceptance.

Knowledge of the relative efficacy (i.e., structural weights) of PU and ease of use in predicting user acceptance for a specific category of an application would be valuable in planning for new development or improvement projects. It could help developers set more appropriate design goals and make better decisions that improve functionality and/or interface characteristics. Better informed planning should improve the initial quality of the system, reduce rework after alpha and beta testing, enhance the payoff from improvement projects, reduce the cost of development, and shorten the development time.

During the alpha and/or beta test phases, users are exposed to the software and their perceptions of its usefulness and ease of use are obtained. Using user response information from these tests, developers plan enhancements of the software’s functional and interface characteristics. But, by this time, most of the systems development resources have been spent. Thus, prior knowledge of the efficacy (i.e., structural weights) of PU and PEU for a category of application is critical to improving the effectiveness of the software development effort.

Research on TAM might provide this prior knowledge [15,28,34]. Davis et al. advocated using TAM for predicting the acceptance/purchases of software during early development phases. PU is an end-user’s subjective probability that using a specific application will increase his/her job performance. PEU refers to the end-user’s expectations that the software is free of effort. IU is important for developers because it is viewed as a major factor influencing software adoption/purchase decisions and, thus, market share. The TAM model is an analytical simplification of how functionality and interface characteristics relate to adoption decisions. In alpha and beta testing, developers desire much more detailed and extensive information on user reactions to specific aspects of the software.

Many studies have been conducted using TAM. However, these studies have reported wide variations in structural weights for both PU and PEU across applications. In aggregate, they are inconclusive and provide limited help to a developer who wishes to know the relative efficacy of usefulness versus ease of use in predicting user acceptance. Thus, developers still have to rely on their own experience or intuition in allocating resources.

This paper illustrates the use of multi-group analysis of structural invariance (MASI) to test for differences in structural weights [9] of PU and PEU in predicting IU across four office suite applications (i.e., spreadsheet, database, word processing, and graphics). While office suite applications are relatively mature, the market is enormous and substantial resources continue to be deployed to enhance their functionality and interface characteristics or redesign these applications for a thin client or Web context.

When latent variables are involved, MASI may provide a more rigorous test of differences in structural weights across groups than analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). The test can answer the question: Do PU and PEU have equivalent structural weights predicting IU across the four office suite applications? Previous research by Doll et al. [16] has tested PU and PEU for measurement invariance. This research extends that study to examine structural weights and test for structural invariance among office suite applications.

PU and PEU have been applied to a variety of applications including e-mail, voice mail, word processing, spreadsheet, presentation graphics, customer dial-up, microcomputers, computer resource center, personal computing, telemedicine technology, scheduling, financial services, accounting services, banking, data retrieval, smart card payment system, and digital library. The reported structural weights are often application-specific, but several studies focus on clusters of applications under the label of microcomputers, computing resource center, or personal computing.

To be applicable for early development and acceptance decisions, a priori estimates of structural weights should be based on initial exposure data. Straub et al. and Szajna [45] have suggested that the efficacy of PEU may decline with experience. It is expected to have lower efficacy among on-going users. However, many of the studies have used samples of on-going use rather than initial exposure.

Thus many studies have used respondents who are ongoing users to predict post-implementation usage (e.g., [11,21,22,41,44,46]). In longitudinal studies, Venkatesh et al. [49], Venkatesh and Davis [47], Venkatesh and Morris [48], and Szajna use both initial exposure responses and later post-implementation responses. The research of Plouffe et al. [39] illustrates the use of a cross-sectional survey to gather initial exposure data.

The studies also utilize different dependent variables. Adams et al. and Igbaria, Guimaraes, and

Davis use TAM to predict perceived usage. Subramanian focuses on predicting future usage. Straub et al. and Igbaria et al. [24] focus on predicting system usage. Taylor and Todd and Hu et al. use PU and PEU to predict attitude, and they use attitude and PU to predict IU. Studies by Hong et al., Szajna, Venkatesh and Davis, Venkatesh and Morris focus on predicting IU. In contrast, Plouffe et al. use PU and PEU to predict intention to adopt.

Most of the studies modify Davis’ instruments in some way. Only Szajna uses the original 12 items for measuring PU and PEU. All other studies customized the measurement scales based on empirical assessments or contextual requirements/preferences.

The structural weights and t-values are summarized in the last four columns. These empirical studies suggest a wide variation across applications. It is thus difficult to compare studies and draw conclusions concerning the relative efficacy of PU and PEU across applications for several reasons:

\- Inconsistent structural weights reported within and among applications;

\- Variety in the applications studied;

\- Difference in the type of data gathered (i.e., initial exposure or post-implementation);

\- Differences in the dependent variables utilized; and

\- Differences across studies in the items used to measure PU and PEU.

In summary, several reasons explain the inconsistency of structural weights across studies. First, some studies modify the original instrument and/or introduce additional dependent variables. Second, the model is sometimes applied to computing facilities in general and not to a specific application. Third, some studies measure self-reported system usage and objective system use rather than IU. Fourth, many of the studies do not use ‘‘initial exposure’’ data, suggesting that the results do not provide a priori information. Thus, the results are not adequate to guide the goal setting and resources allocation decision of developers for major application suites.

## 2.2. Hypotheses

Most of what we know about the efficacy of office suite applications is based on Adams et al.’s study of

WordPerfect, Lotus 1-2-3, and Harvard Graphics and Chau’s [10] study of Microsoft Word and Excel. These studies suggest that the structural weights for PU and PEU differ widely across the office suite applications.

Adams et al.’s and Chau’s studies also show substantial differences in structural weights within the same type of application (WordPerfect versus Word, Lotus 1-2-3 versus Excel).

Office suite applications are used for different purposes—data analysis, data storage/inquiry/reporting, writing, and presentation preparation. They are also used by end-users with different levels of sophistication and for tasks of different levels of complexity. These differences may cause variation in the structural weights across applications. Of the four office suite applications, database is often used by more knowledgeable users doing more complex data storage and retrieval tasks.

However, the two studies used different scales for usefulness and ease of use, different dependent variables (perceived usage and IU), and different types of data (ongoing use versus initial exposure). Thus, it is also possible to argue that these observed differences across and within applications may be due to the differences in scaling, dependent variables, and/or the use of ongoing use versus initial exposure data.

It is plausible to argue that, if a study of office suite applications was conducted without these inconsistencies in methods and dependent variables, the observed differences in structural weights across some applications may disappear. Therefore, we proposed two null hypotheses:

\- H1: The structural weights of PU on IU are invariant across spreadsheet, database, word processing, and graphics applications.

\- H2: The structural weights of PEU on IU are invariant across spreadsheet, database, word processing, and graphics applications.

## 3. Research methods

The development of knowledge of structural weights to be used in guiding initial planning decisions should be based on initial exposure data; use the same measure of PU and PEU; and measure IU in the same way across all applications. Our research design achieved these goals by using a large sample of initial exposure respondents who answered a questionnaire based on Davis’s original measures of PU (6 items), PEU (6 items), and IU (2 items).

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to estimate the structural weights for PU and PEU for each of the four office suite applications. Harris and Schaubroeck [20] suggested a minimum sample size of 100 for confirmatory analysis, but recommended at least 200. Multi-group analysis of structural invariance was used to test whether observed differences in the structural weights across the applications are statistically significant [31,33].

## 3.1. The sample

A wide range of subjects representing a variety of backgrounds, demographics, education, and experience were surveyed in a lab experiment. The sample consisted of students in introductory undergraduate courses in IS at two large western and mid-western US universities. The courses were part of the core curriculum required for all business students, thus the respondents represented a number of business majors in marketing, management, finance, economics, hotel administration, human resources/industrial management, accounting, and MIS. The student population at each institution included a significant portion of non-traditional students (about 25%) having prior work experience.

The demographic dimensions included type of application and prior experience with computers in general (no experience, novice or experienced). The respondents with no experience were omitted from this study. Doll et al. indicate that respondents with no prior computing experience are not found to be tauequivalent, that is, the PU scores for users with no prior experience may be on a different scale and thus not comparable to other subgroups. This, along with the concerns raised by Adams et al. that respondents need some prior computing experience, led us to eliminate respondents with no prior computing experience. We also excluded responses that had missing data, yielding a usable sample of 742. The application subgroups included 224 spreadsheet users, 144 database users, 168 word processing users, and 206 graphics users. All of the application subgroups exceed a minimum sample size of 100.

## 3.2. The experimental treatment

Students were assigned to an application for which they had no prior application-specific experience. Then, they were grouped by this application and given a brief (1 h) demonstration of the software. During this, they were exposed to the breadth of functionality possible. Subjects were not allowed to use the software during this demonstration. We wanted to make sure that all subjects were focusing on the functionality being presented rather than ‘‘playing’’ with the software.

Immediately following this demonstration, subjects were given a brief (one-half hour) period to experience their assigned application in a hands-on manner. During this time, subjects were allowed to perform any type of experimentation. No assignment was given because: (1) the application types were different, and (2) the researchers do not want the subjects to feel that they ‘‘failed’’ to complete any assigned task. Finally, questionnaires are administered.

The data represented ‘‘initial exposure’’ responses, where the user did not have previous applicationspecific experience with the software. Thus, the lab experiment simulated the early development or prepurchase trial context for which TAM was designed. Favorable psychometric properties under conditions of relatively brief initial exposure were assumed to be encouraging relative to their use as early warning indicators of user acceptance [17].

We feel that findings based on student data are transferable to the referent population of business respondents. Goodhue [18] argues that system, task, individual characteristics, organizational characteristics, and beliefs about the expected consequences of use influence user evaluations of systems. Student findings are transferable because they use the same office suite applications, and they use them for the same purposes. The office suite applications are generic tasks. Thus, system and task characteristics are similar. Organizational characteristics should have less importance when using generic office tasks.

The two respondent groups overlap and merge over time. They overlap because many of the students (approximately 25%) were non-traditional, having at least some business experience. They also merge over time, because today’s business students become tomorrow’s business respondents. Thus individual characteristics of business respondents may differ from the business student population, but the two groups should have most characteristics in common. Today’s students may be inexperienced but the application was generic and we expected that student beliefs about the expected consequences of software use would be comparable to those of business respondents under similar exposure conditions.

The use of business students allowed us to control the initial exposure, ensuring uniform treatment and to control the types of application studied. Finally, given the large sample sizes required for invariance analysis, the use of student subjects almost a practical necessity.

## 3.3. Invariance analysis methods

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) models of factorial invariance enable one to test explicitly the structure of a model or its individual parameters for equivalence across subgroups or conditions. When parallel data exist for more than one group, CFA provides a particularly powerful test of the equivalence of solutions across the multiple groups. The researchers are able to fit the data subject to the constraint that any one, any set, or all parameters are equal in the multiple groups. The invariance analysis conducted utilized LISREL 8.3 [27].

Tests of factorial invariance across multiple groups involve a hierarchical ordering of nested models. Any two models are nested as long as the set of parameters estimated in the more restrictive model is a subset of the parameters estimated in the less restrictive model. Bentler [5] and Bentler and Bonnet [8] note the value of testing a series of nested models $\mathbf { M } _ { 0 } , \ldots , \mathbf { M } _ { i } , \ldots , \mathbf { M } _ { k } , \ldots , \mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { s } }$ in which $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathbf { o } }$ is a suitably defined null model, $\mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { s } }$ the saturated model with $\mathrm { d . f . } = 0$ and $\mathbf { M } _ { i }$ and $\mathbf { M } _ { k }$ are models with positive d.f. of intermediate complexity.

When one model is a subset of a larger model, the difference between them can be tested by subtracting the two chi-square values and testing this value against the critical value associated with the difference in degrees of freedom [29]. This chi-square test is very powerful and, where the hypothesis of equal factor loadings or structural weights is not rejected, it provides strong support that observed differences across subgroups in parameters may be explained by chance [32].

## 3.4. Sequence of invariance analysis

Prior to invariance testing, each subgroup was assessed. The decision to exclude a subgroup depended on a comprehensive assessment of itemfactor loadings and model-data fit. Low item-factor loadings would suggest that the factors did not have the same meaning in a subgroup, making comparisons meaningless. Item-factor loadings above 0.60 were considered acceptable [4]. Failure to achieve adequate model-data fit on one or more criteria should not, however, exclude a subgroup from further analysis. After these prerequisite assessments of subgroups, we tested first for equal pattern, then for equal item-factor loadings $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ equal lambda), and finally for equal structural weights (i.e., equal gamma).

The invariance of the item-factor loadings is critical since a failure to prove the invariance of the measurement model across the subgroups of interest pragmatically invalidates any further examination of model parameters. If the measurement model was not invariant across subgroups, then any examination of structural parameters could not be substantiated, even if that examination indicated structural invariance. Such a finding would be highly suspect, since any predictive aspects would be based upon measurement models that varied across subgroups [7].

Thus, the analysis was accomplished by evaluating two models nested in a hierarchical sequence with the less restrictive model used as a baseline for the evaluation of the more restrictive one. Thus, the chisquare difference between the nested models provided a test of invariance.

## 3.5. Criteria for evaluating model-data fit

Fit indices assess the congruence between the data and the model. No single fit index is advocated. Instead, it is suggested that several be reported, depending upon the type of analysis [50]. While the chi-square statistic is a global test of a model’s ability to reproduce the sample variance/covariance matrix, it is sensitive to sample size and departures from multivariate normality.

Here, several fit indices were used to assess modeldata fit. Steiger and Lind’s [42] root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), Bentler and Bonnet’s non-normed fit index (NNFI), and Bentler’s [6] comparative fit index (CFI) were all used to evaluate model-data fit. RMSEA below 0.050 suggests good model-data fit; between 0.050 and 0.100 it suggests acceptable model-data fit. NNFI and CFI indices greater than 0.90 suggest good model-data fit. NNFI and CFI indices greater than 0.80 suggest adequate model-data fit.

## 3.6. Analysis of covariance methods

Since analysis of covariance is a traditional method used to test for differences in regression coefficients among population subgroups [36], we were interested in comparing results obtained via multi-group analysis of structural invariance with those obtained via analysis of covariance. The ANCOVA is an analysis of variance model that contains one or more independent quantitative variables, or covariates, related to the dependent variable. It is widely used to test differences between treatment and control groups, even when latent factors are involved [19]. ANCOVA improves the precision of a statistical test by combining the features of analysis of variance and regression analysis. It reduces the variance of the error terms: this gives improved ability to detect treatment effects [38].

Here, the dependent variable was IU, the independent variables are the three dummy variables (representing the four office suite applications) and the covariates were PU and PEU. The covariance model can be stated as follows: $\mathbf { Y } = \mathbf { X } \beta + \varepsilon ,$ where Y is the vector of IU values, X a matrix containing the values for the dummy variables representing the different office suite applications and the covariates, $\mathbf { \beta }$ the vector of independent variables, and e a normal error terms such that $E ( \varepsilon ) = 0$ and $\mathrm { v a r } ( \varepsilon ) = \mathbf { I } \sigma ^ { 2 }$ , where I is the identity matrix and $\sigma ^ { 2 }$ the variance of the error terms. In this analysis, the items measuring PU, PEU, and IU are summed or averaged and treated as observed scores without measurement error.

## 4.2. Multi-group analysis of structural invariance

To test the invariance (equal weights) across applications, we conducted a multi-group analysis of structural invariance. The first step was to establish an ‘‘equal pattern’’ baseline model (Model 1 in Table 3). This equal pattern model had RMSEA, NNFI, and CFI of 0.119, 0.91, and 0.92, respectively. The baseline chi-square for the equal pattern model was 1065 with 296 d.f. These results indicated that the same pattern of parameters was able to fit the data for each application, but not that the parameter estimates took on the same, or even similar, values for the different groups.

Measurement equivalence was first verified using the method of Doll et al. Each item-factor loading (lambda) was forced to be equal across the four applications yielding Model 2 in Table 3. Model 2 has an RMSEA, NNFI, and CFI of 0.113, 0.92, and 0.92, respectively. The chi-square for Model 2 is 1100 with 329 d.f. The equal factor loadings model (Model 2) was nested within Model 1; that is, the set of parameters estimated in the more restrictive model (Model 2) was a subset of the parameters estimated in the less restrictive model (Model 1). Thus, the chisquare difference between Model 2 and Model 1 (35 with 33 d.f.) provided a test of the pre-condition (equal item-factor loadings) for testing the invariance of structural weights. Since the measurement model appeared to be invariant across subgroups $( P = 0 . 3 7 3 )$ , we could continue by testing the hypotheses concerning the structural weights.

The standardized structural weights for PU and PEU are shown in Table 4 for all the applications. These standardized structural weights were estimated with the item-factor loadings held equal across applications. Thus, they are the best estimates of the true structural weights. They were not affected by differences in itemfactor loadings across applications.

There were substantial differences in these structural weights (gammas) among applications. For spreadsheet, PU $( \gamma = 0 . 4 1 )$ and PEU $( \gamma = 0 . 4 4 )$ had roughly equivalent efficacy in predicting IU. ## 4.3. Post-hoc exploratory analysis of the differences in structural weights

Since we found that the four applications had different structural weights for both PU and PEU, we wanted to explore the nature of these differences to gain further information about which office applications may have similar or different structural weights for PU and PEU. The structural weights of database usage for both PU and PEU stand out as quite different from those of the other applications. For example, database had by far the highest structural weight for PU (0.73) and the lowest structural weight for PEU (0.10).

To determine whether database alone explained the significance of differences between usefulness and ease of use across applications, we examined whether PU and PEU were equivalent across spreadsheet, word processing, and graphic applications. The results of this post hoc analysis across the three applications (database omitted) suggested that their structural weights may be equivalent for PU, but not for PEU. A further pairwise analysis among the three applications suggested that PEU may have a higher structural weight for spreadsheet than word processing or graphics. In addition, word processing and graphics appeared to have equivalent structural weights for PEU.

While the post hoc analysis provided us with some potential insights on the nature of the differences in structural weights among applications, these results must be interpreted with caution. This post hoc analysis is exploratory in nature. All findings require cross-validation in another sample.

## 5. Implications for research and managerial decision making

The results have implications for studies that test the hypothesis that there are no differences in structural weights among subgroups. In latent variable models, multi-group analysis of structural invariance provides a rigorous or controlled test of differences in structural weights across applications (i.e., subgroups). The sequence of invariance analysis requires a demonstration that pre-conditions (equal pattern, equal item-factor loadings) are satisfied before testing for the equivalence of structural weights.

We view these two techniques for testing differences in structural weights/regression coefficients among groups as complementary rather than competitive. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) may be more useful in studies using observed scores with little or no measurement error. Multi-group analysis of structural invariance may be more appropriate in large sample (e.g., greater than 100 respondents per group) studies using latent variables. Smaller sample studies using latent variables are more problematic. With multiple groups, the required sample size may limit the applicability of multi-group analysis of structural invariance to studies that are well endowed with respondents.

If researchers use ANCOVA in latent variable models to test differences in regression coefficients across groups, they should be cautious in interpreting results. They should be aware that scale differences across groups might confound their analysis. Regardless of whether significant differences are found, the findings may be due to measurement differences (e.g., item-factor true scores) among groups rather than real differences in regression coefficients.

Since this study dealt with latent variables and the sample size was adequate, our discussion of the practical implications of the results was based on the multi-group analysis of structural invariance. The results suggested that there are substantial differences among office suite applications in the importance of functionality and interface characteristics in predicting IU.

In setting development goals and allocating initial resources, the results suggested that database is untypical of other office suite applications. Of the applications, it had the largest and most significant structural weight (0.73, t-value = 11.00) for PU, but a small and non-significant coefficient for PEU (0.10, tvalue = 1.33). This suggested that PU is the dominant consideration in predicting IU a database application. PEU appeared to be a negligible consideration.

Few end-users opt to utilize a database application out of casual need. The motivation to utilize a database application is usually precipitated by significant need and generally requires substantial domain knowledge. Simple data manipulations are typically addressed using manual techniques or a spreadsheet. Users expect a database application to be somewhat difficult to use. Thus the greater the complexity of the required data manipulations, the more substantial the required domain knowledge, and the greater the user’s need for the capabilities offered by the software package, the more negligible the influence of PEU and the more dominant the role of usefulness in predicting IU.

For spreadsheet applications, PU and PEU have roughly equal importance in predicting IU. The standardized structural weights were 0.41 and 0.44, respectively. Thus, software development managers, who are responsible for the continued development or redesign for a thin client or the Web context, need to balance the goal of improved functionality with that of improved interface characteristics.

This balance of the importance of functional and interface characteristics in spreadsheet applications makes some intuitive sense. First, relative to database, use of spreadsheet applications typically requires somewhat less complex data manipulations and less new domain knowledge. Second, there are several alternatives to spreadsheets, such as calculators. Thus, the user has a point of comparison to judge the spreadsheet’s ease of use and, perhaps, a lower perception of the need for the unique capabilities offered by spreadsheet application. Third, while many software packages have features that enhance the functionality or interface characteristics, a spreadsheet has many features that simultaneously enhance both usefulness and ease of use. For example, when a user copies a formula with relative addressing from one cell and pastes it elsewhere, the parameters within the formula change automatically. This copy–paste feature improves both functionality (usefulness) and interface characteristics (ease of use).

Both word processing and graphics applications showed similar patterns of the efficacy of PU and PEU on IU. While both PU (structural weight = 0.51 and 0.55 for word processing and graphics, respectively) and PEU (0.27 and 0.25 for word processing and graphics, respectively) were important, PU had a higher structural weight than PEU in predicting IU in both applications.

Thus, everything else being equal, managers who are responsible for the initial development or improvement of word processing and graphic applications need to allocate more resources to functionality than to interface characteristics. While the results are clear, the reasons for this pattern of structural weights are not.

## 6. Conclusions

This paper illustrated the use of multi-group analysis of structural invariance (MASI) to test differences in structural weights across population subgroups. We suggest that MASI and the betterknown analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) are complementary rather than competitive tests. For models with latent variables, MASI is more appropriate for testing hypotheses about differences in structural weights across subgroups if the sample size is adequate.

This study improves our understanding of the relative efficacy of PU and PEU in predicting IU across office suite applications by providing a large sample with consistent measures. The findings have implications for making decisions concerning development goals and resource allocations to improve the functionality and/or interface characteristics of software systems (e.g., office suite) or modules. For example, in the design of graphic output modules in enterprise applications, the weights that users place on usefulness and ease of use may be expected to be similar to the weights found here for Harvard Graphics.

While this study suggests that substantial differences exist among office suite applications in the importance of functionality and interface characteristics, it does not identify the factors that cause these differences.
