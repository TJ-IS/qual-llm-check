## 1. Introduction

Social commerce has become a new stream of e-commerce with the development of social media. Applications, such as adding commercial features to social networking sites (SNSs) and adding social networking capabilities to e-commerce sites empower people to generate content and share information, experiences, opinion, and knowledge about products or services, which forms various types of social interaction, coordination, and participation.

Social interactions include not only personal chats and discussions, product reviews and evaluations, offering suggestions, and giving information, but also sharing opinions, ideas, recommendations, and referrals [1, 2].Recommendations and referrals have been acknowledged for many years as a major influence in online commerce. For example, recommendations strengthen the relationship between customers and a company [3]. Credible recommendation posts affect consumers’ willingness to purchase products [4]. Online recommendations influence consumers’ online product choices [5], and the perceived usefulness and trustworthiness of bloggers’ recommendations have significant influential effects on blog users’ intention to shop online [6].

Since recommendations play an important role in commerce [7], modeling recommendation behaviors remains an important research area. Investigating why users engage in product recommendation has drawn attention for many years. For example, high levels of positive emotional experiences among tourists, emotional responses in general, and satisfaction are associated with a high propensity to recommend [8–12]. A destination’s characteristics and affective image influence tourists’ intention to recommend it to others [13, 14]. In an online environment, actionable website characteristics and website quality were acknowledged as major influences on recommendation behavior [15, 16]; however, users' satisfaction and obtained hedonic benefits also determine recommendations [17].

Considering that social media platforms facilitate individual interaction and promote a shift from inefficient individual-based consumption decisions to collaborative sharing and social shopping [18], recommendation behavior in an SNS context has also garnered attention in recent years. Some researchers have also found that consumer’s attitudes toward a brand, ad, and viral advertising, in general, all significantly influence pass-on behavior on SNSs [19]. Yang (2013) found that young Chinese consumers’ frequency of product recommendations on social networking websites was determined by their social media use, viral marketing attitude, and motive to help the company and market mavenism [20].

In contrast to other online social networking communities actively nurtured by one company, online social networking communities in the paper refer to the purely established and maintained ones by the users in the communities. The community is usually constructed from an everyday-based circle of people, such as family, friends, schoolmates, and acquaintances [21]. Sharing supportive information in the communities is significant to maintain ties and supportive relationships. Specifically, while electronic word-of-mouth (e-WOM) communicators often do not feel much responsibility for the consequences of their recommendations because their postings will be read by complete strangers [22], a receiver might attribute his or her dissatisfaction to the recommender when he or she is dissatisfied with the product in the online social networking community [23]. Some researchers also found that consumers were less willing to engage in electronic word-of-mouth on social sites (sWOM) than in WOM, and perceived social risk influenced people’s desire to engage in sWOM [24]. This also demonstrates that product recommendation is different from other types of information sharing when considering that an individual’s reputation is embedded in the behavior, and it increases social risk in an everyday-based circle of people.

Thus, despite the research acknowledging the importance of studying consumer’s attitudes, emotions, and ability regarding a product in referral behavior in online communities [25, 26], they ignore the influence of atmospheric cues. Indeed, atmospheric cues’ influence has been confirmed in different environments, such as hospital, tourist destination traditional store, and online shopping environment [27–30]. Thus, we argue that several atmospheric cues could be salient and play significant roles in online socia networking communities. Moreover, academic researchers have not yet analyzed whether community atmosphere is particularly effective in product recommendation in the SNS context. Consequently, our theoretical understanding of the role of the online social networking community atmosphere in referral behavior is limited.

#

To address the gap, we adopt the stimulus–organism–response (S–O–R) framework and build our study by combining community reciprocity, community receptivity, and the affective social distance concept. Using these theoretical lenses, we focus on the role of atmospheric factors by examining how community reciprocity and community receptivity influence the sender's behavior and by exploring the role of affective social distance in a recommendation to reflect the importance of the relationship between users in a social networking community.

This paper makes three main contributions. First, it establishes atmospheric cues determining individual product recommendation behavior. Second, it distinguishes community atmospheric cues and analyzes how these cues influence members’ produc recommendations in an online social networking community. Third, it examines the mediating role of affective social distance, considering users’ internal processes in a socia networking environment. The findings enrich our understanding of product recommendations in social networking communities and provide new insights into S–O–R analysis in online social networking communities.

In the next section, we present the conceptual framework and research hypotheses. We then describe the research methodology and present empirical evidence, after which we discuss the theoretical and practical implications. Finally, we conclude by discussing the limitations of our study and future research avenues.

## 2 Theoretical background and hypotheses

## 2.1 Theoretical model

The theoretical model used for the current study is based on environmental psychology as proposed by Mehrabian and Russell (1974). This model claimed that environmental stimuli (S) influence users’ behavioral responses (R) through affect organism process (O). Stimuli can be conceptualized as the factors influencing internal states of an individual. Organism refers to the internal processes and outcomes of the stimulus, normally playing a mediating role between stimulus and responses. The term response refers to the outcome of a stimulus and an organism [31, 32].

The application of the SOR paradigm as a theoretical background is appropriate for this study for two reasons. First, exploring individual cognitive and affective processes are important research areas in an E-commerce context. The S–O–R model solves the “rationality assumption” problem of the original input–output model by emphasizing individual cognitive and affective processes [33]. An individual focused on a specific aspect of a situation will most likely select the meaning attached to that aspect in cognitive processing, which usually decreases or magnifies emotional responses, as well as changes

#

the emotion itself [34]. Some researchers have confirmed that customers’ emotional (pleasure, arousal, flow, and perceived enjoyment) and cognitive states (perceived usefulness, cognition involvement, and engagement), as organism reactions, influenced consumer’s evaluation, purchase, and post-purchase behavior according to the S–O–R model in an E-commerce context [35–39]. In customers’ online environment, the stimuli refer not only to actual stimuli but also to customer assessments of the stimuli to denote the stimulus segment of the model [40]. Community atmosphere, as one type of stimul assessed by users, has been used in some researches [30, 41]. In the paper, community atmosphere including community reciprocity and community receptivity can be perceived by users in community. Thus, S–O–R model is fit to explore cognitive and affective processes in online social networking community environment.

Second, users in an online social networking community face a complicated and varied environment shaped by individuals’ interactions. The content generated by users not only gradually alters the environment but also exerts influence on peer members. The S–O–R framework may be a better fit to model psychological phenomena in a complicated and dynamic environment [31, 37]. Based on the S–O–R model, Hu and his colleague confirmed that both technical and social cues shaped the environment which impacted a consumer’s evaluations and decision-making process while using a social shopping website [42].

In the current study, the stimuli are operationalized as various atmospheric cues embedded in SNS environments. The organism is perceptual affective social distance that users elicit when they communicate in one social networking community. The responses are operationalized as users’ product recommendation behavior. The components of stimuli, organisms, and behavioral responses in SNS environments are further investigated below.

## 2.2 Product recommendation

With the development of social media, people interact with others in an online context and create user-generated content (UGC) such as product reviews, opinions, experiences, and products and services recommendations. As one new type of UGC, recommendations and referrals play an important role in social commerce [43]. E-commerce sites embodying social media features, as one main type of social commerce, obtain more attention. Some researchers have explored recommendation behavior in the context of E-commerce sites and found that information from recommendations impacted box office sales and consumers’ online choices [5, 44, 45].

However, it is difficult for receivers to determine the quality and credibility of the product recommendations supplied by senders in E-commerce sites’ online platforms due to weak ties between the senders and the receivers [46]. In fact, individuals tend to trust more of information that comes from familiar and similar others, since these interpersonal sources have no commercial intent and therefore benefit from a higher credibility [47]. For instance,

#

when a friend on an online social network makes a product recommendation to other members, they are likely to have a high level of trust in the product.

Users’ recommendation behaviors in the social network communities, which are nurtured by them, are essentially Individuals’ voluntary behaviors. In the context, product recommendation is sharing of supportive information sent by users who have not been taken advantage of by the commercial social sites. The motives to recommend a product or a service may be to obtain psychological gratification and to enhance friendship and trust among members. That, the senders perceive their efforts as being needed, valued by peers who have been helped by the recommending information, should be the main mechanism of product recommendations in the communities.

## 2.3 Community reciprocity and product recommendation

Reciprocity is a powerful determinant of human behavior. Generalized reciprocity refers to behavioral responses to perceived kindness and unkindness. Some researchers define reciprocity as voluntary and discretionary behaviors related to helping not only those who help the giver but also other members in the virtual community who need help and who would provide assistance on request [48]. Some researchers note that reciprocity does not involve direct reward, but indirect benefits in a social exchange situation with a benefactor and oneself [49]. In sum, researchers regard reciprocity as an extremely important concept when investigating sharing and resource exchanges among individuals in social systems [50]. In the study, we adopt Chang and Chuang’s (2011) definition [51], that is, community reciprocity is individuals’ perception of fairness in sharing information, experiences, and other content mutually in a social networking community.

Patterns of social relations would affect actors’ motives. A strong reciprocity norm enhances individuals’ trust in others and reinforces the bonds between people and creates the basis for high-quality relationships in a group [52]. The strength of a consumer’s relationship elicits consumer’s intrinsic motivation to interact and cooperate with community members, such as helping other members and participating in joint activities in the brand community [53]. Some research also confirmed that individuals guided by a norm of reciprocity will contribute more information sharing and helpful responses to electronic networks of practice [49, 54]. Thus, we expect the perception of community reciprocity to elicit members’ intention to produce a positive outcome.

In particular, community reciprocity will enhance the behavior of supplying recommending information which is helpful to others in the community. Usually, customers intend to make a recommendation when they experience a satisfactory outcome from a product or a service [15]. In other words, the product, which satisfies the users requirement, will be helpful to others. In a community with reciprocal atmosphere, members can perceive community’s norm and its impact on individual behavior.

Correspondingly, it would be natural for community members to share their own unique experience information as an obligation to support each other.

On this basis, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1. Community reciprocity has a positive impact on product recommendation behavior.

## 2.4 Community receptivity and product recommendation

Cues in a certain environment will influence people’s behavior by conveying important information or triggering an affective reaction [55]. Community receptivity, as one type of environmental cue, will promote social norms’ influence on behavior.

Social norms usually exert an influence on behavior when they are primed with cues from the environment [56]. In the SNSs context, social norms, especially the receiver's norms on the evaluation of others, could influence senders’ online behavior [57]. Individuals will feel that no one cares or pays attention to them and experience feelings of alienation if he or she often receives no responses after posting content in an online community. Some research also confirmed that the feeling may adversely affect the learning processes in virtual classrooms [58]. Thus, an individual may be uncertain about the norms that are being established in the group and are inhibited regarding community involvement when the members do not respond to his or her communication.

Community receptivity can be perceived in virtual community. For example, when teachers encourage students to express their own opinions and discuss various issues with people who have different opinions in class, it fosters an atmosphere of classroom receptivity, and the atmosphere perceived by students has a positive effect on students learning [59]. Further, people wish to feel good about themselves, and therefore, they react positively when positive evaluations from others are received [60]. Thus, when a community provides an environment where numbers can freely express their opinions, members will increase the level of engagement [61].

Additionally, computer-mediated interaction is less organized and rule-oriented [62], which requires people to exert additional effort in judging which behaviors are appropriate in different situations. Some research also confirmed that people will decrease willingness to offer sWOM compared to WOM when faced with a greater social risk in an SNS context [24].

Community receptivity decreases members’ social risk. When perceiving others’ open attitude and kindness, a user is less worried when suggesting related product or service information to peers in the community. Conversely, if the community lacks receptivity, members will cautiously decide whether supplying a recommendation is an appropriate behavior.

Thus, we have the following hypothesis.

H2. Community receptivity has a positive impact on product recommendation behavior.

## 2.5 Affective social distance and recommendation behavior

Social distance refers to the degree of intimacy or acceptance that people feel toward others in social relationships (Graham 1995). As an associated construct, affective social distance describes how much or little sympathy the members of a group feel for other members. Our conception of affective social distance also focuses on affectivity as proposed by Shi, Hong, Wang, and Pavlou (2014) [63]. That is, affective social distance focuses on the affective personal relationship of sympathy toward other members in an online community

Furthermore, when users experience a large affective social distance in an online community, it means that they have the least knowledge of other people in the community. They cannot clearly understand other members’ motives, preferences, and intentions; therefore, they tend to adopt a serious attitude toward interactions. In contrast, a close affective social distance between people in an online community denotes an intimate relationship and can be characterized by a higher level of understanding. They share similar values, have many topics to talk about, and know each other very well [64].

Social distance may influence people’s evaluations and actions [63]. The more they saw the partner as receptive and understanding, the more they judged the partner as friendly and dependable. Also, the more they felt connected to the partner, the more they liked the partner [50]. Furthermore, some researchers have found that the social distance will positively influence the receiver's perceptions about the review when browsing an online consumer review [65].

When consumers face a substantial amount of product options in online commerce environments, consumers’ limited processing capacity can become cognitively overloaded if they attempt to process ‘‘too much” information in a limited time, and this can result in confusion, cognitive strain, and other dysfunctional consequences [66]. Considering that online information overload results in less satisfied, less confident, and more confused consumers [67], they need reliable sources of information to resolve the problem. Product recommendations from their closest friends or peers are helpful for decision making on purchases because it provides consumers with “accurate’” experiences based on abundant mutual understanding. Thus, product recommendations reduce individuals’ information overload, especially when they face uncertain and ambiguous choices. Moreover, laboratory experiments have found that altruistic behavior is promoted through the reduction of social distance [68, 69]. Thus, we expect that affective social distance will be significantly associated with product recommendation behavior.

Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

H3. Affective social distance has a negative impact on product recommendation behavior.

## 2.6 Community reciprocity, affective social distance, and product recommendation

We posit that community reciprocity can also be indirectly related to product recommendation behavior through affective social distance, which describes a specific relationship between an online group’s atmosphere and individual behavior. Reciprocity may influence members’ evaluation and perception. In virtual communities, consumers’ reciprocating behaviors affect their attitudes toward the community and behavioral intentions to co-shop with others in the community [48]. As Mathwick and colleagues (2008) suggest, reciprocity significantly increases the community’s perceived informational value [70].

Social distance also influences people’s evaluation and actions, which is elicited by interpersonal similarity [62, 69]. Interpersonal similarity refers to the degree to which individuals who interact with one another are congruent or similar in certain attributes, and it is closely related to the concept of homophily [70, 71]. The assumption that interpersonal similarity conveys social closeness has formed the basis for research on comparison processes with regard to close and distant others [62, 72]. The less similar another is to oneself, the more socially distant he/she seems [69, 72]. Therefore, the behavior of a dissimilar other (socially distant) requires a higher level of construal than the behavior of a similar other (socially close) [73, 74].

According to Graham (1995), social distance can be reduced by identifying similarities between people, such as needs and values and interest [71, 72]. Johar (2005) found that value similarity can affect the social distance between supervisors and subordinates, which is the result of activating norms associated with relationships [64]. Kim, Lee, and Wong (2015) concluded that leaders’ use of humor may reduce social distance between leaders and subordinates by enhancing similarities [73]. Su and his colleague (2015) found that interest similarity is one critical driver of friendship between hotel brands and consumers in the context of SNSs [74].

In an SNS context characterized by community reciprocity, that everyone abides by the same social norm creates the belief that another person has a similar value. Specifically, users’ value similarity is enhanced by a sense of mutual reciprocity. Thus, perceptions of community reciprocity are more likely to elicit a closely intimate affective social distance.

Furthermore, social norms are most likely to be involved when individuals perceive their efforts as being needed, valued, and appreciated [75]. According to Hernández-Ortega (2017), the receiver will evaluate the information in a self-referential manner, feel greater emotional intensity, and perceive psychological proximity with the reviewer if the experience-based review is consistent with the receiver's own life [65]. People also fee more empathy toward people who they perceive as being more like themselves [76], and there is considerable evidence that feelings of empathy are strongly connected to altruistic and prosocial behavior [68, 77]. Thus, considering that members will obtain more psychological gratification when they perceive close affective social distance, the belief and norm anticipation of people’s mutual reciprocity would influence product recommendation behavior through affective social distance.

However, people in online social networking communities without a distinct reciprocity norm are essentially strangers to each other. They have no intention to develop more intimate relationships and are less likely to elicit perceptions of close affective social distance. Hence, they will not experience strong emotions and thus will get less psychological gratification through making recommendations, and it becomes reasonable to reduce the behavior. Other research has also confirmed a similar mechanism in a sales context and found that shared similarity increased participants’ feeling of connectedness with the trainer, and this feeling mediated the relationship between incidental similarity and the attitudes formed [78].

Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4. Community reciprocity has a negative impact on affective social distance.

H5. Affective social distance mediates the positive effect of community reciprocity on product recommendation behavior.

## 2.7 Community receptivity, affective social distance, and product recommendation

Experimental studies in social psychology confirmed that environmental cues have an influence on individuals’ behavior [79]. Community receptivity, as one type of community atmospheric cue, should have an impact on users’ product recommendation behavior. A relaxed communication atmosphere encourages frequent social contact in an SNS context, which makes members in a group more likely to understand each other. According to Hidalgo, Tan, and Verlegh’s (2017) study, in successful online social sharing of emotion, “first the initiator is engaged in a process of narration of an emotional experience, and second, a listener provides socio-affective feedback, acting in an affiliative stance and thus experiencing a certain degree of empathy towards the initiator” [80]. In this process, individual members and their activities increasingly encounter one another, and they may be more likely to build relationships and foster warm and close personal relationships. Similarly, individuals can find and provide emotional support, companionship, and encouragement in social interactions [81]. Therefore, individuals in an online social community who are open and receptive will perceive a close affective social distance.

#

Psychological distance can be approximated by familiarity and affection [82]. A positive affect fosters an enhanced responsiveness to situations [83]. In fact, some researchers have suggested that people who feel connected to others perceive them as more friendly and tend to experience greater emotion sharing [84]. Since personal but irrelevant communication significantly decreased social distance and increases altruism and trust [85], we expect that a relaxed communication atmosphere affects an individual’s internal response to a peer’s situation and decreases affective social distance, as well as elicits enhanced responsiveness and altruistic motivations and behaviors in social media platforms.

A similar mechanism has also been confirmed between supervisors’ use of humor and employee outcomes. Supervisors’ self-enhancing humor elicits a positive affective experience in their supervisees and a relaxing environment, which may reduce the social distance between supervisor and subordinate. Correspondingly, the outcome of positive emotion triggered by the environment is increased employee psychological well-being and job performance [73].

Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H6. Community receptivity has a negative impact on affective social distance.

H7. Affective social distance mediates the positive effect of community receptivity on product recommendation behavior.

Additionally, considering that individuals’ past behavior is taken as anchoring biases and affects their future behavior [49, 86], we control the effect by including the variables of previous experience using social media platforms in our conceptual framework. Furthermore, since education level and gender are usually included as a control variable in related studies [49, 87], we also control the effect of education level and gender in our conceptual framework.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Research setting

We approached WeChat in China, a mobile instant text and voice messaging communication service developed by Tencent Holdings Ltd., to test our hypotheses. In the mobile Internet era, WeChat and other mobile social applications (APPs) have the advantage of convenience, and WeChat is the most widely used social networking service in China. It has become an important social media platform for computer-mediated communication [88]. Users can communicate and interact with friends and others through text messaging, hold-to-talk voice messaging, one-to-many messaging, many-to-many messaging, and photo/video sharing (http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WeChat).

#

Usually, economic incentive is an important driver of consumers’ review and referra behaviors in online communities and viral advertising [19, 89]. With weak ties, people typically have exchange relationships, driven primarily by self-interest. In such relationships, participants try to maximize their own outcomes and minimize their costs. Conversely, with strong ties, people tend to have communal relationships in which they feel a general concern about the other person’s welfare. Thus, the rewards are not particularly effective in increasing referral likelihood to strong ties [23].

The social network community established by WeChat users is different from other online communities composed of strangers. WeChat communities are mainly formed by strong connections and supplemented by weak links, and more than 80% users in WeChat group are friends, acquaintances, and colleagues who are familiar with each other in real life (http://www.cnnic.net.cn/hlwfzyj/). The composition characteristics indicate that environmental cues are more effective than economic incentives in most online communities on the WeChat platform.

## 3.2. Development of measures

To test the research model, a questionnaire was developed based on the literature review in the previous sections. The questionnaire consists of two parts. The first part captures the socio-demographics of the participants, including age, gender, educational levels, and experiences with WeChat. The second part includes the variables identified in the research questions.

Consumers often turn to others for a recommendation when they are thinking of purchasing [90]. In the online context, potential consumers often require peers’ experience and evaluation to determine whether the description of a product coincides with their understanding, especially when they face an uncertain and ambiguous choice. From a community member’s viewpoint, a product recommendation is not only a response to others’ requirements but also a voluntary and active sharing behavior in the online community. Thus, we use the items related to product recommendations based on Yang (2013) [20]. The scale of community reciprocity measures the degree to which numbers perceive community fairness, experience and information sharing, and other content mutually in a social networking community. The scale was adapted from Chang and Chuang’s (2011) and Diep and his colleagues’ (2016) work [51, 91]. Community receptivity measures the extent to which the members listen and are open to one another’s ideas in an online community; the items were adapted from Pai and Tsai (2016) [49]. Affective social distance refers to the degree to which numbers perceive sympathy toward the others in an online community. The scale was modified from Hernández-Ortega (2017) [65]. The items related to product recommendation are measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from (1) never, (2) seldom, (3) sometimes, (4) often, to (5) always. All the other items are measured on a 5- point Likert scale ranging from (1) strongly disagree, (2) disagree, (3) neither agree nor disagree, (4) agree, to (5) strongly agree.

After the questionnaire was developed, experts in the field of information sciences and consumer behavior were consulted for the wording and face validity of the items. Subsequently, the questionnaire was translated into Chinese and afterward back-translated by a native-English speaker to ensure that equivalent sentences were used. All the following measures were pilot tested in April 2017 with a sample of undergraduate students. Some items were revised after the reliability check, and the final items are listed in Appendix A.

## 3.3. Sample and data collection

The samples were randomly collected from the database of Sojump (www.sojump.com) which is a company providing questionnaire services. The members in the database consist of students (26.3%), enterprise staff (39.2%), administrative staff (10.2%), scientific researchers (9.7%), public servants (4.2%), professionals (3.1%), freelancers (1.8%), and others (5.5%). According to the company’s procedures, users should post the questionnaire on the website first. The company will randomly select respondents according to users requirements and invite respondents to complete the questionnaire online. Afterwards, users will receive the respondents’ data from the website.

Following this procedure, the Internet survey was collected during May 15–18, 2017. Correspondingly, the Internet survey firm randomly distributed questionnaires to 400 potential respondents. Each respondent was required to answer the first question regarding whether he or she has joined a WeChat community consisting of relatives, friends, schoolmates, or colleagues. The survey was automatically terminated if the answer was “no.” Via this procedure, 388 questionnaires were collected. To validate the quality of sample collection, we rejected the survey if respondents completed their questionnaires too quickly or if participants answered “strongly disagree” or ‘‘strongly agree’’ to all questions. Finally, 307 valid questionnaires were coded for analysis. Table 1 presents a summary of the respondents’ characteristics regarding gender, age, educational level, and frequency of using WeChat.

## 3.4. Common method bias

Because we collected data using a single source method with self-reporting scales, the results could be affected by common method bias. We used the procedural remedies suggested by MacKenzie and Podsakoff [92] during data collection and analysis to address this issue: the procedural remedies include guaranteeing participants’ anonymity, the items of independent variables were listed before the items of dependent variables, and respondents could not access their responses to previous questions. In addition, testing for common method variance was necessary [93]. First, Harman’s one-factor test was employed on the four principal constructs in the conceptual framework. The analysis resulting from the Harmon one-factor test suggested that the principal factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were generated with no single factor accounting for the majority of the variance in the items. The most covariance explained by the first factor is 34.85%, indicating that common method bias is not a serious concern.

Second, considering that the measured latent marker variable approach can help detect and control common method bias [94], we use the construct-level correction approach with three unrelated items to apply the marker variable method. ## 4. Data analysis

The proposed model and hypothesized relationships among constructs were evaluated by using SEM-PLS modeling in SmartPLS 3.0M. PLS has become popular in modern research, particularly because it has specific advantages, such as minimal demands on measurement scales, sample distribution, and sample size. It excels at causal-predictive analysis, in that hypothesized relationships are complex, and few bases have been established [95].

To assess the adequacy of our sample, considering that Cohen’s formula has been shown to correctly predict power in most cases [96], we calculated Cohen’s power for PLS analysis, and the analysis indicated that our sample size is able to detect a medium effect size at the alpha level of 0.05 and the power level of 0.95 [97]. Additionally, to determine the conditions which meet the PLS requirement [98], we summarized the distributional shape of the data which is reflected by its skewness and kurtosis (Appendix A). According to Martin’s (2009) and Goodhue’s (2012) suggestion [99, 100], the results in the table indicate that we adopted a partial least squares (PLS) estimation technique as this method is appropriate.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1 Theoretical Implications

In an SNS context, the growing literature of social media use contains a quite consistent finding that social networking websites are used to maintain offline relationships with friends, relatives, colleagues, and other acquaintances [20].Some researchers have studied information searching, browsing, sharing, evaluating alternative products, and purchase behavior in social commerce [109], while few studies have explored product or service recommendations on social media platforms. Although consumers tend to trust more of information from recommendations that come from familiar and similar others due to their perception of the recommender’s good reputation, product recommendations may involve different mechanisms with general information-sharing behaviors, considering that the receiver might attribute his or her dissatisfaction to the recommender when he or she is dissatisfied with the product [23].

Some prior studies explored the antecedents to recommendation behavior (e.g. satisfaction, attitude [15-16]), yet further research exploring atmosphere cues’ influence is limited. The results of this study show that community reciprocity and community receptivity are important atmosphere cues in influencing members’ product recommendation behavior. These results further confirm and explain the role of atmosphere cues’ influence on online behavior in the context of social commerce. The importance of community atmosphere in community behavior is also consistent with existing research. For example, Yang and Huang (2017) found that perceived freedom of expression has a positive effect on member commitment in an online community [110]. All the results reinforce the importance of community atmosphere— a critical factor for online community success. Although the factors we identify in this study are only a part of atmosphere clues, they shed light on this new phenomenon and serve as a precursor for recognizing other atmosphere clues that are crucial to understanding product recommending behavior in social media environments.

Additionally, our study contributes to the reciprocity literature. We use the concept of community reciprocity, which represents the extent to which members perceive community fairness. This concept is extended from the concept of Chang and Chuang (2011). They noted that reciprocity reflected “individual perception of fairness to share content mutually in a virtual community” and focused on individual feelings of others giving help, which is different from the norm of reciprocity in Pai and Tsai’s study (2016) [49], where they focused on identifying factors that foster individuals’ reciprocity beliefs and obligations related to helping others in a community. We consider reciprocity as a type of community atmosphere and confirm that community reciprocity plays a direct and crucial role in determining members’ product recommendation behavior in social media, which differs from reciprocity’s role as examined by existing studies.

At the same time, based on a synthesis of findings from previous literature related to social distance, it is confirmed that social distance may influence consumers’ prosocial beliefs and behaviors [111–113]. Since social distance plays an important role in influencing people’s evaluation and actions, how to mitigate the sense of distance has also drawn mounting attention in the literature. Previous research indicates that the similarity perceived by the receiver in the online consumer review significantly influences the social psychological distance [65]. Our results complement the antecedents to social distance in that we examine the role of community atmosphere. It is important to note that community reciprocity plays a more important role than community receptivity in fostering affective social distance. A possible explanation is that community reciprocity atmosphere enables community members more willing to engage in positive social interactions that are consistent with their expectations. In turn, social distance will decrease when the community members obtain a positive benefit (e.g. friendship) from the social interactions.

Based on the S–O–R model, our study extended affective social distance’s interpretation on product recommendation in an SNS context. Affective social distance not only directly

#

impact individual product recommendations but also mediated the relationship between community reciprocity, community receptivity, and product recommendation. Previous studies that applied the S–O–R framework demonstrate that individual internal state (organism) can mediate between stimuli and response behavior [35 40]. Community reciprocity and community receptivity, as atmospherics cues, can be evaluated by members in online social community. In turn, community reciprocity and community receptivity impact members’ internal state. The results of this study indicate that the effect of community reciprocity and community receptivity on product recommendation is partially mediated by the affective social distance’s organism. In fact, the mediating model shows that the community atmosphere’s effect on product recommendation is enhanced through the mechanism of mitigating affective social distance. In other words, it implies that the change of members distance perception impacts online community behavior. Compared with members holding not-so-intimate affective social distance, members holding close affective social distance are more willing to engage in positive and cooperative interactions.

## 5.2 Practical implications

Our results will increase social platform managers’ understanding of the recommendation mechanism in online social network communities. For SNSs, our results show that affective social distance is a critical factor in members’ product recommendation behavior. A close affective social distance will enhance inclinations toward making product recommendations, and strong evidence shows that personal but irrelevant communication significantly decreases social distance [85].

Thus, for social platforms like WeChat, it is necessary to develop some technologies which encourage various types of communications and promote the emergence of a closer social distance. Integrating multiple communication functions as a portfolio (e.g. sending text messages, voice chatting, posting pictures and video) will enable users to make more flexible interactions with peers in online communities. In addition, integrating multiple tools to benefit collective-based communication (e.g. group chatting, group video chatting, social network games) will promote the formation of members’ belief that they belong to a certain group.

In addition, similarity is an important antecedent of affective social distance. WeChat providers can develop colorful peer characteristic label tools to help users manage the various similarities between users and their peers, which facilitate users’ interaction with peers and trigger the sense of close connections in an online social community. Social objects are essentially ambiguous and can be constructed through social processes [114]. Users attach labels to different types of communities and selectively assign positively valued identities to those whom they prefer, which facilitate to resolve ambiguity and make perceived communities environment meaningful. Furthermore, once labeled, communities tend to be perceived in terms of the schema affixed to the label [115]. For example, labeling

#

a community as "close friends" represents not only user's feelings about them but also reinforcement of those perceptions.

Moreover, social platforms should provide tools to facilitate the convert and release of information considering that recommenders represent reliable sources of information for members in online communities. Some researchers also confirmed that non-monetary sacrifices negatively influenced intention to recommend product [17]. Recommenders often introduce cross-platform pictures and videos into online communities in order to accurately respond to other members’ queries. The more efforts recommenders put on convert information from different platforms, the lower the perceived benefit obtained from recommendation. Thus, convenient software tools enable recommenders to increase recommending intention through decreasing extra efforts.

The study shows that community reciprocity not only directly influences member’s product recommendation behavior, but also is more important with respect to reducing affective social distance, which in turn, influences members’ product recommendation behavior. When atmosphere of community reciprocity exists in the community, an environment of more friendly relationship can be formed. Thus, we suggest that virtua community cultivators should focus more attention on fostering community reciprocity atmosphere. For example, in order to promote and maintain the atmosphere of community reciprocity, virtual community cultivators can positively and timely assess members’ published recommendation information, experience and statement, and let others know that the recommendation solves the problem of the members in the community. As such, a close social distance may be elicited by the positive interactions.

## 5.3 Limitations and future research

Like all empirical studies, the current study has some limitations. First, the empirical research is subjected to inherent methodological limitations. This study has been restricted to a cross-sectional empirical research. Future research could build a longitudinal database to observe how an atmosphere of community reciprocity and receptivity emerges through users’ interactions within online communities on social networking sites.

Second, this study demonstrates that community reciprocity and receptivity have a direct effect on product recommendation behavior. In fact, some environmental cues such as the strength of interpersonal ties, openness, homophily, familiarity, and perceived expertise also have an influence on social networking sites [40, 109, 116]. Future studies could explore the effects of other environmental cues on product recommendation behavior. Furthermore, the study focuses on online social communities composed of users without transactional intention. In fact, some researchers have found that SNS types would affect customers emotional trust [117]. Future studies should consider a comparison of users’ communities and communities organized by companies (such as brand communities) and focus on

distinguishing the effect of different communication characteristics on product recommendation behavior.

Third, this study only examines the mediating effect of affective social distance toward product recommendation. Future research is encouraged to investigate other possible mediators such as attitudes toward communities that may enhance the association between community reciprocity, community receptivity, and product recommendation.

Finally, some researchers have confirmed that social benefits, social enhancement, economic incentives, and message intrigue were consumers’ new motivations to spread social word-of-mouth [118]. Future researchers are advised to compare the differences of mechanisms under different scenarios.
