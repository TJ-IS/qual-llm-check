# PRODUCT MEANING IN DIGITAL PRODUCT INNOVATION<sup>1</sup>

Gongtai Wang Smith School of Business, Queen’s University, Kingston, Ontario, CANADA {gongtai.wang@queensu.ca}

Ola Henfridsson Miami Herbert Business School, University of Miami, Coral Gables, FL, U.S.A. {ohenfridsson@miami.edu}

Joe Nandhakumar Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry, U.K. {joe.nandhakumar@wbs.ac.uk}

Youngjin Yoo Weatherhead School of Management, Case Western University, Cleveland, OH, U.S.A. {youngjin.yoo@case.edu}

Digital product innovation involves a meaning-making process. Designers of digital innovations often challenge established product meanings as they digitize physical products, such as cars, toothbrushes, and water bottles. A significant problem for product designers, however, is striking the right balance between the newness and comprehensibility of product meanings. Failure to do so may result in a digital product innovation that is too conventional or difficult to relate to or understand. Yet, the extant digital product innovation literature pays little, if any, attention to product meaning. To fill this void, this study examines a digital product innovation project in which product designers created a digital theater with product meanings beyond those of the traditional movie theater. Our theory, grounded in in-depth data collection and analysis, explains how product designers attribute meanings to their products in the process of digital innovation by enacting two meaning-making loops: a reinforcing loop that makes the product meaning comprehensible, and a differentiating loop that captures emerging product meanings. The two loops come together via meaning sedimentation, through which a new core product meaning is created. Our study contributes to the digital product innovation literature by shedding light on the essential role of meaning-making in innovation and offers an explanatory process theory.

Keywords: Product meaning, digital innovation, embodied cognition, case study, meaning-making loop, meaning sedimentation

## Introduction

From cars to diapers, door locks to toothbrushes, traditional products are becoming increasingly digital (Lyytinen et al., 2016; Yoo et al., 2010a; Yoo et al., 2010b). In this process of digital product innovation,<sup>2</sup> the incorporation of even the simplest software often triggers rethinking of an established product’s meaning (i.e., what a product is known for)

(Faulkner & Runde, 2009, 2013; Lyytinen et al., 2016; Verganti, 2017). For example, digital water bottles with hydration tracking software prompt us to rethink water bottles as more than just “a utensil for holding water”; digital shoes with fitness tracker software prompt us to rethink shoes as more than just “a covering for the foot”; and so on. Examples such as these illustrate an intriguing fact—digital product innovation is not only about functional innovation but also concerns the creation of novel meaning (Norman & Verganti, 2014; Yoo et al., 2010a; Yoo et al., 2010b).

The current wave of digital product innovation marks a moment in which a focus on meaning is warranted. The meaning of traditional physical products, specified by designers during the design process, has evolved through competition among alternative designs (Hargadon & Douglas, 2001; Bijker et al., 1987) or creative reinterpretation of takenfor-granted meanings by users (Faulkner & Runde, 2009). In contrast, physical products with embedded digital capabilities exhibit malleability (Faulkner & Runde, 2013, 2019; Kallinikos et al., 2013; Yoo et al., 2010a), offering the possibility to move beyond the given meanings of the original product. On the one hand, the reprogrammability of digital components gives physical products significant scope for functionality extension and, therefore, changes in meaning; on the other hand, physical products extend the reach of digital components beyond their initial design intentions (Faulkner & Runde, 2013, 2019; Kallinikos et al., 2013; Yoo et al., 2010a). This presents unique challenges to designers of digital product innovations, as they can proactively shape existing product meanings, both expressed and unexpressed, while bringing existing product meanings to the fore. Designers have to proactively think about how users might interpret and reinterpret the meaning of the digital product innovation, both in terms of the physical and the digital components (cf. Lyytinen et al., 2016; Norman & Verganti, 2014; Verganti, 2017; Yoo et al., 2010a; Yoo et al., 2010b).

Business success is often the result of a process in which products are attributed meanings that extend, or even replace, existing product meanings in an unambiguous way (see Norman & Verganti, 2014; Trabucchi et al., 2017; Verganti, 2017). In contrast, the failure to accomplish this may lead to limited business impact even if products are equipped with advanced digital components. In a worst-case scenario where product designers only think about the functional excellence of digital components, they may bring together components with contradicting meanings, making the final product confusing. For example, Google Glass<sup>3</sup> ended up being a confusing product that eventually failed because it combined digital components that assumed an “always-on information collection and processing” meaning with a physical product (glasses) that assumed a “use in all private occasions” meaning. Despite the growing body of research on digital product innovation, the process by which designers seek to strike such a delicate balance has not been carefully examined. To this end, we address the following research question: How do product designers attribute meanings to their products in the process of digital product innovation?

Empirically, we conducted an in-depth case study of the design of a digital theater that comprised three phases, in which meaning was attributed to the emerging digital product innovation. Adopting a longitudinal replication logic (Langley et al., 2013), we analyzed 15 incidents of product changes involving physical and digital components. Our analysis progressively climbed the ladder of abstraction (Bygstad & Munkvold, 2007) from empirical data via descriptive narratives to the development of an explanatory theory (Gregor, 2006).

We used embodied cognition (Clark, 1997; Lakoff & Johnson, 1980, 1999; Varela et al., 1991) because of its focus on corporeal experiences. We did not employ the embodied cognition lens ex ante but rather allowed it to “earn its way” (Strauss & Corbin, 1998, p. 292) through its empirical relevance. Our data collection and analysis revealed that the product designers attributed meanings to the digital theatre as they corporeally experienced its emerging design. Our resulting theory explains digital product innovation as a process constituted by two meaning-making loops: a reinforcing loop that makes core product meanings clear, and a differentiating loop that captures emerging product meanings. As such, our theory moves beyond existing research focusing on architecture (see e.g., Henfridsson et al., 2014; Kallinikos et al., 2013; Lee & Berente, 2002; Yoo et al., 2010a), organizing logics (see e.g., Barrett et al., 2015; Lusch & Nambisan, 2015; Yoo et al., 2012), and innovation tools (see e.g., Barrett et al., 2012; Ciriello et al., 2019; Verstegen et al., 2019) by explicating the meaning-making process in the design of digital product innovations.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. We first review previous research on digital product innovation and product meaning. We then present the conceptual basis of embodied cognition and contextualize it for studying digital innovation. After introducing our methodology and research results, we develop and integrate our findings into an explanatory theory and discuss its implications, as well as directions for future research.

## Literature Review

## Digital Product Innovation

Digital product innovation research seeks to move beyond “extant IT innovation research that has been primarily occupied with process innovation” (Yoo et al., 2010a, p. 725) to understand the broader implications of digital technology for established and emerging product categories. Departing from a traditional IT innovation perspective (e.g., Kohli & Melville, 2018), we consider digital product innovation as a process in which taken-for-granted ideas of products are challenged (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014; Lyytinen et al., 2016; Svahn, 2012). In prior literature in which digital product innovation is the central focus, we identify three dominant research streams<sup>4</sup>: (1) product architecture, (2) organizing logic, and (3) innovation tools.

## Product Architecture

Studies in this stream, which focus on changes in product architecture through the integration of digital technology (Henfridsson et al., 2014; Lee & Berente, 2012), trace changes in extant product ideas back to features of digital technology (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Yoo et al., 2010a). For instance, the layered modular architecture caters to the increasing combination of digital and physical components by presenting digital product architecture as a hybrid of the layered architecture of digital technology and the modular architecture of specific products (Yoo et al., 2010a). Empirical studies in this tradition show that such hybridity presents product designers with challenges in reconciling opposing logics of architectures (Hylving & Schultze, 2020), and with opportunities to resolve problems in one layer with a new solution for another layer (Lee et al., 2020). Whether they confront these challenges or tap into their opportunities, the consensus of these studies is that successful integration of physical and digital components into the product architecture is vital for successful digital product innovation (Henfridsson et al., 2018; Hylving & Schultze, 2020; Lee et al., 2020; Nambisan et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2010a; Yoo et al., 2012).

## Organizing Logics

Scholars in this stream examine how new organizing logics enabled by digital technology can challenge established ideas of products. For instance, this research stream gives attention to digital platforms as a new organizing logic for economic and social activities (Barrett et al., 2015; de Reuver et al., 2018; Lusch & Nambisan, 2015; Yoo et al., 2012). One important mechanism is the generativity resulting from the increasing numbers and quality of platform participants (e.g., YouTube content creators) (de Reuver et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2017; Hukal et al., 2020). For instance, scholars in this stream examine the role of third-party developers in expanding the scope and functionality of digital platforms (Saadatmand et al., 2019; Um et al., 2015). In this regard, it is imperative to conceive new ways of engaging and organizing platform participants through, for example, signaling (Hukal et al., 2020), boundary resource design (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Eaton et al., 2015), governance (Tiwana, 2013; Saadatmand et al., 2019), and incentive systems (Trabucchi et al., 2020).

## Innovation Tools

Innovation tools research presumes that the appropriate use of innovation tools is significant when challenging taken-forgranted ideas of products. This might include the use of new digital tools for presentation (Ciriello et al., 2019), information modeling (Verstegen et al., 2019) and robotics (Barrett et al., 2012), and their influence on digital product innovation. In addition, studies in this stream investigate how common artifacts, such as prototypes, mockups, whiteboards, and project documents (Nandhakumar et al., 2013; Pershina et al., 2019), are used to aid and stimulate the development of new digital products. This research stream demonstrates the importance of boundary and epistemic objects in the increasingly unbounded innovation processes of digital innovation (Nambisan et al., 2017) in which the alignment of cross-boundary collaboration with creativity is imperative (Nandhakumar et al., 2013).

As indicated above, each of these streams briefly touches upon the role of meaning-making in digital product innovation. For example, the identification and combination of appropriate components (product architecture)—involving “nearly limitless possibilities for recombination” (Yoo, 2013, p. 230)—requires rethinking the purposes of the entire product that they will comprise (Henfridsson et al., 2018). The engagement and organization of platform participants (organizing logics) involve a rethinking of the product as it transits from a stand-alone product to a platform ecosystem (Sandberg et al., 2020; Svahn et al., 2017). Similarly, the alignment of cross-boundary collaboration in innovation processes requires innovation tools that can enable product designers to determine the most desirable outcomes (Hanelt et al., 2017; Nandhakumar et al., 2013; Verstegen et al., 2019).

However, only a handful of studies explicitly observe that digital product innovation necessitates consideration of product meaning. For example, Yoo et al. (2010b, p. 1) point out that “the evolution of cameras [into digital cameras] is as much about changes in the meaning of what a ‘camera’ is.” Similarly, Lyytinen et al. (2016) suggest that digital innovation should fundamentally rethink and redefine the basic qualities of familiar products. Despite these observations, our understanding of product meaning-making in digital product innovation is limited, as scholars have rarely if ever examined the inner workings of digital product innovation.

## Product Meaning

Product meaning is often seen as an articulated quality for which a product should be known (Verganti, 2008, 2017). Product meanings are important interpretive schemes that actors employ to distinguish a product from other products. For instance, taking the classic example of the movie business (Hsu, 2006a, 2006b; Zhao et al., 2013; Zuckerman & Kim, 2003), a “movie theater” should be known for providing a “movie experience” (Durand & Paolella, 2013, p. 1111)—when people enter a place described as a “movie theater,” they understand that it implies a “movie experience,” and when they are in a place that offers a “movie experience,” they see the place as a “movie theater.”

Product meaning is critical to innovation as it offers the very reason why a new product will be used (Norman & Verganti, 2014; Trabucchi et al., 2017; Verganti, 2008, 2009, 2011, 2017). For example, analysis shows that the reason why people choose Alessi’s kitchenware over other brands relates to the product meaning<sup>5</sup> imbued by its designers (Verganti, 2008, 2009). In this regard, a new product meaning can make the product stand out from its competitors (Anthony et al., 2016, Verganti, 2017), and product designers can innovate existing products by attributing new product meanings to them (Norman & Verganti, 2014; Verganti, 2008, 2009, 2011, 2017).

Some innovation scholars even claim that new product meanings are equally if not more important than improving or adding functionality (Norman & Verganti, 2014). As a result, they view proactive change in existing product meanings as an essential part of product innovation (Ferraris et al., 2017; Jahnke, 2013; Öberg & Verganti, 2014; Trabucchi et al., 2017; Verganti & Öberg, 2013). In fact, the inception of a new interpretive scheme might render other existing products obsolete (Norman & Verganti, 2014; Koçak et al., 2014). For example, Nest’s thermostat challenged traditional product meanings of thermostats (“programming,” “controlling,” and “sophistication”) by invoking new product meanings (“comfort,” “trust,” and “simplicity”). As a result, the absence of these new meanings may be interpreted as a sign of a lowerquality thermostat (Verganti, 2017, p. 6).

Designers of radical innovations do not necessarily need to ask for users’ opinions (Verganti, 2008), as new product meanings created without consulting customers can still be loved by users (Jahnke, 2013; Verganti, 2009, 2017). Users are often limited by their experiences with existing products and may be incapable of expressing or even knowing their hidden wants and needs (Watson et al., 2011). As a result, total reliance on user input for innovation may lead to incremental product improvement only (Radnejad et al., 2020). Of course, “innovating without engaging users” and “innovating without thinking of users” are two different things (Radnejad et al., 2020; Verganti, 2017; Vlaskovits, 2011). The former is possible because product meanings should not be seen as an individual’s arbitrary interpretation of a product (Kleine & Kernan, 1991). As research on innovation (Grodal et al., 2015; Navis & Glynn, 2010) and design (Ingold, 2013; Schiffer, 2001) shows, a product meaning is an interpretation shared by product designers and users as they form similar beliefs, ideas, and values through corporeal experiences of similar products in everyday social life. Product designers, therefore, can harness such experiences to create new product meanings that will be understood in similar ways by their customers.

Ignoring product meanings may result in two problems. First, product designers may be blinded by established product meanings and fail to see innovation opportunities (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014; Verganti, 2009, 2017). For example, the business failure of Motorola and Apple’s codesign of ROKR can be traced to the inability of product designers at each company to transcend their familiar product meanings: on the one hand, Motorola decided to limit ROKR’s storage to 100 songs as they interpreted it as a “mobile phone” for “making calls,” failing to see why a “mobile phone” would carry more than 100 songs; and on the other hand, Apple insisted on transferring music via USB cables from a computer rather than through mobile networks, because they interpreted ROKR as an “iPod” for “extending desktop iTunes.” As a result, the ROKR was a product that functioned as a mediocre cell phone and a vastly inferior iPod. Second, product designers may deviate too far from existing product meanings and make new products confusing. Even when product designers create new product meanings that are understandable to customers, this does not guarantee that these meanings are comprehensible by default. In fact, prior research shows that when a product assumes new product meanings, both product designers and customers may struggle to understand it before the product is established (Hargadon & Douglas, 2001; Lempiälä et al., 2019).

Attention to both of these product meaning problems is essential. However, our literature review of digital product innovation shows that existing streams of literature, such as research on product architecture, organizing logic, and innovation tools, virtually view meaning as a byproduct of the innovation process. In this regard, they are inadequate for developing a deeper understanding of the meaning-making process as designers seek to strike a balance between newness and the comprehensibility of product meaning through digital product innovation. To this end, we present embodied cognition as an effective perspective for understanding the meaningmaking process in digital product innovation.

## Theoretical Foundation

## Embodied Cognition

Embodied cognition theory (Clark, 1997; Lakoff & Johnson, 1980, 1999; Varela et al., 1991) problematizes the traditional view of cognition as the manipulation of symbols by the brain. In particular, it argues that this traditional view separates the body from the world of meaning-making (Steels, 2008). This is problematic, embodied cognition theory argues, since meaningmaking is influenced by corporeal experiences of the world (Cerulo, 2019) in two important ways. First, humans make meaning of objects or events by mustering previous corporeal experiences of them (Cerulo, 2019; D’Aloia, 2015). Such reliance on corporeal experience also applies to the meaningmaking of abstract concepts, as all human concepts are based on concepts derived from corporeal experiences (Lakoff & Johnson, 1980, 1999). Humans are also able to make sense of unfamiliar objects and events (new or fictional) through mental simulation via their corporeal experiences of familiar things (D’Aloia, 2015). In complex settings, humans make sense of objects or events by mentally decomposing them into familiar parts and then reassembling them (Cerulo, 2019; De Bruin & De Haan, 2009). For example, at the launch event of the first iPhone, Steve Jobs described the device as “an iPod, a phone, and an internet communicator.” Each part corresponded to products the audience had previous corporeal experiences of, which could be mustered and assembled to make meaning of the unfamiliar product.

Second, the characteristics of human bodies and their physical surroundings shape corporeal experiences, which, in turn, shape mental structures and eventually meaning-making (Adam & Galinsky, 2012; Cerulo, 2019; Chiel & Beer, 1997; Churchland et al., 1994; Eerland et al., 2011). In this regard, technology associated with human bodies is especially relevant in a world in which human cognition largely relies on mediation by technology (Brey, 2000; Cerulo, 2019; Heersmink, 2012a, 2012b). When a technology is well-suited to motor, perceptual, or cognitive systems, humans tend not to act on this technology but rather to adopt it as part of their body schema, using it to act on the world (Clark, 2007). In other words, humans act, perceive, and think with technology (Heersmink, 2012a, 2012b). In addition, human cognition changes along with changes in the technology, because such changes enable or constrain the cognizance of new problems or task possibilities (Heersmink, 2012a, 2012b). Therefore, coupling a human body with the right technology may increase cognitive performance, while removing certain technology may lead to a drop in performance (Cerulo, 2019; Clark & Chalmers, 1998). For example, dentists rely on the mediation of dental probes to “feel” cavities in patients’ teeth, which would be much more difficult without the tool (Verbeek, 2005).

The embodied cognition perspective is useful for our purposes, as it acknowledges product meanings without excluding product physicality (Cerulo, 2019). This feature of the theory allows it to be contextualized for studying digital product innovation in a way that can better express the unique characteristics of digital products (Yoo, 2013; Yoo et al., 2010). First, product meanings are not limited to physical constitution (e.g., a digital camera is physically constituted by parts, such as a lens, shutter, image sensor, memory card, screen) or to function (e.g., taking, keeping, and sharing digital photos) (Faulkner & Runde, 2009, 2019). Second, meaning-making in digital product innovation does not concern how a specific product dominates its components nor about how a digital component makes any product a “digital” product (Henfridsson et al., 2014; Yoo et al., 2010a). In what follows, we discuss these two points to contextualize the embodied cognition perspective for studying digital product innovation.

## Meaning-Making in Digital Innovation through an Embodied Cognition Lens

We approach meaning-making in digital product innovation as a process that is subject to two cognitive dimensions, which we refer to as product-component and digital-physical (see Table 1) respectively. First, the structure of cognition entails a dynamic and reciprocal relationship between the whole and parts (Cerulo, 2019; Lakoff, 2014; Vasterling, 2015).

As Groth’s (2017) analysis of knife-making illustrates, the whole offers a focal interpretive attention for mustering experiences of its parts, while the whole can only be experienced once the parts are assembled. This part-whole structure also aligns with Polanyi and Prosch’s (1975) explanation of meaning that “is generally created by an act of synthesis whereby particulars of which one is aware subsidiarily are integrated into a new whole of which one is focally aware” (as reviewed by Gulick, 1992, p. 11). In the context of digital product innovation, Yoo et al. (2010a) illuminate this structural relation between a product as a whole and its components as parts. This relation implies a product-component dimension of meaning-making in digital product innovation, corresponding to the part-whole structure of cognition. A component provides a cue that reminds product designers about products that are often associated with it, while a product informs them about its prototypical qualities and what and how its corresponding components should be created. The meaning of a product and the meanings of its components mutually establish each other (Boland & Day, 1989; Myers & Young, 1997). The inclusion of a new component—either digital or physical— of a product generates a potential for new meanings that may contradict the expected meaning of the product as a whole (Groth, 2017). To main coherence, designers therefore shift their attention between the product and its components (Lindgaard & Wesselius, 2017).

The second cognitive dimension taps into the material aspect of cognition (Cerulo, 2019; Verbeek, 2005). As discussed above, the coupling of a human body with its external elements changes cognitive competence, and the human meaning-making of reality increasingly occurs through technical mediation (e.g., scientific instruments) (Chang, 2004; Ihde, 1990, 1998; Verbeek, 2005). Similarly, it is also necessary to discuss how materiality shapes meaningmaking in the process of creating new products (Lindgaard & Wesselius, 2017). In the context of digital product innovation, this is the digital-physical dimension, relating to the mutual reciprocally mediating relationship between physical materiality (i.e., “artifacts that can be seen and touched, that are generally hard to change, and that connote a sense of place and time”) and digital materiality (i.e., “what the software incorporated into an artifact can do by manipulating digital representations”) (Yoo et al., 2012, p. 1398). On the one hand, the physical mediates the meaningmaking of the digital, whether the physical artifacts are smartphones, autonomous cars, 3D TVs, or VR headsets, our interactions with them are afforded by screens and buttons. For example, Yoo (2010) underscores that even when the digital (e.g., a wood block in the game Minecraft) is the end itself, its manipulation must be mediated by the physical (e.g., a mouse and a keyboard). Changing the physical can therefore potentially reshape the understanding of the digital. For example, Apple’s removal of the cursor arrow keys from the keyboard reshaped users’ meaning-making of the operating system and applications as they began to perceive point-and-click navigation as effective (and indispensable) rather than redundant (and optional). On the other hand, the digital mediates the understanding of the physical. For instance, citing Ihde’s (1990) thermometer example, Yoo (2010) explains how users can indirectly experience physical characteristics of the real world (e.g., cold weather) by directly interacting with the digital (e.g., numeric digits on a thermometer). In this regard, rectifying the digital mediation is helpful for a better understanding of the physical (similar ideas can be found in Burton-Jones and Grange’s (2013) discussion on representation fidelity), and vice versa.

Table 1 summarizes our conceptual foundation. We next turn to the data collected through an in-depth case study of the design of an innovative digital theater with a focus on product meanings and how both cognitive dimensions can shape their creation.

## Research Method

We develop an explanatory theory (Gregor, 2006) of an under-researched phenomenon—meaning-making in digital product innovation. Given the focus on meaning-making and the theory type, our approach is best described as interpretive (Klein & Myers, 1999; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991).

## Research Setting

We studied a digital product innovation project at TopTech (pseudonym), a pioneering digital entertainment technology company in China that designed a digital theater (see Figure 1). Founded in a Chinese public university in 1998 and then spun off in 2005, TopTech had been in the entertainment business for more than 15 years by the time of the digital theater’s debut in 2014. Early on, it operated in animation service provision and was involved in the production of many blockbuster movies, animations, and games. Today, it is recognized as a leading producer of original digital entertainment products, known for its ambitious projects transforming the entertainment business with innovative virtual reality technologies. The turning point was the design of the digital theater offering highly immersive 3D experiences without the use of 3D glasses. It has proven to be a successful product, as fifty digital theaters were sold between its 2014 debut and September 2020. In recognition of its success, TopTech has been granted numerous awards by governmental authorities and domestic and overseas industry associations and is described as a “high-tech star” by Chinese media.

The design of the digital theater is an ideal case for studying product meaning in digital product innovation. It reflects what Gerring (2007, p. 91) refers to as a “representative case,” which is suitable for inductive analysis with the ambition to develop a theory that speaks to other cases of digital product innovation. We deemed TopTech an appropriate representative case for the following reasons. First, it featured the defining characteristic of digital product innovation, i.e., “the carrying out of new combinations of digital and physical components” (Yoo et al., 2010, p. 725). This is demonstrated by the 15 events of combining digital and physical components outlined in Figure 2 and Tables 2-4. It is also evidenced by the continuum from digital to physical of the project’s variety of patents and copyrights.<sup>6</sup> Second, TopTech’s design of the digital theatre involved three phases of significant rethinking of the meaning of the product. In fact, already at our initial interview with the CEO, it became clear that TopTech designers constantly strived to understand the meaning of a “movie theater” and to create new meanings for it. Lastly, the case largely resonates with our interpretive research methodology, in which it is important to convey “the story which the data tell” (cf. Klein & Myers, 1999). For instance, the research participants frequently referred to the digital theater product as a “digital entertainment technology innovation,” meaning that their language reflected the core framing of our analysis well.

## Data Collection

We started our data collection by familiarizing ourselves with the research setting. Prior to entering the research site, we gathered information from business magazines, online articles and videos to obtain a basic understanding of the company and its products. On our first visit, we conducted a 150-minute interview with the CEO and engaged in observation at TopTech’s headquarters. The CEO provided us with textual, photographic, graphic, and video archival data on their products, services, business vision, and corporate culture.

In the main phase of data collection, we gathered interview, observational, and archival data. First, we conducted 40 semistructured individual and group interviews with 43 interviewees, ranging between 19 minutes and 114 minutes (see Appendix A for interview questions)<sup>7</sup> between June 2015 and February 2016. The interviewees represented a wide range of functional divisions and management levels. The interviews were recorded as there were no objections from the interviewees. Our informant selection strategy was purposeful snowball sampling (Biernacki & Waldorf, 1981; Patton, 1990). We began with the CEO, who referred us to the managers of each department. All the managers were frontline staff who had been engaged in the digital theater project since its inception. Therefore, they had significant hands-on knowledge of the project history. The managers then referred us to their staff who had also been involved in the project since its early stages. The staff then referred us to other staff when they did not have sufficient knowledge to answer certain questions.

Second, we carried out two rounds of participant observation to gain direct experience of the project context’s complexity (Nandhakumar & Jones, 2002). The first round was conducted in June 2015 (48 hours), and the second between December 2015 and February 2016 (252 hours). The locations included TopTech’s headquarters, customer premises, and trade fairs. The observational data were recorded in the form of field notes and photos.

One of the authors went to the headquarters every weekday, mainly to shadow the animation director who, at the time of this study, had been promoted to the chief director of the company. Since he did not have his own office but preferred to move around to actively interact with other staff, shadowing him enabled us to extend our observations to contingent critical moments of product design in different places that we could not have planned beforehand (Nicolini, 2009).

Third, we supplemented the interviews with project documents. Specifically, we accessed 230 pages of documents on the technical details of the digital theater, 19 pages of the CEO’s handwritten notes on his vision and initial ideas for the project, 137 pages of scripts, mood boards, style frames, and animation storyboards, and 175 photos and 23 videos of the manufacture, installation, and use of the digital theaters. Combined with the observations, this data material helped us address potential distortion of informants’ retrospective accounts (Berchicci & Tucci, 2010) and deepen our understanding of the project (Leonard-Barton, 1990).

Lastly, we had numerous informal conversations with project members in day-to-day interactions. We also conducted follow-up interviews either in person or online, usually with the intention to clarify witnessed events and validate our interpretation of the data.

## Data Analysis

Our data analysis was conducted in tandem with the data collection. We continuously moved back and forth among the empirical data, the literature, and the emerging theoretical conceptions.

## Step 1

We started with the identification of design artifacts, i.e., objects created from design ideas (Gregor & Hevner, 2013), developed during the design of the digital theater. We identified 15 design artifacts, which enabled us to establish a timeline of how the design of the digital theater evolved along with the project (Figure 2).

## Step 2

We examined the design decisions and processes for each of the 15 design artifacts. The analysis adopted longitudinal replication logic (Langley et al., 2013, p.7) to identify recurring theoretical mechanisms over time (Tsoukas, 1989; Van de Ven, 1992). With this logic, we extract first-order categories (Tables 2, 3, and 4) and second-order themes (Figure 3) through the lens of the sensitizing concepts (Blumer, 1954) reviewed above (Table 1) to extract theoretical understandings from empirical observations.

Specifically, we first combined the phrases and terms in the descriptions related to product meanings stressed by the respondent (e.g., theater as a space for immersive experience), and the reasons why the product meanings were stressed (e.g., the designers believed moviegoing experience was critical, and immersion was a critical quality for enhancing the experience). Related archival data including patent documents, newspaper interviews, and field notes were used to corroborate the descriptions. The analysis revealed three significant product meanings along the design of the digital theater that were meaningful to the designers. We then sought to achieve a theoretical-level understanding of the first-order findings in terms of the evolution of product meanings along with the creation of the design artifacts. This further analysis generated second-order themes, which were then aggregated into theoretical dimensions.

## Step 3

We integrated the insights into a theory explaining how product designers attribute meanings to their products in the process of digital innovation. Although our purpose was not to develop a theory for deterministic use, we sought to generalize our insights by elevating them to a higher level of abstraction that could go beyond the studied context (Klein & Myers, 1999).

The idea of creating a digital theater originated from the CEO’s visit to Universal Studios in Hollywood in 2010. The experience of the Simpsons’ Ride deeply impressed him and revealed to him the potential to enhance moviegoing experiences with physical experiences. On that very day, he spent most of the night in his hotel room with his notebook generating and documenting ideas for a new product. What he envisioned was a new movie theater that would provide a truly exciting moviegoing experience.

However, translating the vision into a novel product proved to be a long and challenging, albeit rewarding, process. After a four-year design process full of trial and error in searching for, creating, and recreating various digital and physical components, TopTech produced a digital theater that put forward three new meanings for a movie theater, which were believed by the informants and industry experts to be the key contributors to its success. The following three subsections present a chronological narrative along with first-order categories and illustrative examples (Tables 2, 3, and 4).

## Product Meaning #1: Theater as a Space for Immersive Experience

Inspired by the CEO’s vision, a new project was embarked upon to design a theater product in early 2010. Drawing on their past experience in the animation business, TopTech designers<sup>8</sup> understood that an excellent moviegoing experience should be a key quality of a successful theater product. Traditionally, movie theaters were understood as a place to watch movies; thus, the moviegoing experience mainly relied on movie quality (e.g., intriguing plot). In contrast, the experience of the Simpsons’ Ride revealed that a sense of immersion could elevate the moviegoing experience to the next level, which therefore should be a critical quality for making an excellent theater product. Based on this understanding, TopTech designers redefined the movie theater as a space for an immersive experience. To embody this new product meaning, they learned from the Simpsons’ Ride to complement animation experience with physical experience, by combining digital animations with various digital and physical technologies.

The digital theater project was officially launched in early 2010 with the introduction of a giant semispherical screen (DA#01, Table 2). The idea was straightforward, as the designers interpreted “immersive” as being submerged into the animation world. Resorting to the experience of their past projects that had also used this type of screen, they anticipated that physically wrapping it around the audience could give them an illusion of being in the place depicted by the animation. Therefore, they purchased a screen from a renowned manufacturer to validate the idea by setting up a temporary stage and projecting a sample animation on it. The trials along these lines, although rudimentary, were positive, as being physically wrapped around by the screen did create a palpable sense of immersion, as anticipated.

The screen alone, however, could not fully create a sense of immersion. In addition, the sense of immersion was undermined by image distortion because the ordinary projector could not handle the curved screen. To address this problem, the screen had to be used in combination with projection technology specialized to mitigate the image distortion.

The first idea that came to the designers was a special projector that used a fisheye lens to adjust the image for the curved screen (DA#02, Table 2). Pursuing an excellent immersive experience, the designers set a very high standard for the projector, as they believed excellent image quality was vital for creating a sense of immersion:

It’s a movie theater. The projection quality must be good. If the visual effect is bad, how can you make audiences feel immersed? It would definitely fail. No

## matter how good its other aspects are. (Chief Director, Multimedia Development Department)

However, the approach of using a single fisheye projector eventually turned out to be unviable, as no commercially available fisheye projectors met their demanding standards. Disappointed by the projectors, the designers decided to collaborate with a domestic projector manufacturer to develop an original fisheye projector. However, this turned out to be an insurmountable challenge because it seemed technically impossible to achieve maximum brightness and maximum resolution at the same time. In their repeated tests, the projector’s brightness always declined as they raised the resolution, and vice versa. As both were critical to image quality, compromising either would impede the sense of immersion. In addition, it was a concern that, even if they managed to resolve the problem, the projector would be prohibitively costly, and if only one manufacturer had the capability of producing the projector, there would be associated risks of single sourcing.

Maintaining the pursuit of excellent image quality to ensure the sense of immersion, in mid-2010, the designers eventually gave up the single fisheye projector approach in exchange for redesigning the projection using multiple projectors (DA#03, Table 2). The new approach used multiple off-the-shelf ultrahigh-resolution projectors with each covering a part of the screen. Together, the projectors formed a whole image of ultrahigh quality. Additionally, the approach turned out to be much more cost-effective, as they found that, even if they used the very best projectors, the cost would still be lower than that of developing and producing top-quality fisheye projectors. They could also have multiple projector suppliers, to reduce the risks of sourcing. Most importantly, while testing this solution, the image was crystal clear, which created a high sense of immersion to an extent that exceeded the designers’ expectations.

The multiple projectors required a new software system for merging and coordinating their projection to form a seamless holistic image. Early on, TopTech outsourced the development to a university research team (DA#04, Table 2), which devised an algorithm that allowed the projectors to be set up one by one, in a straightforward way and provided a satisfactory merged image quality. This system, however, was shelved, as the designers found the system to be problematic because it needed installation personnel to run back and forth between the console computer and the projectors. They had to first adjust two projectors to match two points on the edges of their respective projection, then run to the console computer to click the “merge” button, and then run back to adjust the third projector, then to the console computer again, and on and on until all projectors were calibrated. It was time- and labor-consuming and error prone, even when multiple people performed the task together. Such troublesome experiences in repeated system testing revealed to TopTech designers the importance of ease of use, which pushed them to abandon the system.

In late 2010, TopTech designers decided to redesign the system in-house (DA#05, Table 2). This decision was supported by their learning and reflection on the testing experience of the outsourced system. As some TopTech designers who had programming backgrounds frequently attended the testing, they began to realize that they had overestimated the difficulty of the system development and they already had enough knowledge and skills to do it by themselves. An additional rationale behind this decision was to distinguish their theater from traditional movie theaters by stressing its “high-tech” aspect, which they believed the originality of their software would legitimate.

The most important driver, however, was the ease of use of the digital theater. The development, therefore, focused on making the calibration of projectors easier while ensuring the same image quality. This led to the creation of a new merging system that could be set up with one click of the mouse. As it was developed in-house, the designers attained full knowledge of the source code, which enabled them to further optimize the algorithm through reprogramming. Progressively pushing the algorithmic efficiency to its extreme, the computation time was significantly reduced from two minutes to thirty seconds and eventually to five seconds (the fastest merging system in the market at the time of this study).

In parallel with the screen and projection technologies, the design of the first animation was also in full swing (DA#06, Table 2). The initial theme of the animation was “car racing,” featuring a fierce sports car racing competition. Although this theme emerged in late 2010, the designers’ efforts to seek an exciting animation had begun long before the digital theater project as they constantly sought to create impactful animation experiences. Given such a pursuit, sports car racing resonated well with past experiences that they found “exciting,” which was also verified through experiments:

[CEO’s name] said we should make something exciting. So, we were thinking, what is exciting? … We had many racing fans. We also searched online to see what had been said about exciting experiences and found that car racing was workable … We also bought a remotecontrol car, glued a mini camera on it, and drove it around to see if it felt right. And, it did look good. (Chief Director, Multimedia Development Department)

As the screen and projection technologies and the animation gradually took shape, the designers began to put these works in progress together to test the overall moviegoing experience. It was around this time when the designers incorporated the motion seat (DA#07, Table 2) into the integration testing:

It [motion seat] ... creates different special effects in accordance with the plot of the movie. It forms a highly immersive environment, giving the audience a realistic experience. (Document\_103622348)

In a similar way to the screen, the incorporation of motion seats was based on their experience of a past project, which allowed them to estimate the feeling of riding motion seats and to what extent it might enhance the animation experience.

As seat movements must be in sync with the animation, there needed to be a seat control system. A common approach was to manually move the seats and let the system record the movements. The system then replayed the movements in later screenings. However, during the system testing, the designers found the recorded movements were inaccurate. Seamless synchronization was necessary for the sense of immersion, as any gap between seat movements and the animation might make the moviegoing experience unrealistic. Besides, the manual setup was very tiring, which was against their new priority of ease of use. Therefore, the designers decided to develop their own seat control system (DA#08, Table 2) that enabled all the movements to be set up through the software:

Our approach is to do everything in the software. It has this window playing the animation, and this one for adjusting the movement of the seat. You can see the timeline of the animation here [pointing at the screen]. So you can adjust the seat’s movement very precisely. (Staff D, New Business Development Department)

The incorporation of the seats revealed an unexpected problem in the animation. Previously, the animation designers had not paid attention to physical experience. They tended to ignore physical laws as long as the animation plot looked exciting to them. In contrast, the designers of the seat control system sought to make the seat movements as natural as possible and programmed the algorithm with the right acceleration rate for a realistic feeling of inertia. As a result, the sense of immersion was broken, as the movement of the seats did not match the animation:

When you’re making an animation, you may forget about that, or you simply don’t bother to make it right. But, when the seat is added, when audiences have bodily feelings, it feels obviously strange if they are not synchronized very well. Imagine if your seat is still moving forward with the correct inertia, and you feel that you should keep moving to the tree, while the animation suddenly stops. (Deputy Manager, Contents Development Department)

Although the mismatch could be corrected by changing either side, the designers decided to change the animation. This was not only because of their pursuit of more realistic movements for the sense of immersion, but also because of their passion for perfecting their creations:

We were full of expectations at that time when we went there [integration testing]. But, when we found the result was “ok,” and there were some technical flaws, we really couldn’t tolerate them. So, we decided to redo it, to make it much, much better. (Deputy Manager, Content Development Department)

The designers began a thorough redesign with a fundamental rethinking for a new animation idea, holding meeting after meeting, searching widely online and offline. This reworking eventually led them to an idea to equip the cars with a flying capability, in a science fiction theme, “Flying Car” (DA#09, Table 2). In this futuristic animation, the audience would take the perspective of a hero driving a flying car, chasing and combating villains in a future Chinese city. As introduced below, this animation redesign not only elevated the animation to the level that satisfied all the designers but also, as will be introduced later, affected how the designers conceived and created other design artifacts.

In late 2012, as the new animation was incorporated into the integration testing, the designers discovered that the seats’ range of motion was too limited. The new animation featured an elaborate plot with wild and intense movements. However, the seat machine could not keep up with the movements. As we saw previously, the mismatch between the animation and the seats could be corrected by changing either side. However, the designers were reluctant to change the animation this time—as its depiction of movements had been corrected to follow reallife physical laws, it was difficult to justify changes to it. More importantly, the designers were satisfied with and even emotionally attached to it:

Our new animation was splendid. It shouldn’t be changed … Although it was more expensive than changing the animation, we decided to change the seat [machine] because the moviegoing experience was at the heart of our company. We didn’t want to compromise on it. (Designer A, Entertainment Experience Design Center)

The redesign of the seat machine (DA#10, Table 2) turned out to be significant, as a change in one component often required changes in other components. For example, while making the movements of the cylinder (a main component controlling the range of motion) more intense, many nuts, bolts, and fasteners as well as the whole seat structure needed to be strengthened. ## Product Meaning #2: Theater as a High-Tech Invention

TopTech designers did not stop at making the digital theater a space for immersive experience, they also considered the building’s interior and exterior to be important components of the digital theater. They believed these components were crucial for distinguishing the digital theater from traditional movie theaters, specifically, by signaling a new product meaning that movie theaters should be known for high-tech invention instead of nontechnical architecture. Different from the “immersive experience” that was given upfront, “high-tech” was more emergent from the earlier half of the project.

At the very beginning, the digital theater did not look very different from traditional movie theaters (DA#11, Table 3) because the design focus was on the “immersive experience.” In late 2010, the initial building design reflected a typical image of traditional movie theaters (Version 1 in Figure 4). When the design was presented to the CEO, he felt it was too mundane. He expected the digital theater to be the turning-point product of their business with a distinctive, iconic appearance that could signal the technical excellence and creativity of the company. With this new requirement, the designers went back to the drawing board and did a broad search seeking the secret recipe behind iconic buildings of modern architecture:

I searched for photos and videos of buildings that were often recognized as beautiful and cool. And I found that they commonly featured translucent exteriors. They used a lot of metal frames and glass panels, which fit well with the public’s aesthetic standards. (Chief Designer, Entertainment Experience Design Center

However, this new design (Version 2 in Figure 4) was again rejected, as the CEO still felt it was not sufficiently visually appealing. In addition, the designers were concerned about production costs. Since the design sought to emphasize the unique screen, the building top was designed as a glass dome neatly covering the screen. However, the massive screen size and glass dome would have require specialized materials, manufacturing, transportation, and installation, which would have greatly driven up the cost. From then on, without a concrete definition of the CEO’s subjective criteria, the building design was postponed. The design took a turn for the better later as the animation gradually took shape and began to exert an influence on work in other design areas.

The influence of the animation was first observed in the seat appearance design (DA#12, Table 3) in early 2012. At that time, the animation was still the old car racing theme. The first seat appearance design sought to echo the theme by resembling motorsport seats. This influence was due to the company’s long history in the animation business, which had ingrained the designers with the idea that animations should be prioritized:

Our company originated in the animation business. It is still one of our main businesses now. So, we prioritize animation in most situations as long as it doesn’t cause serious problems. So that’s why I designed this [seat appearance] initially. I wanted to create the feeling of riding a racing car, to let it echo the animation theme. (Designer B, Entertainment Experience Design Center)

The influence of the animation became increasingly significant as the flying car animation progressively took shape and turned out to be favored by everyone in the company. As test screenings were repeated, the building designers were frequently exposed to the new animation and their work was influenced by it (DA#13, Table 3).

In mid-2012, the designers changed the building design dramatically (Version 3 in Figure 3), seeking to make it echo the animation’s futuristic theme. This decision was also supported because it happened to echo the “high-tech invention” product meaning. As noted earlier, the decision to develop the merging system in-house had an additional motivation, which was the possibility of distinguishing the digital theater from traditional movie theaters by enhancing its “high-tech” aspect. Watching the futuristic animation offered the designers an opportunity to connect a “futuristic” appearance with the notion of “high-tech” to conceive a more visible form for the abstract concept:

I watched its preview [flying car animation] and thought I should change my design to their theme … Then, we thought maybe we should make the whole thing sci-fi. So, it [the digital theater] could look more high-tech. (Chief Designer, Entertainment Experience Design Center)

However, the sophisticated new designs were estimated to be expensive and difficult to manufacture and construct. Meanwhile, the CEO, an enthusiast of Apple’s design, embraced their concept of “simplicity being the ultimate sophistication.” Considering this new criterion, the designers created a new version, featuring a main theater, and an annex housing a box office and a waiting area (Version 4 in Figure 4).

The design was further simplified into a single globe with the auditorium moved to the upper floor and the ground floor used as the waiting hall, because the CEO felt the annex compromised the simplicity of the design (Version 5 in Figure 4). However, the designers identified an engineering problem during computer simulations: since the theater hall on the upper floor accommodated a large number of motion seats, the building shook with the seats, which in turn shook the screen and the projectors. This had the potential to damage the image quality and mar the moviegoing experience, which was unacceptable. The designers therefore moved the theater hall back to the ground floor (Version 6, Figure 4) and eventually eliminated the box office to create a design resembling buildings in sci-fi games (Version 7, Figure 4), which they believed pushed their pursuit of the concepts “simplicity” “futuristic” and “high-tech” to the maximum.

Similarly, the new animation again affected the seat appearance design. The seat designers felt it was necessary to redesign the seat (DA#14, Table 3) to make it better echo the futuristic animation and the futuristic building, and to emphasize the “high-tech” aspect of the theater:

It [the animation] was later changed to the Flying Car. If I didn’t change [the seat design], wouldn’t they become inconsistent? And, we conceptualized it [the digital theater] as a high-tech product. It seemed like all our designs needed to go in that direction. (Designer B, Entertainment Experience Design Center)

## Product Meaning #3: Theater as a Movable Platform of Moviegoing Experience

In 2013, a third product meaning that TopTech designers had never intended before progressively emerged. The constant improvement of software systems for “ease of use,” and the effortful simplification of the building design laid the groundwork for its emergence. Together, they made it possible to reassemble the digital theater, meaning it could be transported to new places. This turned out to be a distinctive feature in that traditional movie theaters are built exclusively for a specific place and once built can only be demolished but not transported for reuse in other places. This, therefore, gave rise to a new product meaning, i.e., the theater as a movable moviegoing experience platform.

The trigger event that enabled the designers to realize this new product meaning was their development of a monitoring system, which was rooted in the design of a new business model. As the digital theater gradually took shape, the designers started to consider its business aspect more closely. They estimated that most companies would not have sufficient financial resources to buy an expensive theater product at full price. Although they had massively reduced the production costs, the full price would still be beyond the reach of many companies. Therefore, they designed a new business model in which they jointly operated the digital theaters with buyer companies. They offered massive discounts to the companies in exchange for a share of their daily income from the digital theaters.

The joint operation required TopTech to understand the daily operations of the digital theaters, for which the designers developed a monitoring system (DA#15, Table 4):

Because of the joint operation, we needed a monitoring system … We thought that if we were going to do it, why not do it better. So, we jammed many functions into it … We can know a lot of things about the theater, like how long it has been running and how many audience members have visited. We can also know the conditions of many devices. (CEO’s Assistant)

The versatility of the monitoring system eventually created an opportunity for the designers to understand their hardware and building from a new angle to realize a distinctive characteristic of the digital theater—movability:

That monitoring system was critical … We were disputing if we really needed to have so many functions. And I remembered someone suggested the possibility [to make the digital theater movable]. Then, we started to think about it … In fact, in the beginning, we didn’t have this idea immediately. Progressively, we realized that it should be able to be reassembled as the earlier design made it very durable. So, we further strengthened key parts and enhanced the concept of movability. (CEO’s Assistant)

This statement underscores the emergent nature of movability. It was not a preplanned idea but emerged from the synthesis of the uniqueness of many different components created in earlier stages. For example, one such component was the design of the building frame connector. Traditional building frames of globe architecture were designed to be assembled through welding frame rods together, which made them nonreusable and required labor-intensive lifting operations that were difficult even for experienced installation personnel. To solve this problem, TopTech designers redesigned the rods and created a unique connector for holding them together. It made the assembly much faster while ensuring a good loadbearing capacity. They also used a similar idea (replacing welding-based assembly with connector-based assembly) to redesign other building parts (e.g., the giant metal hoop on the edge of the screen). Those designs, initially created for ease of assembly in one setting, gave designers the experience of disassembling the building for the next testing. This, in turn, gave them the opportunity to realize an emerging quality that was significantly distinguishable from traditional movie theaters, which motivated them to further improve those designs to enhance the quality—for example, by making them more durable for frequent reassembly:

As you may need to disassemble it [the digital theater] often, if you use screws, it [the rod and connector] will not be durable for a long time. So, we used rubber for the interface [of the connector]. You can simply plug it in and out when you assemble it. (Staff A, New Business Development Department)

Taking advantage of the movability, they further designed a leasing business model in which they rented out digital theaters to clients for temporary use. As more and more digital theaters were leased, the “movable platform” proved to be a meaningful quality for which the digital theater was recognized. ## Meaning-Making in Digital Product Innovation

Consistent with embodied cognition theory (Clark, 1997; Lakoff & Johnson, 1980, 1999; Varela et al., 1991), our findings show that TopTech’s digital product innovation relied on the product designers’ corporeal experiences of interacting with the different aspects of materiality of the design artifacts (Groth, 2017). For example, the designers understood the abstract concept “immersive experience” based on their corporeal experience of the animation, screen, projectors, and motion seats from past projects. In turn, testing the screen, projectors, and motion seats similarly revealed the limitation of traditional merging systems and seat control systems. The product designers’ corporeal experience of testing the merging system, seat control system, and monitoring system then provided the material for the concepts “high-tech” and “movable platform,” which were at least partially beyond the initial plan, which in turn led to new design artifacts.

## The Dimensions of Meaning-Making in Digital Product Innovation

A closer examination of the data reveals how TopTech designers’ attribution of product meanings to the digital theater was related to the digital-physical dimension and the product-component dimension.

## Digital-Physical Dimension

Regarding the material aspect of cognition (Cerulo, 2019; Verbeek, 2005), we found that the digital and the physical reciprocally mediated and complemented each other for the designers to experience (Chang, 2004; Idhe, 1990, 1998; Yoo, 2010) and establish the three new product meanings. “Immersive experience” was created as the designers complemented the experience of digital animation with the experience of the physical screen, “high-tech” was established as they designed the exteriors and interiors to make the technology hiding behind the scenes visually experienceable, and “movable platform” was captured as the designers’ experience of testing the physical building frame and the digital monitoring system was synthesized.

We also found that digital experience and physical experience sometimes impeded each other. For instance, while the unique shape of the screen could create a great sense of immersion, it also could mar the experience by distorting the images from ordinary projectors (DA#01 and #02). As a result, TopTech designers faced the persistent challenge of digital product innovation, i.e., the necessity of bringing together the digital and the physical coherently (Lyytinen et al., 2016; Yoo, 2010; Yoo et al., 2012). Subject to this, the designers had to constantly shift between the digital and the physical, validating one with the other. For instance, when designing the animation (DA#06 and DA#09), the animation experience was validated through the use of the motion seat, which, in turn, was validated by the animation (DA#07, and #10). By shifting between them, the designers sought to determine whether the artifacts should be included and how to refine them so that they could together contribute to the realization of the “immersive experience.”

We further found that shifting between the digital and the physical led to opportunities for improving the components or creating new components beyond the current design. First, additional product criteria were realized when the components were validated across the digital-physical boundary. For example, while the designers were satisfied by the car racing animation, watching it when testing the motion seats revealed the importance of using a realistic acceleration rate (DA#06 and #08). Second, the shift between the digital and the physical exposed heterogeneous design logics. For example, the experience of testing the animation constantly changed how the designers defined what should be a proper theme for the seat appearance (DA#12) and the building appearance (DA#13). In both of these ways, shifting sides created the opportunity to shift the “locus of control” (Yoo et al., 2012, p. 1399), letting the design on one side be driven by the design logic on the other side.

## Product-Component Dimension

Instead of a fixed product specification decomposed into detailed criteria for each component (Ulrich, 1995), the design of the digital theater began with a broad product concept (“movie theater”) as a reference. With such a general reference, the product was more emergent as the designers strived to understand what its specific qualities should be and what components would contribute to the qualities. Manifesting the structure of cognition (Cerulo, 2019; Lakoff, 2014; Vasterling, 2015), the overarching idea only exerted limited binding force on the design of the components. Instead, the design of the components sometimes served as an opportunity for “reflection-in-action” (Schön, 1983, p. 50) to rethink what qualities might make the product excellent and should thus serve as the core. In other words, component meaning and product meaning exerted equal force in shaping the creation of the new meanings of the final new product. As a result, the designers had to constantly iterate between components (in local design) and the emerging product (in integration testing) to figure out what should be the right product meaning and whether the components served it (Boland & Day, 1989; Gulick, 1992; Polanyi & Prosch, 1975). Such iterations between a product and its components are common in design practice. However, it is traditionally assumed that the iterations are intended to check whether the components meet predefined specifications. In contrast, in the design of the digital theater, the preplanned specifications were minimal. Rather than simply checking how well the components met specifications, the iterations were more emergent, explorative, and experimental. In this process, the meaning-making of the emerging product and the meaningmaking of components mutually shaped each other.

We found that as the designers iterated between the product and the components, one driving force was the necessity to achieve and maintain congruence between the product meaning and the component meaning. Metaphorically speaking, the relationship between the digital theater and its components is analogous to that between a book and its chapters (Myers, 2004). The meaning of the digital theater was established only when the meanings of its components congruently contributed to it. In reverse, the meaning of the digital theater set the tone for the meanings of its components. For example, “immersive experience” was established, as its animation was “natural” (DA#09), its screen was “physically wrapping around” (DA#01), and its motion seats were “seamlessly synchronized” (DA#10). These component meanings were stressed, instead of the many possible others, not for their own sake but because they all contributed to an “immersive experience.” As we have seen above, those that did not contribute to it were rejected (e.g., the car racing animation, DA#09).

We also found that the iterations between the product and the components were driven by emerging opportunities to discover new product meanings in component design as the designers encountered new component meanings that were less coherent with the product meaning. Their corporeal contact with the components let them develop local meanings (cf. Cerulo, 2019; D’Aloia, 2015), which sometimes were less coherent with the overall meaning. As discussed above, as the initial product meaning only had limited binding power, a new local meaning could challenge the overall meaning and in turn other components, especially when the designers had the urge to go beyond a current design. For example, the dispute of whether they should make the monitoring system versatile triggered the rethinking of the digital theater, which then led to the reflection on components created earlier and eventually gave rise to a “movable platform” (DA#15). The pursuit of a versatility unnecessary in traditional monitoring systems, however, was accepted because of the designers’ urge to transcend traditional movie theaters.

## Modalities of Meaning-Making in Digital Product Innovation

Our literature review on product meaning points out two meaning-making problems in innovation: deviating too much from (Hargadon & Douglas, 2001; Lempiälä et al., 2019) and being too bound to (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014; Verganti, 2009, 2017) an existing product meaning. The former makes a product confusing, while the latter results in overlooking innovation opportunities. The findings in the previous section reveal that the key to solving the former problem was to attend to the incongruence between the digital and the physical and that between the components and the product, while the key to solving the latter problem was to proactively probe and capture emerging opportunities for new product meanings. By digging deeper into the data with a focus on how TopTech designers dealt with such problems, we found four “modalities of action” (Rescher, 2000) in the meaning-making process of their digital product innovation.

## Connecting

Connecting is the process by which designers bring together the digital and the physical in such a way that they complement each other. As we have seen before, digital and physical artifacts follow different design logics (Henfridsson et al., 2014; Kallinikos et al., 2013). The difference drives their design with different understandings of what qualities should be prioritized. As a result, the digital and the physical sometimes conflict with each other when they are experienced together. For example, the animation design prioritized “exciting” movements (DA#06), while the design of the seat machine and the seat control system saw “natural” movements as meaningful (DA#07 and #08). Each of the qualities was entirely reasonable with stand-alone components. However, the quality on the one side rendered that on the other side meaningless when they conflicted with each other. To resolve such issues, TopTech designers constantly brought together physical and digital components in integration testing and refined each side until they were congruent with each other. For example, the animation and the motion seat were continuously aligned for seamlessly synchronized movements (DA#09 and #10).

## Unifying

Unifying refers to the process by which designers align the meanings of product components with the meaning of the product as a whole. Design, especially that of complex products, often consists of various design fragments that must follow a unified thought (Buchanan, 1992). Again, in the design of the digital theater, different design areas had a different understanding of what qualities were meaningful to them. When the local understanding was incongruent with the overall meaning (cf. Lindgaard & Wesselius, 2017), it seriously undermined it. For example, if the screen were still treated only as a device to reflect the projection light rather than to create a sense of immersion, it would not be made semispherical to physically wrap around the audience; in turn, with a flat screen, the digital theater would not be recognized as providing an “immersive experience,” or at least not to the same extent. To ensure that the components contributed to the product meaning, the designers iterated between the components and their integration, validating component meanings against it. Designers retained local meanings only when they supported the overall meaning of the emergent whole, and continually and readily adjusted local meanings whenever they did not. For example, the major driver behind the relentless redesign of the projection technology was to create a projection technology that could be recognized for “excellent image quality” that could produce an “immersive experience” (DA#02 and #03).

## Repositioning

Repositioning refers to the process by which designers rethink the design of the digital from the perspective of the design of the physical, and vice versa. Beyond traditional innovation, digital innovation often features a less bounded process (Nambisan et al., 2017), making digital product designers attentive to a larger variety of design areas and heterogeneous design logics. Therefore, accidental discoveries (Austin et al., 2012) were frequent in the design of the digital theater. TopTech designers were not fully contained by their own design area but had certain freedoms to probe outside it. For example, the designers redesigned the building’s appearance and the seat appearance as they attended the test screening of the new animation where they encountered the “futuristic theme” (DA#13 and #14). Although the discoveries were by chance, our findings show that the designers sometimes proactively brought their (conceptual or material) design work to different design areas with the intention of borrowing heterogeneous design logics for rethinking their work. For example, the pursuit of simplicity in the design of the building appearance borrowed design logics from consumer electronics (DA#13).

## Reinterpreting

Reinterpreting refers to the process by which the designers rethink the meaning of the product in light of new component meanings. Although interpretations on the component level are influenced by meanings on the product level, the designers of components also appropriate the product meaning from their local standpoint (Myers, 2004). Such reinterpretation creates new local meanings that are then elevated to the product level as a new overall meaning. For example, providing an “immersive experience” was partially reinterpreted by the animation team as a need for “exciting” themes (DA#09), which then led to the “futuristic” theme that affected the design of the building appearance (DA#13); as the abstract “futuristic” became more concrete and visible along with the building appearance design, the earlier “high-tech” meaning that emerged in the early design of the software was then elevated to the product level. As with repositioning, we found that reinterpreting was not all by chance but could also be intentional. As we have seen in the TopTech case, because of the lack of rigid specifications, the designers were allowed and obliged to develop their own visions through trial and error in their local design, probing what a meaningful local quality should be (e.g., the monitoring system’s versatility) and evaluating its implications for the whole product (e.g., movable digital theater).

## Theoretical Integration and Discussion

Our research seeks to address the research question: How do product designers attribute meanings to their product in the process of digital product innovation? In integrating our findings, we have developed an explanatory theory (Gregor, 2006) (Figure 5). In what follows, we present this theoretical integration step by step as we discuss the findings in light of the digital innovation literature.

## The Digital Product Innovation Context

Digital product innovation takes place in a particular context that is shaped by the innovation trajectory of the focal firm (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014) in a broader industry-level innovation environment with different actors participating in the reproduction of the socially shared meaning of a product (Verganti, 2009, Kaplan & Tripsas, 2008), dominant designs (Tushman & Murmann, 1998, Benner & Tripsas, 2011), and various forms of institutional arrangements that support such a meaning (Porter, 1980; Van de Ven et al., 1999). This extant innovation environment establishes existing product meanings. To pursue new strategic opportunities, product designers inevitably challenge the existing product meanings through their innovation.

In line with the digital innovation literature (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Nambisan, 2017, Nambisan et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2010a), our findings confirm that as digital components are integrated into a traditional physical product, traditional product boundaries are often challenged, opening up the digital product to opportunities for new interpretations. As a result, new product meanings continually emerge during the design process, which may be related or unrelated, or which may contradict an existing product meaning shaped by the extant innovation environment. As the binding power of the traditional product meaning begins to lose its grip, product designers need to figure out what should be the core product meaning for the new digital product. In this regard, meaningmaking in digital product innovation involves “reflection-inaction” (Schön, 1983, p. 50) that is continually subjected to serendipitous discoveries (Austin et al., 2012; Nylén & Holmström, 2018).

This serendipitous nature of digital innovation (Nylén & Holmström, 2018) means that it is at product designers’ discretion to capture or ignore emerging product meanings. Therefore, the introduction of digital components into an existing physical product provides only a necessary condition for creating valuable innovation opportunities. The discovery of valuable innovation opportunities is contingent on designers’ actions. Our findings show that a significant driving force that propels product designers to embrace emerging product meanings is the strong desire for constant betterment of what has been created, i.e., design attitude (Boland & Collopy, 2004; Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014).

## Meaning-Making Loops

While going beyond an existing product meaning leads to innovation opportunities (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014; Verganti, 2009, 2017), deviating too far from it makes the new product confusing (Hargadon & Douglas, 2001; Lempiälä et al., 2019). Our findings, grounded in an embodied cognition perspective (Clark, 1997; Lakoff & Johnson, 1980, 1999; Varela et al., 1991), show that designers seek to manage the balance between these two positions through what we refer to as two loops of meaning-making: the reinforcing loop, which consists of connecting and unifying, and the differentiating loop, which consists of repositioning and reinterpreting.

First, the reinforcing loop refers to a meaning-making process, where connecting (digital-physical) and unifying (productcomponent) mutually enhance each other toward a coherent core meaning of the digital product. A core meaning can be planned a priori or can emerge from earlier design stages that designers capture and decide to keep as an essential meaning for the digital product (Verganti, 2009). Connecting brings together digital and physical components that implement their respective core meanings in a compatible way. Unifying organizes the components toward a coherent delivery of a core product meaning. While connecting creates more concrete component implementations that sustain the core product meaning, unifying uses the core product meaning to align the component implementations. In this regard, connecting and unifying have a reciprocal relationship (Bacharach, 1989) whereby the core product meaning becomes increasingly clear as digital and physical component meanings make the core product meaning become increasingly congruent, and vice versa.

Second, the differentiating loop denotes a meaning-making process through which repositioning (digital-physical) and reinterpreting (product-component) mutually promote each other in creating new product meanings. An essential source of creative outcomes of digital innovation is serendipitous discoveries (Austin et al., 2012; Nylén & Holmström, 2018). Therefore, the possibility of new creations can be increased by increasing the chance of serendipitous discoveries. Our findings show that product designers often encounter serendipitous discoveries through repositioning. When they reposition their design work in heterogeneous (digital versus physical) design areas, they often find inspiration for creating new meanings. Further, the new meanings do not stop at their local design but have the potential to be elevated as a new product meaning by synthesizing new meanings from multiple components, or by rethinking the overall product in light of a new component meaning, i.e., reinterpreting. Conversely, the new product meaning may open new design directions that shift the control between digital and physical components, which therefore promotes repositioning. Thus, repositioning and reinterpreting also have a reciprocal relationship (Bacharach, 1989) whereby new meanings from digital and physical components give rise to new product meanings, and vice versa.

## Meaning Sedimentation

Product innovation research often depicts meaning-making as a staged process constituted by earlier stages for creating and fixing new product meanings and later stages that implement the meanings into design artifacts (Verganti, 2017). Although the stages can be iterative (Verganti, 2017), the view still features a separation between meaning-making and the creation of design artifacts. Our findings offer a slightly different picture that suggests that meaning-making in digital product innovation follows an ongoing progressive unfolding of designers corporeal experiences (cf. Cerulo, 2019; Lakoff & Johnson, 1980, 1999). The designers discover new possibilities that emerge as they go through two meaning-making loops that generatively provide seemingly unbounded possible connections of digital and physical components (Henfridsson et al., 2018). At each iteration of the meaning-making loops, designers can progressively uncover new meanings that could have not been seen in earlier iterations and base subsequent design choices on those uncovered meanings. In this regard, each iteration of the meaning-making loop builds on top of previous iterations, but also may change earlier meanings, while conditioning future iterations. Analogically, meaning-making in digital innovation looks like a “sedimentation” process, in which product designers stir up new meanings and settle them to form the waterway for further design. Given such an analogy, we term the process by which product designers combine reinforcing and differentiating loops for creating a core product meaning that is progressively extended with new meanings as meaning sedimentation.

The final question is: When do product designers end the process of meaning sedimentation? As discussed above, meaning-making in digital product innovation faces constant challenges to existing product meanings. Successfully dealing with challenges means creating a digital product with a new and comprehensible meaning. However, our findings show that this judgment is based less on optimization and more on satisfaction (Hatchuel, 2001; Simon, 1969). One possible reason is because the incorporation of malleable software assumes endless functional extension and improvement (e.g., the continuous speed-up of the merging system after product launch, DA#05) (Yoo et al., 2010a). Left alone, product designers may be driven by their personal design attitudes, seeking constant betterment beyond existing criteria (Boland et al., 2008; Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014). Even when a design artifact meets given functional criteria, the designers may still reject it (e.g., outsourced merging system, DA#04). In such scenarios, there is not a specific threshold to end the process. Compared with finding a specific exit point, it is more meaningful to monitor whether and how well the meaning of the digital product being designed meets the challenges associated with the existing product and the desire for betterment. The exit depends on the designers’ satisfaction (Hatchuel, 2001; Simon, 1969).

## Implications

Our study makes several important contributions to the growing body of the digital product innovation literature. First, it contributes a model that explains product meaning in digital product innovation. In this regard, it extends prior studies of digital product innovation with a distinct articulation of how meaning-making intersects with product architecture (Henfridsson et al., 2018; Hylving & Schultze, 2020; Yoo et al., 2010a), organizing logic (Barrett et al., 2015; de Reuver et al., 2018; Lusch & Nambisan, 2015), and innovation tools (Barrett et al., 2012; Nandhakumar et al., 2013). Our model suggests that meaning-making loops mediate the innovation process even if there are changes in the product architecture, organizing logic, or innovation tools that spark the change. Future research on the reciprocal relationship between meaning-making loops, product architecture, organizing logic, and innovation tools would be useful.

Second, our study contributes to the literature by demonstrating how physical materiality plays an important role in digital product innovation. This contrasts with the extant literature, which often focuses exclusively on digital objects (Faulkner & Runde, 2019; Kallinikos et al., 2013; Yoo et al., 2012), i.e., software such as machine learning algorithms (Gregory et al., 2021; Yablonsky, 2019) or digital platforms with apps (Eaton et al., 2015; Tiwana, 2013, 2015; Um et al., 2015). Such exclusive attention to the digital at the expense of the physical does not adequately reflect the reality of digital product innovation, which typically requires the integration of physical and digital materiality (Yoo et al., 2010a). Our study, drawing on an embodied cognition lens (Clark, 1997; Lakoff & Johnson, 1980, 1999; Varela et al., 1991), offers an alternative view by showing that the meaning of a digital product emerges through experiencing both the digital and the physical. Our study further shows that the digital and the physical mutually shape each other. It is precisely in the dialectic relationship between them that the very meaning of a digital product emerges. Our study reveals that the design of physical and digital components should not be considered in isolation and IS scholars should attend to the currently underemphasized role of physical materiality in the context of digital product innovation. In particular, we should seek to understand how the materiality of physical components shapes and conditions what is considered to be malleable as well as the ambivalent nature of digital components (Kallinikos et al., 2013).

Third, while confirming new product meanings as essential innovation outcomes, our study further shows that new product meanings are an effective innovation driver (Verganti, 2017). Traditionally, research focuses on what stimulates innovation outcomes, how to redesign the architecture of a product for more functional extensibility (Henfridsson et al., 2014; Yoo et al., 2010), and how to engage peripheral stakeholders (Hukal et al., 2020; Saadatmand et al., 2019). Our study shows that innovation outcomes can also be stimulated by new product meanings, as they offer new interpretive schemes that free product designers from how a product is traditionally conceived (see Lyytinen et al., 2016). Because the differentiating loop offers opportunities for serendipitous discoveries (Austin et al., 2012; Nylén & Holmström, 2018) for new product meanings, a single firm may still make significant innovative leaps through active engagement of the loop to rapidly evolve the interpretive scheme in use and, in turn, quickly iterate their product design. How to promote the differentiating loop, therefore, deserves more attention from future digital innovation research.

Lastly, prior research on innovation focuses on the social construction of technology (Bijker, 1995; Bijker et al., 1987), the evolutionary process of how a dominant design emerges and how it shapes the competitive landscape (Tushman & Murmann, 1998), and how industry architecture emerges in a nascent industry and affects firms’ abilities to extract economic rents from its innovations (Jacobides et al., 2006). A common thread across all these studies is that the competition among alternative meanings shapes the interpretations of the different products, and it is through such competition among meanings that a dominant design emerges, eventually establishing an industry architecture. What has not been studied is how these different competing meanings are created by different firms, especially the internal process of innovation. Our study uncovers the inner working of digital product innovation by offering a detailed explanatory account of how designers go through a meaning-making process through two meaningmaking loops and four constituent modalities.

Our study also offers important practical implications. First, it suggests that product designers should maintain a balanced emphasis on both the novelty and comprehensibility of product meaning as they pursue digital product innovation. On the one hand, because of the need for a clear understanding of what should be a proper project outcome, product designers may fall into endless trial and error and guesswork. To navigate through the chaos, the comprehensibility of the product’s meaning is key. On the other hand, designers should be vigilant with regard to the risk of only emphasizing comprehensibility, as it may drive them down a repetitive and unproductive trajectory (such as TopTech’s attempts to create an excellent fisheye projector). To avoid this trap, they should adopt a design attitude that probes and captures emerging innovation opportunities (Boland & Collopy, 2004).

Another implication warrants a demonstration of how our findings may help product designers avoid potential design mistakes. Take Google Glass as an example: The failure of Google Glass can be traced to the meaning conflict between “glasses” for “use on all private occasions” and digital components known for “always-on information collection and processing.” This meaning conflict could have already been discovered in the design process, which would have allowed the designers to evaluate component meanings and figure out the most proper product meaning for Google Glass. Imaginable alternative trajectories for repairing the design mistake may have been conceived by putting the “use on all private occasions” meaning into or removing it from the reinforcing loop. Putting the meaning into the loop would have emphasized “privacy” and expelled components out of scope (e.g., the camera). Removing the meaning from the loop allowed “always-on information collection and processing” to emerge in the differentiating loop to overthrow the notion of “glasses” from the reference-product position. Then, the designers would have been free to refocus the product as a computing headset for heavy information collection and processing tasks for business occasions, akin to Microsoft’s HoloLens (Roach, 2019). It should be noted that from a product meaning perspective, this should not be considered as a simple shift of use context but a rethinking of every component in light of the new overall product meaning. For example, on the “computing headset for business occasions” trajectory, the product may have also assumed a “work safety” meaning, necessitating the redesign of its tiny screen to improve eye comfort (bbc.com, 2014). This is a rough examination of the Google Glass case with our theory as a basis. However, it shows how practitioners could use the theory to keep alert to the product meaning aspect of their digital product innovation—especially in terms of what the core product meaning should be, what new meanings are emerging, and whether component meanings are conflicting.

## Boundary Conditions and Limitations

There is a set of boundary conditions for the application of our theory. Using Rivard’s (2014, vi-vii) vocabulary, boundary conditions take the form of a set of “conceptual assumptions” inherited from our conceptual basis and a set of “contextual assumptions” of the conditions under which our theory is most likely to hold. First, our theory is a “theory for explaining” (Gregor, 2006, p. 624) how product designers attribute meanings to their products in the process of digital product innovation. For instance, prediction (and other theory traits belonging to other theory types) is outside the scope of this research. Second, we inherited conceptual assumptions (Rivard, 2014, vi-vii) that (1) digital product innovation implies hybrid materiality (Yoo et al., 2010a), and (2) meaning innovation should be driven by product designers (Verganti, 2017); therefore, our theory does not intend to explain digital innovation projects without physical products or projects for which product designers need the permission of key users to pursue opportunities outside of planned specifications.

We also recognize some limitations of the study. First, our study investigated digital innovation on the organizational level. External factors, such as the political, economic, and especially the sociocultural environment of China were not within the scope of this study. Although Verganti’s (2017) analyses of multiple cases can justify the notion that meaning innovation leads to market success in different political, economic, sociocultural environments, our study is unable to validate insights along this line. Second, our single-case study design is limited by its contextual characteristics. Most previous studies use smaller-size products (e.g., digital glasses and smart scales) or pure-digital artifact (i.e., software products) as examples of digital product innovation. However, our case features a product of colossal size and manifests a complex amalgamation of the pure digital, the pure physical, and the hybrid. While such characteristics offer the opportunity to achieve an in-depth understanding of digital product innovation, they also caution against overgeneralizing across all digital product innovation cases. We suggest that future research critically examine our ideas in various digital product innovation settings.

## Concluding Remarks

Firms do not compete on functionality alone. They often compete on meanings. With increasing digitalization, competition based on meanings will only be intensified. Yet little is known about how firms create new meanings. We have taken an initial step in understanding how designers uncover and pursue new meanings and reinforce familiar meanings as they seek to integrate digital components into an existing product. We hope our explanatory theory of meaning-making will inspire further rigorous empirical investigation and further theorization of this important but often neglected aspect of digital product innovation.
