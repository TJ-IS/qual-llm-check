# A model for exploring the impact of purchasing strategies on user requirements determination of e-SRM

Ing-Long Wu <sup>\*</sup>, Yuh-Chen Shen

Department of Information Management, National Chung Cheng University, 160, San-Hsing, Ming-Hsiung, Chia-Yi, Taiwan

Received 20 June 2003; received in revised form 20 November 2003; accepted 29 November 2004 Available online 23 March 2006

## Abstract

Recently, business managers have touted supplier relationship management (SRM) as the next core competency and a source of competitive advantage. This suggests the need of an e-SRM installation. SRM domain involves relatively wide and complex business processes and without the support of IT, it would not be accomplished easily. As the Internet became popular and many firms were quick to launch e-SRM, however, the effect went farther than was expected. In particular, the fundamental is in the determination of user requirements, commonly considered to be the key to the success of system installation and often inappropriately planned. This study involves a strategy-based planning process to guide user requirements determination. Moreover, the implementation step in this process, identification of supplier selection criteria, has rarely been considered and the literature has only discussed it isolatedly for the choice of a supplier. Basically, the process includes three steps: define purchasing strategies, identify supplier selection criteria, and determine system requirements. Empirical results have indicated that purchasing strategies significantly influence the performance of user requirements determination. <sup>#</sup> 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information requirements determination; Supplier selection criteria; Supplier relationship management; Purchasing portfolio model

## 1. Introduction

The rapid growth of the Internet has fundamentally reshaped business structure and offered unprecedented business opportunities for e-commerce (EC). According to Forrester Research, business-to-business (B2B)

e-business will soon reach \$1.3 trillion, outpacing business-to-consumer (B2C) by a factor of 10. The compound average growth rate is estimated to reach a relatively high rate about 90%. Supplier relationship management (SRM) has been recognized as an important issue in B2B EC. The traditional view focuses on leveraging transaction-oriented processes to achieve the lowest purchase price while assuring supply. The fundamental assumption is that trading partners are interchangeable and encouraged to reduce cost due to fierce competition. Tremendous changes have occurred in supplier management practices during the last decade [16,35]. In the automobile sector, for example, all three U.S. manufacturers and most of their European competitors have launched programs to decrease their level of vertical integration, reduce their total number of direct suppliers, and move toward publicly declared strategic partnerships. This practice was basically built on a foundation of trust and commitment from trading partners [29].

Consequently, future competition will not only be firm-to-firm, but also strategic partnership-to-partnership [12]. However, while creating new value, partnerships are costly to develop, nurture, and maintain. In addition, they are risky, given the specialized investments they require. However, managers and business consultants believe that effective supplier relationships will offers firms competitive advantage [27]. In essence, the SRM domain involves relatively wide and complicated processes, including the management of numerous suppliers of raw material, parts, components, sub-assembly, etc., the delivery of various products’ quantity, value, and quality, and suppliers’ reputation, financial condition, and technical ability. The Internet has offered tremendous opportunities for developing web-based online inter active systems to support the SRM process (e-SRM) [9,19,30]. Many firms rushed into e-SRM but the results were less than expected [1], probably due to lack of strategic planning to guide information requirements determination [4,15,34].

This approach can be described as that a type of purchasing relationship with suppliers is once developed for a firm and as a result, shifts the paradigm of organizational buying behaviors and further creates a need for understanding the detailed procurement process. Obviously, purchasing strategies will have fundamental impact on requirements determination of e-SRM. Therefore, the solution proposed here aims at setting a well-planned and unique SRM strategy and further, upon which the determination of e-SRM system requirements is based. This would assure that system requirements is guided by or matched with marketing strategies of relationship building. This solution process basically includes three steps: define purchasing strategies, identify supplier selection criteria, and determine system requirements. Existing literature has not discussed the important role of purchasing strategy in developing an effective e-SRM. The further implementation step for this process, such as identifying supplier selection criteria, was only discussed isolatedly for choosing a specific supplier. There has been a lack of an integrated approach in literature to provide a specific requirement determination process of e-SRM. The techniques used to analyze the steps were also discussed. Finally, this framework was examined by an empirical study to understand its practical applications.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. IT planning and requirements determination

The major reason that IS does not meet user expectation is a failure to obtain the correct and complete set of user requirements. The process involves gathering and modeling information about the system and has been recognized as the most difficult part of system development [13,18,32]. Difficulties in requirements determination arise from [8]: cognitive issues resulting from human understanding; the variety and complexity of the requirements; the complex patterns of interaction among users and analysts in defining requirements; and political or behavioral issues resulting from unwilling to provide the requirements. My research focuses on problem structuring issues; i.e., the unstructured problems of the SRM domain.

A number of techniques have been proposed for developing the IS plan. The focus of these techniques is mainly on the issue of organizational information requirements analysis. The model developed by Bowman et al. [3] stated that strategic alignment should precede the determination of organizational information requirements and subsequent resource allocation. The stimulus for systems development has typically been considered as an organizational problem. The analysis can be seen as a four level pyramid structure, with organizational strategies at the top, business processes at the second, tasks at the third, and information at the bottom [20,37]. Thus, the review of these techniques would provide the theoretical basis on developing the proposed development framework of e-SCM. In sum, the above discussion illustrates an important linkage that an effective approach for user requirements analysis should start with proper organizational strategies, and in particular, purchasing strategies under e-business environment for e-SRM development.

## 2.2. Supplier relationship models

Effective requirements elicitation of e-SRM should start with appropriate purchasing strategies. Thus, portfolio models of purchasing relationship are important in the management of the company’s entire portfolio of supplier relationships. Such models have primarily been used to support resource allocation decisions among strategic business units. Perhaps, the most used and discussed model is the Boston Consulting Group’s (BCG) growth/share matrix. As purchasing management has become more strategic, portfolio models could be applied to improve the allocation of scarce resources by identifying which groups of products, suppliers, or relationships deserve greater attention than others. The following summarizes some representative models.

A portfolio model of supplier relationships was discussed to categorize suppliers and select appropriate procurement strategies—Red, Amber, Green (RAG) [25], as indicated in the left of Fig. 1. However, the range between a vendor relationship and strategic alliance is a continuum without defined boundaries and a product may move from a strategic category to a commodity item during its life cycle. Also, a supplier relationship framework could be a useful guide for mapping EC applications to support different types of supplier relationships, as indicated in the right, although EC applications in the mapping were simple and basic. Inter-enterprise communications at the strategic level may be enabled by the exchange of documents using e-mail and the exchange of product drawing through technical data interchange (TDI) leading to improved time to market and reduced costs through joint processes, explained by effective use of IT to support supplier relationships fully integrated into the business architecture and IT. This also implies that the selection of supplier relationships could be a useful guide to requirements determination of EC applications.

Kraljic [17] developed a product portfolio model to be used for classified purchases and setting strategies. Building on this approach, a portfolio model was proposed by Olsen and Ellram [23] with four types of relationships: leverage, non-critical, strategic, or bottleneck, as indicated in Fig. 2. The strategic importance of a purchase describes factors internal to the firm and includes competence, economic, or image factors. The competence factor describes the extent to which the item is needed for the company’s core competencies. The economic factor describes the importance of the purchase in terms of the dollar value and its impact on company profits. The image factor describes the importance of the purchase among customers and suppliers. The difficulty in managing the purchase occurs because of factors external to the firm, such as the supply market, environmental, or product characteristics. The product characteristics include the novelty and complexity of the product/ service to be purchased. The supply market characteristics assess the supplier’s power, the number of them, their technical competence, etc. The environment characteristics include an overall assessment of the risk and uncertainty associated with a purchase.

The leverage category includes purchases that are easy to manage but strategically important to the company. When managing these purchases, it is important to identify the value added by the purchase and leverage volume across product lines and suppliers to lower the costs. The goal is to create mutual respect in the supplier relationship and communicate future requirements. The two-way relationship should be established and may be handled through system contracting. The non-critical category includes purchases that are easy to manage and have little strategic importance. The company should reduce the number of suppliers and the number of duplicate products/services (they should standardize); it could then use blanket order and system contracting to improve the relationships. The supplier relationship should basically manage itself and thus reduce administrative costs.

The strategic category encompasses purchases that are difficult to manage but important to the company, which should manage such purchases by establishing a close relationship with suppliers, focusing on early involvement and joint development of products and services, focusing on long-term values and lowering performance cost. Finally, the bottleneck category includes the purchases that have a low strategic importance but are difficult to manage. To manage these purchases more effectively, the company should try to standardize the purchases or find substitutes if possible. The company should try to establish some sort of relationships focusing on concurrent engineering and involving the supplier in value analysis in order to lower the cost of operations.

Bensaou [2] administered a survey questionnaire, in English and Japanese, to a total of 447 managers in three U.S. and 11 Japanese automobile manufacturing. Searching for naturally occurring patterns of supplier relationships within the data collected, he found that the level of specific investments made by either partner in the relationship significantly correlates with supplier relationships. He segmented the two national samples into four generic cells using this criterion (see Fig. 3). The vertical axis represents the buyer’s specific investments. These are tangible investments in buildings, tooling, and equipment dedicated to the supplier or in products and processes customized to the components procured from the suppliers. Buyer’s investments also include intangible investments in effort spent learning the supplier’s business practices and routines or spent exchanging information, best practice, and knowledge to further develop and nurture the relationship. The horizontal axis represents the supplier’s specific investments. Tangible investments include plant, specialized facilities, warehouse location, etc. Intangible investments include sending guest engineers to buyers and developing IS compatible with the buyer’s proprietary databases or electronic data interchange protocols.

In the ‘‘strategic partnership’’ cell, both parties post highly idiosyncratic assets in the relationship. Economists call these assets credible commitments or hostages; practitioners speak of real commitment or tying their hands to each other. The ‘‘market exchange’ cell represents the cluster of relationships in which neither of the parties has developed specialized assets to work with the other; they can work together using general-purpose assets. Each party can turn to the marketplace and shift to another business partner at lower cost and minimal damage. The ‘‘captive buyer’’ cell refers to asymmetric relationships in which the buyer is held hostage by a supplier free to switch to another customer. Finally, in the ‘‘captive supplier’ relationships, the supplier enters the trap of unilaterally making idiosyncratic investments to win and keep the business with the customer.

## 2.3. Supplier selection criteria

A type of purchasing relationship is developed and, as a result, it shifts the paradigm of organizational buying behavior and creates a need for further understanding the process of supplier relationship (such as supplier selection). This is important because it commits resources while simultaneously impacting activities such as inventory management, production planning and control, and product quality [21,26]. In essence, an understanding of this criterion is key to the success of firms’ practices. Subsequently, this analysis would offer more substantial information to assist requirements determination of e-SRM. Choi and Hartley [5] surveyed purchasing managers to find their supplier selection practices in the US automotive industry. To address this, they built on the criteria of Dickson [10] and Weber et al. [36]. They compiled the lists and considered criteria that were consistent across the lists. This initially resulted in a list of 26 criteria. Furthermore, principal component analysis with varimax rotation was used to identify the underlying constructs from these. This analysis finally converged to eight factors (finances, consistency, relationship, flexibility, technological capability, service, reliability, and price; see Table 1).

Swift [31] discussed the process of developing supplier selection criteria in an analysis to determine whether there were substantial differences in criteria between purchasing managers having dissimilar preferences for product sourcing decisions. A review of various scales in the supplier selection literature resulted in the use of that of Spekman [28]. In the original study, the 31 items were subjected to factor analysis, which reduced it to 21 items. Furthermore, factor analysis with varimax rotation was used to extract five factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 (see Table 2). Ellram [11] described the factors that influenced a company to choose a supplier. He contended to use a contingency approach because the factors and their importance would vary from company to company. The factors are summarized in Table 3. Cusumano and Takeishi [7] listed a number of potential criteria and further conducted a survey of Japanese and US auto makers to examine their differences. The criteria include finance, price, quality, delivery, technological capability, and past business relations.

## 3. Theoretical framework

Effective supply chain management requires choosing a type of relationship appropriate to product and market conditions and adapting management practices to it. The design is a strategic decision because it affects how the firm defines its boundaries, competitive environment, and core activities of the value chain. Over recent years, it has been difficult for firms to practice SRM successfully due to the complexity of this process, in particular, inter-organizational collaboration and online interaction. The advances of Internet and communication technologies have provided resources for accomplishing e-SRM. Though many firms installed e-SRM, they failed to achieve expected results, probably due to a lack of a strategy-based approach. Moreover, to implement the purchasing process, the organization must understand the issues of the supplier selection practices. As a result, an integrated analysis would provide a useful guide to requirements determination of e-SRM. However, there has been relatively little research on the role of strategy in building effective e-SRM. Therefore, this study proposed an integrated framework for requirements elicitation based on a strategic decision of supplier relationship, as indicated in Fig. 4.

## 3.1. The first step: define purchasing strategies

Three representative models have been presented for managing a company’s portfolio of supplier relationships. Basically, the Olsen and Ellram model is more general for use in different industries, while the Bensaou’s model is mainly developed from automobile manufacturers, and the RAG framework merely reports a part of the portfolio of supplier relationships. As the Olsen and Ellram model has more power in interpreting research findings, we used it to analyze purchasing relationships.

## 3.2. The second step: identify supplier selection criteria

This step determines the important supplier selection criteria, guided by the purchasing strategies. Various classification schemes for supplier selection criteria are similar in terms of the criteria, such as finance, technology, price, etc., and the descriptions of the selection criteria show little difference. Comparatively, the Choi and Hartley scheme covers the common characteristics and thus, it was adopted here.

## 3.3. The third step: determine systems requirements

The supplier selection criteria are then used to derive the specific IT-supporting features for building the company’s e-SRM; e.g., if consistency and service factors are identified as importance, the e-SRM should include an integration of customer orders with inventory management, production scheduling, and delivery scheduling through the media of Internet and Intranet technologies for just-intime delivery as well as web-based on-line prompt response and computer–telephone integration through the medium of Internet technology for high quality service. In particular, the major focus is on the relationship between purchasing strategies and the requirements determination of e-SRM.

This process was developed from a literature review, and thus further verification using empirical data was needed. The following two null hypotheses were proposed for this.

Hypothesis 1. Purchasing strategies have positive impact on supplier selection criteria.

Hypothesis 2. Supplier selection criteria have positive impact on the performance of user requirements analysis.

## 4. Research design

A survey was conducted to collect empirical data.

## 4.1. Instrumentation

The survey instrument contains a four-part questionnaire. The first part uses a nominal scale, while the rest use seven-point Likert scales.

## 4.1.1. Basic information

Information was collected about organizational characteristics, including industry, annual revenue, number of employees, and installation of e-SRM, together with respondent’s characteristics, including education, age, experience, and position.

## 4.1.2. Purchasing strategies

The measurements for the two dimensions were adapted from the Olsen and Ellram study. The strategic importance of the purchase described the three factors of competence, economic, and image with three items, four items and two items, respectively. Thus, nine items are used in measuring this dimension. The difficulty of managing the purchase uses the three factors of product, supply market, and environment with six items, five items, and five items, respectively. As a result, a total of 16 items is defined for measuring this dimension.

## 4.1.3. Supplier selection criteria

This was adapted from the descriptions shown in Table 1. There are eight criteria, finance, consistency, relationship, flexibility, technological capability, service, reliability, and price, as well as certain items for them; e.g., four items of financial conditions, profitability of supplier, financial records disclosure, and performance awards defined for finance criterion. The first four criteria have four items to measure each and the second four have two items for each.

## 4.1.4. Assessment of system requirements

This part was adapted from the measurement of assessing information requirements elicitation given in the Teng and Sethi’s study [32]. Thus, it includes five items: accuracy and free bias, completeness, shortening of user determination time, usefulness of output information, and ease of use.

## 4.2. Pretest

To measure reliability and validity, a pretest was conducted using a representative sample of thirty companies. The measure is reliable if it supplies consistent results, as measured by Cronbach a [6]. According to Price and Mueller [24], a value of 0.60 or higher is generally viewed as acceptable for the measure. In this study, validity is of two types: content and construct validity. The content validity is the extent to which it provides adequate coverage of the topic. The construct validity attempts to identify the underlying construct(s) being measured and determines how well the test represents them. Factor analysis was used for the analysis of construct validity. The rule of eigenvalue greater than 1 as the criterion extracts the factors [14]. The second, third, and fourth parts of the questionnaire were all adapted from the literature and have been reviewed carefully by practitioners; thus, content validities should be relatively acceptable. Construct validities were confirmed using principal component analysis as the defined factors/criteria in the second and third parts of the questionnaire: they all have eigenvalues greater than 1.98 and the percentage variance accounted for greater than 70.3. The Cronbach’s a all are 0.71 and above. The fourth part of the questionnaire was converged to one factor with eigenvalue of 3.18 and the percentage variance accounted for of 73.5%. The Cronbach a is 0.88.

## 4.3. Sample organizations and respondents

The study requires analysis of buyer–supplier behavior in developing a relationship with suppliers. This process involves strategic planning and also requires upper management agreement to large IT investments. Thus, it was assumed that larger firms in the manufacturing sector would be more likely to have this experience. Basically, this sector can be further divided into high-tech and traditional subsectors. A company sample of 400 high-tech and 600 traditional manufacturing firms was thus selected from the year 2001 listing of commonwealth 1155 manufacturing. Furthermore, this study is to explore the planning and development of e-SRM from a strategic perspective. CIOs may be the persons more likely to be familiar with the IS activities while purchasing managers are likely to be responsible for procurement activities. Thus, CIOs were selected as primary respondents and purchasing managers as associate respondents for each firm in order to avoid response bias by using a single informant. Examining the responding data across the two groups of respondents (two sub-samples) indicates a pattern of relative consistency in the firms’ experience.

## 5. Analysis and findings

## 5.1. Demographics

Of the 1000 firms contacted, 199 replied, with five incomplete responses deleted, resulting in a total sample of 194 firms for a 19.4% response rate. The responding sample consisted of 85 high-tech manufacturing firms (43.8%) and 109 traditional manufacturing firms (56.2%). This indicates that the distribution of this sample in terms of high-tech and traditional manufacturing firms is approximately the same as that of the sample frame. The relatively low response rate raised a concern of non-response bias. A test for it was conducted using two sub-samples: early and late respondents. They were correlated on their annual revenue and working experience. There was no significant non-response bias in the sample.

## 5.2. Empirical analysis

## 5.2.1. The first step: define purchasing strategies

This step empirically clustered the responding sample into four types of supplier relationships to analyze strategic management of purchasing practices. Knowledge of such practices would be useful in guiding the development of purchasing strategies for companies in a competitive market. Firms were clustered into the non-critical category if the values of the strategic importance of the purchase and the difficulty of managing the purchase situation were both less than 4 (the mean value) in response to the questions. Firms were clustered into the strategic category when the values of the two variables were both greater than 4. ## 5.2.3. The third step: determine system requirements

## 6. Conclusions and suggestions

While SRM was recognized as an important issue in B2B EC, the selection of an ideal relationship with suppliers would be a major advantage to a firm and a way to achieve competitive advantage. This justifies the need for better supplier relationship management. SRM involves relatively complex business processes and without the support of IT, it would not be accomplished easily. Moreover, the Internet and communication technologies have increasingly become popular in support of SRM. This approach proposed a three-step process to effectively develop e-SRM: define purchasing strategies, identify supplier selection criteria, and determine system requirements. In general, the manufacturing firms in Taiwan balance a portfolio of different types of purchasing relationships rather than rely on only one type. Successful SRM practice, therefore, requires management of a portfolio of relationships with suppliers. There are significant differences in the choice of supplier selection criteria for various supplier relationships.

The major implication for academicians lies in that the particular connection of purchasing strategies and supplier selection criteria for commonly determining user requirements of e-SRM is unique in literature. Moreover, this study contributes not only in developing a well-defined process of user requirements determination but also determining useful techniques to effectively implement it. In conclusion, the new approach fundamentally overcomes the impact of problem unstructured issues associated with SRM domain on user requirements determination of e-SRM. The implications for practitioners are as below. A strategy-base analysis is important because firms are able to adopt various purchasing strategies to match their various product characteristics, market competition, and supplier conditions. Thus, system develop ment unit should start to communicate with relevant functional units to define the appropriate type of supplier relationship. Alternatively, system developers can approximately identify the purchasing strategies in terms of the firms clustered as high-tech sub-sector or traditional sub-sector in this analysis.

Moreover, the emphases on supplier selection criteria, which are the underlying determinants of user requirements of e-SRM can, therefore, be determined from this analysis of the relationship between purchasing strategies and supplier selection criteria. Leverage and non-critical types of relationships both consider the price factor as one of the emphasis on selecting suppliers. Strategic and bottleneck types of relationships both consider consistency factor as one of the emphases on selecting suppliers. Finally, the user requirements for the particular e-SRM would be determined in a more efficient and effective way. Although this study has produced some interesting results, it has some limitations. First, although the response rate was low, systematic non-response bias did not exist in the sample; it is representative of the sample frame. Second, CIOs from larger firms were chosen to be the participants in this survey; however, some questionnaires might have been completed by subordinates and thus the data may include bias.
