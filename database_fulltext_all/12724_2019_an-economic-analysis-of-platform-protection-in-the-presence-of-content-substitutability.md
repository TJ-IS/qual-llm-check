ABSTRACT: Online platforms, such as App Store and Kindle, are facing a common dilemma: while the implementation of technology-based protection impedes piracy and hence boosts demand from legal users (positive effect), the resulting restriction meanwhile imposes some level of disutility on the same due to inconvenience (negative effect). This paper investigates a monopolistic platform’s optimal protection level and pricing strategy under an agency business model (content agency model or advertising agency model). We find that the platform’s protection strategy hinges on the relative magnitude of the two opposing effects of protection. When the negative effect dominates, the minimal protection is optimal. However, as the positive effect becomes more salient, the platform has an incentive to increase the protection level. We also find that although the demand of non-pure platform users always increases as the level of content substitutability rises under the minimal and maximal protection regions, it is not necessarily the case under the medium protection region. The study advances our understanding of the content protection against piracy from a platform’s perspective. Our findings also provide insights into business model decision when platform protection is endogenously determined.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: online platforms, two-sided platforms, platform protection, online piracy, pricing, content agency, advertising agency, content protection.

Two-sided platforms have created value through facilitating interactions or transactions between participants (i.e., users and content providers) from both sides [29]. For example, App Store connects app developers and users; Kindle connects publishers and readers; PlayStation connects game developers and players; RealNetwork’s Rhapsody connects artists and listeners. Unfortunately, due to the relatively easy reproduction nature of digital content, piracy has always been a serious issue for the information service industries, such as e-books [1], movies [10], music [37, 50], and software [48]. Piracy has been reported to cause huge economic losses to various platforms. For instance, nearly three million people search for unauthorized downloads of the top 90 books on Amazon every day, which can be translated into a \$3 billion annual revenue loss [15]. Nintendo, the market leader in game platforms, has lost \$41 billion due to illegal online downloads.<sup>1</sup> Moreover, piracy has also caused huge losses to content providers who provide digital products or services for platforms. A study by 24/7 Wall Street reports that Apple and the companies that sell software to App Store have lost over \$450 million due to piracy since the store opened in July 2008.<sup>2</sup> Developers Neptune Interactive Inc. and Smells Like Donkey Inc. claim that their apps were available in pirated versions within 40 minutes of their release on App Store. The average piracy rate is estimated to be as high as three, meaning that the volume of pirated content is about three times higher than the volume of paid applications sold in the App Store.<sup>3</sup>

Huge revenue loss resulting from piracy induces platforms to implement platform protection. Platform protection refers to the technological deterrence that platforms adopt to fight piracy, typically achieved by implementing Digital Rights Management (DRM) systems. For example, DRM platforms for digitally delivered products include SecureMedia Encryptonite (which is embedded in many digital televisions), Macrovision SafeCast, Apple’s FairPlay, and Microsoft Windows Media DRM Series [45]. Video games and Apple Music typically use DRM technology to limit unauthorized copying and sharing activities. However, the effectiveness of platform protection has been questioned, because not only piracy may continue if pirates find a way to break the platform protections, but also some platform protection enforcements such as DRM can impose some inconvenient restrictions on legal use. Consequently, adopting an appropriate level of technology-based protection becomes a new challenge for platforms. In fact, different platforms exhibit different preferences about platform protection. For instance, Deutsche Telekom’s Musicload, one of the largest online music stores in Europe, is strongly against DRM.<sup>4</sup> Apple stopped adopting the FairPlay DRM in its iTunes Store since April 2009.<sup>5</sup> Interestingly, Apple’s works sold on iTunes, such as apps, movies, and TV shows, are protected by DRM.<sup>6</sup> Motivated by the above market evidence, this paper tries to investigate the reason behind it.

There are two different business models adopted by platforms. Most commonly, the content agency model, originated in the e-book industry [24], is employed by content providers to offer contents on platforms and set the prices while sharing a contractually fixed percentage of their content revenue with platforms (e.g., iOS app developers yield 30 percent of app revenue to Apple). Another one is the advertisement agency model, which has been adopted by Apple iOS platform and the Google Play platform. In this model, content providers offer contents for free, grant the right of setting advertisements in contents to the platform, and share revenue from advertisers with it. Interestingly, although the platform recognizes the dangers of piracy, a minimal amount of attention has been paid to study the impact of piracy in different business models and the optimal level of platform protection. We attempt to fill this gap in this research.

The objective of this paper is to examine the effect of the platform protection and the optimal level of the platform protection under two different business models. Specifically, we present our findings by answering the following key questions: What is the optimal level of platform protection under two different business models? How does the level of substitutability of contents, the negative effects of protection on pure platform users’ utilities, and the positive effect of protection on non-platform user demand affect the degree of platform protection, prices, numbers of advertisements, demands of contents, and players’ profits? What is the difference in platform protection strategies under different business models? What is the optimal business model in the presence of platform protection?

To address the aforementioned questions, we developed a theoretical model in which two content providers provide contents on a platform and where the contents are potentially copied. There are two types of consumers: pure platform users and non-pure platform users. The pure platform users only buy the legal content from the platform due to their low moral acceptability of copyright violation [20] or sufficiently high additional value provided by platforms. The non-pure platform users are not fanatical platform users and consider both the legal and pirated contents as acceptable options. Different from the pure platform users, the nonpure platform users may either switch to a platform user or become an illegal user. The platform chooses and commits to a platform protection policy. Higher platform protection makes the contents on the platform become more attractive since limited content will “leak out of the platform,” which consequently promotes the legal demand from non-pure platform users. However, higher platform protection, such as DRM-based technological deterrence, often imposes paying users some inconvenient restrictions from which legal users derive some disutility. We refer to the former effect as the positive effect of platform protection and refer to the later one as negative effect of platform protection. Prior to purchases and consumption, users know the platform’s protection level, the value and substitutability of each of authorized contents; they then choose to buy the content provided by either provider, or both.

The main findings are fourfold. First, we found that the relationship between protection and content prices (or the numbers of ads) is determined by both of the positive and negative effects arising from platform protection. When the positive effect is weak, the price or the number of ads decreases in the level of protection. As the magnitude of the positive effect increases, the level of protection increases the relative attractiveness of the authorized content and thus boosts demand from the non-pure platform users. The result is that the content providers or the platform will increase the content price or the number of the ads.

Second, we show that the platform’s protection strategy also hinges on the relative importance of the platform protection’s positive and negative effects. When the negative effect dominates the positive effect, the optimal protection policy is to adopt the minimal protection to capture more profit from the pure platform users. However, as the positive effect increases, the demand increases with the platform protection since the demand of pure platform users increases in the platform protection. The platform can benefit from choosing a medium protection policy. When the negative effect is dominated by the positive effect, it is optimal to impose maximal protection to capture more non-pure platform users.

Third, we analyzed the effect of the level of the substitutability of two contents on the equilibrium outcomes. As expected, the equilibrium prices under the content agency model, the optimal numbers of ads under the advertisement agency model, the total demand for each content, the platform’ profit, and the content providers’ profits decrease in the level of the substitutability of contents, and thus platform protection decreases in the substitutability. Nevertheless, we find that the relationship between the demand from the pure platform users (and that from the non-pure platform users) and the level of the substitutability of two contents is related to the degree of the platform protection. In the minimal and maximal protection regions, the demand of non-pure platform users (pure platform users) always increases (decreases) in the level of content substitutability. However, in the medium protection region, when the positive effect of protection is large, the demand of non-pure platform users (pure platform users) decreases (increases) in the level of substitutability of content.

Finally, we compared the platform’s profits and the levels of platform protection under the two different business models. We find that when determining the relationship between platform protection and profit, we should consider the business model the platform adopts and some significant factors, such as the proportion of profit division, the strength of users’ price sensitivity, the average revenue rate of each advertisement for a pure platform user, and the strength of users’ advertisement sensitivity. We also find that there exists consistency between platform protection and profit: under certain conditions, adopting an appropriate business model and higher protection may generate more revenue.

## Literature Review

This study is mainly related to 3 streams of literature: piracy, two-sided platforms, and business and revenue models.

The first stream is piracy. One branch of this stream investigates the economic effect of piracy and firms’ attitudes toward piracy. Conner and Rumelt [14] found that the firm’s profit decreases with technology-based protection when the product displays positive network effects; otherwise, it increases. More recently, Sundararajan [45] showed that a seller’s optimal level of technology-based protection is at the technologically maximal level in the absence of price discrimination. Geng and Lee [17] showed that reducing piracy quality and increasing piracy search costs are both effective in controlling piracy. Bhattacharjee et al. [6] found that in the presence of piracy, a retailer’s profit is maximized if its product is a good digital experience. Lahiri and Dey [32] showed that a lower enforcement of piracy increases the quality of legal products in certain situations. Smith and Telang [43] found that the pirated content has no effect on the post-broadcast DVD sale by using and analyzing the date of all movies shown on the over-the-air and cable television during an eight-month period in 2005-2006. Ma et al. [38] found piracy affects the product’s lifecycle, and pre-release piracy is more harmful than the postrelease piracy. Godinho de Matos et al. [18] found resisting piracy is more harmful through an empirical analysis. Some literature determined that it is profitable to tolerate or support piracy. For instance, Zhang [50] found that removing DRM increases digital music sales by 10 percent, and it also increases the sales of lowerselling albums significantly. Leung [37] determined that music piracy contributes 12 percent to iPod sales. Finally, music piracy decreases record companies’ profits due to lower record sales; whereas, it increases artists’ revenue from other music sources such as concerts (e.g., Gayer and Shy[16] and Mortimer et al. [40]).

Another branch of study examined ways to combat piracy. Zwass [52] points out that “preventive controls (increasing the cost of piracy by technological means) can be used to combat software piracy.” Cheng et al. [13] showed that providing free functionality of the software for consumers is an effective measure against piracy. In summary, previous literature discusses when and how certain tools, such as nonlinear pricing, versioning [49], sampling [10], bundling [19], content delivery technology [5,27], patch distribution [28], free trials [41], or digital rights management (DRM) [45], may help to combat piracy. The aforementioned literature studies piracy from the perspective of the one-sided market. They do not address the issue from the perspective of the two-sided platform. Our paper fills this gap in the extant literature.

Second, our study also contributes to the literature on two-sided platforms (e.g., Hagiu [22] and Rochet and Tirole [42]). Some studies show that platforms can manipulate both the price level (the price charged to members on both sides) and the price structure (how the total price is split up) [42, 46]. Some literature shows that platforms can implement subsidies or discounts to one side and determine whether the two sides of the platform are single or multiple [3]. Li et al. [33] found that if 2 platforms are relatively undifferentiated, trying to increase the cross-group network benefits for the buyers can be counterproductive. Katsamakas and Bakos [29] found that the design of the two-sided network is highly asymmetricly independent of its ownership structure under available technologies. Mantena and Saha [39] found that small asymmetries in platform technologies can make a huge difference in profits. Lin et al. [35] showed that the platform’s optimal price for sellers depends on the sellers’ surplus, and the optimal price charged to buyers mitigates sellers’ price competition by considering seller-side innovation and price competition. Kim and Tse [30] found that when the accumulated knowledge changes slowly, an online knowledge-sharing platform may lose its leadership if the latecomer invests less in investigating the effects of the price structure of advertising on two-sided platforms. Bakos and Katsamakas [4,7] showed that due to network externalities, a platform will focus its investment on one side to ensure the other side’s participation. Previous articles on two-sided markets focused on pricing structures and some relevant strategic choices but neglect the impacts of piracy on the platform. In our paper, we not only examine the effect of platform protection on all players, but also endogenously determine the optimal platform protection.

Third, this paper is also closely related to the growing stream of literature of different business and revenue models [9, 11, 12, 23]. Some papers focus on one type of revenue model and study the optimal strategies. For example, Hao et al. [23] analyzed the optimal advertising levels and optimal advertising revenue-sharing contract in a mobile platform. A number of papers compared different revenue models. For example, Zhang et al. [51] compared the freemium and bundling strategy and found that whether to choose freemium strategy depends on the product quality. Chen et al. [11] considered the settings in which a platform will choose an advertising model or brokerage model and found that the choice of the business model is related to the advertising space and matching probability. Casadesus-Masanell and Zhu [9] analyzed the optimal business model choice among four business models, including a content agency model, an ad-sponsored model, a mixed model incorporating both subscriptions and advertising, and a dual model in which one product uses the ad-sponsored model and the other uses the mixed model. They found that the firm prefers the subscription-based or the adsponsored model. Lin et al. [36] considered settings in which online service providers may offer an ad-free service, an ad-supported service, or a combination of these services in both monopoly and duopoly. They found that for a monopolist, it is optimal to offer both ad-free and ad-supported services; however, for duopoly, one firm should offer both services if, and only if, the ad revenue rate is sufficiently high. Our study differs from theirs in that we not only compared the content agency model and advertisement agency model in the presence of piracy, but also analyzed the impact of the platform protection and content substitutability under the 2 different business models.

## Model

We consider 2 content providers providing respective products through a monopolistic platform on which users purchase either one of the contents or both. On one hand, the platform provides services to facilitate interactions and the operation of exchanges between users and content providers. On the other hand, the platform provides a suitable technology-based protection, such as, by implementing digital rights management (DRM) systems, to protect the contents from being pirated. We assume that the development costs associated with the platform and contents are sunk and normalize the marginal costs for contents to zero. In the remainder of this paper, we refer to the platform as “it,” to the content provider as “he,” and to the user as “she.”

Consistent with the practice, we consider that the platform can choose from 2 different business models: a content agency model and an advertisement agency model, both of which are prevalent in the platform economic [23]. There are several differences between the 2 models. First, in the former, users should pay the content provider(s) for the access to the content(s). In the later, however, users can use the contents for free but incur some nuisance cost from advertisements bundled with the contents. Second, in the former, the content providers decided the prices of contents and pay a share of content sales revenue 1 R to the platform. In the later, however, the platform decides the number of advertisements in contents and pays a share of advertisement revenue r to content providers. We use superscripts “S” and “A” on variables to denote the scenarios under content agency model and advertisement agency model, respectively.

Huge revenue loss resulting from piracy provides platforms an incentive to implement technology-based platform protection. An effective DRM-based technological deterrence often imposes paying consumers some inconvenient restrictions from which legal users derive some disutility. For instance, encryption for digital video and music limits the download times of legal contents. Following the previous literature (e.g., Sun et al. [44] and Sundararajan [45]), if a platform sets a level of platform protection $k \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ , users on platform will incur a disutility $e k ^ { j } \ ( j = S , A )$ , where $e > 0$ and measures the strength of the effect of platform protection on the users’ utility. However, when platform provide platform protection, more contents will be only obtained from the platform, which in turn leads to users who would otherwise be outside of the platform will consider the content on the platform more value and will join the platform. Consequently, the effect of platform protection on the demand of potential legal users is $a k ^ { j } ,$ , where $a > 0$ represents the strength of the effect of platform protection on the potential demand. Note that platform protection might result in some extent of negative effect on the demand of non-pure platform customers. Once this situation occurs, the platform vendor must have a strong incentive to create a total positive effect on the demand of non-pure platform customers through several active actions such as reducing price and reducing platform protection level.

Following Lahiri and Dey [32] and Wu et al. [48], we consider 2 segments of users for each content. The first segment is pure platform users. These users of the segment only purchase and consume the legal content(s) from the platform, since they are “moral” and have no interest in pirated content or they consider that platform will give them more other valuation except contents. The other segment is not fanatical platform users (hereafter, non-pure platform users). These users of the segment are out of the platform and may use pirated content or out of the market. First, we describe the pure platform users. Non-platform users will be described in the latter subsections.

The number of pure platform users is normalized to 1, and the pure platform users have 3 options: buy Content 1, buy Content 2, or buy both. A user will gain an intrinsic utility V once she joins the platform. In addition, if a user only adopts Content 1 (2), she will gain an intrinsic utility $u _ { 1 } \ ( u _ { 2 } )$ . If a user adopts both, the utility she will derive is $u _ { 1 2 }$ . Following the previous literature [8], let $u _ { 1 2 } = u _ { 1 } + ( 1 - \lambda ) u _ { 2 }$ , where λ represents the level of substitutability of contents. The contents are partial substitutes for $0 ~ \leq ~ \lambda < ~ 1$ . Contents 1 and 2 are perfect substitutes when $\lambda = 1$ , and they are independent when $\lambda = 0$ . Notice that $\lambda = 1$ corresponds to the standard case in the literature, where users might use only a single unit of either content. When $0 < \lambda < 1$ , it represents a scenario in which users prefer to buy a similar content. Some people still have WPS after purchasing office software. The larger the value of λ is, the more substitutable the two contents are.

In the next subsection, we describe the specific characteristics of all players in the market under the 2 business models. In particular, we first introduce the content agency model followed by the advertisement agency model.

## Content Agency Model

## User Utility

A pure platform user must pay $p _ { 1 } \ ( p _ { 2 } )$ for Content 1 (Content 2). Pure platform users are heterogeneous with respect to their preferences for the 2 contents and are uniformly distributed over a Hotelling line with range (0.1). Following the previous literature (e.g., Li et al. [34] and Nan et al. [41]), the unit misfit cost for contents is 1. As a result, a user located at x will incur disutility x. We also assume that each user has a unit demand for the content(s) [26], and the intrinsic valuation of platform and that of content(s) is large, relative to the misfit costs and price, such that the market is fully covered [31]. The user’s utility is summarized as follows:

The net utility of a pure platform user buying Content 1 is given as:

$$
U _ {1} ^ {S} = V + u _ {1} - p _ {1} - x - e k ^ {S}\tag{1}
$$

The net utility of a pure platform user buying Content 2 is given as:

$$
U _ {2} ^ {S} = V + u _ {2} - p _ {2} - (1 - x) - e k ^ {S}\tag{2}
$$

The net utility of a pure platform user buying both contents is given as:

$$
U _ {3} ^ {S} = V + u _ {1} + (1 - \lambda) u _ {2} - p _ {1} - p _ {2} - x - (1 - x) - 2 e k ^ {S}\tag{3}
$$

When $U _ { 2 } ^ { S } = U _ { 3 } ^ { S }$ , we obtain $x _ { 2 } ^ { S } = - e k ^ { S } - p _ { 1 } + u _ { 1 } - \lambda u _ { 2 }$ . Let $d _ { 1 } ^ { S } = x _ { 2 } ^ { S }$ , which is the demand for Content 1. Now, we describe the demand from the non-pure platform users for each content. When the platform provides protection, the pirated option becomes less attractive since very few contents will “leak out of the platform,” which may attract more potential users. We use parameters $d d _ { 1 } ^ { S }$ and $d d _ { 2 } ^ { S }$ to denote the demands for Contents 1 and 2 for non-pure ethical users, respectively. We formulate $d d _ { 1 } ^ { S }$ and $d d _ { 2 } ^ { S }$ as follows:

$$
d d _ {1} ^ {S} = h _ {1} + a k ^ {S} - b p _ {1},\tag{4}

## Content Providers

Under the content agency model, content providers set the price of contents and keep only R of the revenue, leaving the remainder to the platform. The value of R is exogenously given (see, e.g., Alam [1] and Chen et al. [11]). Each content provider’s objective is to maximize his profit by determining the price of his content $( \mathrm { i . e . , } p _ { 1 } \ \mathrm { o r } \ p _ { 2 } )$ . The profits of the content providers are given as:

$$
\pi_ {1} ^ {S} = R D _ {1} ^ {S} p _ {1},\tag{8}
$$

$$
\pi_ {2} ^ {S} = R D _ {2} ^ {S} p _ {2}.\tag{9}
$$

## Platform

To prevent piracy, the platform provide protection at the cost ${ \textstyle \frac { 1 } { 2 } } k ^ { S ^ { 2 } }$ . The platform’ objective is to maximize its profit by determining the optimal level of $k ^ { S }$ . The profit of the platform is given as:

$$
\Pi^ {S} = (1 - R) \left(D _ {1} ^ {S} p _ {1} + D _ {2} ^ {S} p _ {2}\right) - \frac {1}{2} k ^ {S ^ {2}}.\tag{10}
$$

## Advertisement Agency Model

## Consumer Utility

Under the advertisement agency model, pure platform users will use the contents for free. However, the platform will put advertisements in free contents, and users will have to view these advertisements when they use the contents, which brings negative effects to users. We denote the number of advertisements shown in Contents 1 and 2 as $A _ { 1 }$ and $A _ { 2 }$ , respectively. Thus, a user’ nuisance cost from advertisements is $c A _ { i } \ ( i = 1 , 2 )$ , where c represents the negative effect of an advertisement on pure platform users. A pure platform user’s utility is as follow.

The net utility of a pure platform user only using Content 1 is given as:

$$
U _ {1} ^ {A} = V + u _ {1} - c A _ {1} - x - e k ^ {A},\tag{11}
$$

The net utility of a pure platform user only using Content 2 is given as:

$$
U _ {2} ^ {A} = V + u _ {2} - c A _ {2} - (1 - x) - e k ^ {A},\tag{12}
$$

The net utility of a pure platform user using the both contents is given as:

$$
U _ {3} ^ {A} = V + u _ {1} + (1 - \lambda) u _ {2} - c A _ {1} - c A _ {2} - x - (1 - x) - 2 e k ^ {A}.\tag{13}
$$

Let $U _ { 1 } ^ { A } = U _ { 3 } ^ { A }$ , we can get an indifferent point $x _ { 1 } ^ { A }$ . Thus, the demand for Content 2 on the platform is $d _ { 2 } ^ { A } = 1 - x _ { 1 } ^ { A } = - c A _ { 2 } - e k ^ { A } + ( 1 - \lambda ) u _ { 2 }$ . Let $U _ { 2 } ^ { A } = U _ { 3 } ^ { A }$ , we can get an indifferent point $x _ { 2 } ^ { A }$ . Thus, the demand for Content 2 on the platform is $d _ { 1 } ^ { A } = x _ { 2 } ^ { A } = - c A _ { 1 } - e k ^ { A } + u _ { 1 } - \lambda u _ { 2 }$

Proposition 1(a) shows that the equilibrium prices increase in the level of platform protection when the positive effect of platform protection is larger than the negative effect. Intuitively, when $a > e ,$ the total demand will increase, which induces the content providers to charge higher prices. Moreover, although protection increases the demand of non-pure platform users, it also reduces the utilities of pure platform users. As a result, when $0 < a < e ,$ the positive effect is too limited to cover the negative effect, which induces the content providers to charge lower prices to capture more pure platform users.

Proposition 1(b) uncovers that the content providers’ profits increase in the level of platform protection when the positive effect of platform protection is sufficiently small. This is because sufficiently small negative effect enables the content providers to set a higher price, which in turn enhances their profits. Similarly, when the negative effect of platform protection is sufficiently large, the higher demand of non-pure platform users makes the content providers generate more profits. Interestingly, when the negative effect of platform protection is medium, the content providers will be hurt by the higher platform protection. This is because in this case, negative effect is enhanced whereas the positive effect is reduced simultaneously, this change asks each content provider to set lower price that leads to a lower revenue.

The reason for the effect of platform protection on the platform is similar with the content providers due to the revenue-sharing scheme with content providers. Although the platform and content providers have an identical preference structure over the platform protection, there are some regions in which they show distinguished preferences for protection. In particular, when $e _ { 1 } < e < a , $ the platform prefers less protection while content providers prefer more. This is because that implementing higher protection demands a higher cost from the platform. When the cost dominates the gain from the revenue share, the platform prefers a lower protection.

Proposition 1(e) uncovers an interesting insight for the effect of platform protection on the customer surplus. In particular, the customer surplus first decreases and then increases in the platform protection. It is determined by the demand expansion effect of platform protection (i.e., the positive effect). When the demand expansion effect is relatively low, users are hurt by the enhanced protection. However, when the demand expansion effect is relatively high, customer surplus will increase by more users who can access the contents. Combining the results of Parts b and c of Proposition 1, we can conjecture that the positive effect of platform protection reaches a threshold, content providers, platform, and users will lead to a “win-winwin” situation.

Under the content agency model, the optimal platform protection is given as:

$$

$$
k ^ {A ^ {*}} = \max \left\{\min \left\{\frac {(u _ {1} + u _ {2} - 2 \lambda u _ {2} + h _ {1} + h _ {2}) s (a - e) (1 - r)}{2 (c + g - (a - e) ^ {2} (1 - r) s)}, 1 \right\}, 0 \right\}.
$$

Proposition 2 establishes the central result of this paper: the platform may not choose to implement the maximal protection irrespective of the business mode. More interestingly, the minimal protection may be adopted by the platform. In other words, the platform may not provide protection, although it is not in line with business ethics.

The total effect of platform protection can be decomposed into two parts. The first part represents the effect on the platform’s profit through influencing the nonpure platform users’ purchase behavior. This effect is indirect and positive since higher protection will result in fewer contents being “leak out of the platform,” which in turn enhance the potential demand of the legal contents. Furthermore, platform protection can also influence the platform’s profit through influencing on the number of pure platform users. This effect is direct and negative since an effective DRM-based technological deterrence often imposes paying consumers some unreasonable restrictions from which legal users derive some disutility. That is, holding the potential demand from the non-pure platform users constant, the platform can benefit from adopting a less stringent protection policy. This is because a lenient protection would lead to a higher utility for users, which in turn attracts more users, or sets a higher price (more advertisements), or both, and hence generates more profit.

The equilibrium relationship between the optimal level of protection and the positive effect on non-pure platform users under the two agency models is displayed in Figures 3 and 4, respectively. When the positive effect of protection (i.e., on the demand of non-pure ethical users) is too low $( \mathrm { i } . { \mathsf { e } } . , \ a < e )$ , the minimal protection is optimal. We refer it to as the minimal protection region. In this circumstance, intuitively, the negative effect (i.e., on the utilities of pure platform users) dominates the positive effect, which induces the platform to adopt the minimal protection to capture more profit from the pure platform users. However, when the positive effect is relatively large $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , a > e _ { 5 }$ under the content agency model or $a > e _ { 6 }$ under the advertisement agency model), the maximal protection is optimal. We refer it to as the maximal protection region. In this circumstance, intuitively, the negative effect is fully dominated by the positive effect, which induces the platform to adopt the maximal protection to capture more profit from the non-pure platform users. Finally, when the positive effect of protection is medium $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } e < a < e _ { 5 }$ under the content agency model or $e < a < e _ { 6 }$ under the advertising agency model), the optimal protection is intermediate. We refer it to as the medium protection region. In this circumstance, neither the positive effect nor the negative effect has a sufficient advantage to dominate the other. As a result, the platform will adopt a relative medium protection and is not partial to either pure or non-pure platform users.

## Comparative Statics

In this subsection, we explore the effects of some exogenous parameters on the equilibrium outcomes. More specifically, we first analyze the effects of different impacts of platform protection on equilibrium prices, numbers of advertisements, profits, demands of contents, and the optimal level of protection. Then, we turn to address the effects of substitutability of contents on the aforementioned equilibrium outcomes.

Platform protection not only positively influences the non-pure platform user demand, but also reduces pure platform users’ utilities. What is the effect of the different impacts (i.e., a and e) on the optimal content prices under the content agency model and the optimal numbers of ads under the advertisement agency model? We summarize the result in Proposition 3.

Proposition 3 (Effects of Different Impacts of Platform Protection on Prices and Numbers of Advertisements):

a. Under the content agency model, the equilibrium prices increase in the positive effect of platform protection and decreases in the negative effect of platform protection, i.e., $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial { p _ { i } } ^ { * } } { \partial a } > 0 , \frac { \partial { p _ { i } } ^ { * } } { \partial e } < 0 ( i = ( 1 , 2 ) ) } \end{array}$

b. Under the advertisement agency model, the optimal numbers of ads increase in the positive effect of platform protection and decreases in the negative effect of platform protection, i.e.,, $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial { A _ { i } } ^ { * } } { \partial a } > 0 , \frac { \partial { A _ { i } } ^ { * } } { \partial e } < 0 ( i = ( 1 , 2 ) ) } \end{array}$

Proposition 3 shows that as the positive effect of platform protection increases or the negative effect of platform protection decreases, the content prices increase. The result coincided with that of Guo and Meng [20]. The intuition is as follows: as a increases, content providers charge a higher price due to higher demand. As e increases, to compensate for the negative effect on pure platform users’ utilities, content providers have strong incentives to lower prices.

In essence, incurring nuisance cost from viewing advertisements is equivalent to paying for disappearing advertisements. We can use the same logic to interpret the effect of a and e on the numbers of ads under advertisement agency model.

What is the effect of different impacts of platform protection on profits? The result are summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition 4 (Effects of Different Impacts of Platform Protection on Profits):

The platform’ profit and the content providers’ profits always increase in the positive effect and decreases in the negative effect. Formally, $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial \Pi ^ { j ^ { * } } } { \partial a } > 0 , } \end{array}$

$$
\frac {\partial \Pi^ {i ^ {*}}}{\partial e} <   0, \frac {\partial \pi_ {i} ^ {j ^ {*}}}{\partial a} > 0, \frac {\partial \pi_ {i} ^ {j ^ {*}}}{\partial e} <   0 (i = (1, 2), j = (S, A)).
$$

Proposition 4 shows that the platform and content providers have the aligned changes for the positive effect of platform protection and the negative effect of platform protection, which is rooted in the nature of business model in which the platform and content providers share the total revenue based on the contract. As the positive effect of platform protection increases, the platform and the content providers will gain more profits due to the increase of the non-pure platform user demand. In addition, the platform and content providers’ profits decrease in the negative effect of platform protection due to the reduction of content prices under the content agency model and of the optimal numbers of ads under the advertisement agency model.

c. The total demand increases in the positive effect and decreases in the negative effect. Formally, $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial D _ { i } ^ { j ^ { * } } } { \partial a } > 0 , \frac { \partial D _ { i } ^ { j ^ { * } } } { \partial e } < 0 ( i = ( 1 , 2 ) , j = ( S , A ) ) } \end{array}$

Proposition 5 shows the relationships between demand and the different effects of platform protection. Proposition 5(a) shows that the number of pure platform users decreases in the positive effect of platform protection. The intuition is that as the positive effect of platform protection increases, the content price under the content agency model or the number of advertisements under the advertisement agency model increases (see Proposition 3), which reduces the incentives of pure platform users to purchase or use the contents. More interestingly, as the negative effect of platform protection increases, the number of pure platform users increases. The intuition behind this result is that under the content agency model (the advertisement agency model), expecting the negative effect of the platform protection, the content providers (the platform) will reduce the prices of contents (the numbers of advertisements in contents) to complement the loss (see Proposition 3). As a result, more pure platform users are willing to consume contents due to the reduction of the prices of contents (the numbers of advertisements), and more pure platform users join the platform.

Proposition 5(b) states that the amount of non-pure platform users increases in the positive effect of platform protection and decreases in the negative effect. It is intuitive that the non-pure platform user demand increases in the positive effect of platform protection. Intuitively, one may expect that the reduced prices resulting from the negative effect of platform protection may attract more nonpure platform users to adopt the content(s). However, we find that the non-pure platform user demand decreases in the negative effect of platform protection, which seems counterintuitive. The reason is that the optimal protection decreases in the negative effect of platform protection. Although the reduced prices have a positive effect on the non-pure platform user demand, it is always dominated by the negative effect previously described on the non-pure platform user demand.

Proposition 5(c) shows total demand has an aligned trend with the non-pure platform user demand toward the effect of the protection. The effect of platform protection on the total demand can be decomposed into 2 parts. The first part represents the positive effect of the protection on total demand through influencing the non-pure platform user demand. This main effect is positive since a more positive effect of platform protection captures more users. The second part represents the negative effect of the platform protection on total demand through influencing the number of pure platform users and that of non-pure platform users. The remaining analysis is similar to Proposition 5(b), which we omitted here.

Based on the different effects of platform protection, 3 cases of total effect may arise. First, when the positive effect is dominant, the total demand increases. Second, when the negative effect is dominant, the total demand decreases. Third, when the positive effect and negative effect are balanced, protection or piracy has no effect on total demand, which verifies the finding of Smith and Telang [43], which states that piracy does not seem to affect the demand of legitimate content.

In the medium protection region, what is the effect of different impacts of platform protection on the optimal protection level? The result is summarized in Proposition 6.

Proposition 6: In the medium protection region, the optimal platform protection is monotonically increasing in a and monotonically decreasing in e. Formally, $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial k ^ { j * } } { \partial a } > 0 } \end{array}$ and $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial k ^ { j _ { * } } } { \partial e } < 0 \mathrm { ~ } ( j = S , A ) } \end{array}$

Proposition 6 shows that in the medium protection region, as the positive effect of platform protection increases or the negative effect of platform protection decreases, the optimal platform protections increase. The intuition is as follows. As a increases, platform has a strong insensitive to set a higher protection to capture more non-pure platform users. However, as e increases, to compensate for the negative effect on pure platform users’ utilities, platform has a strong incentive to lower protection to capture more pure platform users.

Proposition 7 states that the optimal platform protection, the equilibrium price, the optimal numbers of ads, the platform’ profit, and the content providers’ profits are monotonically decreasing in the substitutability of 2 contents. The intuition is as follows. The greater substitutability of two contents leads to the greater competition between contents. The increased competition results in lower prices of contents or fewer ads, which further reduces the profits of the platform and content providers.

Intuitively, one may expect that the reduced prices resulting from the substitutability effect may ask platform to adopt higher protection to capture more users. However, we find that the platform protection decreases in substitutability effect, which seems counterintuitive. The reason is that the loss from higher protection dominates the gain from increased demand.

Intuitively, one may expect that the higher substitutability effect, the lower the demand. However, we find that this is not necessarily the case. Proposition 8 formally proves that the demand may be larger in a higher substitutability effect.

## Platform Protection in the Presence of Consumers’ Fairness Concerns

Some literature has witnessed the effect of users’ fairness concern. For example, Bolton et al. ## Conclusions

The objective of this paper has been to develop a parsimonious model to reexamine the effect of the platform protection on the monopolistic platform adopting content agency model or advertising agency model, and duopoly content providers with some extent of content substitutability. We characterize conditions under which it is optimal to enforce perfect, imperfect, or even zero restriction on unauthorized copying. We find that the platform’s protection strategy hinges on the relative magnitude of the positive and negative effects of platform protection. We also find the demand from pure platform users and that from non-pure platform users may increase in the level of substitutability of contents when the platform adopts different protections. Finally, we find that there exists the consistence between platform protection and profit: under certain conditions, adopting an appropriate business model and higher protection may obtain more revenue.

Our research has several implications. First, we underscore that the platform should adopt different levels of protection under different conditions. When the negative effect of platform protection on the pure platform users brought by some inconvenient restrictions on legal usage for content is larger than the positive effect of protection on demand from non-pure platform users, the platform should enforce zero level protection. When the positive effect on the potential users is sufficiently large, the platform should enforce perfect protection. When the positive effect is greater than the negative effect, it is optimal to enforce only imperfect restrictions on unauthorized copying for the platform. Correspondingly, when designing copyright management technologies, the platform should focus more on how effective its solution will be in preserving the value provided to legal users and how effective it will be in reducing contents “leak out of the platform.”

Second, we provide some suggestions for the choices of business model. There are some factors the platform must consider when choosing the business model. The advantage of the content agency model includes the right for setting the proportion of profit division and no advertisements. The advantage of the advertisement agency model includes the right for determining the account of advertisements in contents and the negative effect of the rule for paying or leaving it. When the former is large, the platform should adopt the content agency model. Additionally, we find that high profits may accompany higher protection. When a platform adopts a higher protection, it may generate more profit if it abandons the original business model and switches to an alternative one.

Third, we also highlight the effect of the substitutability of contents. We find it detrimental to have products with similar content in the platform, although substitutability of the contents reduced the level of protection and reduced platform costs. The price of the content or the number of advertisements in the content decreases with the level of substitutability, the variation is too limited to alter the reality that consumers obtain less the utility because of the substitutability of the contents; thus, total demand of the platform content decreases. Therefore, the platform and content providers’ profits decrease in the substitutability. This provides the platform manager with good revelation. The platform manager should segment the platform market and critically review content substitutions to reduce content substitution.

Content providers may influence the platform protection strategy and the choice of business model. If the platform chooses the content agency model, the content provider will determine the price of the content, which will affect the number of platform users and the strength of platform protection strategies. If the content providers set a relatively high price, he will induce the platform to choose the advertisement agency model. Therefore, a content provider should set a reasonable and relatively low price if he wants to maintain the price his content. In addition, content providers should also reduce the substitution of contents and provide good product function segmentation.

For the policy maker, platform protection does not necessarily hurt consumer surplus, and extremely stringent protection may be better off consumer surplus. Imperfect protection can be harmful to consumers. Policy makers can increase the legal concept of non-pure platform users or increase their time cost searching for pirated content. They should also encourage the platform to provide a stringent protection or do not provide protection.

This paper suggests a few directions for future research. First, we only considered the protection behavior of the monopoly platform and do not study the platform protection under the competition platforms. Second, we do not consider that the platform may adopt a hybrid model in which both the content agency model and the advertising agency model are adopted. It would also of interest to study the platform protection when a platform adopts a hybrid model. Finally, platform protection might improve the platform security and consumer privacy protection. A more granular study could investigate the positive effect of platform protection on legal users.<sup>7</sup>
