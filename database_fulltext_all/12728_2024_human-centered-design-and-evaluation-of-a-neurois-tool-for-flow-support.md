# Human-Centered Design and Evaluation of a NeuroIS Tool for Flow Support

Marc T. P. Adam,<sup>1</sup> Lukas Bonenberger,<sup>2</sup> Henner Gimpel,<sup>3</sup> Julia Lanzl<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Information and Physical Sciences, University of Newcastle, Australia, marc.adam@newcastle.edu.au <sup>2</sup>Technical University of Applied Sciences Augsburg, FIM Research Center / Branch Business & Information Systems Engineering of Fraunhofer FIT / University of Hohenheim, Chair of Digital Management, Germany, lukas.bonenberger@fim-rc.de <sup>3</sup>University of Hohenheim, Chair of Digital Management / Branch Business & Information Systems Engineering of Fraunhofer FIT/ FIM Research Center, Germany, henner.gimpel@fim-rc.de <sup>4</sup>University of Hohenheim, Chair of Digital Management / Branch Business & Information Systems Engineering of Fraunhofer FIT / FIM Research Center, Germany, julia.lanzl@fim-rc.de

## Abstract

Flow is a mental state in which a person is fully immersed and actively involved in a task, even during extrinsically motivated activities at work. IT-mediated interruptions can disrupt flow, with ramifications for workers’ well-being and work performance. In this design science research paper, we develop and evaluate design knowledge for neuroadaptive flow support systems. Building on foundations in flow theory, we conduct expert interviews and present a conceptual framework, three meta-requirements, and five design principles for flow support systems. We then implement the design principles in three prototypes and evaluate these prototypes in a lab experiment and a field study. With this paper on flow and IT-mediated interruptions in the work domain, we present an approach toward flow support systems that enable intelligent interruption management.

Keywords: Design Science Research, Flow, IT-Mediated Interruptions, NeuroIS

## 1 Introduction

Over the past few decades, work environments have fundamentally changed. This is evident, for example, in the ubiquitous use of information technology (IT). Besides the numerous benefits IT creates for organizations and individuals (e.g., cost reduction, virtual collaboration), IT’s ubiquity also entails risks for knowledge workers (Tarafdar et al., 2013)—that is, workers who think for a living and whose tasks involve manipulating knowledge and information (Davenport, 2005). Among the risks threatening knowledge workers are IT-mediated interruptions that can affect performance by disrupting work routines and concentration.

A critical psychological concept in this regard is flow. Flow describes a positive mental state in which a person is totally involved and entirely concentrated on a task at hand (Csikszentmihalyi, 1975). Research has shown that flow creates important positive outcomes in the work domain in terms of subjective well-being (Csikszentmihalyi, 2004; Ilies et al., 2017) and work performance (Demerouti, 2006; Eisenberger et al., 2005). Further, because many aspects of today’s work environments are IT-mediated, researchers increasingly investigate how IT systems design affects the emergence and/or disruption of flow in the workplace (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000).

Concentration-breaking interruptions are a pervasive threat to flow at work (Rissler et al., 2017b), with negative effects on knowledge workers’ well-being and productivity (Hemp, 2009). Knowledge workers check their emails at least four times per hour on average (Hair et al., 2007), and several minutes of recovery are required before one can return to the original task (Jackson et al., 2003). Somewhat ironically, ITmediated interruptions threaten the boost in productivity and work performance promised by IT adoption at work (Chen & Karahanna, 2014). The economic consequences of interruptions are tangible, with estimated performance losses for the US economy alone totaling US\$500 million annually (Spira & Feintuch, 2005). In addition, the increased stress experienced by workers is associated with a range of health implications for individuals (Galluch et al., 2015).

Existing research provides important insights into how IT-mediated interruptions impair knowledge workers’ flow, well-being, and work performance. Research has already proposed flow-aware information systems (IS) that support users in interruption handling, intelligently scheduling interruptions (Chen & Karahanna, 2014), and analyzing biosignals for interruption scheduling (Rissler et al., 2017a). However, given the lack of knowledge on how to design such systems for flow support, coupled with the fact that regular work breaks benefit individuals’ health and well-being (Fritz et al., 2011), the aim is not to extend flow to unhealthy periods but to support individuals in increasing the number and duration of flow states.

Design objective: We follow a design science research (DSR) approach to develop and evaluate design knowledge for neuroadaptive flow support systems (FSS). FSS guide knowledge workers in adapting their work behavior and environment so that they will experience flow more often and for longer periods by providing retrospective feedback based on biosignals and interruption data.<sup>1</sup> It is important to emphasize that this feedback does not directly affect knowledge workers’ ability to experience flow in future tasks. The feedback supports knowledge workers in establishing individual conditions that make future flow states more likely (e.g., a work environment that fosters focus or a set and order of tasks that fit users’ challenge-skill balance). Hence, the focus of this research is on leveraging synergies between human behavior and IT systems design to enhance human performance (Gestalt mode of DSR; Adam et al., 2021).

FSS are neuroadaptive systems (vom Brocke et al., 2020) as they combine technology, people, and processes in a way that facilitates adaptation based on biosignals. Neuroadaptation in FSS is twofold. First, an FSS provides adaptive feedback to the user. Second, through this adaptive feedback, the FSS supports the user in self-adapting their environment to establish individual conditions for more frequent and longer flow experiences. The physiological information an FSS requires to facilitate this support is extracted from biosignals (e.g., brain or heart activity). In this DSR paper, we focus on how to design FSS and evaluate their impact on knowledge workers’ flow experiences in multiple studies. Based on these outcomes, we draw on existing research on flow’s positive effects on subjective well-being (e.g., Csikszentmihalyi, 2004; Ilies et al., 2017) and work performance (e.g., Demerouti, 2006; Eisenberger et al., 2005). Another positive effect of flow support is mitigating IT-mediated interruptions and their negative consequences.

To achieve the design objective and create prescriptive FSS knowledge, we followed an iterative, evaluationfocused DSR process (Sonnenberg & vom Brocke, 2012). Following Gregor (2009), our research merges two modes of theorizing in DSR. We linked the exterior mode of artifact construction by exploring existing system classes from the interior mode through constituting meta-requirements and design principles for FSS. We integrated a body of literature from various disciplines related to flow support, complementing it with qualitative expert interviews. Building on the results, we developed a conceptual framework, three meta-requirements, and five design principles for FSS. We instantiated this design knowledge by developing three prototypes at different stages of maturity and evaluating them in two steps. First, we conducted a lab experiment with 22 participants and a prototype for flow detection to evaluate the prototype’s capabilities of measuring flow from biosignals. Second, we conducted a field study with 15 knowledge workers and two FSS prototypes to evaluate their acceptance, applicability, feasibility, and impact on users’ work behavior in a real-world setting.

Regarding the theoretical value of our research, we complement NeuroIS and flow research by demonstrating that flow detection using biosignals is feasible and applicable in a practical knowledge work field setting. Further, this study contributes to ITmediated interruptions and feedback research by providing a mechanism for knowledge workers’ interruption management. In terms of the practical value of our research, we provide employers, workers’ councils, and worker unions with design knowledge for a tool that can benefit their employees. We offer a blueprint for IT system designers to bring such systems to life.

## 2 Related Work and Conceptual Foundations

## 2.1 Related Work on Neuroadaptive Information Systems

FSS are neuroadaptive IS as defined by the research stream of NeuroIS (Riedl et al., 2014). NeuroIS is “a subfield in the IS field that uses neuroscience and neurophysiological methods, tools, and theories to better understand the design, development, and use of information and communication technologies (ICT) in organizations and society” (Riedl et al., 2014, p. ii). Neuroadaptive IS can sense and react to users’ physiological states, constituting the fourth key area of the NeuroIS research agenda (vom Brocke et al., 2020). To achieve our design objective, our study builds on two interrelated research streams on neuroadaptive IS: biofeedback and mobile stress assessment systems.

Live biofeedback systems give users real-time feedback about their physiological state (Lux et al., 2015). Examples of application domains include work (Perera et al., 2016) and sports (Rijken et al., 2016). Providing users with biosignal-based information can support users in regulating their physiology, with positive implications for behavioral outcomes such as productivity (Riedl & Léger, 2016). Given our design objective of increasing users’ well-being and work performance, we build on the concept of biofeedback systems to inform our FSS design knowledge. Conceptually, biofeedback systems aim to close the body-mind loop by providing an additional stream of information into users’ minds, facilitating changes in users’ physiological states (Al Osman et al., 2013; Riedl & Léger, 2016). In doing so, biofeedback systems comprise users, biosensors, data processing, and a feedback component (Al Osman et al., 2013; Al Osman et al., 2016). Our DSR study relies on an integrative framework for live biofeedback (Lux et al., 2018), building on the seminal transmission model of communication (Shannon & Weaver, 1949).

Mobile stress assessment systems use mobile sensors on the user and in the user’s environment to assess stress caused by work overload, personal conflicts, or health conditions (Gimpel et al., 2019a). Examples include stress assessment for smartphone users (Gimpel et al., 2019b) and long-haul passengers (vom Brocke et al., 2020). Notably, there are substantial overlaps between mobile stress assessment systems and FSS. For example, heart rate variability (HRV) has been used to assess strain in the stress assessment domain (Trimmel et al., 2003) and also for flow (Knierim et al., 2018). Further, there is an inherent link to IT-mediated interruptions. Frequently interrupting users impairs flow (Knierim et al., 2018) and causes stress (Galluch et al., 2015). Using mobile sensors for stress assessment facilitates location-independent continuous monitoring (Gimpel et al., 2019b). Therefore, our research also builds on portable, noninvasive devices for flow detection. Gimpel et al. (2019a) established a general architecture for mobile stress assessment systems. Our paper builds on this architecture to conceptualize FSS components (e.g., the user, their environment, or data collection and transformation components).

## 2.2 Flow Theory

The central theoretical construct underpinning our FSS design knowledge is flow. The flow phenomenon (or flow state) describes the holistic sensation during immersed and concentrated work on a task (Csikszentmihalyi, 1975). Early research investigated flow in the context of the intrinsically motivated behavior of artists and athletes (Csikszentmihalyi, 1975). There are different dimensions of flow, such as concentration, an action-awareness merge, a loss of self-reflection, and a sense of control (Csikszentmihalyi, 1991). When investigating flow, one must carefully consider task complexity and the skills of the person performing a task. If a task does not challenge a person, boredom may occur, and if the person’s skills do not meet the requirements for a more challenging task, they may feel anxious (Csikszentmihalyi, 1991). As a critical outcome, flow leads to task enjoyment, called autotelic experience (Csikszentmihalyi, 1975; Jackson & Marsh, 1996).

This task enjoyment, which can also arise when engaging with IT applications, renders the flow concept relevant to the IS field. Early IS research indicated that flow can emerge in extrinsically motivated activities such as working with spreadsheets and email (Webster et al., 1993). Building on this notion, research investigated the influence of user interface (UI) design on flow in e-commerce (Guo & Poole, 2009; Jiang & Benbasat, 2004) and flow’s role in winning customers’ interest through brand appearances in virtual worlds (Nah et al., 2011). Recent IS research suggests that task-relevant feedback facilitates flow (Schaffer & Feng, 2022), a finding we leverage for our FSS design knowledge.

A construct related to flow is cognitive absorption. It describes an individual’s holistic experience, specifically when interacting with or using IT (Chandra et al., 2012). Cognitive absorption emerged from IS research and comprises temporal dissociation, focused immersion, heightened enjoyment, control, and curiosity (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). In addition to flow theory, cognitive absorption builds on the theory of cognitive engagement (Webster & Ho, 1997) and the psychological trait of absorption (Tellegen & Atkinson, 1974). Cognitive absorption is an extension of Csikszentmihalyi’s (1991) flow concept in that it encapsulates flow and curiosity (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). At the same time, cognitive absorption is more limited than flow, as cognitive absorption describes “a state of deep involvement with software” (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000, p. 672), whereas flow is not constrained to software. Cognitive absorption research suggests that flow (an important subcomponent of cognitive absorption) likely plays a positive role in technology acceptance. The hedonic-motivation system adoption model (HMSAM) indicates that cognitive absorption is a powerful predictor of behavioral intention to use a system, given users’ intrinsic motivation (Lowry et al., 2013).

In the past, flow has mainly been assessed through interviews (Jackson, 1996) or self-reporting questionnaires such as the Flow State Scale (Jackson & Marsh, 1996) or the Flow Short Scale (FKS) (Rheinberg et al., 2003). While these approaches have provided important insights into flow, they have limitations. For example, flow self-assessment can only retrospectively detect flow experiences and only for specific points in time (Wang & Hsu, 2014). Further, self-assessment risks disrupting flow (Tozman et al., 2015). Therefore, researchers have explored using biosignals to analyze changes in central (CNS) and peripheral nervous system (PNS) activity related to flow (Knierim et al., 2018). Regarding PNS activity, researchers have measured HRV to analyze flow in piano players (Manzano et al., 2010) and knowledge workers (Rissler et al., 2018) and have measured electrodermal activity to detect flow in playing computer games (Nacke & Lindley, 2008). Regarding CNS activity, studies have relied on functional nearinfrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) and electroencephalography (EEG). For example, EEG has been used to study correlations with self-reported flow (Katahira et al., 2018) or flow’s link to learning performance (Wang & Hsu, 2014).

## 2.3 IT-Mediated Interruptions

In addition to flow theory, our research draws on the growing body of research on the effects of IT-mediated interruptions, that is, “perceived, IT-based external events with a range of content that captures cognitive attention and breaks the continuity of an individual’s primary task activities” (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015, p. 233). This definition includes interruptions from the system itself (e.g., a battery status alert) and interruptions from other individuals via, for example, emails, instant messaging, or social networks (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015). With IT’s ubiquity in work environments, knowledge workers experience many IT-mediated interruptions (Addas & Pinsonneault,

2015). IT-mediated interruptions can disrupt knowledge workers’ flow because they are distractions detected by the same sensory channel that processes the interrupted task (Speier et al., 2003).

IT-mediated interruptions disrupt users’ task execution, which can negatively but also positively affect their well-being and work performance— depending on the situation and the interruption’s content (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015). On the negative side, interruptions can cause stress (Galluch et al., 2015), decreased work quality, and increased mental workload (Gupta et al., 2013). In safety-critical multitasking domains (e.g., dispatching emergency calls or aircraft operating), interruptions can even lead to catastrophic errors (McFarlane & Latorella, 2002). On the positive side, relevant interruptions, critical warnings, and alerts are essential communication modes to inform users of impending danger or difficulty (Walji et al., 2004). Further, some interruptions may benefit the individual by conveying information relevant to task execution (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015, 2018).

To assess the impact of an interruption, its content and the individual’s situation are critical. Interruptions can either be irrelevant (e.g., an unrelated text message) or beneficial to the primary task (e.g., an email with upto-date information while writing a sales report) (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015). Another aspect of ITmediated interruptions relates to their cause and manifestation (Rissler et al., 2017b): an interruption’s cause comprises an initiator, meaning, and content, whereas the interruption’s manifestation pertains to its presentation, timing, and transmission channel. Hence, one cannot simply eliminate interruptions to fight their detrimental impact on users’ well-being and work performance. What is needed is a comprehensive approach that avoids negative distractions for users but also considers environmental requirements (e.g., availability at the workplace).

## 3 Design Science Research Approach

To create FSS design knowledge, we follow the DSR paradigm and adapt the iterative design evaluation approach by Sonnenberg and vom Brocke (2012). This DSR approach applies continuous evaluation activities to assess the adequacy of developed design artifacts, thus decreasing the risk of creating irrelevant artifacts (Sturm & Sunyaev, 2019).

The design evaluation approach consists of four activities: identify problem, design, construct, and use (see Figure 1), followed by a corresponding evaluation (Eval 1-4; Sonnenberg & vom Brocke, 2012). Eval 1 ensures the selection of a relevant DSR problem. Eval 2 confirms that the design principles result in a solution to the stated problem. Eval 3 provides a primary demonstration of how well the implementation of the design principles interacts with organizational elements. Eval 4 validates the implementation’s usefulness and applicability in practice. Our evaluation follows the technical risk & efficacy evaluation strategy (cf. DSR evaluation framework; Venable et al., 2016), comprising early formative evaluation activities (Eval 1, Eval 2), mid-research summative evaluations (Eval 3), and naturalistic evaluation by the end of the research process (Eval 4). To do so, we drew inspiration from existing DSR studies, such as those on systematic search systems (Sturm & Sunyaev, 2019) and process guidance systems (Morana et al., 2019).

Identify problem: At the outset of our research, we identified the problem of IT-mediated interruptions interfering with flow states at the workplace, which can reduce knowledge workers’ well-being and work performance. We explored extant literature on flow and IT-mediated interruptions to define a problem statement, research gap, and design objective (see Introduction). We then conducted four semi-structured expert interviews with specialists from research, practice-oriented research, and practice to justify our research endeavor (Eval 1). The interviewees provided unanimous support for the identified research foundation.

Design: Building on the validated design objective, we developed a conceptual framework for FSS and established three meta-requirements. Additionally, we derived five design principles that guide the construction of FSS and aim at addressing the metarequirements. We validated the design knowledge using two evaluation methods (Eval 2). First, we conducted a literature review on neuroadaptive IS research related to FSS in four scientific databases (Web of Science, AIS eLibrary, IEEE Xplore, and ACM Digital Library). In the results of this literature review, we discovered design structures such as a live biofeedback framework (Lux et al., 2018) and a general architecture for mobile stress assessment systems (Gimpel et al., 2019a) to confirm and complement the first draft of our design knowledge (i.e., deductive reasoning). Second, we conducted expert interviews to validate the clarity, mutual exclusiveness, collective exhaustiveness, and level of detail of the meta-requirements and design principles. Using the results of the literature analysis and interviewees’ feedback, we revised and complemented the conceptual framework, the meta-requirements, and the design principles for FSS (i.e., inductive reasoning).

Construct: A fundamental capability of FSS is recording biosignals to detect flow. Thus, we developed a prototype to capture biosignals and detect flow (Prototype A). In the construct activity, a developed artifact is under evaluation regarding its applicability in an artificial context (Sonnenberg & vom Brocke, 2012). Accordingly, we tested Prototype A’s biosignal measurement and flow detection capabilities in a lab experiment (Eval 3) with 22 participants. Prototype A recorded HRV and EEG. The experiment involved a within-subject design with four repeated measures in different conditions to analyze the link between perceived flow and physiological activity (see Section 5.1).

Use: To evaluate an FSS in a real-world work setting, we extended Prototype A to include a UI and the capabilities to recognize IT-mediated interruptions and provide flow feedback to the user. This extension resulted in two FSS prototypes that detect flow from HRV (Prototype B) and EEG (Prototype C). In the use activity, developed artifacts are under evaluation in an organizational context with real-world tasks, systems, and users (Sonnenberg & vom Brocke, 2012). Thus, we tested the prototypes in a two-stage field study with 15 knowledge workers (Eval 4). The field study helped us to identify aspects of our FSS design knowledge that require revision to achieve the design objective fully. In addition to the field study, we conducted a focus group with eight participants to evaluate the prototypes regarding their (neuro)adaptive properties (see Section 5.2).

## 4 Designing Flow Support Systems

## 4.1 Conceptual Framework

To conceptualize the interplay of neuroadaptive IS, flow theory, and IT-mediated interruptions, we drew from literature to develop a conceptual framework for FSS (see Figure 2). The framework constitutes the positivist, deductive component of our research. It comprises a social component (i.e., the FSS user and relevant stakeholders), a technological component (i.e., the FSS software embedded on a user’s mobile device), and interactions between these two components. Thus, the framework provides a suitable scope for approaching a problem in IS research (Lee, 2001).

Because flow is a psychophysiological phenomenon (Knierim et al., 2018), our conceptual framework considers both (1) the user’s mind, reflecting their perception and behavioral adaptation processes, and (2) the user’s body, reflecting flow-related physiological processes. As part of the technological FSS component, the data collection module captures users’ biosignals and interruption data from the mobile device. Further, the UI analyzes detected flow states (flow analytics), reports these to the user, and generates insights on improvement areas that support users in adapting their work behavior and environment (flow support insights). Through these adaptations, users establish conditions in their individual environment that make it more likely for them to experience flow more often and for longer periods. Users provide feedback on their flow perception to the UI component. Other relevant stakeholders in the framework include, for example, worker’s councils, data security officers, clients, and users’ co-workers. Their interests and needs shape the FSS requirements.

The retrospective biofeedback in the FSS builds on Lux et al.’s (2018) live biofeedback framework and the underlying transmission model of communication (Shannon & Weaver, 1949). The transmission model includes five parts for communicating a message or signal: an information source, a transmitter, a transmission channel, a receiver, and a destination (Shannon & Weaver, 1949). Contextualized to FSS, users’ physiology serves as the information source, whereas the data collection module acts as a transmitter that relays flow and interruption data to the UI (i.e., the receiver). The transmission channel visualizes the information for display through a UI. The UI signals the data to the user’s mind (the destination), which can translate it into behavioral adaptation. This adaptation allows the user to establish conditions in their individual environment (e.g., by changing their devices’ notification settings or rearranging their tasks) conducive to experience flow more often and for longer periods. Users’ physiology, in turn, reflects these flow experiences and, thus, closes the biofeedback loop. The biofeedback must be retrospective because users experience a loss of selfreflection during flow. Hence, real-time feedback would disrupt the user’s concentration on the task, which is crucial for flow. However, as soon as flow is over (e.g., when users have been interrupted by their mobile device), users are receptive to the feedback. They can then choose whether to act on it to set up better individual flow conditions.

If flow occurs in working on the “right tasks” (in the sense of being appropriately prioritized as the best thing the employee should work on at that time), it is to the benefit of the employee and the organization, as both benefit from the employee’s well-being and productivity. If flow occurs in working on tasks that the employee should not work on at that time, not at all, or not in that intensity, then there might still be positive well-being effects but a lack of effectiveness. An FSS cannot determine the priorities of tasks for a person in a specific situation. This determination is up to the user, likely involving discussions with their supervisor, and constitutes an essential reason for FSS not to interfere directly with users’ work environments. Instead, the FSS takes effect via the users’ minds (i.e., through adapting their work behavior and environment, thus, establishing individual conditions conducive to more frequent and longer flow experiences).

FSS are NeuroIS tools because they use biosensors as built-in functions (vom Brocke et al., 2013). Specifically, FSS track flow-related measures of CNS and PNS activity. In our prototyping, we used HRV as a common NeuroIS measure of PNS activity (Dimoka et al., 2012), which prior research has linked to flow (Manzano et al., 2010; Rissler et al., 2018). Further, we used EEG as a common measure of CNS activity (Dimoka et al., 2012) linked to flow in prior research (Katahira et al., 2018; Wang & Hsu, 2014). Any physiological measure is only an imprecise measure of a mental state, like flow. As such, the best approximation of the flow experience can likely be achieved by triangulating among multiple different measurements, such as combining HRV and EEG data. For example, several studies showed that fusing data from multiple biosignals delivers better assessments of cognitive load than individual sensors (Ahmad et al., 2020; Ikehara & Crosby, 2005).

In the present DSR study, our prototypes measure HRV and EEG and consider them separately as flow correlates. The reasons for including these measures in our prototypes are the practicality of usage with consumer-grade devices at work, limited data availability for calibrating sensor fusion models (thus not combining HRV and EEG data), and treating the measurements separately in line with our purposes. Our prototyping aims to evaluate design knowledge rather than to develop optimal measurement and data analytics functionality.

The use context of FSS is knowledge work. Due to the sensitive data that FSS process and potentially resulting privacy concerns, usage should be voluntary, not mandatory. Benefits at the individual level aim at increased enjoyment of work tasks (Csikszentmihalyi, 1975; Jackson & Marsh, 1996) and a sense of control (Csikszentmihalyi, 1991), which caters to the basic psychological need for autonomy (Ryan & Deci, 2017). Further, flow includes concentration and the merging of action and awareness (Csikszentmihalyi, 1991), which could lead to higher work productivity and quality. The workers’ organization might directly benefit from improved work outcomes and indirectly from higher employee satisfaction, which could drive customer satisfaction and productivity (Eskildsen & Dahlgaard, 2000).

## 4.2 Meta-Requirements

After building a conceptual framework for FSS from previous research, we collected meta-requirements (and, subsequently, design principles) that guide the FSS design. During our research process, we identified three meta-requirements for FSS (see Table 1).

Table 1. Meta-Requirements for Flow Support Systems

<table><tr><td>MR1—holistic assessment</td><td>To ensure that FSS are broadly applicable and adaptive, flow support needs to be holistic regarding selecting biosignals and interruption sources that impair flow.</td></tr><tr><td>MR2—stakeholder acceptance</td><td>An FSS requires acceptance by all stakeholders. To achieve this acceptance, an FSS must meet high privacy standards due to data sensitivity, integrate into users’ work environments, and not distract them from their work.</td></tr><tr><td>MR3—reliability and validity</td><td>Flow support needs to be reliable and valid. Physiological measurements, flow detection, and flow analytics must provide the highest possible reliability and validity, considering fluctuations of biosignals and overall data quality.</td></tr></table>

MR 1—holistic assessment: Flow support must be holistic regarding the selection of biosignals and interruption sources. By considering a range of different biosignals, FSS can flexibly switch between different approaches for flow detection, depending on availability or other constraints, and adapt to different user demands and work conditions. The holistic assessment also applies to the sources of IT-mediated interruptions at work, which are increasingly multifaceted (e.g., email, messenger, phone, and application programs). Supporting this notion, one expert positively evaluated the idea of considering various sources. Holistically considering different biosignals and interruption sources also allows for flexibility regarding general budget constraints for introducing a specific FSS. Depending on the use case, selecting only specific biosignals and interruption sources may be economically reasonable while still supporting users in experiencing flow more often and for longer periods.

MR2—stakeholder acceptance: FSS require acceptance by all stakeholders. IS research has a rich body of knowledge on technology acceptance, such as the technology acceptance model (Davis, 1989) and the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (Venkatesh et al., 2012). The typical antecedents of technology acceptance, such as perceived ease of use, effort expectancy, and social influence, apply to FSS. The HMSAM builds a theoretical bridge for connecting these constructs with FSS. It suggests that subcomponents of cognitive absorption (i.e., curiosity and joy), which are highly related to flow, mediate the relationship between perceived ease of use and the user’s intention to use a system (Lowry et al., 2013). The HMSAM applies to hedonic-motivation systems. However, since intrinsic motivation is a flow prerequisite, it follows that its propositions also apply to FSS.

Eval 1 interviews indicated two critical antecedents of FSS acceptance: data privacy and ease of use. Interviewees emphasized that data privacy relates to legal obligations and is scrutinized by data security officers and work councils (especially in countries with strong worker representation, such as Germany and Australia). Further, given the sensitivity of biosensor data, users might fear misuse by the employer. Prior research suggests that privacy concerns influence individuals’ attitudes, such as their willingness to be profiled (Slyke et al., 2006) and their technology acceptance (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011). Users’ privacy concerns increase risk perceptions and reduce their technology adoption intentions (Lancelot Miltgen et al., 2013). Privacy is an issue, especially with sensitive data on a user’s body and mind (Lancelot Miltgen et al., 2013). As for ease of use, experts stressed the importance of non-invasive data collection and FSS integration into users’ existing work applications. Users must not be distracted by the FSS. Focusing on privacy and ease of use here does not imply that other antecedents of technology acceptance are irrelevant for FSS. Instead, it suggests a specific focus for FSS.

MR3—reliability and validity: Given the importance of the subject matter, each technological FSS component (i.e., flow detection, flow analytics, and flow support insights) must provide reliable and valid outcomes. Flow support must be reliable and valid, especially because of the inherent fluctuations in individuals’ biosignals and the complexity and context dependency of interruptions. Feedback from an expert in Eval 2 indicated a link between the requirement for holistic assessment for FSS (MR1) and their reliability and validity (MR3). As a result, we revised MR3 to emphasize the demand for reliability and validity from each core component of an FSS, not only data analysis in general.

## 4.3 Design Principles

Building on the conceptual framework and the metarequirements, we developed five FSS design principles (see Table 2) in multiple revision cycles based on the Eval 2 expert feedback on each principle's applicability, feasibility, completeness, clarity, and level of detail. Following Gregor et al.’s (2020) anatomy of a design principle, we carved out an aim, a mechanism, and a rationale for each design principle.

Table 2. Design Principles for Flow Support Systems

<table><tr><td>DP1—flow detection</td><td>Use biosignals and data on IT-mediated interruptions to detect flow unobtrusively.</td></tr><tr><td>DP2—personal mobile device</td><td>Constrain all processing of sensitive biosignal and interruption data to a personal mobile device with exclusive user access to protect user privacy and integrate into users’ work environment.</td></tr><tr><td>DP3—flow analytics</td><td>Report flow states to the user to allow them to identify potentials for flow support and take users’ feedback to improve flow detection.</td></tr><tr><td>DP4—flow support insights</td><td>Provide users with impactful insights on improvement areas to allow them to self-adapt their working behavior and environment in order to establish individual conditions to experience flow more often and for longer periods.</td></tr><tr><td>DP5—retrospective biofeedback</td><td>Provide an FSS with a biofeedback loop between the system’s modules and users’ minds and bodies to ensure that users’ adaptations to their work behavior and environment gradually improve their individual conditions conducive for more frequent and longer flow experiences.</td></tr></table>

DP1—flow detection: To use biosignal and interruption data to detect previous and support the emergence and continuance of future flow states, FSS require a module for data collection and flow detection. They may consider one or multiple biosignals, depending on the use case. The data collection module should capture various interruption types to best account for positive impacts (e.g., helpful information for a current task) or negative impacts (e.g., distracting information) on flow caused by interruptions. Implementing this design principle ensures a holistic assessment of users’ flow (MR1) and provides a foundation for reliable and valid flow detection (MR3).

DP2—personal mobile device: To ensure data privacy and provide temporal and spatial flexibility, FSS should run on a mobile device with exclusive access by the user, such as a mobile computer, a tablet, or a smartphone. We provide two rationales for this design principle. First, setting up the system on a personal mobile device addresses privacy concerns (MR2). In this way, the sensitive biosignal and interruption data are stored locally and not accessible by employers, which reduces users’ concerns about the potential misuse of their data. Second, a personal mobile device allows integration with users’ work environment (MR2) and can capture IT-mediated interruptions from these applications. The more information from a user’s work environment is available to the FSS, the better flow detection can become. For example, if the FSS captures notifications and knows the calendar, whereabouts, message senders and content, or how users work in workflow systems, the system can provide richer information about detected flow states. However, establishing all these interfaces is costly and may lead to privacy concerns and decreased acceptance. FSS designers must therefore think carefully about how deep the integration of an FSS into users’ working environment should go.

DP3—flow analytics: To comprehensively inform the user about their flow states, the FSS’s UI needs to include a flow analytics module that reports data on detected previous flow states from the flow detection module (DP1). In this way, users do not need to recall changes in flow themselves. Reporting previously detected flow states to the user is essential to allow them to identify the potential for establishing individual flow conditions themselves and adapt their work behavior and environment accordingly. For example, this may help users observe that they never experience flow when working in a room with others, leading them to make sure they have a private office available for tasks where flow is essential. Even though feedback must be retrospective to avoid disrupting flow, the FSS can provide feedback as soon as the flow state has passed (i.e., the feedback is as close to real-time as possible). The feedback’s temporal closeness to the corresponding situation allows for better feedback interpretation. In addition to reporting previously detected flow states, it is critical that the flow analytics module also considers users’ feedback in order to recognize possible detection errors, thus improving the reliability and validity of flow support (MR3).

DP4—flow support insights: To provide users with impactful insights on improvement areas regarding their flow experiences, FSS need a module to generate flow support insights. This module builds on semiautomation and hybrid intelligence (Dellermann et al., 2019). That is, the FSS will not act self-determined. Instead, its flow support insights module will indicate improvement areas for user actions, such as customizing their notification settings or shifting work tasks across the week to free up time blocks for flow compatible tasks. The feedback channel for signaling improvement areas to the user may be multimodal: frequently recurring improvement areas (e.g., choosing a more flow-compatible task to increase the chance of experiencing flow) could also be conveyed to users swiftly through auditory or tactile signals. Given that it is difficult for users to directly derive conclusions from and act on the information provided by the flow analytics module alone (DP3; history of detected flow states), the flow support insights module aims to directly suggest easy-to-understand improvement areas to the user that they can act on if they deem it valuable. By acting on this advice (e.g., by changing their devices’ notification settings or rearranging their tasks), users adapt their work behavior and environment and establish individual conditions that increase their chance of experiencing flow more often and for longer periods. Thus, the adaptations that the FSS facilitates lie in users’ conscious execution of these recommendations. Not letting the FSS act in selfdetermined ways is vital because users must keep complete control over their availability at all points and not be distracted from their work by the FSS (MR2). A fully automated FSS would likely do more harm than good (e.g., withhold a beneficial interruption in timecritical situations).

DP5—retrospective biofeedback: To ensure that users’ adaptations of their work behavior and environment improve their flow experiences iteratively and effectively, the FSS must provide a biofeedback loop between users’ minds and bodies. The FSS translates multi-modal input data—that is, biosignal, environmental (on IT-mediated interruptions), and perceptional data—into information on previous flow states (DP3) and flow support insights (DP4). The FSS signals this data to the user via a visual or multi-modal (i.e., in combination with an auditory or tactile) feedback channel. The user perceives this information and can then consciously decide if and how to adapt their work behavior and environment with the aim of more frequent and longer flow experiences through the improved individual flow conditions they establish. These flow experiences, in turn, affect users’ physiology, which the FSS captures (i.e., users’ physiology reflects more frequent and more prolonged flow states). The biofeedback must be retrospective because real-time biofeedback is an additional interruption source that could disrupt flow. Also, during flow, users experience a loss of selfreflection and cannot take this real-time feedback into account. Retrospective means that an FSS must not give users feedback during flow states. For example, an FSS could implement this retrospectivity by displaying feedback to users only upon request or when it recognizes that a user definitely is not in flow anymore (i.e., as close to real-time feedback as possible). Integrating retrospective biofeedback into an FSS contributes to reliability and validity (MR3) since the technical system component (i.e., flow detection, flow analytics, and flow support insights) allows users to adapt their environment without disrupting flow.

## 5 Prototyping and Evaluation

## 5.1 Laboratory Evaluation (Eval 3)

To evaluate the presented design knowledge, we developed three prototypes and evaluated them in a lab experiment and a field study. Prototype A (see Figure 3) demonstrates the biosignal measurement capabilities of an FSS. We developed it as part of the construct activity of our design evaluation approach. It is not a full FSS because it only implements DP1 and DP2. Prototype A features neither a UI nor a module for automatic flow detection. The prototype can record HRV with a Polar OH1+ biosensor and brain activity with a MUSE 2 EEG-headband. We chose these devices because of their noninvasiveness and consumer-grade purchase price and use, which we consider crucial for FSS adoption. Prototype A stores the recorded data, processes it, and extracts relevant features for subsequent analysis.

To evaluate the flow detection capabilities of Prototype A (Eval 3), we conducted a lab experiment in which participants experienced flow for some time and no flow at other times. The experimental phases were not intended to mimic actual work routines. Instead, the aim was to relate participants’ flow perception to HRV and EEG measurements. Because knowledge workers may experience work at varying levels of task difficulty, participants completed computer tasks of varying difficulty.

## 5.1.1 Methodology of the Laboratory Evaluation

The lab experiment used a within-subject design with four phases (see Figure 4). We decided on a withinsubject design for two reasons. First, biosignals have substantial individual differences. The within-subject design allowed us to identify the effect of flow on the background of individual differences better than a between-subject design with a limited sample size would have. Second, the individual-level switch from periods without flow to periods with flow is typical in work life. Thus, analyzing intrapersonal differences between these periods is a crucial FSS requirement. Power analysis suggests that 20 participants are required for ANOVA testing (assuming medium effect [f = 0.25], 10% significance level, 80% power). We recruited 22 participants (8 females, 14 males) between 19 and 30 years of age from a German research center. As an incentive, participants had the chance to receive one of five €20 online shopping vouchers. An institutional review board approved the lab experiment. The experiment’s overall duration was approximately 45 minutes and comprised an introduction, five computer phases (white boxes), and accessory activities (gray boxes). We conducted all sessions in a neutral and quiet environment. All participants gave written consent for their participation. We recorded the participants’ HRV and EEG with Prototype A. We synchronized the measurement data obtained from the biosensors used during the lab experiment using builtin time stamps (we synchronized the devices with UTC prior to the experiment).

Before the first experimental phase, the participants engaged in a resting phase as a baseline for the recording. Subsequently, the participants underwent four experimental phases, solving tasks of varying difficulty (easy, medium, hard) on a computer. The tasks aimed to underchallenge the participants, overchallenge the participants, and create a challengeskill balance to induce flow. All experimental phases comprised a sequence of two different alternating subtasks: assigning geometrical shapes and typing words. The tasks are part of a game originally developed for short-term manipulation of HRV (Schaaff & Adam, 2013). Each type of subtask appeared twice before the next subtask popped up. We chose five minutes for each experimental phase according to the gold standard for HRV recording (Task Force of the ESC and the NASPE, 1996). At the end of each phase, the participants filled out a version of the FKS Flow Scale from Rheinberg et al. (2003) with randomized item order to avoid bias.

Phase 1 (easy condition) aimed to induce boredom by not challenging participants enough to create flow. We set an elementary difficulty level with lulling background music. Phase 2 (medium condition without interruption) aimed to create a challenge-skill balance suitable for flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 1991). We raised the difficulty to be modest, set the background music to a neutral theme, and changed the game UI to provide clear task feedback. Phase 3 (hard condition) aimed to make excessive demands by posing a nearly unattainable challenge. We raised the difficulty to be very high and modified the UI to provide overcharging feedback, such as jittery background music and distracting UI elements. Finally, Phase 4 (medium condition with minor interruption) was again designed to induce flow using the same difficulty as in Phase 2. However, halfway through Phase 4, a pop-up error message required participants to briefly deal with a (simulated) database problem. We integrated this interruption to examine its potential impact on flow.

Manipulation check: As expected, a repeatedmeasures one-way ANOVA confirmed that task difficulty significantly influenced flow, as measured by the FKS questionnaire $( F ( 3 , 8 4 ) = 2 4 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ As a post hoc analysis, we conducted Bonferronicorrected pairwise t-tests between the conditions (see Table 3). The analysis showed that flow values differed significantly between phases, except for the medium conditions where a minor IT-mediated interruption (i.e., the error message in Phase 4) did not impair flow. In summary, we conclude that the experiment successfully led to different flow levels by manipulating tasks difficulty.

HRV features: We extracted five HRV features to investigate their correlations with variations in perceived flow: standard deviation of interbeat intervals (SDNN), root-mean square of successive differences (RMSSD), power of the low-frequency band (LF-HRV), power of the high-frequency band (HF-HRV), and ratio of LF-HRV to HF-HRV power (LF/HF). We selected these features because they reflect both sympathetic and parasympathetic PNS activation (Shaffer & Ginsberg, 2017) and thus indicate a wide range of autonomous physiological responses that can be associated with flow. The latter three features (i.e., frequency domain features) have been used in prior flow research (Knierim et al., 2018).

EEG features: To investigate variations in perceived flow related to CNS activity, we extracted the following EEG features: probability spectral density (PSD) of the delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma frequency bands (Müller-Putz et al., 2015) averaged over the four recording electrodes of the EEGheadband. Our EEG analysis approach adapts an existing approach for flow analysis in games (Berta et al., 2013).

## 5.1.2 Results of the Laboratory Evaluation

HRV results: We conducted a panel regression with a random-effects model to confirm that Prototype A can detect flow based on HRV. To detect HRV variations between the phases, we used the easy condition as a baseline. After excluding one participant’s data due to recording errors, the analysis considered 63 observations from 21 participants over three phases (all but the baseline easy condition). Because the HRV features differ in magnitude, we z-standardized the variables between participants. This procedure is typical for HRV analysis in NeuroIS research (e.g., Korosec-Serfaty et al., 2022) and results in aligned feature magnitudes at the within-participant level.

Table 4 presents the regression results, with controls added for task difficulty and participants’ age and sex. While ∆LF-HRV correlated negatively with flow, ∆HF-HRV and ∆LF/HF exhibited positive correlations. As already confirmed by the manipulation check, task difficulty affected flow. Further, while male participants exhibited lower flow levels than female participants, their age had no effect.

The regression analysis confirms a connection between participants’ perceived flow and their HRV. Thus, we consider using HRV features feasible for a flow detection module (DP1). The results align with existing research. For instance, Manzano et al. (2010) reported a positive correlation between LF/HF and flow. Tozman et al. (2015) observed a decrease in HF-HRV with increasing task demand in an experiment involving a driving simulator. In contrast to our study but not conflicting with our objective, Tozman et al. (2015) concurrently observed a negative correlation between LF-HRV and flow.

EEG results: After a preliminary analysis, we had to remove the data of 13 participants due to data quality issues, leaving the data of seven participants for analysis. The quality issues arose from defective recordings of the manufacturer’s software and insufficient skin contact of the headband electrodes due to an overly loose fit (determined by the headband’s design). The quality issues include missing recording data and poor data quality according to a predefined quality metric by the headband manufacturer. Overall, the reliability of the MUSE 2 headband was not sufficient.

## 5.2 Field Evaluation (Eval 4)

To evaluate our FSS design knowledge in a real-world work setting, we developed two more prototypes (Prototypes B and C) as part of the use activity (see Figure 6). The prototypes differed in the employed biosignal. Prototype B focused on heart activity based on the same HRV sensor as in the lab evaluation (Polar OH1+). By contrast, Prototype C focused on brain activity using a higher-quality EEG headband than the one used in the lab experiment (Emotiv Insight 2.0). The prototypes also differed in their capabilities to detect ITmediated interruptions. While Prototype B’s interruption detection was restricted to certain interruption types (i.e., emails, calls, SMSs, instant messages, and social media notifications), Prototype C could detect all push notifications from users’ smartphones as IT-mediated interruptions. Data collection and flow detection worked similarly for both prototypes. The prototypes preprocessed biosignal and interruption data and stored them in the local device and a secure online backup. The prototypes extracted relevant features for flow detection (Prototype B: HRV features, Prototype C: EEG features) and detected flow states using a simple, self-developed heuristic for demonstration purposes. The heuristics for both Prototype B and Prototype C required periodic feedback from the user on their self-perception, captured by the UI via the flow analytics module. This module further signaled periods to the users in which the FSS detected flow states (or not) and associated IT-mediated interruptions. Prototype C additionally included a module for flow support insights. This module communicated improvement areas to the user, building on data from flow detection.

## 5.2.1 Methodology of the Field Evaluation

The field study comprised two stages, thus taking advantage of the design evaluation research approach (Sonnenberg & vom Brocke, 2012). The two-stage approach allowed us to exploit the findings from Prototype B in Stage 1 to develop and use a revised Prototype C for Stage 2. Two institutional review board certificates approved both stages of the field study. To be truthful and nondeceptive toward the participants, we refrained from using a control group, which would have required providing fake feedback on flow through the prototypes. We recruited 15 knowledge workers (nine females, six males, 24 to 35 years old) from the same research center used for the lab experiment. The participants were not compensated. In Stage 1, three doctoral students, one student research assistant, and one IT supervisor participated. In Stage 2, all 10 participants were doctoral students.

Field study—Stage 1: In Stage 1, five participants integrated Prototype B self-reliantly into their everyday work routine for seven consecutive days. Participants could decide how many of the seven days they would use the prototype. They installed the prototype on their smartphones. At the end of each day, the participants answered a brief questionnaire on how the feedback from the flow analytics module matched their perception and how they used the feedback to adapt their work behavior and environment to experience flow more often and for longer periods. At the end of the study, the participants answered a final questionnaire and reported their experiences in a semistructured user interview.

We developed a simple heuristic building on the connection between flow and HRV to detect flow for Prototype B. The heuristic determined whether flow was present subject to LF-HRV and HF-HRV values and user feedback on perceived task difficulty (i.e., low, medium, or high) for each 20-minute recording time window. If the perceived task difficulty was rated low or high, by definition, no flow could be present. If the task difficulty was rated medium, the decision, if flow was present, depended on comparing current and previously recorded HRV values. Using this heuristic, Prototype B displayed a binary flow value (flow present/not present) after each recording session and all captured IT-mediated interruptions for every 20 minutes of recording time.

Because Stage 1 focused on flow detection, Prototype B did not include a module for flow support insights (DP4). Instead, we evaluated this feature building on a conceptual description and a screen mock-up as part of the Stage 1 user interviews. The mock-up showed a list of improvement areas that a module for flow support insights could generate, such as rescheduling work activities or managing interruptions.

Field study—Stage 2: In Stage 2, 10 participants who had not participated in Stage 1 integrated Prototype C self-reliantly into their everyday work routine for two consecutive workdays. Prototype C ran on participants’ mobile work computers and detected ITmediated interruptions from their smartphones. Participants answered four brief questionnaires on how well the results communicated by Prototype C’s flow analytics module matched their perception and how valuable they rated its flow support insights. At the end of the study, the participants reported their experiences in a semi-structured user interview.

Prototype C detected flow states from participants’ EEG recordings using PSD features. Like Prototype B, Prototype C’s flow detection heuristic required user feedback on perceived task difficulty. Compared to Stage 1, this feedback was more fine-grained on a seven-point Likert scale. Consistent with Stage 1, Prototype C ruled out the presence of flow when participants reported perceived task difficulty as low or high. Unlike Stage 1, Prototype C did not estimate flow states based on fixed-length time windows but on sessions of variable length. Prototype C determined if flow was present based on PSD values of each participant’s previous recorded sessions. Using this self-developed heuristic, Prototype C gave participants a result report twice daily. The result report comprised three parts: (1) a binary flow value for each recorded session along with the averaged PSD values and a dynamic threshold signaling when it would have detected a flow state; (2) an overview of all recorded sessions showing the associated binary flow result and all detected IT-mediated interruptions for each session; (3) one improvement area from the module for flow support insights that aimed to induce an adaptation in users’ working behavior and environment and thus establish individual conditions that lead to more frequent and longer flow experiences. The communicated improvement areas comprised themes such as setting clear work goals, managing smartphone notifications, or controlling task difficulties.

Focus group on adaptivity in FSS: To complement the two stages of the field study, we conducted a focus group on the adaptive characteristics of FSS with eight interested participants from the field study. All these participants were IS scholars and thus had suitable expertise in evaluating system characteristics such as adaptivity. In the focus group, we first introduced and discussed adaptivity in (information) systems for a shared understanding (Hübler & Wotherspoon, 2009; Martín H. et al., 2009). Further, participants brainstormed and discussed examples of adaptation in IS in general. Finally, participants evaluated and discussed whether and where they could recognize adaptivity in our conceptual framework for FSS and Prototypes B and C.

Figure 7 summarizes the field evaluation’s primary outcomes. All participants in both stages regularly used the respective FSS prototype and pointed out positive effects. The prototypes were generally accepted, applicable, and feasible for real-world work settings and positively impacted users’ work behavior. Task context suitability: Before proceeding to the results, we confirm that the task context was suitable for experiencing flow, which requires clear task goals and a suitable challenge-skill balance (Nakamura & Csikszentmihalyi, 2009). Regarding obtrusiveness, four participants from Stage 2 reported that the EEG headset became uncomfortable after approximately 45 minutes. Personal mobile device (DP2): In Stage 1, participants used Prototype B, which had been installed on their smartphones, on a different number of workdays over the seven-day study period (ranging from four to seven). In Stage 2, all 10 participants used Prototype C, which had been installed on their mobile work computer, on both study days (see Figure 7b). Half of the participants indicated that they were more comfortable using the prototype in their home office, where they were exposed to less physical disruption. Two participants added that they would feel uncomfortable wearing an EEG headset in front of colleagues at the office. However, using the FSS at home was easy for all participants because both prototypes ran on personal mobile devices. Individual participants from both stages of the field study noted that it would be helpful if they could capture even more different interruption sources, such as from desktop applications not connected to their smartphones. In Stage 2, three participants explicitly noted the ease of use of Prototype C’s software. None of the participants reported interruptions caused by Prototype C. However, some participants reported occasionally checking the EEG recording status on their own initiative. In summary, we conclude that running an FSS on participants’ mobile devices was feasible and accepted by the participants on both their smartphones (Prototype B) and mobile work computers (Prototype C).

Flow analytics (DP3): All participants regularly looked at and analyzed the flow detection results and captured interruption data (see Figure 7c). Thus, most participants (10) indicated that the respective prototype’s results matched their self-perception of flow (see Figure 7d). One Stage 1 participant reported that the prototype did not capture any flow states during the study, but they did perceive a feeling of flow during the usage. Three Stage 2 participants reported that flow detections diverged from their selfperception. Thus, flow detection did not work equally well for all participants. Two Stage 1 participants reported being surprised by the high amount of ITmediated interruption feedback provided by Prototype B. In contrast, although Prototype C was able to capture virtually all IT-mediated interruptions from users’ smartphones, participants did not report feeling substantially distracted by smartphone interruptions. Some Stage 2 participants reported already having established coping mechanisms to avoid being interrupted by their smartphones before the field study. Nearly all (13) participants from both stages stated that the feedback from the flow analytics modules enabled them to reflect on their flow states and IT-mediated interruptions. Consequently, they reported that they planned to adapt their work behavior in the future—for example, by defining time slots for concentrated work or avoiding multitasking (see Figure 7e).

Flow support insights (DP4): In the Stage 1 user interviews, participants consistently rated the concept of flow support insights that signal improvement areas for flow support positively. All participants believed that communicating improvement areas could assist in establishing individual conditions to experience flow more often and for longer periods. Encouraged by this feedback, we complemented Prototype C with a module for flow support insights suggesting such improvement areas. Prototype C thus suggested that participants set clear work goals for themselves, deactivate smartphone notifications for the apps sending them the most notifications, and rearrange task difficulties to establish individual conditions for entering flow states more often and making them last longer. Even though these insights were relatively simple because of the limited amount of data available for each participant in the field study, most participants considered them to be helpful. One participant reported that they planned to adapt their work behavior, prioritize their tasks, and set clear goals for concentrated work sessions based on the improvement areas. Another participant indicated that they planned on managing their computer’s notification settings (i.e., their work environment) to avoid multitasking by repeatedly answering instant messages while they were concentrating on work. All participants confirmed that they found the general concept of flow support insights to be helpful.

To evaluate where adaptivity specifically lies in an FSS, we conducted a focus group as part of Eval 4. Participants recognized adaptive characteristics on both the user and technology sides. On the user side, the adaptivity lies in establishing individual flow conditions through users’ self-regulation of their (work) behavior due to feedback on flow states and flow support insights. On the technology side, there is a consistent adaptation in flow detection capabilities concerning users’ feedback and the available data. The longer and more often one uses an FSS, the more it will adapt to users’ routines and habits, thus generating more individual feedback.

Retrospective biofeedback (DP5): Both Prototypes B and C provided users with retrospective biofeedback. In Stage 1, participants could directly access this feedback (i.e., detected flow states) after each recorded session. In Stage 2, a twice-daily results report via email detailed all recorded sessions since the last report. The purpose of retrospectivity was to avoid disrupting participants’ existing or emerging flow states by providing real-time feedback during recording sessions. As confirmed by Stage 2 participants, this worked well for the most part. Only one Stage 2 participant reported coincidentally receiving a results report from the morning while recording a session in the afternoon. This situation could easily be avoided in the future by adjusting feedback times.

## 6 Discussion

## 6.1 Theoretical Discussion

In this research paper, we created and evaluated prescriptive knowledge on the system class of FSS. To this end, we developed, implemented, and tested three prototypes at different stages of maturity. Our design knowledge for FSS comprises the conceptual framework, the meta-requirements, the design principles, and the three FSS prototypes. We evaluated the design knowledge in four evaluation activities (Eval 1-4) and multiple evaluation cycles. The evaluation suggests that the design knowledge is novel, relevant, complete, feasible, applicable, and effective (i.e., it positively impacts users). Our work contributes to (IS) research in the following ways.

First, our FSS design knowledge contributes to a nascent design theory supplemented by situated IT artifact implementations (Prototypes A, B, and C) according to an established classification of DSR contribution types (Gregor & Hevner, 2013). However, considering a prudent use of the term design theory (Iivari, 2020), we refrain from this designation and instead label the outcomes of our work as design knowledge. With this design knowledge, we complement IS research on flow by leveraging synergies between individual behavior and IT systems design to enhance human performance (Gestalt mode of DSR; Adam et al., 2021). Previous research on flow and cognitive absorption in major IS outlets has mainly focused on providing valuable insights for organizations in the context of platform design—for example, to increase customer and sales numbers in ecommerce. This previous research, for example, demonstrates that flow and cognitive absorption can serve as tools to drive customers to (re)visit companies’ presences in virtual worlds (Goel et al., 2011; Nah et al., 2011), create and enhance virtual product experiences (Jiang & Benbasat, 2004), and improve online shopping websites (Guo & Poole, 2009). Complementarily, our research on FSS builds on flow theory and focuses on supporting the individual in the knowledge work context. Hence, it focuses on providing support for the individual to experience flow more frequently and for longer periods at work, positively impacting their subjective well-being and work performance. Ultimately, these outcomes align with organizations’ business interests and responsibility concerns, yet the agency lies with the individual.

Second, our DSR study advances flow research by demonstrating that flow detection using biosignals is feasible and applicable in a practical knowledge work field setting. In doing so, we follow a call for complementing flow self-assessment with biosignals (Rissler et al., 2017a). Using neurophysiological flow measures, especially through a DSR approach, is another critical difference from previous flow research, which relied on surveys to measure flow (e.g., Guo & Poole, 2009; Nah et al., 2011). Biosignals can be recorded in situ without interrupting flow or relying on ex post recalls prone to cognitive or contextual biases. Self-reports take place after flow is over (Cowley et al., 2019). Consequently, events occurring between the flow task and the self-report, even in a minuscule period, can affect the perception of the flow state. Further, it is impossible to discern whether users’ perception is experienced in the respective moment or only triggered by the post hoc questionnaire (Schwarz, 1999). Measuring physiology at the moment when flow occurs eliminates the risk of such bias. Building on previous groundwork on measurement techniques (Knierim et al., 2018; Müller-Putz et al., 2015), our contribution is the design knowledge of leveraging heart rate and EEG data in an FSS for the knowledge work context.

Third, we contribute to IT-mediated interruptions and feedback research by providing a NeuroIS-based tool for knowledge workers’ interruption management. Our work shows that FSS, mainly through flow support insights (DP4), can make knowledge workers better aware of how to deal with IT-mediated interruptions. Thus, we respond to a call for research on interruption management mechanisms (Chen & Karahanna, 2014). Through flow support insights, our work may further address another previously raised research question: How can one provide task-relevant feedback to knowledge workers without compromising their performance (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018)? Research has found that providing feedback that supports individuals’ learning for familiar tasks increases their performance (Kluger & DeNisi, 1996). Thus, individuals tend to be better at perceiving positive (as opposed to negative) feedback; thus, feedback must be customized for it to be effective (Ilgen et al., 1979). Consistent with these theoretical foundations, the improvement areas suggested by FSS that aim to support users in establishing individual conditions for more frequent and longer flow experiences for their familiar work tasks consist exclusively of positive feedback and adapt to the user based on biosignals and personal interruption data. Participants in the field evaluation were generally aware that IT-mediated interruptions could be harmful. However, some had not previously used coping measures. The FSS’s flow support insights module emphasized the harmful nature of IT-mediated interruptions. As a result, the participants indicated that they will likely adapt their interruption settings, which will likely yield gains in work performance (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2015; Galluch et al., 2015). Overall, our FSS design knowledge contributes to feedback research by presenting a way for feedback interventions to improve work performance with a neuroadaptive IS as the feedback source.

Fourth, our FSS design knowledge contributes to the research stream of NeuroIS. FSS are neuroadaptive systems because they involve sensor-actor interaction sensitive to neurophysiological states (vom Brocke et al., 2020). The NeuroIS field recognizes these neurophysiological states to include not only CNS activity (e.g., measurable by EEG) but explicitly also PNS activity (e.g., measurable by HRV) (Riedl et al., 2014; vom Brocke et al., 2020). FSS differ from other neuroadaptive systems, which only adapt the technological component, for example, by adjusting the UI. In the case of FSS, the key adaptation is users’ self-regulation. Flow support insights (DP4) draw on the cognitive theory of self-regulation (Bandura, 1991), according to which successful behavior regulation requires anticipatory self-monitoring in close proximity to the behavior worth changing and clear performance feedback. FSS provide feedback through the information on users’ flow states and allow for timely self-monitoring by making the flow support insights accessible shortly after data collection. In this regard, our evaluations align with previous research demonstrating that biofeedback supports users’ behavioral adaptations (e.g., Lux et al., 2018; Riedl & Léger, 2016).

Based on prior research (e.g., Csikszentmihalyi & LeFevre, 1989; Eisenberger et al., 2005; Ilies et al., 2017), there is reason to believe that FSS-enabled behavioral adaptations not only support flow for knowledge workers but also increase their subjective well-being: that is, pleasant emotions and satisfaction at work. In addition to the previously noted positive effects on well-being by reducing harmful interruptions, flow experiences also positively affect knowledge workers’ performance (Demerouti, 2006; Eisenberger et al., 2005).

## 6.2 Practical Implications

Our FSS design knowledge aims to support all knowledge workers, regardless of whether and how much they have previously engaged in reflecting on their flow experiences and interruptions from IT. For knowledge workers who have yet to consider these aspects, FSS may support them in adapting their work behavior and environment to establish individual conditions for flow in their work. Through the improvement areas, an FSS further conveys why the proposed adaptations might be reasonable, creating an understanding of the interplay between one’s work behavior, work environment (e.g., regarding ITmediated interruptions), and flow. Knowledge workers who have previously self-reflected on flow and IT-mediated interruptions might already have established individual flow conditions. In this case, FSS may assist knowledge workers in (re)evaluating these conditions and adjusting or selecting additional behaviors or environmental conditions. Naturally, the room for improvement in establishing flow conditions will diminish with every successful adaptation to the work environment. However, due to the fast pace of work changes, new opportunities to improve flow conditions will emerge.

Switching the view from FSS usage implications to implications for providing FSS to knowledge workers, tech companies, employers, workers’ councils, and worker unions are significant recipients of our FSS design knowledge, as they will be the ones implementing it. From the perspective of these stakeholders, supporting employees’ well-being and work performance is critical for successfully operating a business. However, for this support to succeed, technology acceptance is essential. With the recent proliferation of fitness trackers and smartwatches in everyday consumer markets, we expect an increasing acceptance of neuroadaptive tools. However, self-measurement through wearables can also introduce adverse effects (Moya & Pallud, 2020). Allowing for discretion is a vital prerequisite for accepting FSS at the workplace. Stage 2 of our field study indicates that half of the participants preferred to wear an EEG headband when working from home because they could set it up privately and did not have to worry about being disturbed or watched by curious or judging colleagues.

While our field evaluation showed that knowledge workers generally accept FSS, an organization must establish policies aligned with MR2 to ensure acceptance in the long run. Such policies include the complete voluntariness of FSS usage and the location of its use (e.g., some participants indicated they would rather or only use an FSS when working from home), strict data privacy (i.e., employers must not access any FSS data), and ensuring that FSS do not restrict individual work habits in any way. Workers’ councils and unions can and must support establishing, monitoring, and enforcing these policies. Only then can FSS effectively benefit employees’ well-being and performance.

Another key stakeholder group that our design knowledge for FSS targets is IT system designers. Assuming that the stakeholders mentioned above agree on introducing an FSS to their organization, IT system designers could use our design knowledge to craft an FSS that optimally fits the organization’s requirements. One expert we interviewed in Eval 1 and Eval 2 provided an example of the practical implications of FSS. They pointed out that FSS will most likely impact job roles “for which flow and permanent concentration loom large, for example, air or railroad traffic controllers.” According to the expert, the FSS’s impact may be restricted to selected job roles because the overall costs for lower-tier positions must remain manageable. Thus, introducing an FSS for all employees of an organization might not be affordable, even though the FSS design knowledge describes systems that can assist all knowledge workers. However, as discussed with the participants in our field study, employees might use an FSS only rarely (i.e., not daily), occasionally recording concentrated work sessions to obtain new insights for adapting their work behavior and environment. Thus, multiple employees could share the hardware for such a system, significantly reducing costs and making it more affordable for knowledge workers in the organization.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Research

Our research is subject to limitations that point toward possibilities for further research. First, this study focused specifically on IT-mediated interruptions as significant potential flow disruptors. In addition to interruptions, controlling for environmental factors in the workplace resulting from, for example, lighting, temperature, or ambient noise could also be beneficial for making future FSS’s retrospective feedback more meaningful and effective.

Second, given our goal of providing a proof of concept for retrospective biofeedback-based flow support, the heuristics of Prototypes B and C are limited to basic flow detection capabilities that work with comparatively little data from the field evaluation. Future research could develop improved flow detection algorithms that minimize inconsistencies between users’ perceptions and biosignals, as in the field evaluation. Because the biosignals we used to detect flow in our study do not necessarily only measure flow (both HRV and EEG have also been used to measure cognitive load—e.g., Ahmad et al., 2020; Haapalainen et al., 2010—and emotional arousal, e.g., Schaaff & Adam, 2013; Cavazza et al., 2014), future research could devise better ways to distinguish between these psychological states.

Third, Prototypes B and C focused on the visual communication of retrospective rather than live feedback to ensure that the FSS did not interfere with users’ loss of self-reflection during flow (DP3, DP4, and DP5). However, the underlying framework for live biofeedback systems (Lux et al., 2018) also considers auditory and tactile feedback. Based on this, future research might consider how quickly FSS could provide multimodal feedback when flow is detected.

Fourth, the Eval 1 and 2 expert interviews, the lab experiment, the focus group, and the field study all had modest sample sizes, providing an opportunity to conduct larger-scale evaluation studies in the future. However, each activity constituted one evaluation episode as part of an ongoing longer journey with incremental knowledge gains at every step and cumulative evidence supporting our FSS design knowledge. Thus, we deem the sample sizes sufficient for the purpose of the present study.

## 7 Concluding Note

FSS hold great potential to support individuals in experiencing flow more often and for longer periods. At the individual level, users may benefit through greater well-being, task enjoyment, and performance. At the organizational level, the downstream effects on workers’ productivity and staff morale align with overall business objectives. To this end, we hope that the FSS design knowledge created in this paper can contribute to tailored solutions in various knowledge work contexts. The conceptual framework could serve as a shared frame of reference between the various stakeholders to build FSS with high technology acceptance.

## About the Authors

Marc T. P. Adam is an associate professor in Computing and Information Technology at the University of Newcastle, Australia. He received an undergraduate degree in computer science from the University of Applied Sciences Würzburg, Germany, and a PhD in business information systems from the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT), Germany. His research investigates the interplay of cognition and affect in human-computer interaction. He is a founding member of the NeuroIS Society.

Lukas Bonenberger is a researcher at the Augsburg Technical University of Applied Sciences and Branch Business & Information Systems Engineering of Fraunhofer FIT and a doctoral candidate in the Chair of Digital Management at the University of Hohenheim. He holds a bachelor’s degree in computer science from the University of Applied Sciences Kempten, Germany, and a master’s degree in computer science from the University of Augsburg, Germany. His research focuses primarily on the design and analysis of information systems for individual health and well-being.

Henner Gimpel is a professor of information systems and holds the Chair of Digital Management in the Faculty of Business, Economics and Social Sciences at the University of Hohenheim. He is a director of the FIM Research Center for Information Management and a member of the Branch Business & Information Systems Engineering of the Fraunhofer FIT. He studied Industrial Engineering and Management and obtained a doctorate from the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology. His behavioral and design-oriented research centers around smart sustainability and digita ethics; digital health and well-being; collective, hybrid, and artificial intelligence; and IT use.

Julia Lanzl is a postdoctoral researcher at the Chair of Digital Management at the University of Hohenheim, Germany. She is also a member of the Branch Business and Information Systems Engineering of the Fraunhofer FIT and co-head of Department of Information Management at the FIM Research Center. She finished her doctoral thesis at the University of Hohenheim in 2021 and studied business and law at the University of Augsburg until 2017. As part of her research activities, she deals with issues relating to digital work, digital stress, and digital responsibility, pursuing the goal of an efficient and human-centered design of the working world using digital technologies.
