# Does Information Technology Improve Open Innovation Performance? An Examination of Manufacturers in Spain

Abstract. Our objective in this paper is to explore the impact of information technology (IT) on the performance of open innovation models. We study the efect of IT on the production of patents and product innovations. Our analysis of a large panel of Spanish manufacturing firms confirms that the efect of the proportion of external research and development (R&D) investments on the production of patents and new products has an inverted U-shape. The results also reveal that investments in IT positively moderate the consequences of open innovation on innovation performance by reducing identification, assimilation, and utilization costs. Our results provide evidence of the viability and benefit of a new strategic alternative to innovation that is diferent than the traditional process of vertically integrated R&D.

## 1. Introduction

In a world increasingly dominated by turbulent and hypercompetitive markets, innovation is of great importance. Indeed, in an age of temporary advantages (D’Aveni et al. 2010, Wiggins and Ruefli 2005), entrepreneurial action, creative destruction, and continuous innovation are crucial for firms’ competitiveness. As knowledge is the most important input of the innovation process, one of the challenges for researchers and managers is to understand how to make it available for invention and product innovation. Information technologies, as information handling devices, are likely to play a key role in enhancing innovation performance.

The objective of this paper is to analyze the impact of information technology (IT) investments on the output of a firm’s innovation activities. We study whether the efect of IT investments depends on the openness of a firm’s innovation strategy. Research on open innovation (Chesbrough 2003) proposes that firms are better of if they change from the old closed innovation model to one that is more permeable to knowledge from outside the firm. Many firms around the world, such as Procter & Gamble, Fiat, and IBM, have moved towards an open innovation model in the past two decades (Dodgson et al. 2006, Hamm 2007, Di Minin et al. 2010). However, firms that incorporate external knowledge into their innovation activities incur several costs associated with openness (Salge et al. 2013). Our argument is that IT investments reduce these costs by enabling the firm to more efectively manage and integrate external knowledge. External knowledge integration has been highlighted as one of the challenges of open innovation models (Sieg et al. 2010). IT facilitates the acquisition of external knowledge through the use of personal computers, email, and Internet-related information technologies (Kleis et al. 2012, p. 44). IT also contributes to external innovation collaboration by improving information exchange with external partners (Kleis et al. 2012, p. 46). At Procter & Gamble, for instance, IT was key to the move towards more collaborative innovation practices (Dodgson et al. 2006).

We theorize on the role of IT in open innovation and examine the innovation-enhancing role of IT in approximately 1,800 Spanish manufacturing firms, between 2003 and 2011, using data generated by the annual Survey on Business Strategies (SBS). By doing so, we contribute to the literature in three ways. First, we explore the impact of IT on reducing the costs of open innovation strategies. These costs are associated with the use of external knowledge (Laursen and Salter 2006, Grimpe and Kaiser 2010, Berchicci 2013) and have been classified into identification, assimilation, and utilization costs (Salge et al. 2013). To our knowledge, previous research on IT and innovation has not examined the role of IT along these critical dimensions. Second, the studies on the complementarities between IT and research and development (R&D) (Ravichandran et al. 2017, Kleis et al. 2012) focus on invention (patents). However, patents have a weaker link with firm performance than new products; they have even been negatively related to performance (Artz et al. 2010). In fact, patents and new products are innovation outcomes that relate to diferent processes (invention and product innovation); research has shown a weak relationship between patent intensity and product innovation at the firm level (Devinney 1993). Exploring whether the role of IT investments is diferent depending on the innovation outcome examined is an interesting task. We test our hypotheses using information on the two outcomes. Finally, extant research has explored the impact of IT on innovation using samples of large firms, mainly from the United States. However, it is not known if existing results extrapolate to small and medium-sized firms (SMEs). Our paper helps address this research gap. We use a sample that is representative of the size distribution of Spanish manufacturing firms. Therefore, it also includes SMEs.

## 2. Theoretical Background 2.1. The Role of IT in Enhancing Innovation Performance

The debate on the efects of IT on performance has recently moved from the macro to the micro level (Dos Santos et al. 2012). Research has focused on the productivity (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1993) and on the value creation efects of IT on firms (Dos Santos et al. 2012). Some authors have moved on to consider specific ways in which firms could create value. A nascent research line in the information systems (IS) literature is focusing on how IT investments facilitate innovation production (Ravichandran et al. 2017, Joshi et al. 2010, Kleis et al. 2012). This literature (see Table A1 in the online appendix) agrees on the role of IT in facilitating knowledge management in the innovation process<sup>1</sup> (Joshi et al. 2010, Kleis et al. 2012). For example, Joshi et al. (2010) study the role of diferent categories of IT applications in the production of new products and patents in a sample of 110 firms ranked by InformationWeek. Their results support the hypothesis that IT has a direct influence on the production of innovations. Other research studying the efects of IT on innovation performance has characterized this relationship as moderation, but with diferent results. Ravichandran et al. (2017) use data on 161 large firms from InformationWeek. They find complementarity between R&D expenditures and IT spending: IT alleviates the diminishing returns to R&D. Kleis et al. (2012) study 201 Fortune 1000 manufacturing firms. Their results show a positive impact of IT on citation-weighted patent counts. However, contrary to Ravichandran et al. (2017), they do not find complementarities between IT and R&D.

These papers suggest that IT improves the results of innovative activities, particularly of R&D investments. However, the type of relationship, the mechanisms, and the evidence difer from one another. Another feature of these papers is that they use information on large firms: Large firms are more likely to need IT in activities such as coordination of R&D teams. Finally, they do not study the possibility of diferent impacts of IT depending on the innovation models implemented by firms.<sup>2</sup>

In this paper, we examine three issues. First, we posit that IT investments have an impact on open models of innovation: We study how firms use IT as a way to reduce costs of using external knowledge in innovative activities. Second, we understand IT as a complementary element to external R&D. Third, we use a representative sample that includes not only large firms but also SMEs. This will help to reduce concerns that the findings may be conditioned by the large size of firms studied in extant research. Finally, we focus on invention and product innovation <sup>3</sup> as R&D has been shown to afect the two (Artz et al. 2010).<sup>4</sup>

## 2.2. Open Innovation and Innovation Performance

Research on the performance consequences of innovation activities focuses on open innovation models (see, for example, Dahlander and Gann 2010, for a review). “Open innovation is a paradigm that assumes that firms can and should use external ideas as well as internal ideas, and internal and external paths to market, as firms look to advance their technology” (Chesbrough 2003, p. 24). This definition considers not only how firms use external knowledge to produce innovation but also how firms profit from the results of the innovation process. In line with the interest of IS researchers, we focus on the inbound innovation activities of firms.

Use of an open innovation strategy provides firms with several advantages (Berchicci 2013). First, sharing R&D investments contributes to the reduction of costs associated with innovative activities; these costs have increased over time (Katz 1986). Second, external knowledge allows firms to overcome the constraints of exclusive reliance on internal resources (Gupta et al. 2006). Investments in acquisition of external knowledge may provide resources that are not available internally, given the path dependency afecting knowledge resources (Teece 1986, Grimpe and Kaiser 2010). In an R&D context, they can provide firms with quality advantages by contracting with a partner that owns “specialized know-how, equipment, and infrastructure” (Grimpe and Kaiser 2010, p. 1486). Following Katila and Ahuja (2002), firms may benefit from external R&D through two mechanisms, i.e., new variations and recombinatory search. External R&D may also foster creativity by exposing internal R&D to new research methods and perspectives (Grimpe and Kaiser 2010).

Research on the economics of innovation is consistent with this reasoning. Cassiman and Veugelers (2006) demonstrate the complementarity between internal and external technological sources. Lokshin et al. (2008) find that the combination of internal and external R&D improves productivity in a sample of 304 manufacturing firms from the Netherlands. However, recent research focuses on achieving the right balance between internal and external innovation activities when producing innovation (see, for example, Laursen and Salter 2006, Grimpe and Kaiser 2010, Berchicci 2013). The reason for this approach is that the firm not only obtains benefits from using an open innovation strategy, it also has to bear the costs. Laursen and Salter (2006) highlight the costs of relying on a large number of innovation sources (search breadth) and of drawing intensively on them (search depth).<sup>5</sup> Among other reasons, they mention the challenges of managing too many ideas in a context of limited managerial attention and the need to build a “shared understanding” from repeated interactions with external partners (Laursen and Salter 2006, p. 136). Similarly, Grimpe and Kaiser (2010) explain the potential disadvantages of R&D outsourcing by highlighting the dificulties of integrating externally acquired knowledge into the firm. Finally, Berchicci (2013) finds that external R&D investments increase the costs of searching, selecting, coordinating, managing, and controlling external partners. All these papers show an inverted U-shaped relationship between open innovation and innovation performance.

The costs of openness may be divided into three categories (Salge et al. 2013), i.e., identification, assimilation, and utilization costs. Identification costs appear when firms access and evaluate external knowledge inputs. These costs increase with openness because firms must search for and evaluate external agents that are more distant geographically, cognitively, and culturally. Assimilation costs are associated with transferring, analyzing, interpreting, and understanding the information obtained from external sources. They include transfer costs (to persuade the external agent to reveal knowledge and facilitate its transfer) and internalization costs (to understand and assimilate external knowledge). These costs are also expected to rise with openness because it increases the number and diversity of external actors. Finally, utilization costs are associated with merging internal and external knowledge. This category includes integration costs, which are created by the dificulty of combining external and internal knowledge. It also includes appropriation costs, which refer to limitations to appropriating the returns when property rights are difuse or when the external agent wants to influence the project’s development trajectory. As the open innovation model proposes that permeable organizational boundaries may exist at diferent stages of innovative activity (Chesbrough 2003), these costs apply to invention and product innovation. In our hypotheses, we propose that these costs are reduced by IT investments, thus moderating the efects of open innovation on innovation performance.

## 2.3. The Moderating Efect of IT Investments on Open Innovation Performance

Review of the literature that focuses on the innovationenabling consequences of IT (Ravichandran et al. 2017, Joshi et al. 2010, Kleis et al. 2012) and on the costs of adopting open models of innovation (Laursen and Salter 2006, Grimpe and Kaiser 2010, Berchicci 2013) reveals an opportunity for integration. The literature on open innovation proposes that the relationship between reliance on external R&D activities and innovative activities is an inverted U-shape. This type of relationship is produced by the additive influence of two latent variables (see the first and second graphs in Figure 1). The first latent variable represents the benefits of incorporating external knowledge into innovative activities (first graph in Figure 1), which increases invention and product innovation. The second latent variable represents the costs of using external knowledge that were described in Section 2.2 (second graph in Figure 1). Subtracting these costs from the benefits gives rise to the inverted U-shaped relationship between the extent of openness and innovative performance (see the third graph in Figure 1 and Haans et al. 2016 for the general framework).

We argue that one way in which IT investments improve the innovation performance of open innovation strategies is by reducing the costs of using external knowledge. In other words, IT moderates the inverted U-shaped relationship between the extent of openness and innovative performance through one of the two latent variables, i.e., the costs of using external knowledge. Figure 2 illustrates the influence of IT on this latent variable. The dotted line in the second graph in Figure 2 shows how IT reduces the costs of openness. Similarly, the third graph in Figure 2 shows the final efect on innovation performance (dotted lines). The result is a steeper inverted U-shape curve (Haans et al. 2016).<sup>6</sup> Given that invention and product innovation are afected by R&D investments (Artz et al. 2010, Barge-Gil and Lopez 2015), we expect this moderation to influence the two outcomes. We ofer an explanation of the mechanisms involved.<sup>7</sup>

First, IT investments may reduce identification costs by facilitating search and evaluation activities. Ravichandran et al. (2017) highlight the importance of IT in establishing and enabling social ties. These ties may be created across firm boundaries and may help to identify useful knowledge and facilitate information flows between researchers. This, in turn, enables the firm to obtain state-of-the-art knowledge. IT reduces search costs (Cesaroni 2004) by connecting the firm to technology markets in which they may find the solution to technological problems. This argument is consistent with Joshi et al.’s (2010) focus on potential absorptive capacity and its impact on ideated and commercialized innovation (i.e., invention and product innovation). Potential absorptive capacity (Zahra and George 2002) includes knowledge acquisition and knowledge assimilation. Knowledge acquisition consists of identifying and obtaining knowledge that is important for a firm’s operations. Sophisticated retrieval technology such as query software, expert finder or knowledge maps may be used to identify useful knowledge from a wide variety of knowledge resources (Joshi et al. 2010).

Second, IT may reduce assimilation costs by afecting their two components, i.e., transfer and internalization costs. Transfer costs depend on firms “establishing and maintaining direct and trusting relationships between the parties involved” (Salge et al. 2013, p. 662). IT investments can improve the performance of an open model of innovation by providing links that facilitate the exchange of information with external partners and that create efective partnerships (Kleis et al. 2012). On one hand, research has shown that the frequency of communication decreases as the distance between ofices increases (Allen 1986), a likely situation when acquiring external knowledge. Information technologies may increase the probability of communicating (Kleis et al. 2012) and improve a firm’s ability to coordinate, manage, and control external partners. On the other hand, IT may have a positive efect on trust and shared understanding between partners (Kleis et al. 2012).

Internalization costs are also reduced thanks to IT. These costs may be created by the rejection of external ideas and the lack of prior related knowledge (Salge et al. 2013). IT investments provide a common language that may be used by R&D personnel to make the communication process more eficient (Ravichandran et al. 2017). IT also improves communication between specialists from diferent fields (Ravichandran et al. 2017). This may facilitate understanding of new knowledge, thus contributing to knowledge assimilation. Improved understanding can also reduce the rejection of new ideas from external agents on the grounds of being inferior to internal knowledge, i.e., the “not-invented here syndrome” (Katz and Allen 1982, Salge et al. 2013).

Finally, IT reduces utilization costs through its efect on integration costs. One key challenge of externallyacquired knowledge is its integration across firm boundaries (Ragatz et al. 1997). IT may facilitate this process by creating organization memory in the form of repositories (Alavi and Leidner 2001, Joshi et al. 2010). This is especially important in contexts where new knowledge is acquired in forms and formats that are not readily internalized. These repositories help the firm to store what it “knows” and include “data warehouses” and asset management systems (Ruggles 1998, Joshi et al. 2010).

Therefore, the open innovation model introduces certain costs that a firm must take into account. These costs increase with the extent of openness, though they may be reduced by use of IT, thus increasing the performance of innovative activities. Moreover, we do not expect the cost reduction efect of IT to be monotonic, but to vary with the extent of open innovation. For the lowest levels of openness the consequences of the costs associated with open innovation are expected to be low and the positive role of IT as a moderating mechanism is expected to be limited. However, we expect the moderating efect of IT to increase as the costs increase as a result of higher levels of open innovation. Finally, for the highest levels of openness we expect the positive efect of IT to be decreasing as openness increases: High levels of openness imply a larger amount of external sources tapped and a higher heterogeneity of knowledge (Salge et al. 2013). In this context, high investments in IT may contribute to information overload, a phenomenon that has been negatively related to innovation (Hemp 2009, Paruchuri 2010). For example, Paruchuri (2010) argues that when information flows increase beyond a certain point they cannot be adequately processed, due to bounded rationality. Information overload may also lead to insufficient attention to any particular idea (Zhou and Li 2012) and to a reduction in learning opportunities. This could hinder the efectiveness of IT in improving innovation performance.

All this reasoning implies that IT steepens the curvilinear relationship between openness and innovation performance (see Figure 2 and Haans et al. 2016). Following these arguments, and considering the diferences between the processes of invention and product innovation (Kleinknecht et al. 2002, Hagedoorn and Cloodt 2003), we develop two separate hypotheses on the moderating efect of IT:

Hypothesis 1A. The inverted U-shaped relationship between the extent of use of external R&D and invention is stronger in firms with high IT investments than in firms with low IT investments. Furthermore, the marginal efect of this moderation will initially increase and then decrease with the extent of use of external R&D.

Hypothesis 1B. The inverted U-shaped relationship between the extent of use of external R&D and product innovation is stronger in firms with high IT investments than in firms with low IT investments. Furthermore, the marginal efect of this moderation will initially increase and then decrease with the extent of use of external R&D.

## 3. Empirical Analysis 3.1. Sample and Data

The data set used in the empirical analysis is the Survey on Business Strategies (SBS), which is conducted by the SEPI Foundation (Spanish Ministry of Industry).<sup>8</sup> These data provide annual information about the strategies of a representative sample of firms that belong to the Spanish manufacturing sector. The sample includes firms with at least 10 employees; firms are selected as follows: (1) it includes the whole population of Spanish manufacturing firms with 200 or more employees and (2) it includes a random sample of the firms with fewer than 200 employees. The final sample consists of 2,921 firms that form an unbalanced panel. Therefore, the sample is much larger than those used by Huang and Liu (2005), Ravichandran et al. (2017), Joshi et al. (2010), Kleis et al. (2012), and Bardhan et al. (2013). Additionally, the analysis does not focus predominantly on large firms, as does extant research, but includes SMEs.<sup>9</sup> The inclusion of SMEs is important as firms with fewer than 250 employees make up 99% of all of the firms in Spain and the European Union (EU-28) (Eurostat 2015). Similar figures are available from the U.S. Census Bureau (U.S. Census Bureau Data 2015). Finally, another diference from previous studies (Huang and Liu 2005, Joshi et al. 2010) is that our sample forms a panel.

The survey includes some questions about the output of innovation; we use these results in our analysis. The survey reports the number of patents registered and the number of product innovations. The data set also provides information about the amount of R&D investments and the investments in information processing equipment. These data allow us to test the role of IT in the relationship between R&D investments and innovation performance. The information to construct some of the variables is available from 1990 on, and we use it to build the stock of R&D investments. However, for one of the components of the stock of information technologies, i.e., training expenses, the information is only available from 2001 on; this limits the final observation window to the period between 2003 and 2011. Details are provided below.

## 3.2. Variable Description and Measurement

3.2.1. Dependent Variable. Our approach to measuring innovation performance uses two variables, one for invention and one for product innovation (see Hagedoorn and Cloodt 2003): (1) the number of patents and (2) the number of product innovations introduced by a firm in a given year. Hagedoorn and Cloodt (2003) observe the existence of some variation in R&D, patents, and new products across industries. The use of two measures of innovation performance is in agreement with their suggestion to use multiple indicators that allow a “better understanding” of innovative performance (Hagedoorn and Cloodt 2003, p. 1368). The measures are also consistent with previous studies on the impact of IT on innovation performance. For example, Joshi et al. (2010) distinguish between ideated innovation (patents) and commercialized innovation (product innovations). The empirical evidence suggests that both measures are associated with firm performance (Artz et al. 2010, Bloom and Van Reenen 2002). However, they also present some diferences. Patents are closer to knowledge transformation and exploitation and do not necessarily imply knowledge commercialization (Joshi et al. 2010). In fact, patents are frequently used to block other firms, preventing suits, and in negotiations (Cohen et al. 2000). Product innovations involve the commercialization of new or significantly improved products. They may be the result of the application of the knowledge generated by R&D investments through patents, but other sources of product innovation are also possible (Barge-Gil et al. 2011).

Invention. We use the number of patents a firm has registered from 2003 to 2011 as a proxy for invention performance (Beneito 2006, Hall et al. 1986, Henderson and Cockburn 1994). Patent count data are highly correlated with citation-weighted patents and with other indicators of firm innovativeness (Rothaermel and Alexandre 2009).

Product innovation. Product innovations involve the complex task of converting inventions into valuable products (Fleming and Sorenson 2003, Kleis et al. 2012). We use the number of product innovations obtained by a firm from 2003 to 2011. The total amount of product innovations includes completely new products and products with suficient modification to be considered diferent from those produced earlier.

## 3.2.2. Independent Variables

Stock of IT. The survey provides yearly information on the investments in “equipment for processing information” and on the “external expenses in training incurred by” the company in “computing and information technologies.” We use the perpetual inventory method to obtain an estimation of the stock of equipment for processing information at time t in the following way: $\mathrm { \dot { S } I P E } _ { t } = ( \bar { 1 } - \delta ) \cdot \mathrm { S I P E } _ { t - 1 } + \mathrm { I P E } _ { t } ,$ where IPE is the investment in information processing equipment, SIPE is the stock of information processing equipment, and δ stands for the rate of annual depreciation, which is assumed to equal 15% (Lichtenberg 1995). To approximate the first-year value, a presample growth rate of 5% was considered. Additionally, and following Dewan and Kraemer (2000), Dewan et al. (1998), and Dewan and Min (1997), we added the capitalized value of IT training <sup>(</sup>T<sup>)</sup>, assuming that computer and IT training creates an asset with a service life of three years. We calculated a three-year capitalization of computer and IT training using a depreciation rate, λ, of 33%. The two dimensions, investments in SIPE and the three-year capitalized value of computer and IT training, are added to calculate the Stock of IT as follows:

$$
S t o c k o f I T _ {t} = (1 - \delta) \cdot \mathrm{SIPE} _ {t - 1} + \mathrm{IPE} _ {t} + \sum_ {k = 0} ^ {2} (1 - \lambda) ^ {k} \cdot T _ {t - k}.
$$

To control for the fact that larger firms tend to have a larger stock of IT, we divided this measure by the number of employees. We take the natural logarithm of this variable as the final measure of IT stock.

Stock of total R&D (SGTD). The survey ofers information on the R&D expenses of the company, distinguishing between “1. External expenses,”<sup>10</sup> “2. Internal expenses,” and “3. Total expenses,” expressed in euros.<sup>11</sup> We use these data to calculate the stock of expenses in total R&D by applying the perpetual inventory method (Griliches 1981). A presample growth rate of 5% and a depreciation rate of 15% were assumed (see Griliches 1981 or Griliches et al. 1981). We divide this measure by the number of employees to control for firm size and take natural logarithms.

Extent of openness (SGERD/SGTD). To calculate the degree to which firms use an open innovation strategy, we use the ratio of the stock of external R&D investments to total R&D investments (see, for example, Bonte 2003 or Berchicci 2013). We calculated the stock of external R&D investments using the perpetual inventory method and with the same assumptions as described above.

## 3.2.3. Control Variables

Size. We control for firm size (Kleis et al. 2012) by including the firm’s total number of employees (in thousands). Larger firms are expected to produce a greater number of patents and product innovations.

Market stability. The nature of the industry in which a firm operates is an important factor to take into account in the relationship between IT and innovation (Xue et al. 2012).<sup>12</sup> To define this variable, we take “the main market or markets which the company serves, so that” they jointly represent “at least, 50% of total sales.” In the survey, these markets “can be identified by the product line, customer kind to which they are sold, or any other characteristic which you might consider necessary.” These markets are characterized as (1) “A. In expansion,” (2) “B. Stable” or (3) “C. In decline.” To control for market stability, we included a dummy variable taking the value 1 when all of the markets served by the firm were characterized as stable, and 0 otherwise.

Diversification. Diversified firms could better appropriate the returns on investments in R&D (Nelson 1959). Accordingly, we included an entropy measure of total diversification (Palepu 1985). The raw data for measuring diversification is not available for confidentiality reasons, but the SEPI Foundation calculated and provided us with the variable used in this paper. They provided firm values for related and unrelated diversification. From these values, we calculated total diversification as their sum.

Industry concentration. Industry structure has traditionally been associated with innovation. However, the sign of this relationship is ambiguous (Reinganum 1981). As in previous papers, we have used the fourfirm concentration ratio $\mathsf { \bar { ( } C R _ { 4 } ) } _ { 4 }$ , defined as the market share of the four largest firms in each industry, to control for market structure.

Industry dummies. The innovative behavior of firms is likely to be diferent depending on the sector (Hagedoorn and Cloodt 2003). We control for the impact of industry-specific efects through the use of 19 dummy variables that account for the 20 sectors surveyed. Each of these variables takes a value of 1 when the firm operates in the reference sector, and 0 otherwise.

Time variables. To control for any temporal efect related to patent propensity or to the generation of product innovations, we included seven-year dummies in the period of eight years considered. Each dummy corresponds to a given year and its value can be 1 or 0. We take the year 2003 as the base year.<sup>13</sup>

## 3.3. Methodology

As mentioned earlier, we measure innovative performance through a count of the number of patents and the number of product innovations introduced by a firm in a given year. We used a log-linear knowledge production model (see Online Appendix C). Given that both dependent variables are discrete and nonnegative, the classical linear model is inappropriate (Blundell et al. 1995). In the presence of count data, researchers frequently assume that a variable follows a Poisson distribution (Greene 2008). Therefore, our regression model specifies that each count data is drawn from a Poisson distribution with parameter $\lambda _ { i } ,$ which is explained by the regressors (x ) as follows:<sup>14</sup>

$$
\operatorname{E} [ y _ {i} \mid x _ {i} ] = \operatorname{Var} [ y _ {i} \mid x _ {i} ] = \lambda_ {i} = e ^ {x _ {i} ^ {\prime} \beta}.
$$

There are two issues we must address to properly use the Poisson model, i.e., equidispersion and the excess of zeros in the data. First, the assumption of equidispersion (mean equals variance) does not frequently hold, and certainly not in our case (see Figures A1 and A2 in the online appendix). The negative binomial regression allows for overdispersion by including an individual, unobserved efect in the conditional mean. Second, this model assumes the same probability process for having a 0 as for any other discrete value. However, there are situations in which this assumption is not valid. Our sample is composed of firms with two diferent cases, i.e., those who have no possibility of obtaining patents/product innovations (structural zero) and those who obtain patents/product innovations with some probability (non-structural zero).<sup>15</sup> In these cases, the application of a zero-inflated negative binominal regression is more suitable (Greene 2008, Coxe et al. 2013). The model estimates two equations simultaneously, one for the excess zeros and another for the counts. Diferent variables can be used in the two equations, reflecting the fact that diferent variables may be influencing the two processes involved (Coxe et al. 2013, p. 45). The Vuong test helps us to choose between the negative binomial regression and the zero-inflated negative binomial.

## 5. Conclusion and Discussion

This paper analyzes the role of IT investments in enhancing the performance of a firm’s open innovation strategy. Open innovation strategies are beneficial for firms because they improve innovation performance.

However, they also introduce identification, assimilation, and utilization costs (Salge et al. 2013). We have argued that IT reduces these costs.

As hypothesized, we find that IT investments make the relationship between open innovation strategies and invention/product innovation stronger. As to patents, our results reveal that their number is afected by open innovation through a curvilinear inverted-U shaped relationship. In other words, we find that the acquisition of external knowledge is beneficial for patent production when the proportion of external knowledge is not high. However, as firms use open innovation strategies more intensively, their costs appear and the relationship becomes negative. This confirms previous results in the innovation literature (Grimpe and Kaiser 2010, Berchicci 2013). In this context, a key finding is that IT investments reduce these costs and increase the number of patents produced. However, when the costs of open innovation are high, IT investments have a limited impact on them. Moreover, the results reveal that the benefits of an open innovation strategy are negligible if firms do not invest in IT.

The conclusions are similar when examining product innovations. For low values of IT investments, the use of more external knowledge has a negative efect on the number of product innovations. As firms invest in IT, they start to benefit from external knowledge, and the relationship between open innovation and product innovations becomes a curvilinear inverted-U. As in the case of patents, we observe that the benefits of open innovation increase as IT investments grow. However, IT investments are less efective in increasing product innovations when the levels of openness are high.

Overall, our analyses share some elements, but also present some diferences, with previous research. First, they confirm the role of IT investments in innovation, contributing to a nascent research (Kleis et al. 2012, p. 43) on the topic. Our study contributes to the literature by showing the impact of these investments on innovation performance. The focus on innovation performance is similar to the approach followed by Kleis et al. (2012) and Joshi et al. (2010), which observe the efects of IT on product innovation and/or patents. As in Joshi et al. (2010), we focus on two measures of innovation performance, similar to their ideated and commercialized innovation. At the same time, this approach is diferent from that taken by Bardhan et al. (2013) in which the authors focus on firm-level performance measures (Tobin’s Q). Another diference from these papers is that we do not restrict our sample to large firms or to firms performing R&D investments or obtaining patents.

Second, our study characterizes the influence of IT investments as moderation. Although previous research has studied the consequences of IT for innovation, they study a direct efect (Joshi et al. 2010); they do not find a significant interaction between IT and R&D investments (Kleis et al. 2012) or they do not directly observe the product of innovation activities (Bardhan et al. 2013). This is important for a complete understanding of the impact of IT on innovation and, ultimately, on firm performance. Our results are consistent with the hypothesis that, to improve innovation performance, firms should simultaneously invest in both types of assets.

Third, this paper is diferent from all previous research in terms of the focus on open innovation strategies. Our arguments and findings are consistent with the idea that IT investments act as a catalyst for the absorption of external R&D investments. This contributes to our understanding of the role of IT investments and to the delimitation of the situations in which open innovation improves innovation performance. Our results show that, to some extent, IT investments allow the firm to take advantage of open innovation strategies by reducing their costs. As such, our findings contribute to the literature on innovation that attempts to determine the degree to which firms should rely on external knowledge (Laursen and Salter 2006, Grimpe and Kaiser 2010, Berchicci 2013).

Our paper also contributes to the literature on innovation. First, judging by the number of significant variables, we are more efective in discriminating between innovating and non-innovating firms than in determining the number of innovations. Assuming that the number of innovations is related to competitive advantage,<sup>19</sup> the explanation of firm success seems to be elusive. Second, investments in R&D are an essential part of the innovation activity, although there are some diferences between patents and product innovations. In the case of patents, the stock of R&D, the extent of openness, and its interaction with IT investments are important in explaining the number of patents issued. This is consistent with seminal studies that find a clear link between R&D investments and the number of patents (Bound et al. 1984, Pakes and Griliches 1984). However, R&D investments show a weaker relationship with the number of product innovations.<sup>20</sup> These results suggest diferences in the innovationgenerating process that may be worth studying. They also seem consistent with the idea that there is a weak link between patent intensity and product innovation (Devinney 1993). The positive interaction between R&D and IT in the product innovation equations could suggest that R&D is efective in generating product innovations with the help of IT.

One explanation for these diferences is that patents are closer to the concept of invention, whereas product innovations are more related to the idea of innovation (Hagedoorn and Cloodt 2003, Artz et al. 2010). Although it has been found that patents and product announcements are related (Artz et al. 2010), there could also be some variety among firms in their production.<sup>21</sup> First, not all of the patents become viable new products or services (Kleinknecht et al. 2002). Some of them are used in negotiations, to preempt competitors in certain research areas or for other purposes (see Cohen et al. 2000). Second, not all of the product innovations are protected by patents. In fact, the use of alternative appropriability mechanisms, such as secrecy or lead time advantages, is widely reported (Cohen et al. 2000).<sup>22</sup>

Although we do not estimate the efect of IT investments on firm performance or competitive advantage, our results also have implications for the analysis of this relationship (see Mithas et al. 2016 and Mithas and Rust 2016 for recent studies). IT investments enable knowledge capabilities to improve innovation and firm performance (Joshi et al. 2010). Our results suggest one of the channels through which IT investments may improve firm performance. By reducing the costs of open innovation strategies, they help produce patents and product innovations. The evidence on the performance implications of patents is less clear, but empirical research suggests a positive efect of innovation on firm performance when product innovation is considered (Artz et al. 2010, Banbury and Mitchell 1995).

More generally, our results support the idea that complementary assets are needed to exploit IT investments. Research on the efects of IT investments on performance has argued that it can only lead the firm to a situation of competitive parity (Clemons and Kimbrough 1986 and Clemons and Row 1991), as the technology is available in the market (Fuentelsaz et al. 2012). One of the challenges to this hypothesis is the idea that IT can be combined with other resources to create competitive advantage. These resources may be of diferent natures, such as marketing, human or

R&D investments. The resulting combinations may be a source of competitive advantage if they are inimitable (Maritan and Peteraf 2011). This idea is consistent with the empirical evidence that firms investing in R&D are more likely to adopt new technologies (Gómez and Vargas 2012).

This paper also has implications for managers. First, IT investments have an important role in explaining the production of innovations: This must be taken into account when designing innovation processes. Our results show that their role as an R&D-enabling factor is important. Second, this role as an enabler of innovation is especially important in a context of open innovation. This is because open innovation strategies involve costs that are reduced by IT investments. Even if these investments did not have a direct efect on innovation outcomes, they would help the firm to acquire external knowledge resources.

This paper has some limitations that should be noted. First, we have selected two of the possible measures of innovation performance. However, there are alternatives, such as patent citations or sales of innovative products (Kleinknecht et al. 2002) that were not available in our case. Although a strong overlap between these indicators has been found (Hagedoorn and Cloodt 2003), these measures may diverge in some cases (Kleinknecht et al. 2002). Second, we have not used information on the role of particular technologies; thus, we have not examined their diferent potential interaction efects with R&D. One distinction could be between information technologies used inside the firm and information technologies used for interorganizational communication (Powell and Dent-Micallef 1998, Dedrick and Kraemer 2010). It seems sensible to expect that the role of diferent technologies could vary (see, for example, Joshi et al. 2010) depending on their main function (design, information storage, communication, etc.). Third, our paper does not clarify the role of IT in cases in which innovations are obtained without R&D (Barge-Gil et al. 2011). In these cases, our paper does not ofer a clear indication about the type of efect that IT could have on the generation of innovations. Finally, we have only considered the efect of IT on open innovation costs: IT may also afect the benefits obtained by the firm. Future research could explore these issues.
