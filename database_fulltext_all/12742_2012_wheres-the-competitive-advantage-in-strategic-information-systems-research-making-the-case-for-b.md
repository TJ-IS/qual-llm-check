# Where’s the competitive advantage in strategic information systems research? Making the case for boundary-spanning research based on the German business and information systems engineering tradition

Hans Ulrich Buhl <sup>a</sup>, Gilbert Fridgen <sup>a</sup>, Wolfgang König <sup>b</sup>, Maximilian Röglinger <sup>a,</sup>⇑, Christian Wagner <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> FIM Research Center Finance & Information Management, University of Augsburg, Augsburg, Germany <sup>b</sup> House of Finance, Goethe University Frankfurt, Frankfurt, Germany <sup>c</sup> City University Hong Kong, Kowloon Tong, Hong Kong

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Available online 16 June 2012

Keywords: Strategic information systems research Boundary-spanning Competitive advantage Industry Business and information systems engineering

## a b s t r a c t

During the last decades, strategic information systems (SIS) research has become an influential stream within the information systems discipline. The success story of the Journal of Strategic Information Systems provides strong evidence. Yet, we believe that there is still a lot of untapped potential in the interaction of SIS research and industry. Put bluntly, it is impossible that results of SIS research are publicly available, reconstructable by subject matter experts, and valid beyond the single or very few cases and at the same time constitute the foundation of competitive advantage. We argue that SIS researchers need to become boundary spanners who actively engage in industry collaboration to help create competitive advantage and who disseminate their insights later on to advance the scientific knowledge base. We outline challenges of boundary-spanning SIS research and provide some ideas and recommendations. Wherever sensible, we draw on our experiences from the traditionally strong industry collaboration of the business and information systems engineering community from the German-speaking countries.

## 1. A critical view on strategic information systems research

Over the last decades, strategic information systems (SIS) research has evolved into an influential stream within the information systems (IS) discipline. The success story of its primary publication outlet, the Journal of Strategic Information Systems (JSIS), provides strong evidence. JSIS regularly receives a ‘‘burgeoning number of papers’’ from an international community of authors (Galliers and Jarvenpaa, 2011). The facts that the journal belongs to the AIS Senior Scholars’ ‘‘basket’’ and features an impact factor of 2.900 are indicative of the standing of JSIS and SIS research within academia. They also indicate that the theoretical advancement of SIS research has increased considerably over recent years. All this suggests that the SIS research community has done very well in advancing the scientific knowledge base of IS for strategic decision making, strategic use of IS, and strategies for IS issues (Cavaye and Cragg, 1993; Gable, 2010; Wilkes, 1991). So the advent of JSIS’ 20th anniversary may be a good time to sit back, enjoy, and continue in the direction that made SIS research successful. But wait! Should the achievement of high theoretical impact and the prospect of further increase really satisfy the SIS research community? Should not we also ask whether results of SIS research actually enable those in industry who have the authority to shape strategy and organizations to create and sustain IS-supported competitive advantage?

Put bluntly, results of SIS research do not have the potential to create competitive advantage. They lose their impact upon publication. They may at best inform managerial decision makers on SIS topics. This paradox is rooted in the characteristic differences of strategy and scientific research. While there are indeed broader interpretations of IS strategy (Galliers, 1993), following the interpretation of Porter (1996) and Barney (1991). Strategy is inextricably linked with entrepreneurial action and competitive advantage. This refers to creating and sustaining a unique mix of customer value that is not simultaneously being implemented by any current or potential player, thereby enabling a company to outperform others in the same industry or market. Competitive advantage is based on information asymmetry between the very few who possess it and the many who do not. Scientific research, in contrast, is characterized by an inter-subjectively verifiable elicitation, systematic documentation, and public dissemination of general as well as quality-assured insights. Hence, results of SIS research cannot constitute the foundation of a unique value mix inimitable even for potential competitors and at the same time be publicly available, reconstructable by subject matter experts, and valid beyond the single or very few cases – no matter in line with which research paradigm and research method they were developed.

On the one hand, one might argue that making decisions on SIS topics, solving SIS business problems, and creating IS-supported competitive advantage is the executives’ cup of tea only. In this first case, it would be legitimate for SIS researchers to focus on advancing SIS theories and to interact with industry for data collection purposes solely. We would then also rely on executives identifying appropriate SIS research results and managing to capitalize them on their own.

On the other hand, who if not the members of a research community that consecrated itself to topics of strategy should make the results of scientific research understandable for industry and thereby ensure that IS research eventually provides ‘‘the business and social benefits that we seek’’ (Galliers, 1991). In this second case, SIS researchers would not only inform, but also become engaged in solving SIS business problems and directly help companies gain IS-supported competitive advantage. SIS researchers would also engage in generalizing, validating, and disseminating novel insights gained through out the solution of SIS business problems to advance the scientific knowledge base.

In our perception, it is the first case that applies to the majority of SIS researchers worldwide – deliberately chosen or not. It is our firm conviction that this is not enough! Moreover, making use of the scientific knowledge base cannot be left to industry. Advancing theory should neither be the only nor the most important raison d’être of SIS researchers. Feeling responsible for advancing companies and becoming facilitators of IS-supported competitive advantages have to be additional imperatives.

In order to achieve this, we need more individuals capable of boundary-spanning. Only boundary spanners can bridge between multiple worlds (Aldrich and Herker, 1977; Carlile, 2002; Tushman and Scanlan, 1981). For many reasons, these individuals will be mainly SIS researchers. Foremost among the reasons is the motivation of researchers to write research papers with a substantial chance to be accepted in academic journals. In an ideal-typical world, IS practitioners may become co-researchers as assumed in participatory action research (Baskerville, 1999). Though, due to the criticality of SIS topics, the probability that SIS practitioners become co-researchers seems to be rather low – even in an ideal-typical world. As boundary-spanning SIS researchers mediate between academia and industry, they face considerable challenges.

In the remainder of this opinion piece, we first elaborate on the challenges of boundary-spanning SIS research (Section 2). We then share some of our insights into the business and information systems engineering (BISE) community from the German-speaking countries (Section 3). The strong industry connections of this community may inspire boundary-spanning SIS research – at least as far as engagement in the solution of business problems is concerned. The insights may also help the IS discipline at large become aware of its internal diversity. Against this backdrop, we sketch some ideas and recommendations on the identified challenges and on what role JSIS may play (Section 4). The paper ends with concluding remarks regarding the consequences for individual researchers, the SIS research community, and JSIS (Section 5).

For increased force of argument, we avoid reiterating well-known debates (e.g. rigor vs. relevance, behavioral vs. design science research) throughout the paper. Moreover, we often abstain from softening expressions that would befit the complex collection of shades of gray when discussing strategic information systems.

## 2. Challenges of boundary-spanning strategic information systems research

The following challenges of boundary-spanning SIS research do not pretend to constitute a full list, but they are the ones we believe to be the most important.

## 2.1. Making SIS researchers ready for industry projects

SIS researchers need to be prepared for expectations and rules governing managerial behavior to differ from the academic world. For example, creating and sustaining competitive advantage requires unique or first-of-a-kind solutions. Such solutions neither have to be perfect nor meet the highest academic standards, but good enough to perform activities differently than one’s rivals do (Harrison and Pelletier, 1997). In contrast to scientific research, the fact that a particular problem is solved typically outvalues the question of how a class of problems can be solved. Moreover, managerial practice in some ways turns the scientific model on its head, seemingly treating success as an independent variable and managing all other aspects accordingly. Since good managers ‘‘make their own luck’’ (Collins, 2001; Tichy and Ulrich, 1984), factors that in typical social science research are accepted to be random are not necessarily random to a success-oriented executive.

## 2.2. Competing against consultancies and biased expectations

Researchers who intend to engage in the solution of SIS business problems compete against consultancies and in-house business development departments whose job is to create and sustain competitive advantage. Companies keep large numbers of consultancies and in-house business development departments busy as compared to the SIS research community, which is quite small. Practitioners also have biased expectations regarding researchers (Hirschheim and Klein, 2003). SIS researchers thus have to make clear that they can contribute something of value that is not already available in practice or cannot be more effectively and efficiently created otherwise.

## 2.3. Accessing high-quality primary data

Why should executives make strategically relevant information available for publication and void a valuable advantage? In a purely competitive world, the appropriate action appears to be withholding or delaying of information to minimize its value to others. Not surprisingly, corporations frequently guard their competitive information. Rarely does one hear a researcher expressing ease about accessing primary data from SIS projects. Cusumano and Selby (1998), in their book ‘‘Microsoft Secrets’’, state ‘‘we cannot overemphasize the unique opportunity we had to probe broadly and freely’’, and refer to ‘‘unusually candid interviews’’ with company informants, including Bill Gates.

## 2.4. Competing against large sample sizes

Even if one knew the right informants and were granted access to strategically relevant information, revealed knowledge may not stand up to scientific scrutiny. After all, academia strongly insists on validity (Benbasat and Zmud, 1999), so much that type I errors (i.e., accepting incorrect and/or invaluable research) are much abhorred even at the expense of type II errors (i.e., rejecting correct and valuable research). Insights gained during the solution of SIS business problems usually have a very small sample size – oftentimes only one. Small case bases, however, invite questions about research rigor, generalizability, and validity (Benbasat et al., 1987; Lee, 1989; Yin, 2009). Relying on the personal accounts of a small number of informants who potentially over-attribute success to a single cause, may lead to causal ambiguity (Rosenzweig, 2007). Both are hindrances of publishing in highly-ranked academic outlets.

## 2.5. Short half-life of competitive information

If submissions based on the insights from solving SIS business problems are subject to overly lengthy review processes, research results may not only have lost the power to create competitive advantage. Even informing managerial decision makers on SIS topics may turn out to be difficult. Multi-round review processes, where each round may take up to 1 year, do not match the pace of a fast moving domain like ours. While being current practice in academia, such reviewing practices are harmful for SIS research. For the same reason, little may be learned from personal accounts of historic strategies, which for instance formed the basis for Chandler’s (1962) groundbreaking work. Comparable to a patent expiring, novel insights from solving SIS business problems should be disseminated immediately after first-mover advantages, criticality, or uniqueness have vanished.

## 3. Some experiences from the BISE community

Below we share some experiences from the BISE community, which is characterized by its ability to fully engage with industry. It comprises all researchers from the German-speaking countries who deal with information systems as their object of research, and bears similarities to other communities from Europe and Australasia (Frank et al., 2008, Pastor in Loos et al., 2010; Winter, 2008). A more elaborate discussion of the BISE community can be found in Buhl et al. (2012).

During the BiSE community's evolution, the importance of intensive industry collaboration has been reinforced repeatedly. It now constitutes an integral part of the community’s self-conception. For example, Mertens (2011), one of the com munity's founders, requests researchers to prove themselves in a decathlon of obiectives. Almost half of the obiectives require boundary-spanning between academia and industry. Scheer (2009), another co-founder, calls upon BISE researchers to link science with entrepreneurship. Even the community's scientific profile emphasizes the creation of utility for industry as a primary objective (Wissenschaftliche Kommission Wirtschaftsinformatik, 2011). Experiences from the BISE community may thus inspire boundary-spanning SIS research regarding strengths and weaknesses of direct interaction with industry. They also increase the awareness for the IS community’s internal diversity.

According to our experience, the BISE community’s strong industry connections are rooted in the fact that researchers have been investing in building up win–win relationships built on trust for the last 50 years. They did not try to be missionaries preaching theoretical results, but listened to what the market demanded and offered corresponding services. This does not only refer to the solution of business problems, but also to education and dissemination of knowledge.

Almost all BISE researchers participate in industry projects according to the engineering tradition in the German-speaking countries. Some researchers even operate ‘‘academic consultancies’’. To name the most prominent examples from the past: IDS Scheer, founded in 1984, evolved into the global market leader of business process management solutions and was sold for more than 900 million euros to Software AG in 2009. The Information Management Group was founded by Hubert Österle in 1989 and sold for a 9-digit amount in euros with about 650 employees at 27 branch offices in 2007. Other professors support former research assistants in the foundation of spin-offs based on the ideas from their PhD theses. Overall, approximately 44 % of the research assistants are funded by industry (Frank et al., 2008). The financial success of the academic consultancies and the high degree of private funding indicate that industry appreciates support from the BISE community. Private funding enables BISE researchers to expand their chairs. Some ‘‘scientific think tanks’’ employ more than 20–30 research assistants. Due to their size, they are capable of conducting multiple projects simultaneously and to subsidize topics with future relevance for industry.

BISE degree and doctoral programs as well as post-doctoral qualification are linked with industry. Companies often become involved financially and by means of additional courses in degree programs. They also participate in curriculum design. BISE researchers typically offer courses that reflect their research interests and project experience. BISE degree programs thus attract numerous students and graduates have excellent job prospects. In Germany, for instance, annual BISE enrollments doubled from 2000 to 2010. Moreover, 42 % of the almost 350 companies that participated in a survey by the Staufenbiel Institute are specifically looking for BISE graduates (Giesen, 2008, 2009). BISE doctoral students usually participate in industry projects during their doctoral studies. Many graduates deliberately decide on the option to complete a doctorate before going into practice because many BISE doctoral programs offer the possibility to improve hard and soft skills and to gain practical experience. As a fact, the German-speaking countries feature a much higher percentage of doctorates in top management or top consulting positions than, for example, the U. S. (Jochmann, 2009; Schild and Herrendorf, 2008). The positive side effect for the BISE community is that many managers and consultants are acquainted with academic and industrial settings and are usually interested in collaborating with their former colleagues and supervisors. Post-doctoral researchers interact with industry to establish their own networks ‘‘keeping them employable’’ in case of denial of tenure. In the German-speaking countries, getting tenured after having gained industry experience is typical for engineering disciplines – it holds true for about 80 % of the respective professors (Mertens, 2011).

Regarding the dissemination of knowledge, the journal WIRTSCHAFTSINFORMATIK has been the central publication for academia and industry for more than 50 years. To foster strong industry connections even within an academic journal, the editorial board and review panels include industry experts. Nevertheless, papers became cumbersome to read for practitioners as the journal increased its rigor standards. To maintain industry as readership, the editorial board initiated a strategic realignment in 2009. The English-language BISE journal was inaugurated as a cover-to-cover translation of the Germanlanguage WIRTSCHAFTSINFORMATIK. In addition, a partner outlet called Wirtschaftsinformatik & Management was launched, which is particularly geared to German-speaking BISE practitioners. It contains much of the practitioner-oriented content as well as management summaries of selected research articles. This strategic realignment would not have been possible without the close connections to industry. The editors raised more than 700,000 euros in industry funding. Apparently, industry appreciates this kind of knowledge transfer as Wirtschaftsinformatik & Management was awarded best newcomer by ‘‘German Business Media’’ in 2010 representing the interests of 420 affiliated publishers with 3800 titles.

Listening to what industry demands and offering corresponding services earned the BISE community high reputation and led to measurable success. There are some severe drawbacks as well. Some say the BISE community follows research that borders perilously on consulting, attaches itself to technological fads, and lacks a long-term research agenda. Another common critique is its substandard output of contributions to theory (Lyytinen et al., 2007). In our opinion, there is much to be learned from the BISE community with respect to engagement with industry. Admittedly, there is not so much to be learned in completing the circle back to the scientific knowledge base.

## 4. Ideas and recommendations

To address the challenges of boundary-spanning SIS research, we now sketch some ideas and recommendations. Wherever appropriate, we provide suggestions on what role JSIS may play and what can be learned from the BISE community.

## 4.1. Be in the thick of it!

The ability to cross the chasm between practice and academia requires unique skills and resources. We have already discussed the different paradigms of thought and action existing between practice and academia. What makes crossing the chasm so difficult is that under each paradigm there exists ‘‘truth’’ that cannot be understood under the other paradigm, as has been explained through Gödel’s incompleteness theorem in different contexts (Gödel, 1962). Preparing individuals consequently requires not just ‘‘industry contact’’, but immersion into industrial practice and strategic decision making (Klein and Rowe, 2008). Despite its weaknesses, the BISE model of scholarship appears to be a suitable role model for this challenge. As described before, it allows practitioners to return to academia even after some time of industry practice.

Doctoral studies often include long-term industry projects. Industry even engages in degree programs to ensure a relevant skill set for future employees. With that preparation, a researcher is not an outside observer who interprets industrial practices within the set of axioms that hold in academia, but someone who can switch (or span) to a different set of axioms and thus reveal truths that might otherwise be overlooked.

## 4.2. Know your strengths!

According to our experience, SIS researchers have other value propositions than consultancies and in-house business development departments. They definitely have the potential to contribute to the solution of SIS business problems. First and foremost, SIS researchers can exploit the scientific knowledge base to substitute gut feelings for well-founded decisions and solutions. Thus, they help companies make use of technology (Porter, 2001) and develop IS capabilities (Peppard and Ward, 2004) based on SIS research results. We must not forget that the scientific knowledge base also provides knowledge about SIS topics gained from research endeavors without direct industry involvement as well as non-SIS knowledge added b researchers from other IS sub-disciplines or different disciplines. When acting as ‘‘engaged scholars’’ or sometimes even as ‘‘academic consultants’’, SIS researchers enjoy at least three additional value propositions, namely a lack of bias a systematic approach, and conceptual capabilities. Lack of bias helps complement the executives’ worldview that may be clouded by routine blindness and influences not directly related to the decision or problem ahead. A systematic approach enables results to be traced and an ability to with the complexity of intricate problem settings. Conceptual capabilities help researchers develop explanatory models and decision models as well as (blueprints for) decision support systems, which in turn help executives prepare strategic decisions and control their effects.

## 4.3. Establish data sharing repositories!

There are some reasons why executives share strategically relevant information. Some are collaborative and partly informed by research about open innovation and open sourcing (Newstead and Lanzerotti, 2010); others are competitive. The collaborative motives include (a) sharing knowledge to get back from others in return (Bliege Bird and Smith, 2005), (b) participating in the creation of strategies for higher-level economic entities such as industries (Chandler, 1962), and (c) testing strategy against public scrutiny (Cusumano and Selby, 1998). Among the competitive motives are (d) making similar competitor strategies obsolete (Wind, 1997), (e) changing the nature of competition from strategy to dynamic capabilities and execution (Harreld et al., 2007), (f) heightening the need for quick implementation and new strategic direction – since everyone else will soon know (Bhide, 1999), or (g) announcing a strategy as a form of ‘‘costly signaling’’ (Bliege Bird and Smith, 2005) to dis-incentivize others from pursuing the same (Wind, 1997). Despite the many reasons, executives run the risk of decreasing their company’s competitive advantage or being held accountable for disclosing current proprietary information.

To overcome the limited availability of high-quality primary data, the SIS community may strive for inaugurating a ‘‘jointventure on strategic information systems’’ where practitioners and researchers collaborate to make primary data available. Primary data, ideally collected at the very time of creation, would be anonymized and placed under the curatorship of a con: sortium, e.g., associated to JSIS or its editorial board. Data would not be immediately broadly available to the researchers, but only after a pre-determined time period, so as to reduce its criticality. In fact, the period of delay could be defined conservatively long in the beginning and then subsequently shortened as participants (especially key contributors) become comfortable with sharing knowledge and the corresponding risks and rewards. Key informants, however, might have access to the information right away. With the contribution of multiple informants and the insight provided by researchers, a highly informative data set would be available in raw and interpreted form to informants and researchers alike, who could subsequently share it with an even broader public. This approach also provides differentiated access to data of different quality (recency) based on contribution, rewarding those who share, while creating residual benefits for the community at large.

## 4.4. Establish collaborative networks of trust!

Intensive industry collaboration builds upon networks of trust. Such networks only work if they are grounded on sustainable and long-term win–win relationships among its members. According to basic economic principles, companies must gain value added from their activities. Collecting and analyzing data as outside observers is not satisfying! Researchers should extend existing relationships, but they have to advance their engagement in the solution of SIS business problems and thus help companies crate and sustain IS-supported competitive advantage, In most cases, this will include academic advisory services – at least in the beginning of a relationship. Successively gaining reputation, however, opens up new opportunities: the greater the reputation, the greater the trust and freedom. According to our experience, it is easier to convince practitioners of using academic standards if they know that you truly understand their business and are capable of providing value added. In this respect, there is much to be learned from the BISE community as it has invested in its industry network over the last 50 years. As already noted industry also plays an important role in education and curricula design.

## 4.5. Foster a broad portfolio of complementary research paper models!

If we seek to explain successes such as Google or Facebook, ‘‘traditional’’ research approaches may not be appropriate. Work that is time-critical and investigates company-individual strategic impact must be allowed to rely on research meth ods that potentially are less robust, but produce results quickly. Researchers may be tasked with investigating a single case only, using a standardized framework and reporting results along a pre-established reporting framework. The single case may have limited explanatory power, but high relevance while also becoming a resource for subsequent meta-analyses.

As we stated in the introduction, the article model currently adopted by JSIS has resulted in theoretical advancement as well as high journal visibility. Thus, it does not need to be replaced, yet it can be complemented. In essence, we suggest introducing – and actively promoting – different research paper models competing for journal space along several dimensions (Straub, 2008). ‘‘Traditional’’ articles with strong methodology and strong data would compete for most space, while articles that are data-strong, but theory-light, or articles that are theory-strong, yet evidence-light, would have a lesser competitive position. Such a portfolio approach could be implemented through different sections (e.g., research results vs. structured case descriptions). ’’Breakthrough’’ articles might be awarded a small amount of space, valuing their uniqueness and informative value, but discounting the lack of theory and evidence. Articles should draw from a wide range of research methods including for instance those methods related to both the natural science and the sciences of the artificial paradigm. In addition to research papers, JSIS should continue the tradition of publishing viewpoint articles and special issues because they play an important role in setting new directions and agendas (e.g., Leidner, 2010; Swanson, 2010; von Krogh and Haefliger, 2010; Walsham, 2005; Wastell and White, 2010).

## 4.6. Rethink the reviewing and feedback processes!

Platforms like AIS Sprouts seem to be promising initiatives to account for the necessity of fast feedback and publishing. As a first step, papers can be made available on the Internet without penalty. Boundary-spanning SIS researchers – and occasionally even practitioners – can access the knowledge stored there. Such platforms also enable the easy measurement of article downloads, which might serve as a proxy for readership. With ‘‘electronic paper’’ there are few hindrances to interactivity, with provisions for ranking the quality of an article – maybe even from the view point of scholars and practitioners separately. Publishers, authors, and even tenure and promotion committees, should take note. Outstanding papers can eventually become reworked and published in the traditional way (then the publications are ‘‘blessed’’ by the editors).

Moreover, the complementary research article models outlined above require a different treatment than ‘‘traditional’’ research papers. They need evaluation criteria that put a stronger focus on relevance and in particular potential business impact. A complementing measure would be to extend editorial boards and review panels by qualified executives whose duty it is to assess the value of submissions for industry, thus comprising not only knowledge about top academic publication requirements, but also profound experience in the application of research results in practice. Such practices have already been implemented and proven useful by the BISE/WIRTSCHAFTSINFORMATIK journal.

The collective intelligence of the community could be employed to provide a much more differentiated measure of quality, thus steering authorship and editorial effort to subjects that are – in addition to minimum rigor requirements – more interesting to the (business part of the) community, and doing so in much less time than traditional publication (and thus citation) cycles demand. We believe that both SIS research and JSIS would profit from a development in this direction.

## 5. What’s next? No more than a strategic realignment!

The proposed recommendations represent a long-term strategic realignment of the SIS research community. They require us to be open to new approaches, not only as authors, reviewers, editors, and members of committees, but also as active stakeholders in our economies. For individuals, incorporating a strong engagement with industry is not possible overnight. Rather, this requires a long-term effort and support by the community. JSIS is in a unique position to positively influence this development and to increase the community’s ability to engage with industry. It is certainly able to support some recommendations, but not all. Nevertheless, following the adage, that for a market leader to maintain the leadership position, it is necessary to attack oneself (Ries and Trout, 1996), thereby preempting attacks by competitors.
