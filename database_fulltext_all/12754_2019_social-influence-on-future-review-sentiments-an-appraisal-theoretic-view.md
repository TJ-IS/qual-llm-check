# Social Influence on Future Review Sentiments: An Appraisal-Theoretic View

ASHISH KUMAR JHA AND SNEHAL SHAH

ASHISH KUMAR JHA (ashish-kumar.jha@rennes-sb.com) is an associate professor of Information Systems at Rennes School of Business, France. His research interests lie in business analytics, Big Data, and technology innovation in IT firms. He has published in such journals as Information & Management, International Journal of Production Economics, and Computers in Human Behavior, among others. Dr. Jha has also presented papers at the top conferences, such as International Conference on Information Systems, Decision Sciences Institute annual meeting, and others. He is an associate editor for Journal of Organizational Computing and Electronic Commerce.

SNEHAL SHAH (Snehal.shah@spjimr.org) is a professor of Organization Behavior, chairperson of the Fellow Program, and head of research at SP Jain Institute of Management & Research, India. Her primary research interest is diversity and inclusion, with a specific focus on corporate governance and career choices. ABSTRACT: Given the importance of online reviews, as evidenced by extant research, we studied an understudied area of the impact of past reviews on sentiments of future reviews. Past studies in the emergent area of incidence of reviews have investigated the generation of ratings but there seems to be a lacuna in the second dimension of the reviews (i.e., the textual sentiments). Through a controlled experiment (N = 232), we analyzed the impact of past reviews on reviewers’ subsequent texts by using the unique lens of social appraisal theory. We found the influence of the selection of reviews on most e-commerce websites could strongly bias subsequent written review sentiments, and this effect is more pronounced when the reviewer experienced higher disconfirmation. We also observed review writers tend to post extreme reviews in the absence of any benchmark or prior reviews. The study results extend the understanding of users’ reviews under social influence and enhance the appraisal theoretic understanding of review generation.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: social appraisal theory, sentiment analysis, online reviews, controlled experiment, e-WoM, user reviews.

## Introduction

Consumers want to make informed purchase decisions and are driven to collect information about products and services and share it with others. This word-ofmouth (WoM) transmission is described as “informal communication directed at other consumers about the ownership, usage and characteristics of particular goods and services or their sellers” [68, p. 261] and has long been considered an important factor affecting product sales and predates the advent of online shopping and reviewsharing websites [55]. WoM communication is known to generate over 3.3 billion brand impressions every day [35] and to influence a broad range of activities from what movies consumers watch to how product developers respond [11, 72, 47].

With digital technology flourishing, much of the social conversation has shifted online. Early papers in this domain suggested 7 percent of WoM had become digital [34], and since the 2000s, this number has grown exponentially. For example, online shoppers in the United States spent \$1,611 per person in 2014, a 17 percent annual increase over the previous year [26].

The expansion in multiple online commercial platforms and sharing of views has led to increased research on the e-WoM phenomenon [17]. Consumers use online product reviews to complement their understanding of the product/service being offered, making it an additional word-of-mouth feedback medium [11, 73], which has led most e-commerce websites to develop an online community feedback or review system that encourages product or service users to share their experiences. When they visit the product page to write the reviews, consumers on most e-commerce websites can usually view a selection of the most popular reviews, most recent reviews, or most positive reviews. Amazon, for instance, typically shows most popular reviews on the product review page soliciting reviews. Figure 1 shows a snapshot of product reviews shown while soliciting a review for a product.

## Influence of e-WoM

Interpersonal communication, whether through word of mouth or through digital media, contributes from 20 percent to 50 percent of all purchasing decisions and “generates more than twice the sales of paid advertising” [6, p. 8]. Although the linkage between such communication and purchase decisions is relatively well-established, not much is known about the behavioral processes that may influence this relationship [4]. For instance, why are individuals motivated to share information? Is it due to a self-serving bias, or is altruism at play? Does this influence their self-image? What are some of the emotions generated through oral or written accounts of product and service experiences? Because much of the behavioral antecedents and implications still need exploration, this has led some researchers to declare WoM as “the world’s most effective yet least understood marketing strategy” [44].

Adding to this complexity is WoM’s peculiar characteristics on digital platforms: e-WoM communications tend to be in written form, lack face-to-face interaction, have a larger audience, accord greater privacy to the individuals than traditional

WoM, foster weak social ties with the online community, and do not afford using social cues such as gestures, physical body language, or tone and intonations of language [4]. Given these considerations, on one level, this study explores the utility of e-WoM as an emotional regulation mechanism based on one’s own experience. Emotional regulation in this context refers to ways by which individuals manage which emotions to pay attention to, and how they experience and express them [25]. On another level, this study investigates a simultaneous occurrence: the communal aspect of e-WoM serving as a coping mechanism in various situations [41], which proposes sharing with others can serve as an important channel to effectively manage emotions [54].

By focusing on individuals’ interpretations of others’ views and the emotions that arise from it, this paper examines more deeply the multiple facets of what drives individuals’ decision making in the e-WoM domain. More specifically, the linkage between one’s own experience and others’ experiences as reflected through their online reviews and subsequent review-writing behavior is explored at different levels, including (a) the role of emotions generated through the stimulus of one’s own product experience and others’ past reviews on the subsequent review decision; (b) the moderating effect of product experience and past reviews as stimuli on writer’s choice to write the review; and (c) the nuanced effect of an expectancyexperience mismatch on review-writing behavior.

## Psychology of Sentiments

Research on emotions has remained the mainstay of the field of modern psychology, and this study uses the taxonomy developed by Fischer et al. [22] to operationalize the measurement of emotions in the context of online reviews and to align it with the current literature in the field of information systems. According to Fischer et al. [22], emotions comprise three distinct components: superordinate, basic, and prototypical scripts. The first category, superordinate, is of prime importance to this research because it involves an appraisal of events as positive or negative in a way that either hinders or advances an individual’s desired goals, and this paper aims to study reactions elicited from an individual’s own interpretation of positively or negatively written online product reviews. These reactions are experienced and interpreted by the individual and are referred to as sentiments.

Although research across diverse domains has used emotions and sentiments interchangeably, we want to elucidate the nuanced differences between the two while connecting to the superordinate category outlined by Fischer et al. [22]. Munezero et al. [49] defined sentiment as a “more or less enduring predisposition in a personality to respond with a positive or negative affect to specific object or entity” (p. 3). Sentiments are formed over time due to exposure to and frequency of an experience with the object or event; emotions are evoked when individuals think of the object or related association [49]. In terms of duration, sentiments are longer lasting, stable, and more dispositional in nature than emotions; in contrast, emotions are episodic reactions triggered by the brain as an autonomic response or a response to behavioral changes [31]. Furthermore, sentiments are typically directed towards an object, but emotions are not always associated with the object [58]. Because this study is interested in uncovering the influence of individuals’ long-term dispositions toward the evaluation of online purchase decisions of goods and services, sentiments (i.e., positive and negative dispositions underlying emotive expressions) will be used as the fundamental building block to understand this behavior.

A dominant view in research on online reviews implicitly assumes reviews are dependent only on the users’ personal experiences. This very strong supposition questions multiple traits psychologists have established about human biases [22] and has led to some recent works analyzing the implications of relaxing this assumption. Moe and Schweidel [45], Sridhar and Srinivasan [63], and Ho et al. [29] are some of the recent major works attempting to study the incidence of online opinions, but most of these studies have two major limitations. First, they deal with ratings, a categorical evaluation of the product/service, and inform us little about the information-rich text reviews. Second, the analysis of product ratings is based on analytical models tested with secondary data. Such a methodological approach makes for a good first step in establishing possible relationships, but the nuances of multiple factors that interact to determine review sentiments cannot be fully uncovered through these methods.

## Research Goals

By exploring the sentiments generated from the textual analysis of the written reviews and their interactions with the product experience, this paper attempts to fill the gap in the current information management literature with multi-fold contributions. First, it presents a unique dependent variable: subsequent review sentiments. Extant literature has looked primarily at other variables, such as review-writing probability and ratings of the review, but not necessarily post-review-writing behavior. Second, it teases out the independent effects of one’s own product experience and assessments of others’ sentiments as interpreted by the review writer in an experimental setting. Third, it measures the moderating effect or the interplay of product experience and appraisal of past sentiments. And, finally, this paper adopts the lens of social appraisal theory to offer an insight into the cognitive psycho-social mechanism that plays an important role in review writers’ decision making of what and how to write.

To achieve this, we brought together strands of literature from marketing, information systems, and organization behavior. We used Social Appraisal Theory [12] as the theoretical lens to understand the behavioral aspect of review writing. To examine the effects of past review stimuli (positive and negative sentiments) on subsequent reviewers’ review writing, we employed a controlled laboratory experiment setup. Finally, the robustness of the study was tested with a secondary analysis in an uncontrolled setup of the effect of past reviews on subsequent review sentiment on products reviewed on Amazon India’s e-commerce platform.

## Literature Review and Hypotheses

## Online reviews and e-WoM

Extensive research on the e-WoM phenomenon [17, 18] has been a result of the proliferation of online commercial platforms, of which there are two types. The first is the third-party platform upon which reviewers write their reviews for products/services they used (e.g., TripAdvisor). The second includes product pages either on the producer’s/service provider’s website or on an e-commerce website selling the product itself (e.g., Amazon). The difference between the two approaches is, for the first one, the online transaction does not take place on the platform; it is only used to share feedback/reviews. In the second case, the product pages, the transaction does occur on the same platform, and, hence, the potential impact of the review is larger [32]. In our study case, we used an experimental setup like a transaction platform because it provided for direct impact reviews, and because it was not on the producer’s website, the producer was prevented from directly manipulating it.

Extant research has established the impact of product ratings on reviewed product sales in such cases of experience products, like movies and books [20], and more utilitarian products [8, 13]. Li and Hitt [38] extended the research further to study the information content of the reviews and its efficacy in communicating the message. We summarized some of the major papers studying the impact of reviews and ratings in Appendix A. Prior research has also established the role that factors like credibility [10, 19], emotional content [5], and single sentiment primacy [48] have in enabling online reviews to have an effect on readers. This body of work draws from almost all contemporary management fields, such as behavioral sciences, marketing, information systems, and strategy, and has attempted to demystify the various factors influencing product ratings.

A thorough literature review indicated scholars’ growing interest in understanding rating behavior and the effect of past ratings. For instance, in a controlled experiment, Schlosser [60] found evidence of users adjusting the ratings they provided. In one of the most comprehensive studies on the incidence of online product opinions and backed by data from an e-commerce platform, Moe and Schweidel [45] found review writers adjust their ratings under different conditions. Sridhar and Srinivasan [63], in their work on reviews of hotel services on a thirdparty website, found evidence of social influence in writing reviews. More recently, Ho et al. [29] found the disconfirmation effect (i.e., the difference in the expectation and experience) is one major factor that leads to the user’s decision to post a rating, and the rating provided is a product of the disconfirmation the user experiences. A thorough examination of the literature summarized in Appendix A led us to conclude that although past research has attempted to analyze the factors affecting the decision to post ratings and what ratings to give, these factors have not been studied from the point of view of sentiments present in textual reviews. This present research is targeted at filling this void.

## Appraisal Theory

An important theoretical concept central to this paper is the appraisal theory. When faced with a real-world phenomenon, individuals tend to seek meaning, make inferences, and engage in an appraisal process to determine a suitable response. This appraisal, in turn, evokes certain emotions that influence outcomes.

Frijda’s [23, p. 349] research argued an organism’s innate need to make meaning or make inferences of observed phenomenon gives rise to emotions, and the fundamental principle of the appraisal theory is that a person’s appraisal is neither just a function of that individual’s personality traits nor only a function of the situational characteristics. Appraisal is a relational construct that reflects an individual’s evaluation of what the situation implies to the individual’s well-being in relation to the specific combination of the person’s needs, goals, resources, and abilities [56, 62]. Therefore, the appraisal process has essentially two important components: (1) an individual undertakes a “meaning” analysis to assess the situational implication to the individual’s well-being and (2) various evaluation mechanisms that evoke different emotions. Thus, if we understand an individual’s appraisal process, we can predict the type of elicited emotion and vice-versa [62].

The appraisal theory and its applicability in offline settings have been reported in multiple studies, but there are limited cases of its application in an online context. However, in the case of sentiment analysis, as applied here, an online context is the perfect ground to extend the theoretical lens to this domain.

An extensive literature search across domains to find a systematic framework to conduct sentiment analysis of online text led to the work of computer and linguistic scientists. Although models in linguistic research exist to capture opinions, judgements, and feelings [3], the most comprehensive treatment of sentiment analysis of text has been offered by Martin [42] and White [69]. According to White [69], an appraisal framework is the “language of attitude, arguability and interpersonal positioning” (p. 1). In other words, it is an approach that focuses on the semantics of the text rather than its form (grammar). This theory works for the concept of appraisal groups: these are a collection of words, sentences, or clauses that engage the appraiser and the appraisee. In an online review context, although a past history or previous connection does not exist between the two parties, it is the form (semantics) and the function (grammar) of the text—as outlined in linguistic sciences—that bind the appraiser and appraisee in a simulated environment that has neither a past nor the promise of a future.

We posit when the user has experienced a certain product or service, the decision of whether to write a review and what to write is preceded by a multi-step, nonlinear appraisal mechanism. The first step of this appraisal is to self-evaluate one’s own experience with the product or service. This could generate positive or negative sentiments that are likely to influence the writer’s decision making. Literature on social sharing of emotions suggests individuals share up to 90 percent of their emotional experiences [54, for a review]. Sharing provides an important channel to express what they feel, why they feel that way, and what to do about it. Sharing, it is argued, helps one better manage one’s own well-being by handling the emotions derived from a stimulus. Prior work in this area outlines some of the antecedents of an individual’s motivation to write a review of an experience of the product, including that sharing information facilitates emotional regulation in the form of (a) generating social support, (b) venting, (c) sensemaking, and (d) redu cing dissonance [4]. Hence, our baseline Hypothesis 1 is as follows:

Hypothesis 1: The product usage experience by a review writer significantly affects the writer’s sentiment of the subsequently written reviews.

This study goes a step further to argue it is not just the appraisal of the experience as positive or negative that prompts the individual to write a certain type of review. It is the intensity of the emotions, reflected in the sentiment of the texts, which may have a differential effect on subsequent reviews. Multiple experiments have shown there are more discussions when the emotional content in certain movies and newspaper articles is higher in intensity [5]. It is also known highly satisfied or highly dissatisfied customers are more likely to share information or provide feedback than those not in those extremes [1, 30]. Therefore, as appraisal theory postulates, individuals engage in evaluating the meaning of a stimulus with the primary purpose of achieving well-being; thus, we hypothesize there is a natural tendency for individuals to pay more attention to negative emotions arising from one’s product experience compared to an equal intensity of positive emotions from an experience. More specifically, Hypothesis 1a states:

## Hypothesis 1a: Negative product experience has a stronger effect than positive product experience on subsequently written reviews’ sentiment.

The appraisal theory previously outlined describes how an individual undertakes a cognitive process that assesses and makes meaning based on one’s own experience. However, we know human beings are heavily influenced by those around them and the context of their environment. Therefore, the second and equally important component of the appraisal process is how individuals are affected by what others write. Online reviews are written accounts of other users’ experiences, and therefore, the reader’s appraisal process is influenced by those others’ experiences, beliefs, and perceptions, which is also referred to as social appraisal.

Social appraisal refers to the human responses guided by the dominant group’s social behavior. Research in the domain of social appraisal has found humans typically conform to the dominant view in a social setting [12] and identify with certain social groups or act to be identified as a part of a majority. Fear of possibly being branded as an outlier is the main motivation for people adapting their views to conform to social norms. The central tenet of social appraisal, thus, is that an individual tends to weigh a response to a stimulus not from a personal perspective, but rather from the perspective of a standing in a social setting.

One important difference between an online and offline setting is the possibility of anonymity. The pressure to conform is assumed to be far lower in an online space because of the lack of any dominant group to identify with or the absence of the fear of being singled out [15, 50]. This is expected to lead to truer responses on online platforms compared to offline platforms where social influence was proven to be very strong [15]. However, as Sridhar and Srinivasan’s [63] and Schlosser’s [60] research on social influence on product ratings suggest, even in an online setting, social influence’s effects seem to drive reviews. Product ratings provide limited information of the dominant social consensus to the user, but they are expected to influence the behavior of subsequent reviewers in the social setting. Hence, we propose Hypothesis 2:

Hypothesis 2: Sentiments from past text reviews (social appraisal) as appraised by a review writer significantly affects the sentiment of the subsequently written reviews.

Staying within the domain of social appraisals, we move to the more-nuanced set of negative social effect. Extant literature has established that negative sentiments affect us more than positive sentiments [57]. This has also been found to be true in the e-WoM domain, in which negative sentiments are more pronounced in reviews than positive sentiments [29]. However, far more positive reviews are written on social platforms, as evidenced by the j-curve of product ratings [45], and it is the rare negative rating or negative review that has a stronger effect. Thus, for our social appraisal setup, we posit the negative appraisal would have a stronger effect than the positive appraisal (Hypothesis 2a).

Hypothesis 2a: Negative past review sentiment (negative social appraisal) has a stronger effect than the positive past review (positive social appraisal) on the subsequently written reviews’ sentiment.

Common wisdom and extant literature both indicate a user’s own product experience has a significant effect on the user’s review sentiment, which is a manifestation of their experience and other social factors. One of the social factors discussed in the preceding paragraph is the social appraisal or the effect of past review sentiments to which the review writer has been exposed. These two effects (i.e., the user’s own product experience and past review sentiments) together affect the eventual sentiment of subsequently written reviews because the effects do not occur in isolation; rather, they happen in concert with each other, and thus past ratings affect the user’s opinion of their product experience (as evidenced by user’s ratings) [29]. Similar research has also indicated the user’s evaluation of the product/service experience is affected by the social acceptance of the product or service [71].

Continuing our theorization of (negative) sentiments’ enhanced effect discussed in a previous subsection, we believe users with a positive product experience would be differentially affected by positive or negative past reviews, which would then influence the reviewer’s sentiments in a subsequent review. Reviewers with positive experiences would be likely pushed to moderate their sentiments considering social appraisal, in which social reception is negative, as evidenced by large negative past reviews’ sentiments. The subsequent review sentiment may be bolstered by conforming social cues in the form of positive past review sentiments. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3: Past review sentiments differentially moderate the impact of product experience on subsequently written reviews sentiment.

## Appraisal and Disconfirmation

Thus far, we have discussed the independent effects of one’s own product experience (Hypotheses 1 and 1a) and others’ evaluation of the product through a past review mechanism (Hypotheses 2 and 2a), which affect post-purchase review sentiments. Hypotheses 3 outlined the interaction effects of product experience and past review sentiments on subsequent reviews.

This present subsection describes the theory and formulation of hypotheses that address how the difference between one’s own product experience and expectations as appraised through others’ assessments influence review-writing outcomes. To explain the phenomenon, we draw upon theories of expectation and social appraisal that affect consumers’ review-writing behaviors in the e-WoM domain. Central to expectation theory, as viewed in consumer psychology, is the assumption that customers gather cognitive data regarding what to expect from a product [51]. This data may come through advertisements, various promotion channels, and, in our case, through other consumers’ experiences. This serves as a base-level expectation.

The extent to which these expectations are met becomes the fundamental building block of one dimension of expectation, the disconfirmation effect. Extant literature has described how this effect plays out in different ways. If the experience is better than expected, it is a positive disconfirmation; if it matches the expectation, it is a confirmed expectation; and if it does not meet expectations, it is regarded as a negative disconfirmation [51]. Understanding and resolving a discrepancy arising out of mismatched expectations continues to remain a fundamental question that prods marketers to explore sources of product satisfaction [1].

Oliver [51] has urged marketing managers to address the issue of satisfaction at two levels. First, to ensure positive outcomes, they can set realistic expectations of the product through various channels. Second, and relevant to the social appraisal postulation in this paper, marketers can feed post-purchase positive disconfirmation details to potential customers to increase customers’ psychological comfort with the purchase decision they are willing to undertake. Although contradictory at times, these two implications, derived from multiple experimental studies, have established the relationship between expectations and product satisfaction. In the context of this paper, we hypothesized the linkage between expectation and outcomes is such that disconfirmation of any type (positive, neutral, or negative) has an effect on various outcomes, including product evaluation. We thus posit Hypothesis 4:

Hypothesis 4: The disconfirmation effect (gap in product experience of reviewer and others) has a significant influence on subsequently written reviews sentiment.

In predicting the effect on various consumer-related outcomes, the extant literature has identified the disconfirmation effect at multiple levels. Cognitive dissonance, or the assimilation theory, posits that in the event of encountering a gap between expectation and experience, an individual is likely to gravitate toward the expectation to reduce the psychological discomfort that arises from a dissimilar experience [61]. The human tendency is to cognitively adjust the evaluations to reduce the dissonance by matching to prior expectations. In this case, the social appraisal theory would predict that there is a natural inclination to “fit in” with the online community; hence, the review writer would adjust the decision toward the mean expectation of others (past review sentiments).

However, contrast theory offers contrary predictions and suggests that in the case of a deviation, the individual would do exactly the opposite from what others are doing. Therefore, if one’s own product experience is negative compared to the expectation, the individual will tend to move toward an enhanced negative sentiment, and if there is a positive disconfirmation (i.e., one’s own experience is more positive than others), the individual will tend to move further positive, rather than shifting toward the mean expectation [61].

Interestingly, there is a more nuanced contrast effect, generalized negativity, which is the focus of Hypothesis 4a. This refers to the notion that irrespective of whether it is positive or negative, as long as there is a gap or a disconfirmation, the individual is likely to exhibit negative subsequent behavior [1]. There could be several reasons why this effect is symbolic of today’s consumer psyche and therefore relevant to this paper. First, customer centricity is increasingly at the core of online and offline shopping experiences; so, consumers are becoming more demanding, more well-informed, and more determined to have their voices heard. Second, as mentioned earlier, the e-WoM environment is characteristic of weak social ties, greater privacy, and the absence of face-to-face interaction. This context may lend itself to more carefree behaviors that do not compel the individual to assimilate or fit in. Finally, technology advancement, artificial intelligence tools, and smartphones have caused precision, accuracy, and instant gratification to become the hallmark of today’s society, especially for the younger generation. If we assume more of the millennial generation is likely to shop online, they may demonstrate lower tolerance to disconfirmation or deviation (positive or negative) than Gen X and Gen Y shoppers. Therefore, given the contextual factors outlined, the preponderance of generalized negativity contrast effect is more likely to emerge, thereby yielding lower ratings for the subsequent review sentiments. More specifically, we propose Hypothesis 4a:

Hypothesis 4a: The higher the disconfirmation in any direction (the greater the gap between the product experience of the reviewer and others), the lower the sentiment on subsequently written reviews’ sentiment.

## Research Method

## Experiment Design

Given the aims of this study, an experimental method using a post-test-only control group [7] with a factorial design was decided to be the most suitable. This experimental method also ensures a true test of causality without endogeneity affecting the results [2]. The experiment was conducted in multiple sessions between October 2016 and March 2017 at a reputed management school in India. To reduce bias, the participants were not informed about the true intention of the experiment, but they were informed the experiment was being conducted to gauge the quality of a new mobile phone being introduced in the market.

Two factors were used in this study: (a) user experience of the product (product experience), which could be good or bad, and (b) past reviews, which could be positive or negative. The total number of conditions in the experiment were six (along with the two control cases), as explained in Table 1. Case numbers 1 and 4 acted as the control/base cases.

Product experience was one of the stimuli variables, and it raised two issues. The first was establishing the difference between product experience and a product’s objective quality. Product experience is widely defined as a composite measure of the individual’s likes and dislikes regarding product usage [43]; it is a subjective measure and may vary from user to user. Product quality usually refers to the objective (or subjectively interpreted) features of a product/service [52]. In our study, product experience refers to the individual’s experience with a product that shapes the review and is not reflective of the product quality [36]. It should be noted that most e-commerce websites (including Amazon) tend to differentiate between the sellers’ reviews (related to the sales experience) and the product review (assessing the product experience). Our stimulus was specifically designed to cater to the second case, and all participants were informed of a uniform and good quality of service from the seller to eliminate any bias due to this.

The second issue related to creating a uniform stimulus to eliminate inter-subject bias, which was central to our experimental design involving experiences. To resolve this issue, we employed a narrative-based experience delivery [21] as a stimulus, rather than an individualized experience. Individualized experiences run the risk of being different from subject to subject and being influenced by individual biases; a narrativebased experience delivery provides all participants a standard experience upon which to base their responses. To design our study, we relied on methodological groundings provided by extant research that has found that virtual and indirect experiences are as, if not more, effective at influencing consumers [14, 33]. Also, as Park et al. [53] have reported, consumers are more likely to relate to the memories of an experience than to the experience itself. We used the narrative-based method because it provides a uniform stimulus that is powerful and devoid of individual biases.

## Experiment Stimuli

The first stimulus, as previously mentioned, was product experience. The narrative for the experience stimulus was constructed by individual users of the mobile phone (both positive and negative). The experience narrative was based on past research detailing the most common and most important features of a smartphone [16, 46], including a camera, phone responsiveness, start-up time, battery life, and charging speed [16, 46]. To ensure that sales experience, which is a part of overall customer experience, did not bias the reviewers, we provided both the positive and negative experience user groups with standardized and positive sales details. The features of phone that were described and provided were drawn from the latest and successful smartphone. To curate the experience, two smartphones (having different qualities, as mentioned in subsequent paragraph) were provided to individuals. The individuals recorded their experiences based on their usage of the phone for a period of seven days. This time period was chosen because research has suggested the maximum number of reviews is written in the initial week of a product’s purchase; therefore, a typical user would not have experience with the phone for more than a week before writing a review [18, 59]. The reviewers were also informed they would be using the phone for a week before they reviewed it.

To facilitate the good experience, we provided a new and functional high-end smartphone;<sup>1</sup> for the bad experience, the same smartphone’s response speed was slowed by using developer options that made window rendering slow (15x). The default camera app was replaced with a BitCam® app to provide blurry images reminiscent of early smartphones of the 2000s. A detailed brief of their experience was written and provided to the respondents who were instructed to read it and act as if this was their experience (available in Appendix B).

The biggest challenge of any narrative-based experience stimulation is to ensure the narrative provided is clear and effective. We conducted a robustness test in which a select group of respondents (different from the main experiment respondents) were provided both Oxford Dictionary and research (references) definitions of experience and quality (details in Appendix C) and were asked to rate the narrative as being true to definition of experience or quality on a scale of 1 to 7. Our analysis found that the narrative received a mean rating of 4.95 for experience against 3.02 for quality (df = 86, and the difference of means was significant for p < 0.001). A moderate score for quality was also expected because quality is one of the factors that comprises experience. The score for realism of experience and personal identity with experience was 4.9 and 4.8, respectively, and the chi-square test proved that it was statistically not randomly distributed (p < 0.001).

For the second stimulus (i.e., past reviews), we used actual reviews written by users on an e-commerce platform for both positive and negative review cases. The reviews were taken from the product page of a smartphone with similar features as the one promoted by our experiment product (i.e., Tekas 5 [5.5 inch screen, USB type-C connector, etc.]). The name of the phone brand or the OS (if any) was masked, and the core content was retained along with underlying sentence structures to preserve original thoughts and sentiments. A set of more than 30 reviews were shown to a set of subjects (N = 30, a different group from the main experiment subjects) for rating of the reviews. The subjects were asked to rate the sentiment of the 30 reviews and their credibility. We used human coding as a method of identifying credibility and sentiments instead of a sentiment-mining algorithm for analyzing the data related to the experiment. Human-coded sentiments, although costlier in terms of both time and money, provide better statistical rigor and triangulation because human coders allow for greater control of geography-specific emotions and sentiment-eliciting vocabulary [66]. Most sentiment-mining algorithms still suffer from limitations of satire identification, complex emotion identification, or regional slang identification [40, 65]. For extremely large datasets, these may not be a critical concern because the limitations are averaged out over large samples, and additionally, the cost of human coding is extremely significant.

The 30 coders rated two items: (a) the sentiment strength on a scale from positive to negative<sup>2</sup> and (b) sentiment credibility on a scale from most credible to least credible [10]. Prior research has found that credibility of a review, whether the rating is positive or negative, has a significant influence on the credence readers give to the reviews $[ 1 0 ] ; { } ^ { 3 }$ therefore, rating for credibility helps to identify the most impactful reviews. The consistently positive eight reviews that had means above 4, standard deviations below 1, and credibility ratings of 3.5 and above were chosen for the positive stimulus.<sup>4</sup> The reviews with sentiment levels below 2 and with credibility ratings above 3.5 were chosen for the negative stimulus. Appendix B lists the positive and negative reviews used for past review stimuli.

## Participants

In August and September 2016, an email soliciting recruitment for an experiment to study a new mobile phone was circulated amongst all the students, faculty, and staff members of a premier Indian management institution. The respondents were then asked to complete a preliminary questionnaire regarding their online shopping experiences. Those respondents with some online shopping experience were invited to participate in the study, which was conducted between October 2016 and March 2017. The respondents were compensated with Amazon India coupons worth Rs. 100 (U.S. \$1.54 in nominal terms and U.S. \$26.46 in $\mathrm { P P P } ^ { 5 }$ terms). For this study, the subjects did not have the typical student population drawback of being unaware of the environment; they were well-versed in online shopping and had used it quite regularly. A total of 232 respondents (49 females [21.1 percent] and 183 males [78.9 percent]) participated.

All the respondents were smartphone users (N = 232), with 75.9 percent of the users (N = 176) having used it for over three years. Because the smartphone’s operating system (OS) has huge implications in its owner’s review because of a high emotional connection due to loyalty [31, 39], we collected data to control for it. Of the respondents, 70.2 percent (N = 163) claimed Android as their preferred OS, and 19.8 percent (N = 69) claimed iOS to be their preferred OS. In our study, we did not reveal the OS of the experiment phone, and it was suitably masked. To test for userlevel bias, we asked the respondents to indicate which OS they assumed the phone they just reviewed had (details in Appendix B): 66.8 percent of the users identified the phone to be Android, and 16.4 percent assumed it to be iOS. Overall, 62 percent of the respondents assumed the OS to be the same as their preferred OS. Table 2 shows some more demographic information about the subjects.

## Procedure

No prior similar studies were available for a reference; therefore, we first conducted the pilot study of our experiment design with about 90 respondents to generate the effect sizes and check for manipulation effects. For the pilot phase and the proposed final experiment design, the same monetary incentive used for the main experiment was provided to the subjects to generate true responses. The effect size of positive past review reinforcement and negative past review reinforcement was computed based on the pilot study. The effect size was computed for both the numerical rating under the stimuli and the descriptive review provided by the subject, which was again manually coded by the expert research assistant. In the pilot test, the difference between ratings for positive past reviews and negative past reviews was computed. A paired-sample t-test revealed a significant difference in the treatment conditions, F(1, 85) = 4.25, p < 0.01.

The main experiment subjects had a similar profile as the pilot subjects (excluding those subjects who had participated in the pilot). The subjects were asked demographic questions and questions pertaining to the control variables as a first stage. The control questions were used to determine the subject’s awareness of and expertise in the domain and establish suitability as a study participant [7, 9]. We informed the subjects about the product under study (i.e., a smartphone by a [dummy] firm we called Tekas). The OS of the smartphone was suitably wrapped through a dummy theme, and it was designed to be generic and not explicitly intimated to the subjects. This was done to control for biases arising out of any specific Android/iOS preferences the subjects may have had. Appendix B lists in detail the control questionnaire.

In the next step, subjects were exposed to the product (i.e., the Tekas mobile phone) with a randomized group to expose them to the two levels of experience (a good product and a bad product) (Appendix B represents the typical product experience for the subject). Subsequently, the subjects were asked to write reviews for the product. For the subjects in groups exposed to either positive or negative prior reviews, the subjects were told the reviews were top-rated reviews to eliminate temporal or recency biases. The reviews were collected in a Google document to eliminate any e-commerce platform-related biases. The subjects were required to provide a numerical rating for the product; however, writing a descriptive review was not mandatory. This aimed to elicit the most valid and true reviews from users. However, we found that most of the population provided descriptive reviews (N = 211). This might be the effect of an experiment in a controlled setting, where subjects felt the need to write a review to justify their involvement. As a control for biases related to the OS of the phone, we also solicited the subjects’ opinions on what they felt was the OS of the phone they reviewed.

## Supplementary Analysis and Robustness Tests

Past research has indicated that individuals show a much stronger preference for their choices of brands and, particularly, their operating systems (OSs) [31, 39]. Therefore, we had masked and captured the subjects’ preferred OSs and assumed OSs of the phone under study. We modeled Equation 1 for review sentiments for the cases where subjects’ assumptions of mobile OS were similar to their preferred OSs and the other case when the assumption was not the same as the preferred OSs. Appendix C shows the detailed results of these models. We found that past reviews played a major role in influencing the subsequent user ratings when the users thought they were reviewing their preferred OS (irrespective of their preferred OS). In case of a preferred OS, a user tends to have higher faith in the community because the community tends to be viewed as the user’s own community. We found no evidence of any deviation based on the users’ perceptions of the OS they were reviewing or based on the OS preferences users may have had. Through this exercise, we were able to determine if the user with a preferred OS (e.g., Android) reacted differently to past review sentiment when the user assumed the preferred OS vs the non-preferred OS is being reviewed. This exercise accounts for any possible bias, which we found to be non-significant on this count.

A supplementary analysis to test the implications of these experimental results on non-controlled real-world review data was performed. Using this supplementary analysis, we intended to check the effect of initial review sentiments on subsequent reviews for different kinds of smartphones. To control for effect of brands/sellers, and so forth, we compared the reviews of the same products sold by different sellers on an e-commerce platform that would result in different reviews and ratings for those products. For this analysis, we gathered the data from Amazon India’s website. Using custom-written code, we scraped user review data from the launch of the product (first review on the product page) to the most current review at the time of the data scraping. Because the product used as an experimental prop was a smartphone, we used the same product for data scraping and sentiment analysis and scraped data for different models/types and brands of mobile phone, which ensured different kinds of user involvement with varied products and its effect on the reviews were not reflected in our data. We performed the sentiment analysis on a single product, but across different brands and price ranges. A common refrain amongst smartphone users is people treat diverse brands/OS/price-range-based smartphones differently. Performing a first-level sentiment analysis over a wide variety of smartphones checked for presence of such an effect. In total, the data consisted of 50,487 reviews.

For sentiment analysis, we used a lexicon-based approach in which the algorithms extracted lexicons and words used in the statement and detected positive or negative sentiments and their strength [64, 66, 70]. We used the tool called SentiStrength,<sup>7</sup> which uses human-designed lexicons for analysis of sentiments. This tool has been extensively used in past sentiment analysis-based research [64, 66, 67]. To test the influence of initial review sentiments, we used the cumulative SentiStrength polarity that we received from the SentiStrength software for each review. We compared the SentiStrength polarities for each smartphone offered by the various sellers on Amazon. Comparing the same smartphone across different sellers enabled us to control for the effect of brand, promotion, quality of smartphone itself, etc. The comparison, therefore, is based on the initial sentiments of products at two different storefronts selling it on Amazon.

All the products’ cumulative SentiStrength polarities were divided into four time quartiles; the time quartiles were the division of the products’ review timelines in four quarters between the first review and the last review. We then analyzed the four quarters of data for the 20 products across 30 storefronts on Amazon India by comparing their distributions to check for similarity of distribution. We decided to use a non-parametric Kruksal-Wallis test to check all 20 products across the four categories in SPSS. The non-parametric test was used because past research has found the ratings do not follow a normal distribution; rather, they follow a J-shaped curve [24].

From the Kruksal-Wallis test for the review sentiments we had mined, we found that except for four products, which had an overall lower number of reviews, the null hypothesis could not be rejected for the remaining 16 products (at a significance level of 0.1). This implied that throughout the four quarters, the reviews could be considered drawn from the same distribution. We also plotted the mean SentiStrength polarities for the user ratings and reviews, as shown in Figure 3. The plots also indicate that the user ratings and review sentiments tend to show behavior where the initial reviews act as a reference point from where the average reviews wavers only to a limited degree.

## Discussion and Implications

## Discussion

Our study extends the research that has examined the incidence of reviews from the point of view of ratings as an outcome [29, 45, 63] and provides insight into the way sentiments in reviews written after exposure to previous reviews are generated. Although we determined that our results are broadly in line with past works in this domain, we found the results of negative appraisal to be particularly interesting. With negative appraisals (i.e., a negative past review with a negative experience), the user’s review sentiments are more positive than the case without any exposure to past reviews. We attribute this to the anchoring effect, which acts to provide both higher and lower benchmarks and limits user’s review sentiments within a topic.

For both positive and negative appraisal cases, we found subsequent review writers with no exposure to the past review often provided extreme reviews; however, the presence of a past review acted as a benchmark and limited the user’s review sentiments in keeping with those of the other reviews.

We also found the social pressure that comes from being different from the group has a significant effect on review sentiment. During our analysis of Hypothesis 4, we noted the effect is agnostic of the direction of deviation. Higher deviation in perceived product experience leads to disconfirmation in a user’s expectation, which we found to significantly lower the review sentiment. Our results are in line with Ho et al. [29], who found users tend to rate products at extremes in case of greater disconfirmation, review writers compose more extreme reviews in the presence of higher disconfirmation, and the extremity is always toward negative sentiments.

## Implications

This research contributes to enhanced theoretical understanding of three domains: sentiment generation, appraisal theories, and the general domain of e-WoM. The appraisal theories lead us to confirm the presence of social appraisal in subsequent reviewing on online e-WoM platforms. In specific contexts, other studies [45, 63], such as for product ratings, have found similar results. On e-WoM platforms, these constraints are the limited levels in which ratings can be provided (generally between 1 to 5 stars). On a more free-flowing media and expression outlet, like textual reviews, true beliefs are exhibited. We find social appraisal theory is limited in explaining this phenomenon. However, some studies in the sociology field have found evidence of limitations of the contagion effect in a freer-flowing and unconstrained discourse, where the discussants defend their own beliefs more aggressively and are less likely to be persuaded by the dominant social beliefs [27, 28].

This study provides multiple insights for practicing managers of product manufacturing and sales firms, e-commerce platform designers, and e-WoM managers. The actionable insights can be summarized in terms of strategies for these stakeholders. We concede there is no substitute for a good product that fulfills customers’ expectations, but our study finds that product managers’ suppression of negative ratings on e-commerce platforms may not be the best strategy for lowrated products. The presence of negative reviews acts as an anchor that pulls the subsequent ratings slightly toward the more favorable spectrum compared to the absence of negative reviews that cause the subsequent reviews to seem even more extreme.

Second, most managers and e-commerce platforms are geared toward ensuring a good rating on the platforms because the ratings can be summarized and easily processed by subsequent buyers; however, review texts do not receive the required attention. Our results indicated managers should carefully cultivate and respond to the textual reviews because with their detailed content, they can create a much deeper social influence. Additionally, the aim of review platform designers is to provide as unbiased a product opinion as possible; thus, we suggest platform designers should incorporate a metric to summarize the responses of the textual reviews. These could be a feature to level sentiment strength, as an example. Such summarized metrics would convey more information to the future consumer.

## Limitations and Conclusion

This research’s generalizability and applicability of the results are not without their share of limitations. The study is limited by the choice of the experimental setup. We acknowledge the subjects did not actually purchase the product for which they were writing a review. To control for product-level effects, we used premium mobile phones, and the effect found in this study may not be the same for other product types, such as utilitarian or experiential products ranging from soaps to holidays.

Also, the reviews shown to stimulate the responses were completely positive or negative for the respective user groups. This was done to elicit the effects of different types of reviews without adulteration due to multiple sentiments in the reviews. Users would rarely encounter such situations on e-commerce platforms, and hence the effect would be slightly subdued. The choice of a narrative experience as a stimulus provided us with the opportunity to measure the effect of different situations on respondents without any inter-respondent bias. However, this is also a limitation because all individuals would ideally evaluate the same product based on their own sets of preferences, beliefs, and biases.

The setup used to display a past review as the stimulus and collect the respondents’ responses was also limited in functionality compared to the e-commerce websites currently doing business. However, within these constraints, the research method allowed us to allocate the subjects in a random order and provide the desired stimuli in a controlled manner so we could separate and study the effects of those stimuli on user behavior.

We did collect background user information and demographic details to ensure the randomness of the population sample; however, multiple constraints did not allow us to conduct a multi-staged experiment to control for randomness in the subjects’ psychological profiles. Although the sufficiently large sample size and the varied backgrounds of the subjects do ensure the randomness of psychological profiles to a great extent, the same cannot be guaranteed and hence remains a weakness of this work.

This paper, to the best of our knowledge, is one of the few works that differentiates between user ratings and review texts and studies the incidence of reviews from the perspective of sentiments within the texts. We add to and extend the growing field of research in review incidence. Through this paper, we also extend the theoretical groundings of appraisal theory. This paper focuses on the antecedents of review-writing decisions through the layered lens of (a) review writers’ evaluations of their own experiences, (b) their evaluations as shaped by others’ opinions, and (c) the interactions of both. This offers a psycho-social view of the appraisal process that is a precursor, or the antecedent, of the review-writing decision. This could enhance managerial understanding of how to place and use past reviews for better marketing and e-WoMbased targeting for their products. As e-WoM increasingly gains more prominence over traditional reviews and WoM, the efforts to influence the same is also likely to increase. Our research could be beneficial not only for firms and future researchers but also for platform players, who are setting the review rules to design their e-WoM strategies, and for consumers to remain vigilant of sentiment manipulation efforts and its outcomes.
