# Technologies of Power in Digital Inclusion

Priyanka Pandey,<sup>1</sup> Yingqin Zheng<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

This study moves beyond the conventional focus on technology access, adoption, and participation in communities, and instead presents a perspective on digital inclusion as a complex process of both empowerment and subjugation. Drawing on Foucault’s theorization on subjectivity and power, our case study of community health workers in rural India reveals that the adoption of an mHealth application simultaneously subordinated and strengthened the subjectivity of the community health workers. The study explicates the process through which the health workers oscillated between the enhancement of their individual efficacy, and their submission to institutionalized power as facilitated by the mHealth application during their everyday work practices. Thus, by shifting the focus from “who” is digitally included to “how” they become digitally included, our research provides a fresh perspective that enriches and deepens the discourse of digital inclusion. It generates both theoretical and practical implications for anyone interested in understanding digital inclusion from a more granulated and practice-based perspective.

Keywords: Digital Inclusion, Power, Subjectivity, Foucault, mHealth, Empowerment

Jason Bennett Thatcher was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on March 30, 2021 and underwent four rounds of revision. This paper is part of the Special Issue on Technology and Social Inclusion.

## 1 Introduction

The discourse on technology and social inclusion often implies social categories or demographic segmentation of identity demarcated by particular division lines, e.g., gender, sexuality, age, race, class, and so on. Certain social groups are defined as subjects of othering, i.e., disadvantaged, or marginalized, who need to be supported, included, and integrated into mainstream society. Research on digital inclusion thus often focuses on digitally connecting the excluded in these categories. However, as research on intersectionality (Rodriguez et al., 2016; Trauth et al., 2016; Zheng & Walsham, 2021) has shown, such categorization is often overly simplistic as identities are multidimensional and intersectional, and there are also divisions within the same identity-based groups.

While research on digital inclusion, i.e., digitally enabled social inclusion, is often concerned with who should be included, less emphasis is placed on how they are included and what conditions enable inclusion or, for that matter, exclusion—i.e., whether individuals are included as passive participants due to the existing power structures and institutional norms or as social actors with a level of subjectivity over their own work and life and their role in society. Zheng and Walsham (2021) call for information systems (IS) research to move beyond the concept of the digital divide, (the gap between haves and have-nots), or a single-axis analysis of digital inequality and social exclusion. They propose conceiving individuals not merely as users of digital technology but as actors who are positioned within multiple layers of power relations. The former implies an emphasis on digital accessibility and adoption, whereas the latter focuses on the digital mediation of the production and constraints of agency in social structures and power relations.

From a technological perspective, the literature on digital inclusion often focuses on understanding why certain groups in a population do not possess access or the skills to use digital technology. Seeing individuals as users entails a focus on their immediate interaction with technology—that is, it ignores their role as actors who are locally situated in a social context that is subsumed within social structures that shape their everyday perception and use of artifacts (Zheng & Walsham, 2021). Thus, any inclusion or exclusion that takes place for a human actor is always in relation to other human actors or institutions, cultures, and norms, which are constituted by power relations and shape the reference point of what inclusion or exclusion means for that actor.

For instance, in some contexts where women are digitally excluded, it is not necessarily due to the lack of skills or access to technology, but because of contextual gendered norms and power structures. For example, in India, people of lower castes may feel excluded in relation to those of higher castes due to their disadvantaged social position (Sankaran et al., 2017). Even when they are online, women from lower classes or marginalized groups are still less likely to speak up or make their voice heard (Jia et al., 2016). Dy et al. (2017) refute the idea that the internet is a neutral platform for entrepreneurship by showing that women digital entrepreneurs in the UK are still subject to systemic inequality, such as the disadvantages associated with class, gender, and race.

In this paper, we move beyond the identity-based approach and the binary oppositions of digital inclusion and exclusion by situating individuals not across the digital divide but as situated in power structures and relations. Through a case study of an mHealth application by community health workers (CHWs) in a primary health care (PHC) setting in rural India, we explore what it means to be included and the terms under which individuals are included, approaching this question from the perspective of Foucault’s (1982) analysis of subjectivity and power. This paper seeks to advance the understanding of digital inclusion in IS research through highlighting the complexity and the paradoxical effect of digital adoption, i.e., how a digital technology shapes human subjectivity as underpinned in the power structures of the context. From this perspective, we show that digital technology, embedded in day-to-day practices, should be seen as what Foucault calls the “technology of power,” which has the dual effect of both subject making and subjugating, or empowering and disempowering. As such, inclusion and exclusion are relative and co-constitutive, and digital inclusion is a process in flux rather than a fixed end point.

In the following, we first review the literature on digital inclusion in IS research, where this paper is situated, and then present the theoretical conception of subjectivity and power by Michel Foucault. We then present the case background on mHealth and community health workers in India and details of the methodology. The findings section reveals the dual processes of technologies of power and the paradoxical effect of empowerment and disempowerment of the CHWs. The final section discusses implications for practice as well as the theoretical implications of the study for digital inclusion research and how the Foucauldian perspective adds value to our understanding of the relationships between technology, power, and social structures.

## 2 Digital Inclusion in IS Research

The research on technology and social inclusion in the IS literature has been centered on the divide between the “haves” and “have-nots,” or diversity in the IT profession (Trauth, 2017). However, it has also long been recognized that social inclusion is multifaceted, and involves multiple dimensions of social differences, inequality, and stratification (Urquhart & Underhill-Sem, 2009; Zheng & Walsham, 2008; Warschauer, 2003). Therefore, social inclusion in the digital society concerns not only whether individuals have the skills and resources to access and benefit from digital technology, but also “the extent that individuals … are able to fully participate in society and control their own destinies” (Warschauer, 2003, p. 8). Thus, digital inclusion goes beyond the digital divide and includes a multitude of other divisions such as age, gender, and education (van Dijk & Hacker, 2003).

The relationship between digital technology and social inclusion is complicated because digital practices are always embedded in social processes, just as individuals are always situated in deep-seated sociocultural norms, power structures, and institutional environments (Madon et al., 2009). Furthermore, inclusion often coexists with exclusion. As Zheng and Walsham (2008, p. 238) point out, “inclusion in one space can coexist with exclusion in another space (p. 238).” Since individuals are positioned at the intersection of multiple systems of power, digital inclusion and exclusion are relational and contingent upon the interaction of a myriad of social factors (Trauth, 2017).

A quick search of IS journals with the keyword “inclusion” shows that the body of research on digital inclusion in IS journals has been growing since 2008, notably burgeoning over the last five years. Table 1 includes relevant papers on this topic from top IS journals (i.e., “Basket of Eight” journals) and two additional multidisciplinary journals: Information Technology and People (ITP), and Information Technology and Development (ITD), which are more open to research on digital inequality. A number of papers have examined how digital technology can facilitate or hinder the participation of refugees in social integration within communities and society (Alam & Imran, 2015; Díaz Andrade & Doolin, 2016; Iazzolino, 2021; Martin & Taylor, 2021; Schoemaker et al., 2021). With the development of mobile banking and fintech, financial inclusion has received some attention over the last few years (Joia & dos Santos, 2019; Kemal, 2019; Senyo et al., 2021; Chatterjee, 2020; Tan et al., 2021). However, gender (Naidoo et al., 2019), disability (Newman et al., 2017), and sexual minorities (Light et al., 2008) remain underrepresented topics in IS research. While our review is not exhaustive, it suggests that only a small number of digital inclusion research papers have appeared in the Basket of Eight journals, whereas the majority have been published in journals like ITD and ITP.

What does digital inclusion mean when discussed in these studies? We identify four levels of digital inclusion (1) digital inclusion as technological adoption, (2) digital inclusion as digitally enabled participation in communities and society, (3) digital inclusion as digitally enabled empowerment, and (4) digital inclusion as structural transformation. While each level of inclusion necessarily entails the previous level(s), e.g., digital empowerment is typically predicated upon some form of technological adoption and social interaction, the majority of studies investigate digital inclusion as technological adoption; smaller subsets of research explore subsequent social participation or how digital technology might facilitate individual empowerment, i.e., the sense of agency and control, as part of digital inclusion.

Table 1 presents some examples of research with different perceptions of digital inclusion. For example, studies on financial inclusion (Joia & dos Santos, 2019; Kemal, 2019; Senyo et al., 2021; Chatterjee, 2020) tend to focus on the adoption and diffusion of digital banking services, perhaps due to the lack of infrastructure and institutional support present in this context. Studies that conceive of digital inclusion as participation often explore the role of technology in building social capital (Alam & Imran, 2015), enabling community participation (Armenta et al., 2012; Baron & Gomez, 2013) or the importance of social networks, community, and institutional support in sustaining the digital inclusion of disadvantaged groups (Madon et al., 2009; Newman et al., 2017; Ratliffe et al., 2012).

A small number of studies have shown the effect of empowerment, <sup>1</sup> i.e., improving the level of agency through digital inclusion. For example, Baron and Gomez (2013) showed that their public-access computing participants felt a stronger sense of selfefficacy, self-esteem, and control over their lives, and also found it easier to connect with friends and family and build social networks. Sen’s capability approach has been applied to examine capability expansion, i.e., greater opportunities for well-being and agency achievement, in digital inclusion (Díaz Andrade & Doolin, 2016; Zheng & Walsham, 2008). Díaz Andrade and Doolin (2016) applied the approach to show how information and communication technology (ICT) enhances refugees’ level of agency and wellbeing, fostering their participation in society and their sense of control over their lives. Zheng and Walsham (2008) argued that social exclusion can be perceived as “capability deprivation” and pointed out that one could be digitally connected yet still deprived of “agency freedom” to access or use information to achieve one’s own goals due to institutional or structural barriers. They emphasized the need to pay attention to the conversion factors from the use of digital technology to capability enhancement.

The fourth level of digital inclusion, which eventually leads to structural change is rarely found in the literature. This refers to the situation where digital users are not only included in terms of participation and empowerment but also become “agents of change” (Pansera & Owen, 2018). One such example is the case study of Alibaba’s successful fintech application Yu’E Bao (Tan et al., 2021), where grassroots consumers were empowered by the saving/investment app that helped improve their skills, capacity, and confidence in basic wealth management, which inadvertently led to the evolution of the technology itself in order to fulfill the growing demands of grassroots consumers.

Structural transformation through digital inclusion is rare, and this study explores some reasons why this might be the case. Existing research on “digital inclusion as empowerment” has largely focused on the positive effect of digital technology, i.e., on individual agency—e.g., improving individuals’ level of autonomy, their capacity to take control of their life, or their ability to contribute and participate in their communities. In other words, it is assumed that individual empowerment usually takes place without any change in existing power structures— the very power structures that create inequality and deprivation of individual agency in the first place. Few studies have investigated the source of digital inequality—namely, the deprivation of agential capacity—by paying attention to existing power structures or considering whether a possibility of empowerment for disadvantaged or excluded groups exists at a structural level and, if not, why.

## 2.1 Incorporating Power in Digital Inclusion

We argue that it is necessary to explicitly incorporate an analysis of power structures because, like social inclusion, digital inclusion can take many forms under intersectional power structures. This is in line with Heeks (2022) who discusses adverse digital inclusion, where individuals are subjected to various oppressive or unjust conditions even when they are digitally included. For example, privacy and surveillance concerns have been part and parcel of digital societies, where individuals also benefit from being included in digital networks.

Conventional conceptions of power assume that power exists as an overarching capacity that is all-possessive and exercised over others in a mechanical manner. Power is seen primarily as something that represses, coerces, or denies (Bloomfield & Coombs, 1992; Clegg, 1998). Such a zero-sum notion of power implies that shifts in organizational power are the result of conforming changes in the organizational distribution of resources, such as information, that confer power on their possessors. This understanding of power can be seen in the early studies of information systems in organizations (Pettigrew, 1972; Markus, 1983; Jasperson et al., 2002). The weakness of this approach is that it fails to consider that power is relational (Clegg, 1998). That is, power is not possessed but is a capacity for action that resides in social relations subsumed in the social practices produced and reproduced in everyday life (Foucault, 1982).

Foucault goes beyond a perspective that centers a locus of power on violence or resistance and instead links human subjectivity, i.e., the creation of selfperceptions and identities, to the exercise of power (Foucault, 1982). Such a perspective of power enables us to understand how power circulates in everyday life and the impact it has on human actors, their identity, their self-perceptions, and their relationships with institutions, organizations, and other human actors. In short, what differentiates Foucault’s concept of power from other theories of power, is that he links power with human subjectivity and examines power not just in terms of control and coercion but also in terms of its positive, enabling, and subject-making effects.

Instead of privileging technological artifacts, Foucault focuses on the behavioral and social technologies encoded and embedded in material technologies (Hassan, 2011; Bakardjieva & Gaden, 2012). Linking Foucault with technology use, Bloomfield (1995, p. 497) stresses that in seeing “reality as materially heterogeneous and relational, it becomes valuable to employ Foucault’s relational notion of power. This is because technology increasingly mediates how power circulates, is exercised and what it produces.”

Within IS research, Foucault’s concepts have been widely adopted—for example, in research on surveillance technologies (Lyon, 1994; 2003), the use of information and databases (Poster, 1996), discipline, information use and technologies at work (Zuboff, 1988; Webster, 1995; Doolin, 2004), the link between power relations and IT (Doolin, 1998; Brooke, 2002; Introna, 2003; 2001), and IT resistance and technology failure (Avgerou & McGrath, 2007). However, no IS research has yet applied Foucault’s theorization of power and human subjectivity (Willcocks, 2004).

Subjectivity in the IS literature usually refers not to human subjectivity but to the “interpretation of subjective meaning” (Narock et al., 2012, p. 180), for example, the inability of a single-context, single interpretation system to be open for meaning in a growing interorganizational context (Schultze, 2000, Narock et al., 2012, Mingers & Standing, 2018; McKinney & Yoos, 2019). In contrast, our interest in this paper is on the production of human subjectivity in everyday social practices (Siles, 2012), that is, a human actor’s individual identity and self-perception and its relationship with structures of power that is shaped in everyday life. This is because an understanding of the circulation of power in routinized social practices and its effect on human subjectivity can help us understand the conditions that enable the digital inclusion or exclusion of human actors. We thus introduce our first research question:

RQ: How does digital adoption interact with power structures and impact the subjectivity of individuals in the processes of digital inclusion?

In the next section, we will further explore the Foucauldian conceptualization (1982) of the subject and power, which will be used to reveal the complex relationship between technology and human subjectivities and its impact on CHWs situated in the existing power structures of the primary health care system.

## 3 Theoretical Perspective: Foucault and “Technologies of Power”

To recap, we start with the conception of digital inclusion not as digital access or participation, but as digitally enabled empowerment (Díaz Andrade & Doolin, 2016; Iivari et al., 2018), i.e., the integration of individuals in formal and informal institutions and social life with a sense of self-efficacy, respect, and agency. However, we believe there is no simple or linear connection between digital adoption and empowerment. Empowerment often occurs simultaneously with disempowerment (Pandey & Zheng, 2019). Digital inclusion is thus a contested and dynamic process entrenched in power relations. To analyze and reveal the complexity of digital inclusion, we turn to Foucault’s theorization of power and subjectivity.

Foucault (1977) states that power is omnipresent, i.e., it is found in all social interactions. It is interwoven with and revealed in all kinds of social relations of everyday life. Power not only has a repressive function but also plays a positive role, in that it also enables actions or capacities of human actors i.e., creates subject positions (Lynch, 2011). In his analysis of power, power is seen as something that enables the identities of human actors but also limits them, and these two actions inseparably constitute power (Foucault, 1980).

This paradoxical effect of “technologies of power” is part and parcel of his concept of governmentality. Government according to Foucault (1977) refers to systematized regulated and reflected modes of power (a “technology”) or a discursive field in which the exercise of power is rationalized (follows a specific reasoning) (Hindess, 1996, p. 106). This reasoning defines the fulfillment of human action and the means to achieve it. In other words, technologies of power are social mechanisms, techniques, and procedures for directing human behavior (Foucault, 1997, p. 68). It is structuring the possible field of action of others, by employing laws or tactics or both, to arrange things in such a way that the means and ends can be achieved in a particular manner by human actors (Rose et al., 2009; Lemke, 2002; Hindess, 1996).

On the other hand, the exercise of power can be instantiated only if the subjects have a certain degree of freedom. Only if there is room for possibilities in which human actors can act, react, or behave in different ways, can power be exercised to limit it (Lorenzini, 2018). Thus, by structuring the field of action of human actors, governmental mechanisms of power, in particular, also shape actors’ relationships with themselves and others. In doing so, human subjectivity becomes something that is not pregiven and outside of sociocultural norms and values; rather it is something that is socially, culturally, and historically constituted (Heyes, 2011).

Human subjectivity (the creation of identities and their self-assessment) then is both individualized in nature but also subject to control. Foucault’s “subject” imbibes two meanings—to either be subject to someone else through control and dependence or to be tied to one’s own identity by conscience or selfknowledge (Foucault, 1982)—“both meanings suggest a form of power which subjugates and makes subject to” (p. 781).

Thus, subjectivity is not an essentialist or fixed sense of the self but is continuously performed in practice. Such a perspective is aligned with that of practice theory and the perspective of performativity (Bourdieu, 1977; Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011), which see social life as emergent and constituted in day-to-day social practice, which in turn shapes human agency and subjectivity, even though the latter is rarely the focus of practice-based studies in the IS literature.

Governmentality illuminates the entanglement between practices of domination and self-formation (Foucault, 1993). Human actors are made docile and useful through numerous vectors of disciplinary practices and management, such as those implicated in systems of education, medicine, military etc., that they engage with in everyday life, while also retaining the ability to self-assess and be self-responsible for their actions, which are largely governed by the very same disciplinary practices (Lemke, 2002). Human actors then become both “autonomous agents” and “clients to be administered” (Dean, 1994, p. 166). Thus, selfmotivation and self-responsibilization are integrated into structures of domination and coercion. Therefore, it is not possible to study technologies of power without an understanding of the rationality or reasoning underpinning them (Yates, 2002; Foucault, 1997). Based on this perspective, we ask: How do human actors embody certain subject positions (are made subjects and subjugated to) and how are they positioned as subjects? According to what reasoning (rationality) are they situated in social relations or power with others? (Yates, 2002)

The above discussion illustrates what we mean when we use the term “power” in terms of its relationship with “human subjectivity.” Through the following case study, we seek to reveal how technology (in the form of mHealth adoption) simultaneously produces and reproduces CHWs’ human subjectivity and its governing power structures in the processes of digital inclusion. The next section presents the case background and methodology.

## 4 Methodology

## 4.1 Background: mHealth and Community Health Worker Processes in India

Community health workers in India build bridges between the formal health systems and rural communities, working to improve the relevance, acceptability, and accessibility of formal health services. Functions of CHWs include conducting home visits, collecting health information about community/village members, reporting health information to local health centers, assessing health and providing preventive disease treatment, and providing education and counseling and referrals for further care (Lehmann & Sanders, 2007; Braun et al., 2013). With their links to the health system, CHWs can also offer an entry point and, at times, directly provide health services, such as contraceptives, home-based care for people living with AIDS, and communityintegrated management of childhood illnesses (Lehmann & Sanders, 2007; Ruton et al., 2018).

Community health workers are mostly rural housewives or young women working of primary healthcare either as unpaid volunteers or low-level, community-based health workers. They typically do not have other employment opportunities outside the household. The greatest value of CHWs lies in the fact that they reside in the very communities they serve. Typically, CHWs share the same linguistic, ethnic, and cultural background as the beneficiaries (Agarwal et al., 2015). Once trained, CHWs are able to build trusting relationships with communities and share health information within their communities using culturally acceptable and understandable methods (Agarwal et al., 2015; Bonnell et al., 2017). This adds a moral dimension to community health work (Hampshire et al., 2016), where CHWs feel a sense of altruism and social responsibility. For instance, Prince and Brown (2016) describe the implicit requirement for CHWs in East Africa as “demonstrat[ing] a commitment to community development underlined by selflessness and the dedication of free labour.” Such institutional rhetoric arguably then “shapes CHWs own political subjectivities, motivations, and capacities” (Maes, 2014, p. 108).

Evidence has also shown that even though CHWs have been viewed as an important component in primary healthcare, they do not always receive adequate political and institutional support (SOCHARA report, 2005; Som, 2016). This is reflected in issues such as lack of training and supervision, poor compensation, insufficient institutional funding, changing managerial and political rationales, and so on (Early et al., 2019; Ruton et al., 2018; Agarwal et al., 2015; Braun et al., 2013).

CHWs are often expected to take on extreme workloads, resulting in delays and errors in data collection and slow response to emergency cases, which incur blame and pressure from PHC centers. In that sense, health workers in their everyday workflow enact more than one subjectivity toward their role. On the one hand, there is an aspect of selflessness and care in providing health care to communities, while on the other hand, there is a sense of compelled compliance with the duties and workload imposed upon them.

mHealth research has shown that the introduction of mobile phones or tablets within CHW practices helps to improve the efficiency of the workflow and the quality of health service delivery (Nyemba-Mudende & Chigona, 2018). Indian examples such as ICTCCS in the state of Bihar (Carmicahel et al., 2016) mSakhi in Maharashtra (Patel et al., 2019), ImTecho In Gujarat (Modi et al., 2017), ReMind in Uttar Pradesh (Prinja et al., 2018), CPHM in Karnataka (Naik et al., 2020), and MfM in Jharkand (Ilozumba et al., 2018) are some of the mHealth interventions that are managed through governmental partnerships with NGOs, where CHWs use mobile communication technologies in the form of smartphones and/or tablets to collect and report health data from their villages, which is then fed into the governmental HIS (health information system) platform through PHC centers.

mHealth applications often produce a mixed impact on the work of CHWs. For example, the ICTCCS mHealth application improved the efficiency and timeliness of providing prenatal and antenatal care to the community yet exacerbated existing coordination problems among CHWs (Carmichael et al., 2016). Other issues include hardware, poor infrastructure, and the lack of training (Ilozumba et al., 2018). Moreover, these issues make it difficult for CHWs to focus on the more social and altruistic aspects of their work, such as meaningful health-related communication within their communities.

The combination of both social and infrastructural barriers questions the very relevance of mHealth interventions. Schoen et al., (2017) suggest that a qualitative inquiry into CHW and mHealth research is needed to understand the various social factors that act as barriers for successful mHealth adoption by CHWs. In addition, a clear theoretical perspective in both systems design and CHW-mHealth research is also needed to understand the acceptability and usability of mHealth programs within CHW work practices (Ilozumba et al., 2018).

## 4.2 Research Site

The PHC center where this study was conducted is located in the foothills of the Chamrajnagar district of the southern state of Karnataka, India. It has a relatively high population of indigenous people—e.g., the Soliga tribal people, who have lived in and around the thickly forested and hilly areas for centuries—and is one of the worse-off districts with respect to health and development (Seshadri et al., 2019).

The PHC center in this area is managed through a public-private partnership model, where the state government of Karnataka, in collaboration with a local non-governmental organization (NGO), is responsible for the provision of the required human resources and logistics to deliver preventive, promotive, curative, and rehabilitative health care services (Nayak, 2010; Prasanth, 2011). The NGO and the government employs health workers, who are categorized into various tiers— Tier 1, 2 3, and their functions include going on field visits, performing antenatal care (ANC) registration, educating expectant mothers about maternal health and children about hygiene, following up with each patient throughout their pregnancy until delivery, and following up on child immunizations (Karun Trust Annual Report 2019-20). The PHC center consists of medical rooms, one medical officer, one administrator, one dentist, one block-health education officer, four staff nurses, one pharmacist, one laboratory technician, a supervisor, four Tier 1 CHWs, six Tier 2 CHWs, 4 to 5 Tier 3 CHWs and two male health workers (MHWs) (Karuna Trust Annual Report 2019-2020).

Despite the integration of the PHC system into the communities, much of the rural population continued to suffer from acute chronic diseases and did not primarily depend on PHC due to their existing faith in traditional medicine and health practices. Living in the hills, many community members find it costly and inconvenient to travel to the PHC center. Hence, health workers have become pivotal in providing this population with preventive services and linking them with the PHC center. In 2015, an mHealth device (Android tablet) was provided to the community health workers to improve their existing workflow, which is the focus of our study.

## 4.3 Data Collection

Data was collected by the first author between January and February 2016. This data collection process was grounded in an interpretive and qualitative approach toward unpacking the impact of the mHealth intervention on the subjectivity of the CHWs. The research phenomenon here is the regular engagement of the CHWs with the mHealth technology and how it shaped the experience of CHWs embedded in existing social relations and institutional norms. Thus, the data collection included not only data on CHWs but also data about a diverse set of relevant social actors in order to capture the interpretations and social relations of all the actors associated with the phenomenon.

Data collection methods included semi-structured interviews with Tier 1 and Tier 2 CHWs, an mHealth engineer, and the PHC supervisor. A focus group interview with community members was also included. Finally, participant observation of Tier 1 and Tier 2 CHWs was conducted during their routine work. The researcher was able to record and investigate the day-to-day engagement with the technology by CHWs, alongside their interactions with community members and the PHC staff, which generated insights into the power dynamics between the PHC staff and CHWs, and CHWs and community members.

Semi-structured interviews started with broad openended questions to understand how CHWs, the PHC supervisor, and the mHealth engineer generally felt about the technology and its impact on their workflow, and slowly moved on to more specific questions where they could expand on their insights a bit more. The questions slightly differed depending on how strongly associated the participant was with the technology (i.e., CHWs emphasized how they felt about using the technology more, i.e., feeling confident or burdened, compared to the PHC staff who focused more on the efficiency aspect of the technology use, for instance, improved data collection and surveillance). Interviews with CHWs were conducted during the observation phase. Due to their busy schedule, it was not always possible to occupy the CHWs for long periods of time with interviews. The interview duration depended on the CHW’s availability of free time during their routine work tasks. Table A1 in the Appendix presents the general field observation guide, Table A2 provides the list of the interviewees and the duration of the interviews, and Table A3 presents the interview protocol.

Lastly, a focus group study was conducted, which included 15 community members. The focus group consisted mostly of beneficiaries (pregnant women, women with infants, a few husbands, and some of the village elderly). The purpose of the group discussion was to observe if the community members felt a change in how the technology was impacting the ability of CHWs to deliver health services to them (Ritchie & Lewis, 2003). The group discussion also helped develop a rich picture of the social context in which the phenomenon was experienced. Community members are an essential part of the phenomenon, as they are at the receiving end of the health services delivered by the CHWs. Any change in the quality of the health services (e.g., Improved emergency case management) was directly attributed to the CHWs, who in turn attributed it to the use of the tablet.

Together, these three sources of data demonstrate the multiple perspectives on mHealth adoption and its entanglement with CHW work practices in this specific context.

## 4.4 Analysis of Data

The data analysis moved between a deductive and inductive approach (Trochim, 2006; Creswell, 2013). The researcher went into the field with the a priori assumption that the mHealth application served as an empowering tool. However, the data collected revealed a rather different pattern from the assumption. Thus, data analysis involved taking a step back and starting an iterative process between data and the literature to find a suitable theoretical lens. Following a long period of exploration and discussion, Foucault’s concept of the subject and power (1982) was adopted to examine human subjectivity as embedded in power relations.

Using thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), the dominant theme that emerged was the simultaneous empowerment and disempowerment of CHWs. We followed the phases of thematic analysis: familiarizing ourselves with the data, generating initial patterns, searching for themes, reviewing themes, naming themes, and, finally, building the construct (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Field notes were used to cross-reference the themes emerging from the interview transcripts and to juxtapose the health workers’ accounts with the accounts given by PHC staff and community members.

Two themes were generated from the data analysis: “empowering the subject” and “reproduction of power.” Table A4 in the Appendix shows examples of the open, selective, and theoretical codes, as well as corresponding quotes. Selective codes such as “streamlining of data collection and reporting processes by the CHWs,” “improved relationship between the CHW and the PHC center,” “changing perceptions of the CHWs by the PHC staff,” and “changing perceptions of the CHWs by the community members” helped us analyze human subjectivity. CHWs started to experience acknowledgment and visibility by their immediate network of people that was previously lacking. An enhancement in the sense of self, albeit derived from their network of power relations, helped address the complex aspects of inclusion. On the other hand, selective codes such as “increase in monitoring and surveillance of CHWs,” “CHWs experiencing lack of institutional support” and the “change in power dynamics between Tier 1 and Tier 2 CHWs” helped us analyze aspects of continued control and domination of the primary health system over the CHWs. The state’s governing system was further strengthened by the monitoring and surveillance capabilities of technology, thus reinforcing the CHWs’ perspective of being treated as a point of blame, which consequently generated a sense of exclusion.

## 5 Case Study

The traditional method of data collection and reporting on maternal and child health, such as ante- and postnatal care registration, childbirth, and immunization registrations, and treatment follow-ups, entails Tier 1 CHWs manually writing and recording data in paper-based registers. Each health worker is allotted a set of household visits to conduct within their given radius. During these house visits, the health workers have discussions with the beneficiaries about their health, ongoing treatments, birth/maternal registration, and follow-ups. They manually enter the required health information into the register. Each day CHWs might conduct 20-30 household visits and the data has to be entered in 25-30 registers. The data is then reported to the local PHC center at the end of the week. Frequently, this is difficult for one health worker to do alone so Tier 2 CHWs might assist them with it. The traditional process is thus labor intensive, timeconsuming, and stressful, often leading to delays and errors in the data. In the management of emergencies, it has proven to be especially problematic, as PHC centers might be ill-prepared to provide the right kind of treatment or care, sometimes even resulting in patient mortality. These systematic problems are typically attributed to the failures of CHWs, thereby damaging their credibility among the PHC staff and in their communities.

In 2015, an mHealth intervention, i.e., an Android tablet, was launched to assist and streamline the health worker’s workflow. The tablet was mainly used by Tier 1 CHWs and a few Tier 2 CHWs who assisted them, prioritizing maternal and child health services. The device supported storage and the retrieval of information; a smoother interface to feed in and view data, syncing the data from the tablet to the computer systems in the PHC center, automatic collation of data through the inbuilt software, and GPS functionality and a reminder system.

## 5.1 The Power of Subject Making

## 5.1.1 Higher Level of Self-Efficacy

The introduction of the tablet considerably streamlined the work processes described above. The tablet hosts an inbuilt software that contains various technical features to assist health workers in entering data systematically, which is automatically collated by the tablet software. The tablet is also amenable to being physically carried to different households with all the necessary information at hand. These changes had an inadvertent impact on the relationship between the health workers and the PHC staff.

After the intervention of technology, the PHC supervisor stated:

We always trusted the judgment of the health workers as they are the ones who directly interact with the community, but due to the poor data quality, it was difficult to take their judgment on the beneficiaries seriously … the data was of poor quality because of them … the registers would be filled with mistakes and delays ...

But today they are the primary users of the tablet and are also the ones who put the data in it which is then reported to us. This improvement in reporting has increased our trust in them, the data has fewer errors and as soon as the tablet catches mobile connectivity, it syncs the data into the PHC center computer system.

When asked about this change, the health workers responded by saying that they felt happy (khushi) with this change in perception by the PHC staff. They feel like they are relied on for feedback, as they are the ones who manage the data that is fed into the tablet. They feel important because they are included in conversations with PHC staff about the critical beneficiaries of the village. Jaya and Kiran stated:

The PHC staff take us more seriously now, and sometimes the PHC staff now even ask for our opinion, especially when it comes to certain serious cases. They ask our opinion when they are going through the beneficiary information that has been put by us into the tablet.

Thus, by being able to contribute opinions, CHWs are able to showcase their knowledge, gained from the field, on how to help critical beneficiaries. Before the technology intervention, CHWs often felt overburdened with data collection and reporting tasks, which would often prevent them from having meaningful health-related conversations with community members. Better communication with beneficiaries helps CHWs gain knowledge about the health-related situation of the community, which is put to use when the PHC staff ask for CHWs’ opinions and they are able to provide them.

Community members also mention CHW improvements in terms of the provision of pregnancy benefits provided by the government to low-income households in the country. While there had previously been frequent misallocations of pregnancy benefits by village committee members, this process has been significantly streamlined. In the focus group conversations, some committee members explained:

Since the introduction of the tablet, the CHWs have started to give us better-quality information about the beneficiaries. This has had a direct effect on the scheme allocation.

Initially, because we didn’t have much information or CHWs provided us incorrect information, it would make it difficult for us to allocate the right scheme to the right person. But today the process of scheme allocation has become much more streamlined, as we now receive accurate information from the CHWs.

## 5.1.2 Increased Appreciation and Altruism

Previously, the CHWs were in a vulnerable social position because the PHC staff, by virtue of their hierarchical superiority, frequently attributed data mismanagement to the incompetency of CHWs. Additionally, community members often expressed their dissatisfaction and blamed CHWs for their inability to fulfill their health-related needs.

With the introduction of mHealth devices, the automatic collation feature of the tablet has helped improve the response time in managing high-risk pregnancies. The in-built feature of the tablet automatically collates the data and starts flashing red if it identifies a high-risk pregnancy in need of immediate assistance. Upon receiving this notification, the health workers alert the PHC staff by calling them from their mobile phone. Additionally, if there is good mobile connectivity, the tablet automatically syncs the information with the PHC computer systems.

The improvement in the management of emergency cases has helped to reduce the blame issue. Thus, CHWs who had previously felt that their hard work was not being recognized, now report feeling less excluded and more appreciated. CHWs stated that they now felt that their hard work and knowledge had become visible in the eyes of the community and PHC staff. These changes have increased CHWs’ sense of confidence and self-efficacy, thus improving their altruistic goal fulfillment, i.e., fostering health-related change in their communities and preventing deaths. One Tier 1 health worker, Bhavna, stated:

We feel driven to do our job now. Before, even the community members would blame us for not being able to deal with emergency situations. There would be a lot of confusion and we would be seen as not being capable of doing our jobs. But today we have more confidence when it comes to dealing with emergency cases. We feel happy to be able to serve our community, prevent deaths, and get appreciated for it.

## 5.2 The Power of Subjugating

Despite the positive effects of technology, there were several instances recorded where technology also reinforced existing inefficient processes that translated into negative effects for CHWs.

## 5.2.1 Increase in Workload

Infrastructure issues and a fear of accountability caused an overall increase in the CHWs’ workloads. Mobile connectivity is a key aspect that is necessary to enable the data from the tablet to be remotely synced to the PHC center computer systems; having enough battery charge on the tablet enables the CHWs to smoothly go about their remote data collection and reporting process. In practice, however, these technical advantages translated into technical issues. For instance, every time the tablet’s battery died or the internet data allowance ran out, the health workers were required to travel back to the PHC center to sync the data with the PHC computer systems or charge the tablet. This requirement was mandated by the PHC center in order to maintain the quality, longevity, and utility of the tablets. As the Chamrajnagar district is a rural and tribal area, electricity issues are prevalent. Thus, health workers frequently had to return to the PHC center located in the foothills, which disrupted their workflow and caused delays. Jaya (Tier 1 health worker) explained:

During busy periods, it becomes quite tiring to go all the way back to the PHC center to sync the data or charge the tablet, especially when I am in the middle of collecting beneficiary information. Sometimes the tablet would have to be left there overnight for charging which means I would have to travel all the way to the PHC center in the morning to pick up the tablet before I start my day.

Moreover, the workload was further increased when Tier 1 health workers were burdened with new tasks aligned with the technology intervention. Health workers were asked to collect beneficiary identification information (IDs) to create a digital repository of village member IDs. Binita and Kiran explained:

You see we were also going from one house to another to collect their identification information. So now if someone loses their ID card or forgets to get it to the PHC or subcenter, they can still come and get treated, as their identification information is digitally recorded.

This process of doubly collecting information in addition to their existing responsibilities made their routine workflow even more time-consuming and cumbersome—especially because many community members were reluctant to provide their individual or family information. Health workers also feared liability from the loss of data. If the tablet had a hardware issue, the data would be lost and the CHW would be held responsible for it by the PHC center. Thus, this led to double data collection (paper-based and tablet-based), i.e., CHWs had to enter the data in both the registers and tablets. While health workers generally preferred using the tablet, as it was easier to carry and afforded automatic collation, they (Jaya, Binita, Bhavna, and Kiran) complained about feeling overburdened:

Initially, we had to only collect the information from our routine house visits. But since the use of the tablet, our workload has increased! We have to take all the existing information from the registers and transfer it to the tablet, to also continue to doubly collect data in both registers and tablets, and finally to also collect the beneficiary identification information to maintain the digital repository of the IDs of the beneficiaries! Too much work.

The fear of data accountability was exacerbated by technical issues and further reinforced the existing problems of health workers feeling more disempowered than before.

## 5.2.2 Heightened Surveillance

The notification feature and the implementation of an electronic dashboard at the PHC center subjected health workers to further scrutiny. If a health worker forgot to complete a task or was running behind schedule, the tablet sent them a reminder and the PHC staff could monitor the progress of the health worker’s task completion. The health workers had mixed feelings about this. Some said that the notification feature was useful in reminding them of forgotten tasks, such as timely immunization visits. Others felt uncomfortable about being monitored during their routine work processes and wanted the space and freedom to do tasks at their own pace and discretion. For instance, due to the institutional rules and norms associated with tablet use, health workers were compelled to perform house visits when the tablet reminded them to or visit the PHC center when the tablet’s battery died. This was further enforced by increased monitoring of their activities via the electronic dashboard. Some health workers now worked under pressure because they were fearful of being held accountable and losing their jobs. Binita and Bhavna commented:

What is this, our routine job of conducting house-to-house visits is hard as it is, and now we are being watched! We would like our freedom to do our tasks when it is suitable for us. We have other responsibilities as well, like taking care of our children and husbands or doing our own household work.

Structurally and institutionally, the health worker role does not permit them to question the legitimacy of technology use. Therefore, they become docile users of technology and do what the technology directs them to, even if they find certain aspects of it inconvenient. Their existing subservient relationship with the PHC center discourages them from raising any complaints or resisting the rules governing technology use. Thus, they normalize being overburdened as part of their work routine. In this situation, technology becomes a medium to reproduce the PHC system’s control over the CHWs, undermining the CHWs’ autonomy and flexibility in their everyday workflow which they were previously entitled to.

## 5.2.3 Exclusion within CHWs

The primary healthcare structure in India specifies an official hierarchy of health workers consisting of three tiers of health workers (Scott et al., 2020). Tier 1, or senior-level health workers, are recruited by the PHC center based on their education qualifications (whether they completed basic schooling) and are paid a salary. Tier 2 health workers are recruited as volunteers and are paid based on meeting their targets (set by the PHC center) and operate at the village level. The primary task of Tier 2 CHWs is to assist Tier 1 health workers in data collection and reporting and to also educate the community about health, nutrition, and sanitation issues. Tier 3 CHWs primarily educate children about health and sanitation and are the most junior level of CHWs. In our PHC center, Tier 3 CHWs were not involved in the use of the tablet, as their primary role did not entail data collection and reporting<sup>2</sup> (Scott et al., 2020).

While in some PHC centers Tier 2 workers directly report to Tier 1 workers, in others they work collaboratively to deliver health services to their community (Scott et al., 2019). In the PHC center where this study was conducted, Tier 2 workers worked collaboratively with Tier 1 health workers, e.g., undertaking certain house visits on behalf of Tier 1 CHWs. However, this collaborative dynamic was negatively impacted by the intervention of technology.

During the implementation phase, the institutional rule (set by the PHC center) on tablet use stipulated that only Tier 1 health workers would be allowed to use it, as they were better educated, experienced, and trained. In cases where the existing relationship between the Tier 1 and Tier 2 health workers followed along the lines of sisterhood, senior health workers, at their discretion, let the Tier 2 CHWs use the tablet, thus enabling the latter to continue to assist the former in routine tasks. But in cases, where the existing relationship between the two tiers was more formal and less personal, senior workers prevented the junior health workers from using the tablet. As a result, the junior workers felt excluded and unimportant or, in some cases, even felt belittled and discriminated against. As Yashti and Rajeshri (Tier 2 health workers) shared:

We were the biggest support they [Tier 1 workers] had before. The process of filling up 25-30 registers and then reporting it to the PHC center was not an easy task. We would often relieve them of some of the burden by doing the house visits ourselves and reporting the data to them, who would then fill in the register. But since they have started using the technology, they do not involve us as much, they want all the recognition for themselves! We sometimes ask the supervisor at the PHC center to let us use the tablet as well.

The inclusion of technology in the existing work processes has thus inadvertently led to the exclusion of Tier 2 health workers. While this was not reported by all Tier 2 workers, some of them did feel less valued and redundant and therefore less motivated to perform their responsibilities. Thus, the introduction of the technology, to some extent, reinforced and formalized the hierarchy among health workers, leading to the exclusion of some.

## 5.3 Analysis: The Subjugation and Subject Making of CHWs

The above case of mHealth adoption reveals why digital inclusion as empowerment may be problematic and contested. From a Foucauldian perspective, the human subject is both tied to their own identity but also subject to control and dependence. The mHealth application shaped the subjectivity of the CHWs embedded within the system of power and reconfigured their positionality and experience of being simultaneously empowered and disempowered, or simultaneously included and excluded, in the PHC system.

The intervention of technology triggered a change in the dynamics of the CHWs’ network of social relations. Due to the streamlining of the CHW’s work processes, they received more respect than blame from the PHC staff and community for their contributions to the health and well-being of the community. Consequently, CHWs felt more psychologically empowered and motivated to perform their routine tasks as community health workers. Technology as enacted in CHW’s daily practices engendered a sense of responsibility through the changed perceptions of the very actors (social relations) that had previously reduced them to a target of blame and exclusion. In a Foucauldian sense, their self-motivation and selfesteem regarding their CHW role were enhanced through the use of technology.

However, this same technology also reinforced a sense of powerlessness and subjugation among the CHWs. The mHealth application reproduced the existing hierarchical relationship and strengthened the disciplinary control over the CHWs (Foucault, 1977), through real-time surveillance and tracking by the PHC center. Therefore, the CHWs’ sense of autonomy was also significantly restricted by the same disciplinary control (Rose et al., 2006), which was further exacerbated by a lack of institutional support. The digitization of data collection and its visible benefits also legitimized the excessive workload associated with data collection that was added to the CHW’s daily workflow at the expense of their well-being and personal lives. Meanwhile, a digital divide emerged between Tier 1 and Tier 2 CHWs, leading to the exclusion of the latter and undermining the existing sisterhood among CHWs. Thus, the heightened sense of autonomy and responsibility was incorporated into the institutional structures of domination at the PHC center.

From the perspective of Foucault’s governmentality (Burchell et al., 1991), the technology artifact is entangled and enacted in the CHWs’ network of power relations, which normalizes control and discipline by the PHC center while also fostering self-motivation by injecting new meanings to the job role of the health worker. The analytical lens of subjectivity and power reveals how technology shapes the reconciliation and tensions between individual efficacy and the domination of power.

To summarize, the empowerment of individuals takes place within existing systems of domination and control and occurs simultaneously with disempowerment. This paper thus makes contributions to the literature on digital inclusion by providing a more granulated analysis. In the following section, we discuss the contributions and implications of the study for both theory and practice.

## 6 Implications for Digital Inclusion Research and Practice

## 6.1 Linking Power, Technology, and the Subject

There is a general neglect of power in understanding digital inclusion in most IS research. In this study, Foucault’s analytics of power is used to explore the constitution of human subjectivity as part of digital inclusion. Foucault has been often applied in IS literature to study surveillance and power (Lyon, 1994; 2003; Doolin, 1998; 2004, Brooke, 2002; Introna, 2001; 2003). However, this paper offers a novel example of applying the Foucauldian conceptualization of the subject and power to examine how the everyday production and reproduction of power can affect human subjectivity (Bloomfield 1995; Markula, 2003).

As subjectivity is constituted in an individual’s day-today practice, the mHealth case can be seen as a type of technology-in-practice (Orlikowski, 2000) embedded in the PHC procedures and processes and enacted in the day-to-day practices of CHWs, thereby shaping their subjectivity as an active member of the PHC and local communities. It is through the integration in routinized practice that digital systems acquire the power of governmentality, namely not necessarily in relation to the state apparatus, but in terms of “directing souls” and shaping conducts (Lemke, 2002). In this case, mHealth relayed and reproduced the hierarchical power of the PHC, which subjugated the CHWs by normalizing higher workloads, constant tracking and monitoring, and differentiating levels of digital skills among various tiers of CHWs.

In contrast, the mHealth adoption also effected the subject making of the CHWs, i.e., generating a sense of empowerment. In this case, the mHealth application appealed to their sense of altruism, self-efficacy, and self-responsibility, which is different from the usual association of governmentality with neoliberalism, which seeks to promote individuals’ sense of autonomy and self-management (Lemke, 2002b; Rose et al., 2006; Moisander et al., 2018). Nevertheless, the mHealth application enacts a mode of power that simultaneously subordinates and produces subjectivity (Mackenzie & McKinlay, 2020). Such a perspective puts emphasis on the relationality of human subjectivities, which paves a pathway toward understanding the complex positionality of the human actor as embedded in everyday social processes that are shaped by multiple power dynamics. Technology is placed at the crux of the contradiction between individual subjectivity and power structures.

In this study, we observe how individual empowerment occurs despite the reproduction of disciplinary power, but there was no evidence of critical awareness on the part of the CHWs with regard to the structural limitations on the self that could be indicative of individual resistance or destabilization of power structures (Raffnsøe et al., 2019). In other words, CHWs are, to a certain extent, included and empowered as more effective, trusted, and respected actors in the community and the PHC system, but not to an extent where they could be considered to be agents of change or even given possibilities for “self-formation” (Munro, 2014). This simultaneous facilitation of disempowerment and empowerment in digital inclusion has been rarely explored within the IS literature.

## 6.2 Digital Inclusion as Empowerment

As reviewed earlier, the IS literature on digital inclusion predominantly interprets the concept as either providing digital accessibility or social capital for community participation. By adopting a perspective of digital inclusion as empowerment, this study advances the existing discourse on the relationship between technology and social inclusion by integrating the dimensions of power and human subjectivities from a Foucauldian perspective. Illuminating the various digitally mediated subjectivities helps us understand that human actors are always positioned at the intersection of various power relations with heterogeneous institutional norms, rules, and rhetoric, which explains why and how digital inclusion and exclusion often simultaneously occur in a given context (Trauth, 2017; Zheng & Walsham, 2021).

This study specifically contributes to digital inclusion research that conceives inclusion as (a contested process of) empowerment—in other words, individuals are included not only as users or participants but also as actors with a sense of agency and autonomy and the ability to make a difference (Baron & Gomez, 2013; Díaz Andrade & Doolin, 2016). Empowerment is multidimensional in nature. It is relational and transformative and encompasses various categories both at an individual level and at broader structural levels (Zimmerman, 1995; Alsop & Heinshohn, 2005). Thus, in our case, we observed CHWs being psychologically empowered but not institutionally empowered. A detailed analysis of the subjectivities of CHWs presents a more nuanced and qualified view of inclusion as empowerment, where inclusion and empowerment are made possible within a larger project of governance with fostered dominance and control. Digital inclusion here means not only a higher level of visibility, recognition, and respect in the community but also increased confidence and self-efficacy. However, at a systemic level, the CHWs are even more restricted than before due to technology’s enmeshment within existing rules, norms, and practices, which sheds light on why the fourth level of digital inclusion (structural transformation), where participants become agents of change, is difficult to achieve.

By seeing technology users as social actors situated in power relations, we highlight the impact of technology in everyday life. It is in everyday life that human actors reproduce social structures in which the social norms and relations that govern their very being are embedded (Orlikowski, 2000). From this perspective, a more granulated and practice-based understanding can be derived in relation to digital inclusion, as opposed to a top-down, category-driven understanding of technology’s interaction with its users. In other words, it addresses the how question rather than the who question and moves beyond a binary opposition of digital inclusion and exclusion.

## 6.3 Implications for Practice

What are the implications of this discussion for public policy and practice? First, digital inclusion takes place at multiple levels, therefore both the public and academic discourse should not only be based on the demographic categorization of social groups—for example, gender, age, disability, and so on—but also on the processes of inclusion where power structures are inevitably engrained. Digital inclusion is therefore more complex and dynamic than providing infrastructure, skills training, and participation. By centering power and subjectivity in the discussion of digital inclusion, we echo the suggestion of Zheng and Walsham (2021) to treat the included not as users of digital technology or a passive recipient of assistance, but as actors positioned in a network of power relations, whose sense of subjectivity is valued.

In some cases, top-down initiatives of digital inclusion are limited to integrating people into a digitalized system to improve its efficiency, while reproducing and reinforcing existing institutionalized power structures (Pandey & Zheng, 2019). This may give rise to the instrumentalization and subjugation of individuals. Instead of seeing individuals as “users” or “recipients,” they could be considered as active, reflective actors embedded in structures of norms, values, and power relations (Pandey & Zheng, 2023).

Digital design and policies may also serve to emancipate and empower individuals beyond merely providing access to technology.

Importantly, this takes us to move beyond the question of “who is included” to “how they are included and under what terms” For instance, when looking at the social inclusion of women in the workforce, even in countries where women enjoy premium employment opportunities, many women are still paid less than men for the same type of work or have to endure a highly masculine organizational culture (Stamarski & Son Hing, 2015; Padavic, et al., 2019). Similarly, regarding digital inclusion, while the digital economy has facilitated social inclusion of disadvantaged and marginalized populations by lowering participant thresholds and transaction costs, these very systems are still entangled in existing power networks and tend to reproduce or exacerbate offline social inequalities, e.g., by subjecting workers to surveillance, exploitation, and precarious work conditions (Greenwood et al., 2017; Malin & Chandler, 2017; Zheng & Wu, 2022). It is for this reason that a focus on human subjectivities adds value by offering a more sensitizing perspective in the discourse of digital inclusion.

For policy makers, digital designers, and facilitators of digital programs, it may be important to consider the objectives and social conditions against which digital inclusion is implemented. Integrating the users of technology in the technology implementation process or engaging continuous feedback from users on their use of the technology can help engineers and designers better understand the social implications of technology on the everyday life of the users.

Finally, it would benefit digital inclusion researchers and practitioners to adopt qualitative and ethnographic methods of data collection. Development program evaluators tend to rely on their own judgment of what is of value versus seeking to understand from the recipient’s perspective how spaces of inclusion or exclusion are being created for them (Kabeer, 2005; 2001; 1999). By adopting a qualitative research design, we were able to investigate the aspect of digital inclusion and exclusion from people’s own retrospective narratives. Interviews and field observations were pivotal for us in understanding the subtle process of the duality of empowerment and disempowerment that was facilitated through technology for the CHWs. Thus, a qualitative research design helped capture the subjective perspective of digital inclusion and exclusion.

## 7 Conclusion

This paper presents a case study of how an mHealth application facilitated the inclusion of CHWs within the system of power of the PHC system. The dual effect of empowerment and disempowerment was only made visible by recognizing how processes of inclusion were rooted within the power structures of the context that governs the social relations and subjectivity of the CHWs. The paper provides a sensitizing perspective to differentiate diverse connotations of digital inclusion, from accessibility and or participation to empowerment and agency for change and various mechanisms that simultaneously reconfigure the practice and implications of inclusion and exclusion. Furthermore, we propose a conception of digital technology as “technologies of power” and constitutive of governmentality, which entails a complex relationship between power and human subjectivity. Such a perspective enriches and deepens our understanding of digital inclusion as a political process that is dynamic and contested.

This study is limited in that it does not explicitly or extensively investigate the role of gender as entangled in CHWs’ subjectivity and work. Although some aspects of this are reflected in the findings—for example, the fact that they are obliged to perform household tasks and respond to the needs of family members at various times of the day, in parallel to working as CHWs. Instead of using any gender-based perspective, we draw upon Foucault (1982) to focus on the importance of the subjectivity of health workers that is produced and reproduced in their everyday practices on processes of digital inclusion. Technology can assist human actors by providing higher levels of self-efficacy and agency, thereby fostering psychological empowerment at an individual level. However, such empowerment does not necessarily challenge existing structures of power or transform one’s position within the dominant system of power.

Future studies on community health worker practices could adopt a gender-based perspective to study how they navigate spaces of inclusion and exclusion as rooted in gendered power structures. For instance, does the CHW’s relationship with her mother-in-law or husband impact the way she carries out her work? And does this add a new layer of exclusion for her in her everyday work? Furthermore, moving beyond the context of this paper, we also encourage future research to examine the issue of intersectionality in digital inclusion (Dy et al., 2017; Zheng & Walsham, 2021). How does being positioned at the intersection of various systems of power impact a human actor’s ability to be included? And what role does technology play in it?

Lastly, it would also be interesting to conduct longitudinal studies to investigate whether the adoption of technology and the related enhancement of self-efficacy and responsibility could, over time, lead to some level of critical awareness of participants in terms of social structures and plant the seeds for broader possibilities of change in policies and institutional arrangements. This level of analysis would contribute to the fourth level of inclusion, which looks at structural transformation.

Priyanka Pandey is currently a lecturer in Public Services and Technology at King’s Business School. Her research investigates the role digital artifacts and platforms play in everyday practices, with a particular emphasis on structures, cultures, institutional mechanisms, and power processes. She is interested in deconstructing the relationship between technology and social and organisational change, within the public sector, and Global South context.

Yingqin Zheng is a senior lecturer in the School of Business and Management, Royal Holloway, University of London (RHUL). Her research interests lie in the social and ethical implications of digital transformation, with a particular focus on digital inclusion and digital justice. She is a co-founder of the Digital Organisation and Society Research Centre at RHUL and serves as a senior editor at Information Systems Journal.
