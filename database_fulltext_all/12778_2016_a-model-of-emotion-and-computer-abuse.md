## A B S T R A C T

Internal computer abuse has received considerable research attention as a significant source of IS security incidents in organizations. We examine the effects of both organizational and individual factors on individuals’ computer abuse intent. A theoretical model is developed based on two theories: abuse opportunity structure and emotion process. We empirically tested the model with 205 working professionals. We found that the abuse opportunity structure in organizations affects an individual’s goal conduciveness, which in turn affects their abuse-positive affect. We also found that morality affects the abuse-positive affect, which in turn mediates the relationship between morality and abuse intent.

## 1. Introduction

More than 40% of security breaches are committed by disgruntled employees inside of an organization [26,28,103]. For example, insider misuse and unauthorized access to information by insiders have been identified as the top two security threats to businesses [128,149]. Current and former employees are the most frequent perpetrators of intellectual property theft, which costs US businesses more than \$250 billion/year [113]. Even this reported statistic on insider abuse might be underestimated, as most organizations are reluctant to disclose such information because it could critically damage their reputation and business [36,97,103].

Prior studies have focused on the issue of compliance or noncompliance with IS security policies [6,115,137,138,149]. Assuming that employees are well intentioned, these studies have examined factors affecting compliance with security policies [15,31,63,116,151]. Although there are a small number of studies on insider threats [66,100,128], a majority of studies have examined the compliance issue in an attempt to maximize deterrence and prevention efforts. Keeney et al. [68] studied 49 cases of insider sabotage against information systems (IS). They found that in 83 percent of cases, grievances held by these perpetrators triggered criminal actions. Recent studies note that organizations need a more holistic approach that encompasses both the thought processes of the potential offender and the organizational context [148,149]. Our study fills this research gap.

To reduce insider abuse, understanding the psychological processes of disgruntled computer abusers within organizational structures is important [86]. Computer abuse refers to the ‘‘unauthorized, deliberate, and internally recognizable misuse of assets of the local organizational information systems by individuals’’ [125, p. 47]. Computer abuse is intricately linked to the psychological attributes of the abuser and to the organizational setting [73]. Prior research efforts have been focused on a controlfunction notion that is based on deterrence theory (DT). DT indicates that organizations should focus on various counter measures (e.g., security policies, security education, training, awareness programs, and monitoring) to deter computer abuse behavior. Such controls are known to be effective for limiting abuse [72]. For example, being aware of security sanctions and the severity of sanctions are found to be effective for reducing computer abuse [26].

Understanding and validating both organizational and individual factors and processes within an insider abuse context is crucial in the prevention of computer abuse. Prior studies of the relationship between the organizational setting and individual factors (e.g.) [146] suggest that they might have a significant impact on computer abuse behaviors. Ekblom [33] contends that explanations of how offenders interact with the settings in which a crime may or may not occur will help us better understand criminal behavior. The majority of prior studies on computer abuse have focused on cognition-based models [53,71,72,122]. These models have captured cognitional responses from the computer abuser and their influence on abuse behaviors. However, several prior studies [86,120] indicate that abuse behaviors such as sabotage and theft may involve emotional factors. Furthermore, recent case studies on computer abuse [9,83,90] have noted the critical presence of emotions as a psychological factor in computer abusers. There is a need to develop a framework that can predict the occurrence of emotions, along with cognitive factors, and explicate their consequences for computer abuse behaviors [116,118].

In this paper, we focus on investigating how organizational settings, such as the level of liberty and facilitation, affect employees’ emotional processes and may lead potential abusers to computer abuse intent/behaviors. Our review of the literature in this area led us to identify the current limitations in the general understanding of how organizational and individual factors (such as emotions and morality) affect computer abuse intent and behaviors.

Our study aims to investigate and empirically validate how organizational settings and individual factors (i.e., emotional factors and morality) influence computer abuse intent. For this purpose, we develop a theoretical model based on a theory of computer crime opportunity structure [147] and on a theory of emotion process [43,44]. In the following section, we review related literature and develop a theoretical model. Then, we report our research methodology and our tests of the model using an experiment. Finally, we discuss our findings and present our contributions.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Insider computer abuse

We conducted a literature review on insider computer abuse in the IS literature. Using security as a main keyword and carefully examining the articles, we collected 116 articles from top IS journals (over the period between 2007 and 2012) including Decision Sciences, DSS, EJIS, ISR, JIS, JMIS, JAIS and MISQ. We found thirteen articles using insider and threat as main keywords. Following this, a forward snowballing search turned up eighteen additional articles. The security orientation of IS research on insider computer abuse mostly follows a control-functional direction (see Appendix A). Such work includes security education and situational crime prevention techniques [146]. This work seeks to explain and justify security improvements through organizational factors and individual psychological factors.

Much of the past research has focused on the effectiveness of deterrent controls anchored to GDT within an organizational perspective (see Appendix A). Such research explores how managers can create effective deterrence policies by being aware of effects and alternative behaviors [123]. These prior studies show that such controls are effective deterrents. The focus of the study of internal abuse has evolved from organizational factors to individual factors and the combination of both as shown in

Appendix A. Prior studies on individual factors have focused on cognitive and rational factors. For instance, recent studies on insider abuse identified individual factors such as policy awareness and self-efficacy. In particular, the internal perceptions of potential abusers, especially with regard to organizational deterrence policies, have proved significant for limiting abuse [72]. In addition, ensuring that potential abusers are aware of policies and the severity of sanctions has also been found to be effective for limiting abuse [26].

Our study is motivated by the need for research focusing on both organizational and individual factors, particularly factors related to emotion. While the psychological effects of controls on organizational computer users are important, a more comprehensive understanding will emerge from situating the psychological process of a potential computer abuser within the context of their organizational setting. A recent study [149] identified the important role of emotion. By expanding the scope of a security study to include an organization-centric component and an individual-centric component (particularly including emotion), we can grasp both the situation in which the individual is embedded and the psychological process that ensues. To achieve this goal, we develop an integrated computer abuse model that incorporates both an organization-centric component and an individual-centric component that incorporates emotion. Then, we empirically test the model. In the following section, we explain the emotion process model.

## 2.2. Emotion process model

To explain computer abuse behaviors, we draw on the study of emotion [8,59], which suggests two approaches: a discrete emotion approach and a componential approach. The componential approach focuses on the components related to emotional experience and their relationships. Much of the IS emotion research (e.g.) [10,14,63,139] has employed a discrete emotion approach that focuses on a small set of fundamental emotions such as anger, fear, shame, guilt, happiness, and joy [8,60,91,131]. This approach enables researchers to explore the causality between emotion and cognition [60]. However, it also limits the analysis of emotion to a few defined sets of basic emotions [89] instead of taking a holistic view of emotion processes. This focus on discrete emotions in IS research suggests that the IS field has only a partial understanding of the emotion processes.

Our study follows a componential approach that situates emotion as a process (e.g.) [3,34,38,59]. Each component (for example, event, appraisal, action readiness, etc.) of the emotion process influences the others as shown in Fig. 1 [43,84]. Instead of limiting the investigation to a particular discrete emotion, this approach decomposes emotional experiences into detailed components (e.g., [43,44,89,111]). It provides a broad set of emotions defined by various types of appraisals [59]. This approach also benefits researchers by permitting a systematic and detailed explanation of how emotions are shaped and interact [89].

Fig. 1 shows an emotion process model adapted from Frijda [42–44], which employs the componential approach and provides theoretical support for the development of our research framework. Frijda argues that once an individual encounters emotionally significant and relevant events or situations, s/he appraises them. This appraisal influences affective response and action readiness, which in turn leads to his/her behavior. Regulation, originating internally in the individual’s conscience, or morality influences appraisal, affect, and action readiness through either attenuation or amplification. Fig. 1 represents key components of the emotion process model. Because we adapt the model parsimoniously, a few components, for example, arousal, behavior, and conditions of components, are omitted from the original model. For example, arousal refers to bodily responses such as feeling hot, a change in heartbeat, and sweat [110]. These bodily responses occur as a sense of mobilization of energy in the body. Measuring these responses reliably and accurately is difficult.

Fig. 1. The central emotion process model [42–44]. Note: It represents an adapted emotion process model and shows key components relevant to our research.

The model benefits researchers by offering a more precise understanding of the formation of emotionally significant behaviors (such as computer abuse) because it describes the spontaneous occurrence (or change) of emotion and the consequential behavior [9,41]. In addition, this model includes an organizational setting component (event), a cognitive component (appraisal), an emotional component (affect), and a behavioral component (action readiness), providing a holistic view of how an emotionally significant behavior occurs. Furthermore, Karstedt [67] notes that the model is applicable to an institutional context that involves regulatory processes and controls (regulation).

We therefore adopt the emotion process model as a building block for explaining computer abuse intentions in organizational settings. We also subscribe to a view that integrates both individuals’ emotion processes and organizational settings [9]. In the next two sections, we describe the organizational and individual factors associated with computer abuse.

## 2.3. Organizational factors

Event. An event is an input with a particular meaning in the emotion process model. An important event for one’s goals or concerns will trigger one’s appraisal and affect [40]. ‘‘Events that satisfy the individual’s goals, or promise to do so, yield positive emotions; events that harm or threaten the individual’s concerns lead to negative emotions’’ [40, p. 349]. In the context of computer abuse, an employee experiences many different types of events as his/her concerns change over time. For example, such events include demotion, pay-cuts, discord with colleagues, and organizational computer monitoring [98]. (To represent the organizational factors that influence individuals’ emotion processes, such an event was used in our experimental scenarios for model testing.)

Abuse opportunity structure. When information systems in an organization are not properly guarded, organizational settings provide a potential abuser with a computer abuse opportunity. After an event occurs, individuals may create abuse goals and assess organizational security settings. We treated the event as an external component in our conceptual model. An organization’s security settings may work as antecedents to the appraisal process. Although individuals may develop the goal to abuse, they would create their abuse intent after evaluating whether an organization’s security settings are conducive to their achieving their goal.

An organization’s security settings may provide an abuse opportunity structure. The notion of an abuse opportunity structure [9] is grounded in prior studies on opportunities for computer crime [22,145,147]. In an abuse opportunity structure, the absence of proper safeguards on information systems and assets in organizations can create a criminal opportunity for computer abusers. In addition, potential abusers may assess the opportunities available as being attractive for abusive acts. An abuse opportunity structure is developed from a perception of an environment where a potential abuser can commit a crime without being caught. This structure describes the nature of computer crime opportunities in organizations. It also explains the motivations and behaviors of abusers. A potential abuser’s assessment of an organization’s abuse opportunity structure plays a critical role in the commission of a crime [146]. The abuse opportunity structure, which includes liberty and facilitation, is described in detail in the research model section below.

## 2.4. Individual factors

Appraisal. Appraisal refers to the cognitive processes that link perception of an event to an emotional meaning [45]. Appraisal can be understood within many different dimensions such as goal conduciveness, novelty, familiarity, controllability, and fairness (e.g.) [43,44,74]. In our study, we particularly choose ‘goal conduciveness’ as a dimension that indicates whether a target abuse opportunity structure is conducive (i.e., supportive) to an abuser achieving his/her abuse goals. Clarke [22] and Willison [145] explain how a potential abuser assesses whether the situation under a specific organizational structure is conducive to committing abuse. Goal conduciveness is a critical construct that allows us to estimate the possibility of a computer abuse event occurring or not.

Affect. Affect indicates the primitive, irreducible, and raw feelings that are apparent in moods and emotions [41,109]. Affect is an umbrella term encompassing a broad range of feelings that individuals experience, including current feeling states, short-term affective experiences, and feeling traits. Feeling traits are more stable tendencies to feel and act in certain ways [8].

Prior studies (e.g.) [7,8,109] conceptualize a circumplex model of affect within two dimensions: the horizontal dimension ranges from pleasure to displeasure; and the vertical dimension ranges from activation to deactivation. A feeling is explained not only by pleasantness but also by activation values. For example, ‘‘I feel happy’’, and ‘‘I feel excited’’ are considered pleasant-activated feelings. In this example, we find information including specific names of emotions such as happiness and excitement and a tendency to experience pleasant and energized feelings. On the other hand, ‘‘I feel pretty dull’’ and ‘‘I’m exhausted’’ are unpleasantdeactivated feelings. The examples indicate a tendency to experience unpleasant and debilitated feelings.

One set of measures for affect based on such a circumplex model is the positive affect-negative affect schedule (PANAS). PANAS measures have been widely used in a variety of disciplines to report the extent to which individuals experience emotions (e.g.) [7,25,127,129,134,141]. Drawing on these measures, we define abuse-positive affect as the pleasant-activated feelings that potential abusers experience when they appraise a situation as supporting the achievement of their goal to abuse information systems. (The idealized abuse-positive thought here is ‘‘I like the idea of ruining this system and I can’t wait to wreck it.’’) A possible abuse-positive affect includes pleasant-activated feelings such as excited, interested, determined, enthusiastic, energetic, and inspired. Abuse-positive affect can be an essential predictor of abuse intent by potential abusers [42].

Action Readiness. Action readiness corresponds to abuse intent in our research model. Action readiness refers to an ‘‘individuals readiness or unreadiness to engage in interaction with the environment. Readiness may consist of action tendency, that is, ‘‘readiness to engage in or disengage from interaction with some goal object in some particular fashion’’ [44, p. 213]. In the original emotion process model, action readiness is a construct that results from appraisal. Based on a study by Bagozzi [4] that suggests that emotion motivates action readiness, we adapt the original emotion process model and cast action readiness as a resultant variable. The concept of action readiness fits well within the context of insider computer abuse when an individual intends to commit computer abuse in the workplace.

Regulation. Regulation maps into morality in our model. Regulation indicates a process that attenuates or enhances the components (such as appraisal, affect, and action readiness) of the emotion process [38,45]. Morality is a potential source for a regulation process that originates from within the individual. The prior literature has speculated that morality is an individual factor that influences abuse intent [26]. For instance, the theory of planned behavior (TPB) explains how individual factors such as normative beliefs and subjective norms can influence abuse intent. Myyry et al. [87] found empirical evidence that morality can affect compliance with information security policy. Their study implied that the relationship between the two is context-bound. In our study, we investigate the influence of individual morality not only on abuse intent but also on goal conduciveness and abuse-positive affect.

## 3.1. Abuse opportunity structure

Clarke [22] identifies three types of factors encompassed by crime opportunity structures: (1) victims; (2) targets; and (3) facilitators. Willison [145] and Willison and Backhouse [147] adapt Clarke’s model in the context of computer fraud in an organization. They adapted victims to mean those organizations abused by offenders. They adapted facilitators to include factors such as the physical environment and a person’s skill. They adapted targets to include factors such as computerized accounting records and bank account details. In developing an emote opportunity model of computer abuse, further work by Baskerville et al. [9] replaced targets with a different concept, liberty, and integrated the crimespecific opportunity structure with an emotion process model. The liberty to access a target (instead of the target itself) is a critical factor of security management in an organization because management has more diverse means for managing liberty than just modifying the target [9]. By adopting this view, our model of the abuse opportunity structure has only two factors: liberty and facilitation.

## 3.1.1. Liberty

The basic notion of liberty is that individuals are free and sovereign; that is, they have the freedom to command their actions. It is a state of nature in which opportunities arise to harm others, and thus society must consent to limit liberty [108]. In societies (and organizations), the practical existence of liberty is a tension between individual sovereignty (freedom) and imposed limits to that sovereignty (controls). Liberty and self-control are conceptually different. Self-control refers to the capacity to change and adapt the self to produce a better, more optimal fit between the self and the world [107]. Hu et al. (2013) found that self-control plays an important role in information security policy compliance.

In our model, we define liberty as the freedom for a potential abuser to access a target due to lack of controls and safeguards on the information systems and assets in an organization [9]. Clarke [22] and Willison [145] consider freedom of movement and lack of controls as important factors that lead to potential offenses. Before any organizational security measures are in place, every employee desires to preserve his/her liberty to use the information systems. Organization–employee contracts, such as agreed computer use policies, often intend to avoid possible conflicts. Inside abusers are those that decide to ignore the organizations’ policy-contracts, even though they have promised to abide by them, because these contracts limit liberty to protect information systems. Some organizations put security controls in place to safeguard their information systems and limit employees’ liberty. Other organizations, however, do not implement even the most basic controls, perhaps out of complacency. This security deficiency creates opportunities for potential abusers to exploit liberty to harm information systems in organizations [22].

Liberty is a simplification/compilation of three constructs from Willison’s model [147]: the physical environment, operating routines, and the target (each of which may have control features). For our work, the construct of interest was whether the physical environment and/or the operating routines failed to form a sufficiently protective envelope around a target for abuse.

## 3.1.2. Facilitation

The concept of facilitation addresses the processes or assets that provide ancillary encouragement for abuse [9]. Not only the knowledge and skills of potential abusers but also the physica office settings and tools (that allow potential abusers to easily abuse information systems) can be considered facilitation. Facilitation can also be processes or assets that provide ancillary encouragement for abuse. In an information systems abuse situation, facilitation might involve lax attitudes about information security that lead to easily available tools for abuse [152]. An example of facilitation would include situations where all of the important software is stored at one central server with no required access ID and password.

## 3.2. Goal conduciveness

Goal conduciveness indicates whether a situation or an event is conducive to or supportive of achieving one’s goals or is consistent with what one wants [35,39,106,112]. When a person perceives a situation or an event to be relevant to his/her goal, s/he appraises whether it appears to favor or hinder it [34]. In the context of computer abuse, feeling betrayed and mistreated by their companies, potential abusers are likely to appraise the abuse opportunity structure (i.e., liberty and facilitation) as being relevant to achieving their goal of computer abuse [147]. Then, they may appraise the levels of liberty and facilitation as supporting the achievement of their abuse goals [22,145]. Here, high levels of liberty and facilitation may be more likely to create an atmosphere in which potential abusers to perceive it to be easy to achieve their goal to abuse information systems.

In the insider sabotage cases at Omega Engineering [83] and Mathis Network Technology [90], for instance, computer abusers who were network server managers had high levels of liberty, and they accessed target servers freely. They also found that they were able to access network servers with no official supervision or related IS security policy in place, which indicates a situation with a high level of facilitation. They could abuse information systems with ease. Therefore, we argue that a potential abuser is more likely to appraise a situation as having a higher level of goal conduciveness when s/he perceives higher levels of liberty and facilitation in the abuse opportunity structure. We thus hypothesize that

Hypothesis 1. The abuse opportunity structure positively influences goal conduciveness.

## 3.3. Abuse-positive affect

Abuse-positive affect refers to pleasant-activated feelings. This construct is conceptualized based on the concept of affect, which is an integral blend of the pleasantness and activation dimensions (e.g.) [7,8,109,141]. We recall that two polarities (activated vs. deactivated) of activation exist in pleasantness. Our study focuses on pleasant-activated feelings.

The concept of abuse-positive affect advances research on computer abuse in two ways. First, pleasant-activated feelings working in the context of computer abuse provides a fine-grained explanation of the feeling traits of potential abusers. Barrett and Russel [7] note that researchers may miss the forest for the trees, that is, they focus only on the valence (pleasant and unpleasant quality) of affect (without considering activation). Their empirical findings indicate that the level of activation and deactivation makes a significant semantic difference. Another study [142] suggests that activation and deactivation tend to associate with approach- and withdrawal-oriented behaviors, respectively. In particular, the activation value can provide insight into expected computer abuse intent and behaviors.

Second, abuse-positive affect is an important predictor of computer abuse intent. Prior studies [86,147] recognize that insider-abusers experience negative affect when they have adverse motives originating from either their personal or their workrelated situations. However, no investigation has yet explored how the positive affect caused by the appraisal of goal conduciveness influences computer abuse intent. For example, potential abusers may experience a certain type of emotion based on their assessment of the security environment. We do not know what emotion they would experience and whether the emotion would lead to abuse intention. Our study addresses this research gap by conceptualizing the abuse-positive construct and testing it.

We argue that potential abusers are likely to feel higher abusepositive affect when they find that the organization’s opportunity structure supports achieving their goal of computer abuse. Here, the potential abuser may feel excited, enthusiastic, energetic, and inspired about abuse, with a high activation level, thus leading to an increase in abuse intent. Appraisal theorists have contended that goal conduciveness indicates the importance of an event to one’s goal achievement (e.g.) [35,38,106,112,135]. Goal conduciveness is an essential appraisal that triggers positive affect based on the consistency of the desired state for goal achievement and the actual state of an event [3,112]. This effect occurs because goal conduciveness is ‘‘a necessary condition for an emotional response to an event or happening . . . a person has a personal stake in it and at the same time judges the event or happening to facilitate or thwart this stake’’ [3, p. 185]. Therefore, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 2. Goal conduciveness positively influences abusepositive affect.

The positive relationship between abuse-positive affect and abuse intent is grounded in the basic premise of the theory of emotion process: a high positive affect is likely to be associated with high action readiness for a particular behavior [42–44]. In a study of goal-directed behaviors, Bagozzi and Pieters [4] support this position by finding that high positive and negative emotions influence dieting and exercising volitions (or intentions) and subsequent dieting and exercising behaviors. They suggest that positive emotions lead to a decision to continue with the present plan to achieve a goal. A pleasant-activated affect can increase the likelihood that goal-directed activities will be performed [142]. Abuse-positive affect is a pleasant-activated feeling that may lead to a certain action. In the study of IS security policy violations, Siponen and Vance [116] observe that people who feel shame are less likely to have intentions to violate IS security policy, although shame, in their study, is included as a deterrent. In the computer abuse context, we argue that an individual is more likely to have abuse intent when s/he experiences a high abuse-positive affect. This leads to the next hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. Abuse-positive affect positively influences abuse intent.

## 3.4. Morality

Morality is a trigger of internal regulation [38]. While morality can be defined differently depending on philosophical approaches, we focus on individual morality as a trigger of an individual’s internal regulation. The theory of emotion process [42,43] provides a basic theoretical background to develop the following hypotheses. The basic premise is that this regulation process can influence the ‘appraisal,’ ‘affect,’ and ‘action readiness’ of an individual. This regulation process suggests that morality may influence goal conduciveness (H4a), abuse-positive affect (H4b), and abuse intent (H4c) in an attenuated manner caused by anticipated adverse effects.

Prior studies suggest that morality has an impact on behavior [133]. While most of the prior studies found that morality impacts behavior directly [2,114] or moderates the relationship between moral judgment and behavior [26,81,101,102,144], some studies did not find a moderation effect of morality on behavior [50,61]. In addition, a few studies suggested that we should identify constructs that mediate the relationship between morality and behavior. For example, judgments of responsibility [55] and technologies [126] are suggested as potential mediators between morality and behavior. Recently, Johnston and Krettenauer [65] found that moral emotion mediates the relationship between the self-importance of moral values and self-reported levels of antisocial behavior. In a similar vein, our study examines a mediation effect of abuse-positive affect on the relationship between morality and abuse intent. We also examine a mediation effect of goal conduciveness on the relationship between morality and abuse-positive affect.

Prior studies [94,143] find considering morality would reduce the influence of perceived sanctions. This reduction suggests that if morality can influence an individual’s perception of security sanctions, it may affect his/her perception or appraisal of goal conduciveness regarding the abuse opportunity structure in an organization. Proposing a model of ethical decision making, Moores and Chang [85] identify four components of morality, which include moral recognition, moral judgment, moral intention, and behavior. Moral recognition regards an individual’s cognitive ability to interpret the situation as being moral. Moral judgment refers to judging which possible action is most justified. Moral intention refers to deciding what one intends to do. They argue that these components are sequential internal processes that result in external moral behavior. This model implies that morality can affect three cognitive steps: recognition, judgment, and intention. An individual with high moral recognition and high moral judgment is less likely to perceive goal conduciveness in the abuse opportunity structure. Thus, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 4a. Morality negatively influences goal conduciveness.

The literature on emotion regulation (e.g.) [32,51,130] suggests that positive and negative emotions can be regulated by morality. Through the process of emotion regulation, ‘‘individuals influence which emotions they have, when they have them, and how they experience and express these emotions’’ [51, p. 275]. Here, morality is a type of trigger that can control individuals’ positive and negative emotions [32]. In the computer abuse context, a potential abuser who has high morality may anticipate adverse effects from uncontrolled emotion. Hence, s/he can attenuate the abuse-positive affect caused by the goal conduciveness appraisal [45]. We therefore hypothesize that

Hypothesis 4b. Morality negatively influences abuse-positive affect.

Prior studies are not consistent regarding how morality influences abuse intent. Some (e.g.) [2] argue that there is a moderation effect of morality, while others (e.g.) [125] claim that it is a mediation effect. Bachman [2], for example, suggests that morality may moderate the effect of a punishment threat on behavioral intention. However, this moderating effect is not found in other studies [70,95]. Because the mediation/moderation effects of morality on IS-abuse-intent are indeterminate, we examine instead how morality affects abuse intent more directly. This position is consistent with a study by Park et al. [93] that suggests that individuals with higher cognitive moral development could have a higher propensity to judge themselves as being responsible for reporting bad news. In the computer abuse context, we posit that a potential abuser is less likely to have abuse intent when s/he has a higher level of morality. This leads to the next hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4c. Morality negatively influences abuse intent.

## 4. Research method

We conducted a laboratory experiment based on hypothetical scenarios to test the proposed research model: measuring dependent variables while manipulating independent variables in a controlled environment [12,13]. Using SurveyMonkey, we controlled liberty and facilitation and randomly assigned participants to four scenarios with various treatment and non-treatment conditions.

The hypothetical scenario approach is an attractive methodological option because one of the objectives of this study is to evaluate an individual’s intention regarding whether to abuse information systems [121]. Our experiment uses a two-factor, four-cell design with two exogenous variables, liberty and facilitation, which were manipulated independently at two levels. We developed four treatment scenarios as shown in Appendix B. We conducted three pilot tests to refine the treatment scenarios and to validate the measures.

Our subjects were professionals working in the US. Collecting data from general working professionals includes ordinary insiders who have easy access to hacking tools. Not all hacking tools are so sophisticated that only expert hackers can operate them. Many easy-to-use computer hacking tools are widely available for anyone to download and use. A total of 205 working professionals were recruited through SurveyMonkey. Sampling working professionals provided results that are meant to reflect the broad spectrum of computer users across business organizations. We had previously sampled IT professionals in a pilot test. Our pilot test with IT professionals showed similar results to those with our final, broader sample. Twelve subjects were dropped because they failed a manipulation check. After the drop, data from 193 subjects (78 males and 115 females) were used for analysis. On average, the subjects’ age is 44.5. Subjects’ education levels were measured by the educational degree they had achieved.

## 4.1. Scenario

Each subject was asked to read a short scenario about a manufacturing company and to assume the role of a network administrator. Critical manufacturing software was located at the server. The scenario presented the subject with information suggesting mistreatment by the company: by promoting the subject’s rival, by demoting the subject, by giving a less than average raise and by considering the intention to lay off the subject. The subject was asked to consider whether to take revenge against the company.

Liberty (as freedom of movement) was manipulated as the authority of a network administrator to access the servers freely. Facilitation (as environment) was manipulated as the atmosphere (i.e., a security policy, a number of target servers, knowledge to install a software time-bomb) within which a subject must work if a software time bomb is to be placed into the servers. In all treatment conditions, the subject was informed of the consequences of the success and failure of a system abuse attempt.

The experiment and its treatment conditions are realistic situations. These simulate two real case studies in which critical information systems were compromised by disgruntled employees [46,47,83,90]. In one case, a software time bomb placed by an employee named Lloyd crippled the company’s manufacturing ability for a long period of time. In another case, a disgruntled employee named James sabotaged the information system at his company. In both cases, the employees were able to access the server freely, in part because no security policies or surveillance systems were in place. Therefore, the experimental manipulations of liberty and facilitation, while hypothetical in nature, are rooted in historical precedents for freedom and environment.

## 4.2. Procedure

In this role-playing experiment, the subjects were informed that this study was about computer abuse by a disgruntled employee and that their answers would remain anonymous. Their participation was voluntary. Subjects were randomly assigned to one of the four treatment conditions (combinations of high-or-low liberty and high-or-low facilitation) created by varying the descriptions of freedom and the settings. In the scenarios, the information on other types of events such as demotion and dismissal was given as background information that simulates the potential abusers’ situation.

The experimental procedures consisted of two phases. In the first phase, each subject received a copy of the scenario corresponding to his/her treatment condition and was asked to read the scenario. In the second phase, each subject was asked to complete an online questionnaire that would measure his/her abuse intent and also to answer several items regarding perceived goal conduciveness, abuse-positive affect, and morality. Each was then asked to provide demographic information.

We applied several procedural remedies to address common methods bias. Two steps minimized evaluation apprehension, which may cause common methods bias. First, the respondents answers were made anonymous. Second, the respondents were assured that there was no right or wrong answer except for the abuse intent items. These steps made the respondents less likely to edit their responses to be socially desirable or consistent with their perception of how the researcher wanted them to respond. In addition, we adopted measures that had been previously validated in the literature (see the next section). In drafting the scenario, we avoided vague and ambiguous terms, using instead concise, simple terms and syntax. The result was a scenario with an easy-ormoderate readability level that a ninth grader would understand.

## 4.3. Measures

We adopted established multi-item measures for abuse intent [63], morality [49], goal conduciveness [35], and abuse-positive affect [7,129]. We adapted the items in the context of computer abuse. These items were revised and validated with a pretest by two IS security experts and three pilot tests. We used a seven-point Likert scale ranging from ‘‘strongly disagree’’ (1) to ‘‘strongly agree’’ (7) for multi-item measures. Appendix C shows the constructs and measures as used in the study.

## 5.2. PLS analysis

SmartPLS 2.0 software [105] was used for measurement validation and to evaluate the hypothesized paths in the research model. While there are published precedents for using Partial Least Squares (PLS) in studies involving information security (e.g.) [26,116], we used PLS because of its suitability to testing theoretical models in early stages [76]. PLS enables us to examine the magnitude of the relationships and the effects between the constructs in the model [82]. We chose PLS rather than regression to test our model because the abuse opportunity structure was conceptualized as a multidimensional second-order construct [80], for which PLS is better suited. PLS is appropriate for testing a complex model that includes formative constructs and mediation [30,76,104]. PLS is useful for maximizing the explained variance of endogenous variables in the structural model [48]. Therefore, we can understand how much variance is explained in the constructs for the abuse-positive construct and abuse intent. Further, PLS is also appropriate in this situation because the measurement model must be established by examining the psychometric properties of the measures before testing the structural model. A measurement model links each construct with a set of indicators measuring that construct while a structural model represents a network of causal relationships linking multiple constructs.

## 5.2.1. Measurement model assessment

Our research model includes both formative and reflective constructs. Construct validity should be assessed differently for these two types of constructs, as we cannot assume that the formative indicators will co-vary. Therefore, traditional approaches for assessing construct reliability cannot be meaningfully applied.

Morality was treated as a formative construct. Multicollinearity was checked for, as it is not desirable for a formative construct. Multicollinearity can increase standard errors, which lead to an adverse effect on measurement reliability. The variance inflation factor (VIF) value should not exceed 3.3, which is the recommended threshold for multicollinearity [16,29]. All VIF values shown in Table 2 are less than 1.815, indicating that a certain unique portion of variance is explained by each formative item and alleviating multicollinearity concerns.

We assessed the convergent validity of constructs by examining standardized loadings. Standardized loadings should be greater than 0.707 to meet the condition that the shared variance between each item and its associated construct exceeds the error variance (with loadings of 0.5 or 0.6 being acceptable if other items within the same block of measures have high loadings) [19]. All of the loadings were 0.842 or higher as shown in Table 3.

We used Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability, and average variance extracted to assess reliability. Prior research recommends 0.70 as extensive evidence of reliability and 0.80 or higher as exemplary evidence [153]. All of the constructs in the measurement model exhibited Cronbach’s alpha of 0.954 or higher, and composite reliability of 0.967 or higher (see Table 3). As suggested by Fornell and Larcker [37], we also report average variance extracted (AVE). All of the AVEs meet the acceptable level of 0.5 or higher [19].

Discriminant validity was assessed using two tests. Each indicator’s loading on its own construct and its cross-loading on all other constructs was calculated (see Appendix D). We observed that the former is higher than the latter. This helps us to establish the psychometric adequacy of our measurement model. A comparison of the AVEs for each reflective construct with the shared variance between all possible pairs of reflective constructs also helps us to establish discriminant validity (shown in Appendix E). Discriminant validity is established when the AVE for each construct is higher than the squared correlation between the construct pairs. This level shows that more variance is shared between the latent construct and its block of indicators than with another construct representing a different block of indicators. The correlations among all constructs are below the .90 threshold, suggesting that all constructs are distinct from each other as shown in Table 4.

## 5.2.2. Structural model assessment

To assess the structural model, we examined the path coefficients, their significance level, and the $R ^ { 2 }$ values. While the path coefficients indicate the strengths of the relationships between two constructs, the $R ^ { 2 }$ values denote the amount of variance explained by the independent constructs [5,20]. The $R ^ { 2 }$ value for abuse intent – the ultimate dependent variable – was 0.45. That is, the research model accounts for 45% of the variance in abuse intent. We also reviewed the $R ^ { 2 }$ values for abuse intent in other studies. The $R ^ { 2 }$ of 0.45 for abuse intent is comparable to results reported by prior studies and provides strong evidence of the explanatory power of our research model.

Path coefficients in the structural model were computed using the entire sample. We employed the bootstrap method with 500 resamples to obtain the t-values corresponding to each path (shown in Fig. 3). The acceptable t-values for two-tailed tests are 1.96 and 2.58 at the significance levels of 0.05 and 0.01. Five of six hypotheses were supported, as shown in Table 5.

To safeguard against rival explanations, we added gender, age, and work experience. We found no significant impact from these control variables on intent to abuse.

## 5.2.3. Test for common method bias

We conducted two types of statistical analyses to assess the threat of common methods bias: Harman’s one factor test [96] and the latent variable test [75]. First, in Harman’s one factor test, the emergence of a single factor that accounts for a large proportion of the variance in the factor analyses would suggest a common methods bias. However, no such factor emerged. We loaded all items used to measure both independent and dependent variables into a single exploratory factor analysis. The analysis produced six factors with eigenvalues higher than 1. Taken together, these factors explained 72.8% of the variance in the data, with the first extracted factor accounting for 31.3% of the variance in the data. Given that more than one factor was extracted from the analysis and that the first factor accounted for less than 50% of the variance, this suggests that common method bias is unlikely to be a significant issue.

Second, we ran the latent variable test [75] by adding a firstorder factor with all of the measures in the model as indicators. With the first-order factor, we added a common method factor to the research model. The results shown in Appendix F demonstrate that the average substantively explained variance of the indicators is 0.658, whereas the average method-based variance is 0.005. The ratio of substantive variance to method variance is almost 131:1; therefore, common method bias is unlikely to be a problem. In addition, most method factor loadings are not significant. Given the small magnitude and insignificance of method variance, common method bias is unlikely to be a serious concern in this study.

## 6. Discussion

This study has several key findings. First, by showing the significant positive impact of an organization’s abuse opportunity structure on goal conduciveness, this study focuses on how the impact of organizational and individual factors, particularly emotion processes, contribute important new findings to the computer abuse and crime literature.

Second, the results confirm the significant positive impact of goal conduciveness on abuse-positive affect, validating appraisal theorists’ arguments [35,38,106,112,135] that, in the context of computer abuse, a high level of goal-conduciveness appraisal triggers abuse-positive affect in potential abusers.

Third, the results show a significant positive influence of abusepositive affect on abuse intent, emphasizing the powerful role of abuse-positive affect in abuse intent.

Fourth, including morality as a trigger of internal regulation, the study elucidates the critical regulatory role of morality in attenuating abuse-positive affect and the abuse intent of potential abusers. In addition, our post hoc analysis did not find a significant moderation effect of morality on abuse intent and abuse-positive affect.

Finally, our mediation analysis found that the impact of morality on abuse intent is partially mediated by abuse-positive affect, suggesting that morality may regulate abuse-positive affect and, in turn, abuse intent.

## 6.1. Contributions to theory

Organizational phenomena are complex and multi-level: they span across both micro factors (such as individual behavior) and macro factors (such as organizational structures) [56]. By engaging with cross-level factors, multilevel research provides a richer and more complete explanation of organizational phenomena [56]. Previous models of computer abuse have studied factors at discrete levels, such as the macro/organizational level [53,72,123] or the micro/individual level [58,73,86,116]. There are also examples of previous multi-level research [52,54,149]. In much of this research, the individual-level constructs have been framed by cognitive models (e.g.) [54,146] or social models (e.g.) [72]. We extend the previous discrete individual and multi-level research with a novel computer abuse intent model that is both multi-level and multifaceted to encompass more extensive individual psychologica factors that include emotion and morality.

Our model spans organizational-level factors (abuse opportunity structure and its underlying concepts) and individual-level factors that are multi-faceted (cognitive: goal conduciveness; and psychological: abuse-positive affect and morality). Our study finds that both cognitive (goal-conduciveness) and emotional (abusepositive affect) factors are involved in activating the abuse intent of potential abusers (or disgruntled employees) and that this intent is also influenced by an organizational context with a lax abuse opportunity structure. That is, potential abusers may appraise whether the abuse opportunity structure in an organization is conducive to committing computer abuse. Then, the appraisal of the situation may trigger positive affect toward the opportunity. For example, when potential abusers find that the abuse opportunity structure supports achieving their goal of computer abuse in organizations, they feel excited and inspired because the situation will allow them to abuse computers easily without being detected. Our multilevel model extends measures and analysis across organizational and individual levels in a multi-faceted way to illuminate the interdependence of the factors at different levels [56].

Our multilevel, multi-faceted perspective led us to distil, at the organizational level, the liberty construct in the abuse opportunity structure. Liberty is a first-order construct that is sufficient in our multi-level work because it compiles process-enabled liberty (such as organizational routines) and the physical environment (such as room assignments). Previous studies of opportunity abuse structures, unfettered by an aim to provide cross-level measures and analysis, have separated the components of liberty to explore the details of organizational factors. Examples include Clarke [22], Willison [145] and Willison and Backhouse [147]. Making liberty a first order construct is more appropriate for developing insight into how both organizational and individual factors lead to computer abuse.

An abuse opportunity structure based on the organizational factors of liberty and facilitation is found in work building on secondary case data [9]. However, this novel structure has not been empirically tested. Our study contributes an empirical examination of this novel abuse opportunity structure and its underlying sources (liberty and facilitation) as independent variables in a model that explains computer abuse intent. Such a novel structure helps us to recognize how liberty interacts with facilitators and the emotional state of the potential abuser to lead to computer abuse. Our study confirms with empirical measures the liberty and facilitation construct hypothesized but untested in previous models [9]. Our results indicate that both liberty and facilitation are significant components of the abuse opportunity structure. This abuse opportunity structure influences potential abusers’ abuse intent indirectly through the assessment of goal conduciveness and abuse-positive affect. This new construct, liberty, is important for understanding how liberty and facilitation combine to incentivize the emotional employee as this employee considers victimizing the organization. Our study also indicates that liberty should be cautiously handled when emotion is a factor.

Prior IS studies [10,14,63,64,139] typically focus on particular discrete emotions, for example, happiness, anger, fear, and shame. We focus instead on a more abstract emotion construct: abusepositive affect. Abuse-positive affect is different from constructs in prior IS studies in that it includes both dimensions: pleasantness and activation. Abuse-positive affect is more particularly relevant to the abuse context and is anchored to well-established research into how individuals experience emotions (as a positive affect negative affect schedule-PANAS) [7,25,127,129,134,141]. This reconceptualization and testing of abuse-positive affect instead of discrete emotions offers additional insight into how the high level of activation tends to relate to approach-oriented intent/behavior [142]. That is, positive feelings – particularly activated feelings with energy and vigor – are likely to lead to abuse intent. Abuse-positive affect is both a precedent for abuse intent and a mediator between morality and abuse intent. This critical role of abuse-positive affect is a novel finding in the computer abuse literature.

Our study also delivers other insights into the psychological processes of potential abusers. For example, we found that the impact of morality on abuse intent is both direct and indirect through abuse-positive affect. Prior studies considered the direct effects of morality [26], and its contextualization [87]. Our study confirms how morality has a direct impact on abuse intent but also helps to explain and elaborate this impact. Morality regulates an individual’s feelings by reducing abuse-positive affect. For example, individuals with high morality feel less excited about a situation that allows them to easily compromise an information system (even when they believe the organization has mistreated them).

Our study finds that there is a mediation effect between morality and abuse intent, while morality does not significantly affect goal conduciveness. Both morality and abuse-positive affect have a significant impact on abuse intent. The main effect of morality on abuse intent is negative and statistically significant. Morality also has an indirect impact on abuse intent via abusepositive affect. Abuse-positive affect partially mediates the relationship between morality and abuse intent. The calculated effect size of abuse-positive affect as a mediation variable is large (0.373) [23]. The result of the Sobel test (see Appendix G) also shows that the mediation effect of the abuse-positive affect is significant. This result suggests that an individual with a high level of morality will have a lower abuse-positive affect and abuse intent. The indirect effect on an individual’s abuse intent is strong and statistically significant. This finding extends our understanding of how individual psychology controls each person’s abusepositive affect and abuse intent. It suggests that the emotional aspect of morality may be even more powerful than the rational aspect. Morality does not influence the appraisal of the situation in terms of whether the situation would be helpful to compromising an information system but rather influences how the individual feels about their intentions toward potential abuse.

Our study also validates the applicability of Frijda’s theory of emotion process in the context of computer abuse. We empirically confirm strong and significant relationships among event, appraisal, affect, and intent components derived from the theory. The theory of emotion process led us to results with levels of explanatory power $\left( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 4 5 \right)$ of abuse intent similar to those of other computer abuse studies [25,26,116].

## 6.2. Contributions to practice

Because the abuse opportunity structure has a significant effect on abuse intent, managers may be able to take advantage of these known factors. Individuals are more willing to abuse information systems when the perceived liberty and facilitation in the abuse opportunity structure are high. Managers should put proper security management practices in place to control employees’ perceived liberty and facilitation level. Examples of such practices include improved access control and training that improves individual attitudes (not just skills) toward security. While such practices are already well known [27], the liberty construct as a goal is novel. Our work indicates that the effectiveness of such security practices should be measured as perceived liberty and facilitation (from the employees’ perspective). When used as measures in security management processes, managers can better determine if the effectiveness of security control practices is perceived as high.

Our insights into the relationship between morality and abuse intent provide further opportunities to improve practice. Morality affects abuse intent directly but also indirectly through abusepositive affect. Organizations can reduce computer abuse behaviors by educating employees in business ethics. We know through prior studies in the fields of education and criminology that moral education helps to control misbehavior [11,132]. Our results provide more granularity for planning such education: such education should aim to particularly improve the moral reasoning that occurs upon the discovery of organizational information security vulnerabilities. Managers can educate employees on moral beliefs and rules in business organizations and settings to help them control their emotional assessment of compromising organizational situations. For example, because we know that an individual with a high level of morality will experience reduced abuse intent (plus a significant indirect reduction of this abuse intent), organizations can deploy ethics programs to help employees control their abusepositive affect to decrease abuse intent.

Our study also suggests that computer abuse is not simply a function of inadequate security practices. Individuals’ appraisal of organizations’ security settings, the emotional experience generated by this appraisal, and morality all play important roles in the commission of computer abuse. Managers can improve their information security management strategy by incorporating both organizational and individual factors.

## 6.3. Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. We asked subjects to play the role of an employee in an organization and assessed their behavioral intentions instead of their actual behaviors. We measured abuse intent rather than abuse behavior. Some authorities will regard this as a weakness in the research design, others will not. Intent does not always lead to actual behavior [24]. Acquisti and Grossklags [1] note that only a small number of people who expressed concern about information security actually took actions to protect the information, even at limited cost. The similar but reverse case may occur in computer abuse cases. However, other work on computer abuse finds that abuse intentions successfully predict abuse behaviors. Particularly, Lee et al. [72] find that induction control intention decrease insiders’ computer abuse behavior. Self-defense intention shows significant negative relationships with both invaders’ abuse and insiders’ abuse behavior. Also criminological research has recognized measurements of intentions as reliable predictors of actual behaviors [117].

Second, our study investigates complex human decision making related to computer abuse in organizational settings using a controlled lab-style research design. We limited the number of variables that would affect an individual’s computer abuse intent. For example, goal conduciveness was chosen from among many dimensions of appraisal. Other variables, unmeasured in our study, are likely to affect an individual’s emotional processes and computer abuse intent.

Third, while the experimental scenarios were based on real cases [83,90], these were selected because of their situational clarity, which in turn reflected somewhat extreme actions such as a rival’s promotion and multiple reprimands by the organization. As with any other real case, the situations might be unique and culturally contextualized. The resulting forms of abuse could also be unique to their context. Because these organizational settings might be unique, more research is needed to explore whether the findings will hold for other types of organizational settings or computer abuse, for example, less extreme organizational actions, minor misuse, or milder server sabotage.

Fourth, such ideal morality scores as those reported in our study (Appendix C) raise issues of face validity even though the construct measures stand up to scrutiny. We applied several measures to address common method bias, which might come from evaluation apprehension and social desirability. We had two procedural measures in place. First, the respondents’ answers were made anonymous. Second, the respondents were assured that there was no right or wrong answer except for the abuse intent items. We also conducted two statistical tests to assess the presence of common method bias. The tests’ results show that common method bias is not a concern in our study. We suspect that the results may reflect a degree of self-selection bias among the subjects that represents a limitation in our study.

Fifth, we chose to define our sample broadly, ‘‘working professionals,’’ in order to draw our subjects from the frame of ordinary business insiders. It is possible that emotion constructs will vary among different subgroups within this broad frame. Further work is needed to explore insider computer abuse among different communities of working professionals.

## 6.4. Future research

There are several areas that are ripe for future research. Separating the construct emotion from the construct abuse intent (to commit a computer crime) required great care in this research. While we achieved a satisfactorily measurable separation between these two constructs, there is a clear need for future research to study the separate measurement and relationship of emotion and abuse intent. Additionally, our study confirms the effect of abusepositive affect and morality as individual factors. We suspect that there may be other interesting individual factors that can also affect an individual’s abuse behavior. Future research is needed into such factors, such as locus of control and business-related morality. In addition, culture may affect how individuals commit computer abuse [79]. Further investigation of what cultural factors may affect individuals’ emotion processes and how may explain why individuals from different cultural backgrounds behave differently in cases of computer abuse. Finally, the moderation effect of morality needs further examination. Although our post hoc analysis did not find a moderation effect of morality on the relationship between positive affect and abuse intent, it deserves further investigation. Future research with more a specific design might better capture a moderation effect of morality by increasing the statistical power for these effects.

## Appendix B. Experimental scenario

You work for ManTech, a company that manufactures high-tech measurement instruments. At the heart of each machine is a specialized software program that controls the movement of the parts for manufacturing measurement and instruments.

For the past 10 years, you have been working on as a network administrator, managing the installation and backup tapes of the specialized software. The company has grown to become a global enterprise over the past 10 years. The culture of the company has changed from cozy and friendly to formal and unfriendly.

You have recently managed several projects. You feel strongly that your project management has been excellent, but factors beyond your control limited their success. Nevertheless, you were written up several times for the mismanagement of projects. You were blamed for the negative outcomes, while your rival was credited with all of the successful aspects. Furthermore, your salary was reduced by 10% for each of the past three years. This salary decrease is humiliating because other coworkers received significant raises of up to 15%. Today, the company notified you that you will be demoted to engineer from supervisor, and you discovered that your rival was promoted to be your new supervisor. Not only is there concern that the company may fire you soon, but you hear a rumor that the company may be planning to sue you for negligence in the mismanagement of customers and products.

At this point, you are now wondering whether or not you will take revenge against the company.

Your liberty at the company is HIGH:

 From your past experience and observation, you know that you can access servers freely.

 You have full liberty to access servers any time.

The security control situation at the company is LOW:

 The server room is openly available.

 The company has no security policy to control access to the server room.

 No ID and password are required to access the server.

 All important software programs running the manufacturing machines are centrally located on a main server in one server room rather than distributed across several servers.

 You know how to insert a software time bomb to the servers and destroy the software programs without leaving any evidence. You know all of the details of the server.

If your attempt to abuse the systems is successful, it would cripple the company’s manufacturing ability for a long period of time without leaving any evidence.

If your attempt to abuse the systems is not successful, it would not do any damage to the company’s manufacturing ability while leaving a lo of evidence.
