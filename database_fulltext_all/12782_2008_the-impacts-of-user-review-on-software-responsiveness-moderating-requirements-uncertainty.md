# The impacts of user review on software responsiveness: Moderating requirements uncertainty

Jack Shih-Chieh Hsu <sup>a,\*</sup>, Chien-Lung Chan <sup>b</sup>, Julie Yu-Chih Liu <sup>b</sup>, Houn-Gee Chen <sup>c</sup>

## Abstract

Rapidly changing business environments and evolving processes increase the uncertainties in IS development. To produce a high-quality system that responds to user needs is challenging. We attempted to determine whether user reviews during the development process could reduce uncertainties and improve the product. Technology structuration theory indicated that users, as actors participating in reviews during the development of a system, could help reduce uncertainty in the organizational requirements and thus improve the software product. A survey of system developers indicated that user requirements uncertainty had a direct, negative effect on software responsiveness but that user review, serving as a moderator, could reduce this effect ©. 2008 Elcavier R V, A1l riσbts

## 1. Introduction

Software quality (product that meets user requirements) is of major concern in IS development. An important dimension of this is responsiveness to customer needs; i.e., providing a broad range of outputs that can be tailored to the needs of the user. The consequences of lack of responsiveness range from customer dissatisfaction to unsafe systems. However, there is no agreement on how management procedures achieve software responsiveness. Determining which practices that lead to better software by reducing the effect of uncertainty is thus crucial in software engineering.

Procedures have been suggested that users should take an active role in software quality management. However, the relationship between user participation and software responsiveness has not been explored. The focus of our study was therefore to examine the relationship between requirements uncertainty and software responsiveness with an attempt to determine whether one specific technique could improve the responsiveness of a product. Specifically, we based our research on technology structuration theory, where the user review moderates the magnitude of the requirements uncertainty impact on software responsive ness.

## 2. Background and research hypotheses

## 2.1. Software quality

Kitchenham and Pfleeger [24] suggested that software quality can be viewed from five viewpoints: (1) transcendental: recognizable but not definable; (2) user: a fit for the intended purpose; (3) manufacturing: conformance to specification; (4) product: tied to inherent characteristics of the product; (5) value-based dependent on how much the customer is willing to pay. Thus the criteria for evaluating software quality varies based upon the stakeholder as well as the requirement criteria of the project.

Some of the most examined software quality criteria include: reliability: the capability of the software to maintain its level of performance when used under specified conditions; portability: the capability of software to be transferred from one environment to another; maintainability: the capability of the software to be modified; usability: the capability of the software to be understood, learned, used and liked by the user, and responsiveness: the capability of the software to provide the outputs which meet a users stated or implied needs [6]. This last measure is considered an external quality item, recognized by the user or using organization, as opposed to that of the software professionals. Therefore, we focused on the external quality viewpoint: software responsiveness in particular.

Rai et al. [34] presented an overview of software quality assurance based on a literature review that identified the areas under investigation. They pointed out theories and principles drawn from other disciplines; but suggested that empirical research on it was still in a rudimentary stage. They advocated research to examine how quality could be enhanced during the development process and the need for better management and intervention during implementation.

## 2.2. User participation and user review

User participation was originally viewed as a user and developer ‘‘cooperatively involved to the extent that the activities of each facilitate the attainment of the ends of the others’’ [40]. Later Barki and Hartiwick separated it into two distinct components: user participation and user involvement. User involvement was considered the subjective psychological state reflecting the importance and personal relevance attached to a system [1], while user participation was the assignments, activities, and behaviors that users or their representatives performed during the system development process. Later, they further divided the user participation component into: user responsibility, where the user was in charge of or accounting for elements of project success; (user IS relationship or user review, involving extent of user communication with and influence on IS personnel; and hands-on activities, dealing with user activity in the physical design and implementation. However today, software development involves more complex relations, including client partnerships.

Prior user participation studies concentrated on user participative activities. No study explicitly examined the impact of user review on system success and software quality. Recently, user satisfaction and system acceptance were the two factors of system success. A summary of user participation constructs examined in previous studies between 1982 and 2005 is shown in Table 1. A variety of theories rose to explain the relations, with the dominant theory being a contingency approach to examining the relationship between user participation and system success. The magnitude of the effect between participation and success was moderated by other situational effects, most often complexity [30].

## 2.3. Structurational model of technology

Based upon theories on social constructivism, IS researchers [12,33] have developed models of technology creating insights into the roles and influences of human agents on technologies in organizations. These models examined how interpretations, social interests, and disciplinary conflicts shaped the production of technology through cultural and social interactions among groups. In particular, one relationship in the structurational model of technology posits that institutional properties influenced the behavior of the agents, who in turn determined the technology product through specific actions [33]. The three components of the model are the (1) institutional properties of the organization including uncertainties due to strategies, requirements, communication, technology, and environmental conditions, (2) human agents who incorporate the properties of the organization and design technology solutions, and (3) technology (material artifacts) including software systems. Of particular concern in our study were the actions, during the design mode, that embed structures in technology during development.

Based upon the technology structuration model, IS is an ‘‘enacted environment’’ whose construction and use is conditioned by an organization’s structures of signification, domination, and legitimation. In the design mode, human agents build interpretive schemes into the technology (rules about the work being automated), certain facilities and norms (organizationally sanctioned ways of executing the work). When the organizational properties, interpretive schemes, facilities, or norms are not well understood or uncertain, there is a need for action to reduce uncertainties and promote effective technology development. High uncertainty requires information-seeking behavior and uncertainty is reduced when communication is increased. Integration among human agents has a positive impact on the quality new products. This logic resulted in our model, as shown in Fig. 1.

Requirements uncertainty, the degree to which requirements change during the project development process, has a negative effect on final system quality. Our question was: can the actions of users lower this impact? User reviews can reduce the uncertainty in a development environment and have a positive effect on product development. The actions of users become crucial, as IS developers may not solve user needs. Furthermore, the exchange of knowledge and expertise between users and IS developers helps clarify requirements and, thus, mitigates the impact of requirements uncertainty. Granting users the authority to change, review, and approve the work at multiple stages can ensure that user needs are properly incorporated into the product. ## 3. Research methods

## 3.1. Sampling

We used a sample of US IS project managers who are members of the Project Management Institute (PMI). They represented the perspective of IS staff involved in development projects; they were selected instead of user participants. It was felt that users, being interested primarily in their own needs and commitments, may not know enough about the entire project operations to answer all our questions about total participation and scope of their company’s systems. A package, including a cover letter and the questionnaire about user participation and software outcomes, was sent to 1000 randomly selected members. All the respondents were assured that their responses would be kept confidential. Participants were asked to answer questions based on their most recently completed project. A total of 152 returned the questionnaire. One returned instrument was unusable due to omissions. The usable response rate was thus 15.1%. Table 2 summarizes important characteristics of the organizations and projects of respondents. The demographic data shows consistency with past research, providing to assurance of external validity.

## 3.2. Constructs

User review was assessed by asking whether users reviewed and approved developers’ work in a certain time period. A total of four items from Barki and Hartwick [2] were adapted to determine user review activities: formally approving work of the IS staff, formally reviewing work performed by IS staff, being informed of progress and/or problems, and signing a formalized agreement. Likert scales (ranging from 1 to 5), with anchors ranging from ‘‘strongly disagree’’ to ‘‘strongly agree’’ were used for questions. Table 3 shows the items for this and all scales.

Requirements uncertainty is a measure of the extent of change of user requirements during the system development phase. A total of three items from Nidumolu [32] were adopted: how requirements fluctuated in the past, were expected to fluctuate in the future, and differed between the start and finish. Likert scales ranging from 1 to 5 with anchors ranging from ‘‘strongly disagree’’ to ‘‘strongly agree’’ were used.

Software responsiveness measured how the system met user needs. A total of three items were adapted from Nidumolu [32] to determine: the ability to generate required output, the range of outputs that could be generated, and the responder’s assessed overall responsiveness. Likert scales (ranging from 1 to 5), with anchors ranging from ‘‘strongly disagree’’ to ‘‘strongly agree’’ were used.

## 3.4. Control variables

To lessen concern that the variables under study and their relationship are not data artifacts, we employed several control concepts. First, the three variables were individually tested against each categorical variable in an ANOVA to determine whether the organizational or project characteristics had any influence. No relationship proved significant at a 0.05 level. In addition, software responsiveness, and all product evaluative measures, is an implementation measure of success rather than of the development process itself. It is generally considered that success of the process is influential in success of the product implemented. Two common single item measures of development project success, staying within the budget and completing on time, were used in the regression analyses in order to eliminate such effects.

## 4.2. Moderating effect

Next, a moderated multiple regression analysis (MMR) was used to test the moderating effect of user review. Carte and Russell [8] urged that researchers use MMR to test moderating effects and emphasize the difference of $R ^ { 2 }$ instead of the coefficient of the interaction term. We adopted a three-step approach to test the moderating effects using MMR with PLS-Graph. First, software responsiveness was regressed with user review and requirements uncertainty, model 1 in Table 6, and the model fit $( R _ { 1 } ^ { 2 } )$ was obtained. Second, we added the interaction term between user review and requirements stability to model 2 and obtained the new model fit $( R _ { 1 } ^ { 2 } ) ,$ , model 2 in Table 6. Third, we calculated the difference of $R ^ { 2 }$ from the first two steps $( \Delta R ^ { 2 } )$ and obtained the f-value for significance of the change. The results of the tests are also shown in Table 6.

The hypothesis that the magnitude of the user requirements uncertainty impact on software responsiveness is associated with the levels of user review (H2) was supported at the 0.05 significance level. We split the sample into high and low user review based on the median of the moderator variable and regressed the independent variable (requirements uncertainty) to the dependent variable (software responsiveness) under two different situations [31]. The derived intercepts and beta coefficients were then used to create the moderating effect diagram of Fig. 2. The chart shows that requirements uncertainty had less effect on software responsiveness when users participated heavily through reviews, but more strongly and negatively influenced responsiveness when the level of user review was low. As the degree of requirements uncertainty increased, software responsiveness of low user review projects was significantly worse than when under high review conditions.

## 5. Conclusions

## 5.1. Summary

The focus of our study was to examine the interacting relationships among requirements uncertainty, user review, and software responsiveness. Specifically, we argued that requirements uncertainty had a negative impact on software responsiveness; however, the magnitude of user requirements uncertainty impact on software responsiveness was found by the level of user review. Our survey confirmed the hypothesis that user review reduced the magnitude of the impact of user requirements uncertainty on software responsiveness. When user review was high, the effect of requirements uncertainty on software responsiveness was partially mitigated. However, when the user review level was low in a project, software responsiveness dropped significantly as requirements uncertainty increased. The result implied that users needed to act to control progress and act as product quality gate keepers in the IS development process. We concluded that users should not only play the role as requirements providers but should continue to engage in the subsequent development process to make sure that user requirements were fulfilled. This study thus adds a perspective in understanding the effect of user participation—how it influences the final system outcomes by mitigating the effects of various risk factors during the system development process.

Meeting customer needs is the underlying principle. This study provided evidence that management efforts to enforce user review can reduce the effects of requirements uncertainty on software quality.

## 5.2. Managerial implications

First, software quality includes several dimensions (e.g. reliability and efficiency) and some are quantifiable (e.g., response time, error rate, and system corruption times). The quantifiable indicators are measurable and attract the most attention from project developers. Different methodologies were proposed in the system design, development, and testing stages to improve software quality. Among these, periodic review and testing were felt to deal with quality issue usefully. To have a comprehensive high-quality system, objective and subjective quality attributes should be considered simultaneously. Project managers should pay attention to all quality attributes, such as better system responsiveness.

Second, of the three major components of user participation, we focused on user review. The practices examined included review and approval of the product at various stages, working under signed agreements, and keeping the users informed about progress. Each of these should be implemented in development projects, and according to the maturity models are best implemented through formal procedures rather than informal ones. Development methods that involve users heavily should still have the more formal steps employed as standard practices to ensure compliance.

## 5.3. Limitations of the study

The first limitation of the study is the use of a survey population that excluded users and targeting project and IS managers. Though the managers could have knowledge of the system, they are unlikely to have the full user perspective. This is important, as users often feel marginalized because their involvement appears to have little or no impact on the product. Secondly, our measures required perceptive evaluations after completion of the project when memories may have been distorted or become mingled results from other projects and processes. Finally, many models have been proposed listing variables that influence software quality and implementation, while our study only controlled for cost and time compliance.
