# DUAL PATHWAYS OF VALUE CREATION FROM DIGITAL STRATEGIC POSTURE: CONTINGENT EFFECTS OF COMPETITIVE ACTIONS AND ENVIRONMENTAL UNCERTAINTY<sup>1</sup>

Inmyung Choi Information Systems and Quantitative Sciences Area, Rawls College of Business, Texas Tech University Lubbock, TX U.S.A {inmyung.choi@ttu.edu}

David E. Cantor Department of Supply Chain Management, Ivy College of Business, Iowa State University Ames, IA U.S.A. {dcantor@iastate.edu}

Kunsoo Han Information Systems Area, Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University Montreal, QC CANADA {kunsoo.han@mcgill.ca}

Joey F. George Department of Information Systems and Business Analytics, Ivy College of Business, Iowa State University Ames, IA U.S.A. {jfgeorge@iastate.edu}

Digital strategic posture (DSP) is defined as a firm’s overall strategic stance toward investing in information technology (IT) initiatives relative to that of rival firms. This study examines how a firm’s DSP affects firm performance. Drawing on the competitive dynamics perspective and contingency view, we demonstrate that DSP influences competitive actions through dual pathways. First, DSP enables firms to take competitive actions that are more appropriate given the level of environmental uncertainty (captured by industry dynamism). In particular, our findings suggest that a proactive DSP enables relatively more innovation-oriented actions in dynamic industries while enabling relatively more operations-oriented actions in less dynamic industries. Second, DSP plays a facilitating role in firms’ execution of competitive actions such that a firm’s value from its proactive DSP is enhanced when there is a fit between the type of the firm’s competitive actions and its level of environmental uncertainty. Specifically, we find that firms with a more proactive DSP achieve superior firm performance from innovation-oriented actions in dynamic industries and from operations-oriented actions in less dynamic industries. Taken together, our findings suggest that a proactive DSP not only allows firms to take appropriate competitive actions that fit their environmental conditions but also contributes to firms performance by facilitating the execution of these appropriate actions, thus enhancing their efficacy.

Keywords: Digital strategic posture, competitive actions, innovation-oriented actions, operations-oriented actions, competitive dynamics, contingency view, business value of IT, IT strategy, industry dynamism

## Introduction

Competitive actions are observable strategic moves initiated by a firm to improve its financial performance and hence gain a competitive advantage over rival firms (D’Aveni et al., 2010; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Smith et al., 1991).<sup>2</sup> With the increasing digitalization of business processes, products, and services, it is imperative for managers to evaluate the potential complementary role that digital technologies and competitive actions may have on a firm’s performance (Ferrier et al., 2010; Mithas et al., 2013; Yoo et al., 2012). Digital technologies are quickly becoming integral to the initiation and execution of competitive actions in that they provide enhanced sensing and decision-making capabilities and thus improve a firm’s ability to explore and exploit potential business opportunities within and across firm boundaries. However, there has been no research on whether and how a firm’s digital strategic posture (DSP) relative to its rivals can increase its ability to take innovation-oriented versus operations-oriented competitive actions and how DSP influences the performance impact of these types of competitive actions under different environmental conditions. This study attempts to fill these gaps in the literature.

The study of DSP is an emerging area of research among information technology (IT) strategy scholars (e.g., Ho et al., 2017; Mithas et al., 2013; Xue et al., 2017). DSP is defined as a firm’s overall strategic stance toward investing in IT initiatives compared to that of rival firms (Mithas et al., 2013; Xue et al., 2017).<sup>3</sup> Therefore, a firm’s DSP reflects its digital strategy (Mithas et al., 2013). When a firm invests in digitally enabled business activities more (less) than its industry peers, it has a proactive (reactive) DSP.

Our study builds upon and extends this nascent literature by considering the interdependent relationship among DSP, competitive actions, and the business environment in influencing firm performance. In doing so, we seek to reconcile the tension in the broader line of research regarding the performance impacts of DSP, as evidenced by the mixed empirical findings on the DSP-performance relationship. While Mithas et al. (2013) found that DSP does not have a significant effect on firm performance, Xue et al. (2017) showed that proactiveness in IT strategic posture enhances market return. Ho et al. (2017) considered the deviation of IT investment in terms of overinvestment versus underinvestment and found that only overinvestment in IT compared with that of industry peers (i.e., a proactive DSP)

leads to superior firm performance. Although these studies provide important implications regarding the business value (or lack thereof) of DSP, prior research, to the best of our knowledge, has focused solely on the direct impact of DSP on firm performance. We extend the literature by theorizing how DSP influences the firm’s tendency to initiate innovation-oriented versus operations-oriented competitive actions and demonstrate the subsequent performance impacts of these actions, contingent upon the level of environmental uncertainty.

Capitalizing on the competitive dynamics (CD) and contingency view literatures, this study proposes a nomological network linking DSP, competitive actions, and firm performance. According to the CD literature, firms take competitive actions to enhance their competitive advantage in the market (Grimm et al., 2006). When firms take a greater volume of competitive actions, they are likely to achieve superior performance (Andrevski & Ferrier, 2019; Derfus et al., 2008). Drawing on the CD perspective, we hypothesize that firms that proactively engage in DSP relative to rivals are better positioned to take impactful competitive actions and are thus able to realize superior financial performance.

We enhance our competitive dynamics perspective by integrating the contingency view, which suggests that there is no universally effective strategy for maintaining and enhancing a firm’s competitive advantage. Rather, the firm’s business strategy should be adapted to the business environment (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967; Venkatraman, 1989).<sup>4</sup> This implies that the role of DSP may depend on contextual factors. In particular, we investigate the contingent roles of environmental uncertainty and the type of competitive actions because environmental uncertainty can have a profound influence on the efficacy of a firm’s business strategy (including DSP) and its competitiveness (Havakhor et al., 2019). Using the contingency view, we examine how a proactive DSP affects the firm’s tendency to initiate innovation-oriented actions versus operationsoriented actions, contingent upon the level of environmental uncertainty (captured by industry dynamism). Further, we examine how a proactive DSP can enhance the performance effects of the two types of competitive actions depending on the level of environmental uncertainty.

Our main thesis is that when firms have a more proactive DSP, they possess stronger digital capabilities compared to their rivals, which can help them explore and exploit competitive opportunities and eventually achieve superior performance (Overby et al., 2006; Gupta et al., 2006; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). In particular, a proactive DSP increases firms’ sensing and analytical capabilities (Sambamurthy et al., 2003), which can enable them to select the “appropriate” types of competitive actions (innovationor operations-oriented) that fit the environmental conditions they face, thereby increasing the potential value of competitive actions. Subsequently, a proactive DSP can help firms realize greater business value from their competitive actions because a proactive DSP provides firms with superior digital capabilities that enable the continuous scanning of the business environment for any strategic and/or operational adjustments that may be needed during to take competitive actions (Barki & Pinsonneault, 2005). While it is possible for firms to take appropriate competitive actions without the support of a proactive DSP, firms with a reactive DSP may derive limited business value from those competitive actions if they cannot execute the actions in a timely and cost-effective manner. Alternatively, even with a proactive DSP, firms may achieve limited value from competitive actions that do not align well with the current level of environmental dynamism (e.g., innovation-oriented actions in low-dynamism environments). Therefore, we suggest that firms that can take appropriate competitive actions in a more timely and cost-effective manner supported by a proactive DSP will eventually realize superior financial performance, compared to rival firms with a reactive DSP or those that fail to take appropriate competitive actions that fit their business environments.

Based on a sample of S&P 500 firms from 2011 to 2015, we found evidence of dual pathways through which a proactive DSP influences competitive actions. First, we found that in highly dynamic environments, firms that have a more proactive DSP take relatively more innovation-oriented actions, whereas in less dynamic environments, having a proactive DSP helps firms take relatively more operationsoriented actions. Second, we identified an interesting interplay among DSP, the two types of competitive actions, and industry dynamism that affects firm performance. In particular, in highly dynamic industries, firms with a more proactive DSP achieve superior firm performance by taking more innovation-oriented competitive actions, whereas, in less dynamic industries, firms with a more proactive DSP realize a higher level of performance by engaging in more operations-oriented competitive actions. Thus, this finding extends prior DSP research by providing a nuanced understanding of how DSP affects competitive actions and firm performance. Our findings suggest that a proactive DSP not only allows firms to take appropriate competitive actions that fit their environmental conditions but also contributes to firm performance by facilitating the execution of these appropriate actions, thereby enhancing their efficacy.

## Theoretical Background: Competitive Dynamics Perspective Augmented with Contingency View

Competitive dynamics (CD) is a well-recognized theoretical perspective within strategic management (Chen et al., 2021; Grimm et al., 2006) and IS literature (Chi et al., 2010; Ferrier et al., 2010; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). This perspective provides a solid basis for studying how and under what conditions firms can develop and maintain a competitive advantage over others (Chen & Miller, 2012). A key component of CD is competitive actions, defined as observable moves and externally directed actions initiated by a firm to improve its market position (Grimm et al., 2006). The CD literature is primarily outwardfocused and provides an understanding of factors that influence a firm’s competitive actions and responses vis-à-vis its rivals in the market (Chen et al., 2021). Although prior CD literature has investigated the direct effect of competitive actions on firm performance, the contingency view suggests that there is no universal business strategy for a firm to secure a competitive advantage over others (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967; Venkatraman, 1989). Prior IS literature has utilized the contingency view to examine how environmental conditions moderate the effect of IT on firm performance (Havakhor et al., 2019; Xue et al., 2012). Therefore, we juxtapose the CD and contingency lenses to examine how a firm’s proactive DSP positions a firm to take competitive actions and subsequently improves firm performance under different levels of environmental uncertainty.

Modern-day CD scholarship is based on the concept of creative destruction developed by the Austrian School of Economics (Schumpeter, 1934, 1942). This concept refers to the dynamic process by which a focal firm and its rivals react to one another to improve their position in the market (Chen & Miller, 2012). This literature has traditionally examined the conditions under which a firm can benefit from its repertoire of competitive actions. To avoid market share erosion from a rival firm’s actions, a firm should not only explore new and complementary business opportunities by introducing new products and services continuously but also exploit and improve its internal operations (Derfus et al., 2008). Thus, using the exploration and exploitation literature (e.g., Gupta et al., 2006; March, 1991), we categorize competitive actions into two types depending on the firm’s intended purpose: innovation-oriented actions (e.g., developing or introducing new products/services) and operations-oriented actions (e.g., creating new manufacturing facilities and investing in supply chain management capabilities) (Li et al., 2010).

When firms take innovation-oriented actions, they search for new market opportunities (March, 1991)—for example, by seeking new knowledge (or departing from existing knowledge) and focusing on providing new products and services to customers and markets (Benner & Tushman, 2003). Likewise, firms initiate operations-oriented actions when they prefer incremental changes to internal operational business activities that are less risky (Jansen et al., 2006; March, 1991)—for example, by increasing the quality of existing products and services and minimizing risk and variations (Benner & Tushman, 2003). However, merely taking more innovation- and/or operations-oriented actions does not automatically lead to a superior position vis-à-vis the competition (Andrevski & Ferrier, 2019). Rather, the effectiveness of a firm’s competitive actions is dependent on the business environment (Derfus et al., 2008).

The contingency view provides a theoretical basis for why firms should pursue a different strategic orientation depending on industry conditions (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967; Venkatraman, 1989). The IS strategy literature has examined how the industry environment can influence IT-enabled agility (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), the IT asset portfolio (Xue et al., 2012), and IT investment (Havakhor et al., 2019). Chen et al. (2010) suggested that firms select an IS strategy—either innovative or conservative—based on environmental conditions. In an uncertain business environment, a competitive advantage may not be sustainable; therefore, firms are motivated to explore new business opportunities (Xue et al., 2012). As a result, innovation-oriented competitive actions, which target new business opportunities, can help firms improve their market position (Havakhor et al., 2019). However, in less uncertain (more stable) business environments, a competitive advantage is relatively sustainable and lasts longer (Xue et al., 2012). As a result, firms may realize superior performance by exploiting existing business opportunities and taking value-appropriation steps (Havakhor et al., 2019). In such conditions, operationsoriented competitive actions are more effective, as they are designed to enhance internal and external operations.

## Hypotheses Development

## The DSP’s Role in Enabling Competitive Actions and the Moderating Role of Environmental Uncertainty

Rapidly changing environmental conditions can compel firms to evaluate the benefits and costs of different types of competitive actions (March, 1991). Because firms have limited resources, they may not be able to allocate resources equally to take every available type of competitive action. For instance, there are costs and benefits to firms when they decide to take innovation-oriented versus operations-oriented actions (March, 1991). Based on the exploration-exploitation literature (e.g., Gupta et al., 2006; March, 1991), we hypothesize that due to these constraints, a proactive DSP influences a firm’s tendency to take innovation-oriented versus operations-oriented actions. In particular, we consider how a proactive DSP can enhance a firm’s ability to take appropriate types of competitive actions (innovation-oriented versus operations-oriented), contingent upon the level of industry dynamism (Havakhor et al., 2019; Sirmon et al., 2007).

In highly dynamic industries, competitor behaviors, market conditions, and customer demand undergo rapid and unpredictable changes that can quickly erode a firm’s previous sources of competitive advantage (Schumpeter, 1934). Because competitive advantage is often temporary (Havakhor et al., 2019), it is critical for firms operating in highly dynamic industries to take innovative competitive actions to retain or improve their position in the market (D’Aveni, 2010; Xue et al., 2012). To do so, they must explore and may ultimately decide to launch new products/services to rapidly capture the competitive advantages of being first to market (Andrevski & Ferrier, 2019; Xue et al., 2012). To succeed in such business conditions, it is imperative for firms to strategize how to identify financially attractive market opportunities for new product portfolios and, more importantly, how to execute new competitive actions.

Firms with a more proactive DSP relative to industry rivals possess superior capabilities for sensing potential opportunities to explore their innovative competitive actions against rival firms (i.e., foresight on actions). Digital technologies are utilized by such firms to detect changes in customer preferences, identify current and potential competitive actions made by rival firms, and respond to changes in the external environment (Chi et al., 2010). For example, John Deere Corporation developed a proprietary enterprise system that connects farmers’ tractors with John Deere’s research and development team using GPS technology, thus providing the firm with improved visibility into farmers’ daily operations. John Deere uses this information to generate new products and services in agricultural practice at a significant advantage compared to competitors (Barrett, 2015).

Similarly, other firms can capitalize on their digital investments to leverage the big data generated from customers and suppliers providing real-time information feeds. These data enhance firms’ awareness of changes in the competitive environment, as reflected in greater forecast accuracy, which enables firms to retain existing customers and add to their overall customer base (Overby et al., 2006; Havakhor et al., 2019; Park et al., 2017). Firms benefit significantly from these and other data science technologies, which can be used to identify future business opportunities in response to rivals’ competitive moves. Therefore, firms with a more proactive DSP can likely sense changes in customer preferences more quickly and take innovative competitive actions in a more timely manner (e.g., in terms of the timing of new product innovations).

In less dynamic industries, changes in market conditions, customer demand, and competitor behavior are relatively stable and predictable (Mithas et al., 2013; Xue et al., 2012). Since existing products and services do not become obsolete quickly, firms are typically reluctant to launch new products or radically innovate due to fear of retaliation from rival firms and the desire to reap long-term profits based on economies of scale advantages (Jansen et al., 2006). Hence, it is desirable for such firms to maintain their current competitive position by exploiting opportunities to improve internal efficiencies, such as further reducing the cost of internal operations and production systems (Benner & Tushman, 2003; March, 1991). In this environment, firms strive to become more operationally efficient to lower their marginal costs and sustain their competitive advantage (Xue et al., 2012). Therefore, firms’ business strategies will tend to focus on the identification of opportunities for cost reduction and operational improvement in stable market conditions (Tushman & Anderson, 1986). In such situations, firms are inclined to maximize the opportunity to benefit from operations-oriented actions to improve operational excellence and reduce internal operating costs (Xue et al., 2012).

Firms with a more proactive DSP relative to their competitors have an advantage in pursuing operations-oriented competitive actions in less dynamic environments. Firms can use digital systems to exploit their operations-oriented competitive actions by learning about the potential value that can be extracted from the reallocation of internal resources and the restructuring of business operations. This could encompass learning from peer firms on how to use digital technologies to best reduce inventory carrying costs, eliminate waste, and substitute labor by improving operational efficiency (Xue et al., 2012). Therefore, firms with a more proactive DSP are likely to have more detailed plans and knowledge regarding how digital technologies can support the use of internal resources more efficiently. For instance, firms with a proactive DSP can benchmark how peer firms are benefiting from the use of enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems and complementary collaboration tools. This competitive intelligence information can allow firms to determine how to digitally transform business processes or organizational structures to support operations-oriented competitive actions better than the competition.

When firms decide to take operations-oriented competitive actions that are substantially different from their prior actions, they would normally require a considerable amount of time and resources to change their competitive action repertoire. However, digital systems configured to support crossfunctional business collaboration can enable firms to rapidly take a different yet complementary set of competitive actions (Barki & Pinsonneault, 2005). In so doing, firms can achieve economies of scale and scope from DSP, as reflected in the ability to coordinate their new competitive action strategy with internal and external partners with greater reach (Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Kleis et al., 2012; Nazir & Pinsonneault, 2012; Wu et al., 2020). In addition, firms can use digital systems to monitor and control their business units, thereby minimizing unnecessary waste and reducing the cost of taking competitive actions (Xue et al., 2012). Therefore, firms with a more proactive DSP can gain an advantage in taking more operationsoriented competitive actions in low-dynamism industries. Based on these arguments, we posit:

H1: In more dynamic industries, firms with a more proactive DSP are likely to take a greater proportion of innovationoriented competitive actions. In contrast, in less dynamic industries, firms with a more proactive DSP are likely to take a greater proportion of operations-oriented competitive actions.

## The Performance Effect of DSP and Competitive Actions and the Moderating Role of Environmental Uncertainty

As noted, prior IS research has reported mixed findings regarding the impact of DSP on a firm’s performance (Ho et al., 2017; Mithas et al., 2013; Xue et al., 2017). In addition, the strategy literature points out that simply taking a higher number of competitive actions does not necessarily yield positive returns for firms (Andrevski & Ferrier, 2019). Thus, we now theorize how a firm’s proactive DSP can enable the firm to benefit financially from taking two types of competitive actions, especially when there is a fit between the type of competitive action and the level of environmental uncertainty. Our motivation is to help reconcile the inconsistent empirical findings in prior DSP literature by examining the interdependent relationship among the DSP, competitive actions, and firm performance. We use the contingency view to explore the extent to which a firm’s proactive DSP enhances the efficacy of the two types of competitive actions depending on the level of environmental uncertainty (i.e., industry dynamism).<sup>5</sup>

A proactive DSP positions a firm to eventually realize superior performance when taking innovation-oriented actions in dynamic industries. Since customer demand and rivals’ competitive moves are rapid and unpredictable in dynamic environments (Derfus et al., 2008), it is critical for firms to initiate innovation-oriented actions to retain and improve their market position (D’Aveni, 2010; Xue et al., 2012). A proactive DSP prepares firms to leverage digital technologies in order to generate business value from innovation-oriented actions. Firms that pursue a more proactive DSP relative to their rivals are better positioned to use technologies to take more effective innovation-oriented actions in competitive environments. Indeed, firms with a proactive DSP can detect rapid changes in customer needs and market demands before engaging in competitive actions based on the market intelligence and simulation capabilities provided by digital technologies.

Consequently, innovation-oriented actions enable firms to satisfy their customers’ needs more effectively with new or improved products/services and thus reap higher profits from the market. In contrast, firms with a less proactive DSP may not be able to engage in the innovation-oriented competitive actions that would yield the highest value because they do not have sufficient digital technology to support actions that would enable them to meet fast-changing customer preferences. Consequently, their market performance will be diminished in highly dynamic environments. Although it is possible to take innovation-oriented competitive actions without the support of digital technologies, the benefits yielded by the competitive actions may be lower. Notably, if competitive actions are not planned or executed with superior digital resources and capabilities, then they can be easily imitated by rival firms, which will erode the competitive advantages of these actions.

In low-dynamism environments in which customer demand does not change quickly and rivals’ moves are predictable, firms with a proactive DSP have an advantage over their rivals in realizing stronger financial performance from operationsoriented competitive actions. Such firms focus on increasing operational efficiency both within and across the firm’s boundaries because a proactive DSP enables the firm to execute its supply chain activities efficiently. A proactive DSP, in conjunction with operations-oriented actions, may result in improved coordination between a firm and its supply chain partners (Sambamurthy et al., 2003).<sup>6</sup> Depending on market conditions, timely and accurate access to information about the firm’s supply base, enabled through proactive planning on how and where to deploy digital systems, can increase the speed by which firms adjust their sourcing decisions. In addition, the standardization and integration across a firm’s internal supply chain functions, such as warehousing and transportation activities, is enhanced when the firm carefully considers the optimal use of digital technologies (Barki & Pinsonneault, 2005; Kleis, 2012; Nazir & Pinsonneault, 2012).

More precisely, firms that analyze and learn how industry peers standardize and integrate business processes using digital systems can benefit from the lessons learned in operating as a unified and cost-controlled organization (Tian & Xu, 2015). Likewise, firms that use similar strategies will be in a better position to leverage their digital systems to improve the performance of manufacturing facilities, which can result in improvements in lean inventory management practices. In sum, compared to firms in more dynamic industries, firms in less dynamic industries will benefit more by taking operations-oriented competitive actions when they proactively position their DSP to support enhanced coordination and collaboration not only within the firm but also with their supply chain partners. Thus, we posit:

H2: The moderation effect of a proactive DSP on the relationship between innovation-oriented competitive actions and firm performance is stronger in more dynamic industries, whereas the moderation effect of a proactive DSP on the relationship between operations-oriented competitive actions and firm performance is stronger in less dynamic industries.

## Empirical Analysis

## Sample and Data

We tested our hypotheses using a unique data set created from several different sources. Our sample consisted of Standard and Poor’s (S&P) 500 firms (e.g., Ho et al., 2017) from the 2011-2015 period. We measured DSP based on data from computer intelligence (CI). Prior studies have utilized CI data as a reliable source of a firm’s IT investment (e.g., Dong et al., 2021; Ho et al., 2017; Tambe & Hitt, 2012; Xue et al., 2017, 2018, 2021). We identified observable competitive actions based on the structured content analysis of news articles related to our sample of firms compiled from Lexis-Nexis from 2011 to 2015. We collected financial data from Compustat to measure firm-level (i.e., firm size) and industry-level variables (i.e., industry dynamism). Our final sample included 447 firms with 2,062 firm-year observations from 59 industries (two-digit SIC).<sup>7</sup> Table 1 provides the distribution of the sample by industry and year. The distribution is similar to that of prior DSP studies (e.g., Mithas et al., 2013; Ho et al., 2017).

## Variables

## Dependent Variables

Innovation- and operations-oriented competitive actions: Following prior studies (Andrevski & Ferrier, 2019; Chi et al., 2010), we measured the volume of innovation- and operationsoriented competitive actions based on a content analysis of news articles.<sup>8</sup> The volume of innovation- and operationsoriented competitive actions is defined as the total number of innovation- and operations-oriented actions initiated by a firm i in year t (Chi et al., 2010; Ferrier, 2001). To measure the volume of innovation-oriented competitive actions, we counted the number of actions pertaining to product development, product enhancement, new product introduction, and production innovation, following Li et al. (2010). We calculated the volume of operations-oriented actions by counting the number of actions related to new manufacturing facilities and supply chain enhancement.

Proportion of innovation-oriented competitive actions: In testing H1, to measure a firm’s tendency to take relatively more innovation-oriented actions, we used the proportion of the firm’s innovation-oriented actions relative to its total competitive actions as the dependent variable. This is in line with prior studies measuring the relative proportion of innovation-related actions (Phelps, 2010; Li et al., 2010).

Firm performance: Similar to prior studies, we used Tobin’s Q to measure firm performance (Bardhan et al., 2013; Dong et al., 2021; Kohli et al., 2012; Ho et al., 2017; Mithas et al., 2013; Sabherwal et al., 2019). Tobin’s Q is a market-based, forwardlooking, and risk-adjusted measure of the expected value of a firm (Bardhan et al., 2013; Dong et al., 2021; Kohli et al., 2012; Sabherwal et al., 2019). We measured Tobin’s Q at the end of year t as follows:

$$
T o b i n ^ {\prime} s Q _ {i t} = \frac {(M V E + P S + D E B T)}{T A},
$$

where MVE is the market value of common equity, PS is the liquidating value of preferred stock, DEBT is the book value of debt, and TA is the book value of total assets.

## Independent Variables

Digital strategic posture (DSP): Following prior literature (e.g., Mithas et al., 2013; Ho et al., 2017), we measured DSP as the difference between a focal firm’s IT intensity and the average value of the industry IT intensity at the two-digit SIC level using Harte-Hanks CI database.<sup>9</sup> To account for the time lag between IT investment and financial payoff, we lagged IT intensity by one year. We calculated the IT intensity of a firm as the ratio of IT investment to total assets (Ho et al., 2017; Xue et al., 2012). IT intensity represents the sum of all expenses regarding IT-related activities (Mithas et al., 2013). Then, we obtained the value of the DSP of a firm i in year t as follows:

$$
D S P _ {i t} = I T _ {i t} - \overline {{I T _ {J t}}},
$$

where $I T _ { i t }$ is the IT intensity of a focal firm i in year t, and $\overline { { I T _ { J t } } }$ is the average IT intensity in industry j, which firm i belongs to, in year t. A proactive (reactive) DSP means that a firm engages in more (less) IT activities relative to industry peers.

Industry dynamism: We calculated industry dynamism using a two-step approach (Sabherwal et al., 2019; Xue et al., 2012). First, the log-transformed value of the total revenue of industries (i.e., two-digit SIC) was regressed against the index variable of years over five years [t, t-4]. Second, the antilog value of the standard error of the regression coefficient was used as the measure of industry dynamism.

## Control Variables

We controlled for firm size to address firm-level heterogeneity (Mithas & Rust, 2016). Firm size was measured using the natural logarithm of total sales (Xue et al., 2017). R&D intensity (R&D expenditure/sales), advertisement intensity (advertising expenditure/sales), capital intensity (capital expenditure/sales), and financial leverage (total debt/shareholder’s equity) were included in the models (Tian & Xu, 2015). We added industry Tobin’s Q to our models to account for the industry-specific variations in Tobin’s Q (Bardhan et al., 2013; Mithas & Rust, 2016; Sabherwal et al., 2019). We also controlled for industry munificence and industry complexity (Han & Mithas, 2013; Mithas et al., 2013; Sabherwal et al., 2019; Xue et al., 2012). In the presence of heterogeneity and a significant panel effect, an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression may not be appropriate (Bardhan et al., 2013; Han & Mithas, 2013). Thus, we employed generalized least-squares (GLS) estimation with firm-fixed effects. There was a one-year lag between DSP and firm performance because the firm needed time to realize benefits from digital technologies (Mithas & Rust, 2016). A one-year lag was also used to attenuate the reverse causality between DSP and competitive actions. We included firm-fixed effects to control for unobserved firm-level heterogeneities and year dummies to control for year-specific effects (Hitt et al., 2002; Mithas & Rust, 2016; Ho et al., 2017; Xue et al., 2012). The average value of variance inflation factors (VIFs) of all independent variables is 1.13, which indicates that multicollinearity was not a serious issue in our estimation (Greene, 2012; Xue et al., 2018).

In addition to GLS estimation, we also used a systemgeneralized method of moments (GMM) estimation. System GMM has been widely utilized as an effective way to attenuate endogeneity caused by reverse causality and unobserved heterogeneity (Bardhan et al., 2013; Tambe & Hitt, 2012; Wu et al., 2018).<sup>11</sup> Specifically, we employed the Arellano-Bover/Blundell-Bond two-step system GMM estimation (Arellano & Bover, 1995; Blundell & Bond, 1998). This approach estimates differences and levels of regressions by considering internal instruments (i.e., lagged levels and differences) and is more efficient compared with methods that use either differences or levels only (Wu et al., 2018). In addition to the internal instruments, we also included an external instrument, the industry-average value of DSP, following prior literature (Xue et al., 2021).<sup>12</sup>

## Main Results

Table 3 presents the estimation results. Model 1 is the full model with DSP and the control variables. To assess the enabling role of DSP in the relative proportion of innovation-oriented actions contingent upon the level of environmental uncertainty (H1), we conducted sample-split analyses based on the median value of industry dynamism (Han et al., 2011).<sup>14</sup> The coefficient of DSP $( \beta = 0 . 0 9 6 ; p < 0 .$ 1 in Model 1, $, \beta = 0 . 1 1 8 ; p < 0 . 1$ 1 in Model 3) is positive and significant in high-dynamism industries, whereas the coefficient of DSP $( \beta = - 0 . 0 6 6 ; p < 0 . 0 1$ in Model $2 , \beta = - 0 . 0 3 6 ; p < 0 . 0 5$ in Model 4) is significantly negative in low-dynamism industries. The result provides support for H1, which posits that (1) a more proactive DSP enables relatively more innovation-oriented actions as a proportion of the total competitive actions in dynamic industries, and (2) a more proactive DSP supports relatively more operations-oriented actions in less dynamic industries.

Turning to H2, we tested how a proactive DSP moderates the effect of innovation- and operations-oriented competitive actions on firm performance under different levels of industry dynamism. Table 4 presents the results. The interaction term (β $= 0 . 0 1 2 ; p < 0 . 0 1$ in Model 1, $\beta = 0 . 0 0 7 ; p < 0 . 0 1$ in Model 3) consisting of DSP and the volume of innovation-oriented actions is positive and significant in high-dynamism industries, whereas the interaction term $( \beta = 0 . 0 0 \bar { 2 }$ in Model 2, $\cdot \beta = - 0 . 0 0 3$ in Model 4) is not significant in low-dynamism industries. Thus, the positive influence of proactive DSP on the relationship between innovation-oriented actions and firm performance is stronger in more dynamic industries. The interaction term $( \beta =$ 0.032; $p < 0 . 1$ in Model $2 , \beta = 0 . 0 5 2 ; p < 0 . 0 1$ in Model 4) involving DSP and the volume of operations-oriented competitive actions is positive and significant in low-dynamism industries but not in high-dynamism industries $( \beta = 0 . 0 1 0$ in Model 1, β = 0.006 in Model 3). Therefore, there is a stronger moderating effect of DSP on the association between operations-oriented actions and firm performance in less dynamic industries. These findings provide support for H2, which posits that environmental uncertainty differentiates the performance implication of DSP and competitive actions.

## Robustness Checks to Address Endogeneity

We conducted several additional analyses to demonstrate the robustness of our main results. First, we employed the extended Garen’s (1984) approach, which is a technique to address selection bias using a residual analysis (Saldanha et al., 2017; Havakhor et al., 2019; Ho et al., 2017; Khuntia et al., 2019). Second, we used two additional approaches to further mitigate the endogeneity concerns: (1) the Hausman-Taylor instrumental model (Saldanha et al., 2021)<sup>15</sup> and (2) the impact threshold of a confounding variable (ITCV) (Busenbark et al., 2022; Gamache & McNamara, 2019).<sup>16</sup> Taken together, these analyses suggest that our research results were not likely driven by endogeneity. ## Discussion and Conclusion

## Implications for Research

Our study contributes to the literature in multiple ways. First, we contribute to the CD literature by differentiating innovation- and operations-oriented competitive actions and examining how they are influenced by DSP. Prior CD literature has examined whether and under what conditions competitive actions taken by competing firms shape a firm’s market performance (Chen et al., 2021). A particular focus of prior CD research was the consideration of the volume, complexity, and heterogeneity of competitive actions (Chi et al., 2010; Ferrier, 2001). Yet those studies did not investigate the role of DSP in enabling different types of competitive actions. We fill this important void in the literature by theorizing how two different types of competitive actions (i.e., innovation versus operations-oriented actions) are enabled by a proactive DSP under different environmental conditions. In addition, we show that firms with a proactive DSP realize superior market performance when there is a fit between the type of competitive actions (i.e., innovation versus operationsoriented) and the level of industry dynamism. Our findings imply that while firms can initiate competitive actions based on proactive DSP, these actions are not effective unless the firm takes the appropriate type of competitive action, depending on environmental conditions.

Second, our work extends the DSP literature (e.g., Ho et al., 2017; Mithas et al., 2013) by theorizing and validating the dual pathways through which DSP affects competitive actions.<sup>18</sup> While prior DSP literature has focused on examining the direct link between DSP and firm performance, our study is among the first to examine the role of DSP in enabling competitive actions and in helping firms capture greater value from competitive actions. Doing so allowed us not only to provide a comprehensive understanding of how DSP contributes to competitive advantage but also to ease the tension created by the mixed findings reported in previous DSP research. For instance, Xue et al. (2017) suggested that proactive IT strategic posture increases the market return of firms based on the arguments related to firms’ strategic orientation on risk-taking and innovativeness. However, Ho et al. (2017) showed that while overinvestment in IT is positively associated with firm performance, underinvestment in IT does not matter for performance. Mithas et al. (2013) examined how DSP influences firm performance but found statistically insignificant results. One possible explanation for these mixed findings is that these prior IS studies focused on the direct impact of DSP on firm performance without considering the strategic role that DSP plays in supporting firms’ engagement in competitive actions, which is the focus of our study.

Finally, our study provides novel insights by demonstrating that it is important to consider contextual factors when assessing the synergistic role of DSP and competitive actions in improving firm performance. We show that environmental uncertainty has a significant bearing on how DSP influences the relationship between different types of competitive actions and firm performance. Firms need to carefully consider the characteristics of their business environment when deciding on the volume and type of competitive actions they should take even if they have a more proactive DSP than their rivals. Specifically, we show that firms that take more innovationoriented competitive actions enabled by a proactive DSP can achieve superior market performance in more dynamic environments. The basis for our argument is that firms seek to quickly capture potentially high profit margins from their innovations by being first to market, which is made possible by competitive advantages derived from their proactive DSP. In contrast, firms in less dynamic environments benefit more from operations-oriented actions facilitated by proactive DSP. In less dynamic environments, firms seek to focus on reducing internal costs because there are fewer benefits from acting more innovatively in stable markets. Operations-oriented actions complemented with a proactive DSP allow firms to operate more cost-efficiently.

## Implications for Practice

Our research offers important managerial implications. First, we provide practical guidance to managers regarding the importance of maintaining a more proactive DSP relative to industry rivals when taking competitive actions. Although managers often use industry benchmark reports to determine the appropriate level of investment in IT resources by monitoring their industry peers’ digital investment, their understanding of the role of digital investments in supporting competitive actions may be limited. Our findings suggest that digital technologies can help firms realize superior performance by allowing them to take more “effective” competitive actions. With the proper prioritization and positioning of IT investments, firms with a more proactive DSP can expand their search spaces for new information and improve their ability to combine existing knowledge, which is necessary to create more innovation-oriented actions. For instance, BASF applied IT systems to analyze research papers to develop new materials (Wu et al., 2020). Similarly, IT systems can enable firms to take operations-oriented actions in a more effective and efficient manner. For example, General Electric (GE) launched the Industrial Internet initiative, an open network that connects its machines, people, and data. GE can use the network to conduct real-time and predictive analyses to optimize operations within/across firms including customers and suppliers (Lakhani et al., 2015), which has allowed GE to stay ahead of its competitors (e.g., Siemens, Phillips, and Honeywell).

Second, our findings underscore that depending on the level of environmental uncertainty, the role of digital technologies in value creation from engaging in competitive actions may vary. In particular, digital technologies should be deployed in ways that support innovation-oriented actions (operationsoriented actions) in more (less) dynamic industries in order to increase the value of competitive actions. In other words, there is no one-size-fits-all strategy in deploying digital technologies and taking competitive actions. Therefore, in order to achieve superior performance, managers should constantly monitor the business environment and rivals’ digital strategies to determine the “right” type of competitive action to take.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study has limitations that provide directions for future research. First, we measured DSP based on the level of the firm’s total IT investment. This approach may have a limitation in capturing the diverse digital activities included in DSP’s conceptual definition. Future research should consider alternative measures that can differentiate a firm’s various digital activities. Second, while we did not consider the relative importance of each competitive action in quantifying the volume of competitive actions, we acknowledge that not every competitive action has equal or similar effects on firm performance. In addition, our measure of competitive action may be subject to measurement errors because firms may not announce all their competitive actions publicly. Third, we did not consider the role of IS governance in building DSP. It would be interesting to examine the differences across firms in terms of how they develop DSP, as it may influence the value implications of DSP in relation to competitive actions. Fourth, our findings are based on S&P 500 firms, which consist of large firms in the United States. Future studies could use samples from small- and medium-sized firms and companies in other regions to improve the generalizability of our findings.

## Garen’s Approach

We employed Garen’s (1984) approach to attenuate the endogeneity concern following prior studies in IS (Ho et al., 2017; Saldanha et al., 2017; Khuntia et al., 2019). Garen’s approach accounts for the selection bias using a continuous endogenous variable based on a two-stage procedure. The underlying assumption of this approach is that DSP is endogenous if companies determine the level of DSP, depending on firm- or industry-specific factors. In the first stage, we defined a continuous variable, DSP, as a selection variable after controlling for the industry-average value of DSP. We have obtained residuals (i.e., ??̃), which are the difference between the actual DSP and the predicted DSP in the first stage. Then, we ran the second-stage model, including residual (i.e., ??̃) and their interaction terms with endogenous variables (i.e., ??̃ × DSP). The estimation results are summarized in Table B1. After controlling for endogeneity correction terms, we obtained qualitatively similar results. In particular, the coefficient of DSP (β = 0.104 in Model 1) is significant and positive in high-dynamism industries, wherea the coefficient of DSP (β = -0.067 in Model 2) is significant and negative in low-dynamism industries. Therefore, we conclude that our results were not likely driven by endogeneity.
