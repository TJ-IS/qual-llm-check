# Big data and firm performance: The roles of market-directed capabilities and business strategy

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Big data resources Data warehousing Resource-based view (RBV) of the firm Market-directed capabilities Business strategy Firm performance

## A B S T R A C T

Despite being the object of much interest, deep insights regarding why and when investments in big data re sources enhance firm performance are lacking in the literature. Building on the resource-based view (RBV) and data provided by 301 senior marketing managers, this study reveals that big data resources primarily improve firm performance by enhancing the market-directed capabilities of the firm. In addition, the data indicate that firms pursuing a differentiation rather than cost-leadership strategy gain most from big data resource in vestments and that such resources account for 13 % of the variance in the performance of firms pursuing a differentiation strategy.

## 1. Introduction

At the end of 2016, it was estimated that 90 % of the world's data had been created in the last two years alone, at a rate of some 2.5 quintillion bytes of data per day [1]. Moreover, the volume of data is growing exponentially, and it is expected that by 2020 there will be more than 16 zettabytes (16 trillion GB) of useful<sup>1</sup> data [2]. A number of factors ac count for this, including the global proliferation of mobile devices; the advent of the Internet of Things (which means that technologies and not just humans are producing data); and of course, the rise of social media, which has made everyone a broadcaster and thus data producer. The bulk of data is also no longer just numerical and is seldom structured. Most of the data is in the form of unstructured text and increasingly video, audio, and graphics.

In the present research, big data refers to techniques, technologies, systems, practices, methodologies, and applications related to the acquisition, storage, integration, analysis, and deployment of massive amounts of structured and unstructured data to support business decision-making [3–5]. Given its potential to fuel meaningful performance gains, the vendor market for big data technologies has grown 40 % annually, from \$3.2 billion in 2010 to \$16.9 billion in 2015. And this growth was predicted to continue at a 23 % rate over 2015–2019, with annual spending estimated at \$48.6 billion by the end of 2019 [6].

Recently, McKinsey Global Institute [7] published a report stating that the value potential of big data remains widely uncaptured and unexploited by firms today. According to this report, the challenges that hinder firms from reaping greater rewards from big data relate to three major factors: (1) information technology (IT) infrastructure; (2) strat egy, leadership, and talent; and (3) organizational structure and pro cesses. More specifically, many firms are unsure of how to make use of big data, are cautious about investing in new information technologies, or simply find big data analytics too complicated [8,9]. Making sense of often unstructured data may require a lengthy process of data prepa ration. To overcome potential technological, skill-based, and organiza tional challenges, firms need to acquire a diverse set of big data–related IT resources [5,10,11].

The important challenges associated with big data–related initiatives notwithstanding, industry surveys reveal that an increasing number of firms regard big data as a major influence on corporate strategy [10–13]. While no business function remains untouched by big data opportu nities, the customer-facing functions (e.g., marketing, sales, and customer service) are the top drivers of such initiatives [12]. The com bination of big data technologies and greater access to consumer data through web-based channels generates customer insights not previously possible [3,14]. Big data insights thus put managers in a superior posi tion to design timely, automated, and highly personalized product and service offerings, with human expertise remaining critical but in a supporting role [5,9,15].

Additionally, existing research emphasizes the role of industries in determining the value-creating potential of big data [16,17]. Certain industry sectors may offer fewer opportunities due to high entry bar riers, limited access to data, and data privacy and protection concerns [11,13]. Surprisingly, however, the role of firm strategy in fostering big data–driven competitive advantage has received little attention in extant literature. Since big data may enhance firms’ market-directed capabilities through two distinct mechanisms, namely, by improving either the efficiency or the effectiveness of activities, it is important to understand how the business impact of big data varies between firms pursuing either a cost leadership or a differentiation strategy [18,19] as a means of achieving competitive advantage.

Some firms, for instance, develop big data solutions to reduce operating costs by optimizing and automating marketing processes to gain a low-cost advantage. This is exemplified by Walmart, which has dramatically reduced their response time between detecting a business problem to finding an actionable solution from an average of 2–3 weeks to 20 min across its 20,000 stores worldwide [20]. Other firms, however, focus on generating customer insights that present firms with market-based opportunities to achieve a differential advantage over competition through product and service personalization [10–13]. To illustrate, Apple is known to collect multistructured data about how, when, and where customers use their different products to predict future customer needs better than their rivals [20].

Despite big data's growing diffusion, known challenges, and potential benefits, academic research is generally silent regarding how, when, and to what extent big data investments impact firm performance [5]. To begin to redress this knowledge gap, we build on the resource-based view (RBV) of the firm [21] and research on big data [3,5], the IT business value paradigm [22,23], and market-directed capabilities [24–26], to propose how big data resources (i.e., the combination of complementary IT resources relevant to the use of big data) on perfor mance outcomes are mediated by its effects on firms’ market-directed (i. e., sense and respond) capabilities [27,28]. Moreover, we posit that the performance gains associated with big data resources are contingent on firms’ chosen business strategy (i.e., cost leadership versus differentia tion), with the nature of this moderating effect a matter of theoretical debate. We test these ideas using data provided by 301 senior marketing executives employed in B2C business units across large firms in the United States and, in so doing, make the following three contributions to the literature.

First, empirical studies focusing on the business impact of big data resources (hereafter referred to as BDR) are scarce. To the best of our knowledge, this investigation is the first large-scale empirical study to examine the action mechanism and impact of strategic big data in vestments on firm performance. It extends management as well as in formation services and marketing research by examining the extent to which BDR enhance market-directed capabilities (hereafter referred to as MC) and, ultimately, firm performance. The contribution and rationale of our research model is based on the complementarity resources theory approach, which is now well articulated in IS and management research [29–31]. This approach suggests that in order to establish the effect of IT/BDR on business value, we must first understand their impact on firm capabilities (i.e., logistics, customer service, marketing and analytics). This is because IT, including BDR, are hardware and software tools that do not create value in isolation. Rather, they work synergistically [32–34] with business processes and other IS and organizational capa bilities (i.e., logistics, customer service, marketing and analytics) to enhance firm outcomes [27,34,35]. Consistent with our theorizing, the study results show that by enhancing firm’s MC, BDR result in an 11–12 % improvement in firm performance.

Second, this study sheds light on the specific bundle and relative importance of big data–related IT resources necessary to influence firm performance. That is, this research extends IT business value research by synthesizing knowledge from recent business analytics literature to formulate and empirically evaluate a parsimonious conceptualization of BDR. Finally, this study contributes to big data research by highlighting the critical role of firms’ business strategy in driving big data success. Contrary to the belief that industry characteristics largely determine the value potential of big data [10,16,17], our findings provide some compelling albeit surprising evidence that differentiating firms are far more likely to benefit from big data than cost leadership firms.

## 2. Theoretical framework

## 2.1. Resource-based view of the firm

We rely on the RBV of the firm as the theoretical foundation of this study for a number of reasons: (1) its applicability across IS research [32, 35], marketing [24,36], and strategy [37]; (2) its focus on within-firm factors to explain differential outcomes of interest [38]; and (3) its comprehensive conceptual network of IT-related and marketing-related resources and capabilities [33,39].

The theoretical arguments of RBV [21] highlight the management of firm resources as the basic units of analysis and indicate that resource heterogeneity across firms accounts for differential performance [40, 41]. A resource refers to tangible and intangible assets or any given strength or weakness of a firm that can be used to improve its efficiency and effectiveness or an available factor owned or controlled by the firm [42].

For strategic resources to become sources of competitive advantage, they must be leveraged by capabilities via organizational processes that create value for the firm [27,39]. A capability enables the firm to leverage bundles of resources advantageously in organizational pro cesses to create value [27,28]. Capabilities are potential sources of competitive advantage that, when actualized, can be called a core competency.

## 2.2. Big data resources

Building on the underpinning of RBV, IT business value research investigates the impact of IT on firm performance [22,23,32]. This discourse posits that diverse IT-related resources are combined into unique resource bundles that enhance firm performance and provide for competitive advantage [32,43,44]. As a strategic, firm-level IT resource, we define BDR as a combination of complementary IT resources neces sary for big data to enhance firm performance.

IT business value research has identified three general types of IT resources: (1) technology resources, (2) human IT resources, and (3) organizational IT resources [44–46]. These resources represent neces sary and complementary dimensions that when combined appropriately result in superior performance [32,35,44]. Consistent with RBV and based on a review of conceptual big data studies [3,5], we identify three distinct IT business value resources that form firm-level, strategic BDR: (1) big data technology resources; (2) big data analytics skills; and (3) organizational BDR.

Big data technology resources refer to novel information technologies that are necessary to handle big data, for example, various data formats and data types derived from interactions between people and machines beyond the ability of current relational databases and legacy systems [3, 13]. Such technologies include nonrelational databases, middleware, data warehousing, and analytic tools, all of which enable firms to cap ture and integrate big data in real-time; they likewise permit firms to deliver analytical results in accessible and understandable form to ex ecutives to support business decision-making [3,4,14].

Once the technological infrastructure is in place, firms often struggle to make effective use of big data [8,9]. Big data analytics skills refer to human resources acquired from internal or external partner sources, sources that have the knowledge to derive market insights from big data [47]. Indeed, the big data skills gap is cited as the primary obstacle to the adoption of a big data–driven marketing strategy [10,11,13,48]. In a nutshell, firms need data scientists who can find patterns in large quantities of multistructured data and transform these into useful and actionable insight [5,15,49]. As most of the data being produced today is in the form of unstructured text and, increasingly, video, audio, and graphics, making sense of it is no longer as simple as with structured and organized data. Individually, most of consumer-produced isolated messages enlighten marketers very little. When agglomerated, however, they can reveal patterns and provide insights that can significantly inform researchers and managers.

Building strategic BDR requires a transformation in organizational culture to embed big data as part of daily operations [5,8,10,11,13,48]. Organizational big data resources thus refer to top management and cul tural support for big data–related initiatives within a firm. Firms recognize that many factors in addition to IT/BDR go into building business resources, which, in turn, positively influence firm perfor mance. These complementary assets can be organizational factors and be tied to the firm strategies/organizational policies; organizational structure; and firm culture, people, and (top) management as well as management routines [23,50–53], among others. Thus, in the literature it is established and known that organizational assets, such as top management and cultural support, are complementary assets [29,34, 54]. A data-driven culture reflects an openness to systematically apply big data analytics to solve business problems [10]. Top management support, in turn, provides leadership and vision that are crucial to ensure that managers are aligned to support big data, given that people are not naturally inclined to trust or understand data-based models [8,48]. Effective users are almost always found in firms where top management highly values big data [10,11,13,48].

We posit that these three stand-alone BDR should be conceptualized holistically in order to identify the combinations of IT resources required for achieving desired performance outcomes [32,35]. While stand-alone big data–related resources can be purchased from strategic factor mar kets, higher-order BDR assets are difficult to copy and imitate. In fact, IT resources may even be cospecialized such that one resource has little or no value without the other, as occurs with hardware and software, for example [22]. Thus, consistent with RBV’s resource complementarity argument, we posit that these diverse resources act in a synergistic fashion.

## 2.3. Market-directed capabilities

RBV has become an influential theory in marketing research to examine the link between market-based resources and capabilities, firm performance, and competitive advantage [36,39]. Several typologies of marketing resources and capabilities have been proposed in the litera ture [39]. To inform our firm-level study, we chose the broadest clas sification that presents MC as a strategic firm capability [25,26,55]. As such, we define MC as the firm’s ability to understand and meet customer needs and deliver its products and services to customers [24, 25].

Firm-level, strategic MCs encompass eight distinct lower-level, operational MC [26]. Four of them are related to transforming re sources into products and services based on the firm’s marketing mix processes and include: (1) pricing, (2) product development, (3) channel management, and (4) marketing communications. Three others – (5) market information management, (6) marketing planning, and (7) marketing implementation – help manage marketing mix capabilities and resource allocations related to their execution. Finally, (8) selling capabilities are processes carried out to encourage customer purchases [26].

These interdependent lower-level MC work in a synergistic fashion [26,55]. Like BDR, we thus posit that MC should be conceptualized holistically to account for the joint effects of lower-level MC on outcomes.

## 2.4. Business strategy

While several conceptualizations of business strategy have been proposed in the academic literature, the partially overlapping strategic typologies by Miles and Snow [56] and Porter [19] have received important attention [18]. The former focuses on the rate of product-market change and the latter on customers and competitors [57, 58]. We adopt Porter’s [19] typology as the most appropriate one for the current study. RBV scholars view it as complementary to RBV because of its emphasis on the firm’s external environment. In this way, it offers an additional perspective to RBV’s internal focus as the basis for achieving competitive advantage [38,42].

According to Porter [19], business strategy refers to how a firm creates value compared to its competitors based on lower costs or dif ferentiation and based on the scope of its target market (i.e., entire market versus market focus). Firms that try to gain competitive advan tage through a cost advantage (cost leadership strategy) are internally oriented and focus on efficiency gains by reducing costs across internal processes. Firms pursuing a differentiation advantage (differentiation strategy) employ an external focus to understand their market envi ronments, including their customers, competitors, and innovations [19].

Building on the strategic management literature, marketing research suggests that the actions undertaken by the marketing department and the role of MC to gain competitive advantage depend on firms’ selected business strategies [25,58]. Firms’ emphasis on cost advantage or dif ferentiation advantage sets different requirements for MC and for their relative importance to achieve superior performance. For example, the contribution of MC to firm performance relative to other strategic ca pabilities, such as operations or R&D capabilities, may vary as a function of business strategy [25]. Compared to a cost leadership strategy, a differentiation strategy underscores the role of MC because market-directed activities and creating and acting on customer big data and market intelligence are critical to achieve a differentiation advan tage. In sum, prior research asserts that the role of marketing is more important in firms that adopt a differentiation strategy as compared to a cost leadership strategy [57,59].

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Building on the IT business value paradigm and resource-based studies in marketing, we advance the conceptual model depicted in Fig. 1. Consistent with our preceding exposition, the model suggests that BDR are leveraged via MC to ultimately enhance firm performance. That is, our model predicts that MC is a mediator of the effects of BDR on firm performance. Furthermore, as we alluded to earlier, we anticipate that these constituent effects are contingent upon the business strategy pursued by the firm.

IT business value research [23] has adopted resource complemen tarity arguments to explain the interactions between IT and non-IT re sources and capabilities and how IT impacts performance [32]. Strategic IT resources do not generally lead to superior firm performance, but those that influence other complementary strategic resources within business processes may yield competitive advantage [43].

Based on this logic, BDR is posited as an enabler of MC. More spe cifically, BDR enables firms to gain market and customer insights, and continuously sense and act on market changes that are critical to suc cessfully execute MC [60]. For example, BDR enable firms to better innovate and optimize any given element of the marketing mix with big data–driven predictive models and experiments [3,4,9]. Furthermore, person-specific, context-specific, and location-specific offerings and communications can be tailored by big data–driven insights [61]. Data is available in real-time and at a significantly lower cost than traditional market research to tap into customer needs [4]. Big data–driven mar keting organizations thus find it easier, faster, and cheaper to orches trate and experiment with the marketing mix to set it at optimal levels.

In sum, prior RBV studies suggest that IT resources have a positive effect on non-IT organizational capabilities [44,45]. Consistent with this research, we expect that BDR will provide for more efficient and effective MC. Therefore:

## H1. BDR have a positive influence on MC.

In contrast with the unexplored relationship between BDR and MC, the impact of MC on firm performance has received substantial support in prior research [26,55]. MC is crucial to understand customers and to deliver offerings that match their needs and to create firm-client re lationships. It is therefore a key driver of superior value [62]. MC can be rare, valuable, nonsubstitutable, and imperfectly imitable and thus have potential for superior performance and competitive advantage [24,26]. A meta-analytic study found that MC influence firm performance more than other core firm capabilities, R&D and operational capabilities, which can be more easily imitated by competitors [25]. In sum, we expect that MC have a positive influence on firm outcomes [26,55].

## H2. MC have a positive influence on firm performance.

Our conceptual model is rooted in the complementarity resources theory approach, which is now well articulated in IS and management research [29–31,63]. The intangible value created by IT is deeply embedded in the business processes which it supports. This “IT-em beddedness” approach means that rather than evaluating the impact of IT in isolation, one must understand how IT/BDR impact firm capabilities first (i.e., logistics, customer service, marketing, and ana lytics) and then how these mediate the impact on business value. IT, including BDR, are simply hardware and software tools that do not create value in isolation; rather, they create value by working syner gistically with other IS and organizational factors to enhance business processes [32–34]. This is labeled “IT-embeddedness” because, in many cases, the IT becomes an integral part of the value creation process. IT-embeddedness thus suggests that IT is not necessarily the “single isolated” driver of firm performance. Instead, it is an enabler of various business capabilities (i.e., logistics, customer service, marketing, and analytics) that are the primary drivers of business value and firm performance.

Prior research offers substantial support for this line of theorizing as it finds that business process capabilities mediate the effect of IT on firm performance [29,34,54,63,64]. Most of these studies concern process-related mediators such as innovation capability, whereas me diators relating to BDR, customer relationship management (CRM), so cial media technology, and firm performance have received considerably less attention [30]. IT business value studies have shown that the impact of IT resources and capabilities on firm performance is indirect, working via complementary non-IT resources and capabilities [45,50]. We highlight several of these studies next.

Akter et al. [65] confirm the mediating role of a big data analytics capability between IT resources and firm performance. Ferraris et al. [66] show that firms that develop more big data analytics capabilities than others, both technological and managerial, increase their perfor mance via improved knowledge management capabilities. Anning-Dorson [67] show that a customer involvement capability me diates the link between innovation and service firm performance. In yet another study, Wang and Kim [68] build on RBV to confirm that social media usage capabilities mediate the relationship between social CRM and firm performance. Likewise, Fosso Wamba et al. [69] show the mediating effects of process-oriented firm capabilities on the relation ship between big data analytics and firm performance. Hao and Song [70] show how strategic marketing and IT capabilities mediate the effect of technology-driven resources on firm performance. As a final example, Mu [71] suggests that marketing capability acts as a mediating mecha nism that transmits the positive influences of a dynamic capability to new product development performance, specifically, and firm perfor mance, in general.

These findings are confirmed in practice as well. For example, the IT in Amazon’s capability of “instant personalized product proposal” is deeply embedded in the customer value creation process, and it is difficult to separate it out to measure its direct impact on Amazon’s firm performance (without taking into account the indirect and enhancing marketing and logistics-supply chain management (SCM) capabilities).

The existing literature thus suggests that it is perhaps not appropriate to consider the value-creation potential of IT in isolation. Rather, prior research indicates that to demonstrate the business value of IT, it is necessary to first consider its effects on those business capabilities (i.e., logistics, customer service, marketing, and analytics) that ultimately enhance firm performance. Importantly, the literature also underscores that there is a difference between creating value and creating differen tial value [23]. Differential firm value and higher firm performance do not come from the adoption of a simple IT system (e.g., big data storage capability) that can be copied and competed away. That is merely a source of value. Rather differential value results when IT can be lever aged via other firm functions – such as marketing, production, supply chain, and HR – to enhance capabilities that can be used to establish competitive superiority. That is, differential value results when firms use IT to create capabilities that are heterogeneous, imperfectly mobile and difficult to imitate [43]. This distinction between the creation of value versus differential value provides further support for our expectation that the effects of IT (in this case BDR) on firm performance are medi ated by the business processes and capabilities it supports.

In summary, we propose that marketing capabilities are a mecha nism through which BDR is leveraged to enhance firm performance [30, 73]. As such, we argue that BDR is a source of differential value via marketing capabilities [72]. This expectation is formally captured by the following hypothesis:<sup>2</sup>

## H3. MC mediate the relationship between BDR and firm performance.

We also anticipate that business strategy influences the relationship between BDR and MC. While no studies have specifically investigated whether business strategy type moderates the effect of BDR on MC, related research has found that a cost leadership strategy has a weak negative moderating effect (at a 10 % significance level) on the rela tionship between market orientation and lower-level MC, namely, new product development and marketing communications, but not on the market orientation-pricing capabilities link [64]. Hence, prior research offers mixed evidence regarding the likely nature of the interaction between BDR and business strategy type in prediction of MC.

Furthermore, BDR can enhance MC through two distinct mecha nisms: by improving the efficiency of marketing processes or by enhancing their effectiveness. First, cost and time efficiencies in MC may be achieved through the optimization and automation of marketing processes. Similarly, RBV scholars discuss the inside-out perspective as one that focuses on the optimal exploitation of internal processes and existing opportunities [24,73]. Cost leadership firms specifically pursue a big data–driven cost advantage that builds on this inside-out founda tion to gain superior performance. Therefore, we expect the positive impact of BDR on MC efficiency to be stronger in cost leadership firms.

Conversely, gains in MC effectiveness are likely to be better achieved by firms pursuing a differentiation strategy. A differentiation advantage requires that firms focus on MC effectiveness by delivering better products and services to their customers. Big data–driven outside-in activities, such as market-sensing and CRM, enable the exploration of new business opportunities [24,60]. Specifically, data-driven insights from the marketplace help firms better anticipate their customers’ and competitors’ behaviors and to innovate by personalizing their products and services as well as other aspects of market-directed activities [5,9, 60]. Firms that emphasize a cost leadership strategy are thus less likely to improve MC effectiveness through market-based product innovation due to their inherent internal orientation.

In sum, we expect that BDR enhances MC through both efficiency and effectiveness mechanisms. However, what is required of MC to achieve superior performance depends on whether the firm strives for low cost or differentiation over its rivals [25,58]. In the context of big data, these opposing strategies would play out differently depending on whether BDR is primarily used to focus on improving MC efficiency or effectiveness. However, there is no empirical evidence that predicts which firm strategic type benefits more from big data–driven MC. Based on these considerations, we formally hypothesize that:

H4a. The positive influence of BDR on MC is moderated by business strategy.

Prior research does suggest, however, that a differentiation strategy enhances the importance of MC as a driver of firm performance [25]. Prior work grounded in the Miles-Snow [56] typology has also shown the moderating effect of business strategy on the relationship between market linking and other lower-level MC on firm performance [74] and between market orientation and firm performance [58,75,76], respec tively. For example, differentiating firms are in a better position to explore new market opportunities, develop new differentiated products and service innovations, and expand to more profitable market segment than defenders, which refer to firms focusing on cost efficiencies [75].

Since MC is the market orientation deployment mechanism, it is reasonable to assume that prior findings on the market orientation-firm performance link may be extended to the MC-firm performance link [55]. As noted earlier, the role of the marketing department and MC are more important to achieve a differentiation advantage because mar keting processes related to market intelligence generation and use are critical [57–59]. Cost leadership firms are more likely to rely on oper ational capabilities to gain superior performance by reducing costs [25]. We thus expect that a differentiation strategy strengthens the relation ship between MC and firm performance.

H4b. The positive influence of MC on firm performance is stronger for firms pursuing a differentiation strategy than for firms pursuing a cost leadership strategy.

Finally, prior research also shows that environmental factors have direct effects on firm performance [26,58]. Therefore, beyond the hy pothesized effects, we control for several firm and industry character istics that could influence the action mechanisms between a firm’s BDR, MC, and firm performance. Widely adopted control variables, including firm (or strategic business unit, SBU) size, goods versus services firms, industry, competitive intensity, market turbulence, and technological turbulence, are included in our model to partial out noise in the variance [26,59,77].

Firm/SBU size is the mean of number of employees [57]. Larger firms benefit from slack resources and economies of scale and scope that may positively influence the level of firm assets and performance [77,78]. Differences between goods and services firms do exist [57]. However, no assumptions are made about the specific direction of potential variances between goods and services firms in terms of their impact on firm per formance [25]. Industry refers to differences between industry sectors, namely, B2C manufacturing, finance and insurance, and retail. Big data–driven industries could vary due to high entry barriers, limited access to data, and data privacy and protection concerns, among others [10,11,13].

Other relevant controls were also used to partial out variance. Competitive intensity is the intensity of rivalry among competitors in an industry and refers to the extent to which firms within an industry put pressure on one another and limit each other’s profit potential. If rivalry is fierce, then competitors are trying to steal profit and market share from one another [57,77]. Market turbulence is the rate of change in the composition of customers and their preferences [57,77]. An unstable economic climate, ever-changing customer needs, and up-and-coming new technology continually stir up market turbulence. Technological turbulence is understood as the rate of technological change in an in dustry [79] and refers to firms operating in markets with rapid tech nological change, aggressive competition, and volatile customer needs, which tend to build stronger marketing assets to cope with such con tingencies [57,77].

## 4. Methodology and data

## 4.1. Method and sampling: coverage of data-intensive and traditional industry types

We employed a field study methodology and professional, permission-based worldwide panel provider-administered online ques tionnaires for data collection.<sup>3</sup> Our sampling frame focused on SBUs in large (> 1000 employees), U.S.-based, B2C manufacturing and service firms that have invested in big-data technologies. We chose these three sampling criteria after careful consideration. First, and consistent with prior research, this study focuses on the SBU level of analysis [57,80]. If there were no distinct SBUs, respondents were instructed to answer at the firm level. Second, big data adoption is hindered by considerable initial investment, entry barriers, and expertise required. Therefore, large firms are more likely to own or have access to BDR [13]. Third, big data investment is more common among firms operating in B2C sectors, as understanding the needs of a large number of customers is more complex in B2C than in B2B sectors, where the number of customers is lower and the role of the salesforce is more accentuated in under standing individual customers' needs.

We used the commercial research panel provider to identify senior marketing executives in SBUs in different B2C industries. Prior studies examining MC have also adopted a multi-industry approach [26,74]. The questionnaire was sent to senior marketing executives in 2497 SBUs. After a rigorous screening process, the final sample consists in 301 re sponses (12 % response rate). Adequate knowledge by informants (respondent competency) was also controlled for by means of a separate question in the questionnaire [81]. Sample characteristics can be found in Appendix A.

The data was cleared for nonresponse biases, which included screening for possible differences in variable means between early and late responders. Specifically, an independent samples T-test was carried out to compare the differences of dependent variable (DV) means be tween early and late respondents [82]. In two-tailed tests with suffi ciently high power (> 0.8), early and late respondent groups did not differ significantly (firm performance, t = 0.128, p = 0.898). Thus, nonresponse bias should not be a problem in generalizing to the sam pling frame.

## 4.2. Operationalization of constructs and measurement instrument

All our measures are directly adopted from or based substantially on scales validated by prior studies (see Appendix B) and were measured on a seven-point Likert scale. Measures include first-order reflective and first-order formative scales and three higher-order constructs. Following measurement model specification guidelines [83], we determined that some first-order scales modeled in prior studies as reflective were formative (Big Data Technology Resources, Big Data Analytics Skills, Organizational Big Data Resources).

For the study’s key second-order constructs, the decision rules by Wong, Law, and Huang [84] were also adopted for model specification purposes. Based on these analyses, BDR is modeled as a Type IV (sec ond-order formative, first-order formative) formative composite construct, and MC and firm performance as Type I (second-order reflective, first-order reflective) reflective composite constructs, respectively. Firm/SBU size is controlled for by means of number of employees [57]. Differences between goods and services firms are controlled via a dummy variable [57,59]. Industry is measured by three industry dummies to account for the three largest big data–driven in dustry sectors (with also the largest number of observations in our dataset), namely, B2C manufacturing, finance and insurance, and retail. Finally, competitive intensity, market turbulence, and technological turbulence are accounted for using two-item scales [85]. Constructs, measurement types, item contents, sources of measures, formative item weights, and reflective item loadings are summarized in Appendix B.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. General approach

Measurement model validation encompassed reliability and validity analyses for reflective measures and validity analyses for formative measures, respectively. After interpreting the measurement model, we estimated the structural model [86]. We used partial least squares (PLS) structural equation modeling [87] because the model is complex, in cludes formative measures, and our objective was to test a novel, refined theoretical approach [88]. Furthermore, PLS is less demanding with respect to sample size and is adaptable for conducting multigroup ana lyses with smaller sub-samples [89]. The version used was Smart PLS 3 [90] with 5000 resample bootstraps to estimate the p-values of mea surement properties [88,91].

We modeled our key second-order constructs BDR, MC, and firm performance using a hierarchical component model with repeated in dicators [87]. A path-weighting scheme (Mode A) was adopted for the reflectively measured repeated indicators of MC and firm performance. A Mode B was selected for BDR’s formatively measured repeated in dicators, an approach that shows the most reliable results for structural models that include formative higher-order constructs [92].

Prior to multigroup analysis (MGA) for moderation testing, the measurement invariance of composite models (MICOM) procedure assessed measurement model invariance across predefined groups [93]. MICOM is regarded as the best method to test for measurement invari ance of reflective and formative latent constructs (composites) between different groups in PLS-SEM [91,93]. Following this was MGA using the PLS-MGA method with 5000 resample bootstraps to test for observed heterogeneities in the total sample by comparing structural models across groups [94,95].

Because of the complexity of our structural model (number of repeated indicators in higher-order constructs), we treated the secondorder factors BDR, MC, and firm performance as composite scores for MICOM and PLS-MGA analyses. The adoption of composite scores is appropriate in our case because: (1) the validity of the three higherorder scales is established in the measurement model testing with the pooled sample (N = 301) [96]; (2) measurement invariance can still be established in MICOM testing across subsamples for the higher-order composites [93]; and (3) given the size of subsamples, using compos ite scores helps meet the minimum sample requirements recommended by PLS scholars to ensure structural model stability and valid results [91].

## 5.2. Measurement model

Reflectively measured constructs were assessed in terms of item-level reliability (Appendix B), construct reliability, and convergent and discriminant validity (Appendix D) for both first- and second-order measurement models. All item loadings, composite reliability, and average variance extracted (AVE) exceeded the thresholds for accept able reliability and convergent validity [88] and the instrument showed reasonable discriminant validity [97]. The first-order and second-order formative measures were validated via multicollinearity (variance inflation factor, VIF, values) and construct validity testing (item weights, loadings, and their significance levels) [83,98]. All VIF values were below 2.3, which is below the recommended threshold of 3.3 [99]. Formative indicator weights and their significances (Appendix A) and loadings (> 0.70) also showed satisfactory psychometric properties.

Since both independent and dependent measures were collected from the same source, we first used confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and Harman’s single-factor test to assess common method bias (CMB) [100]. Eight factors had eigenvalues greater than 1, and together they accounted for 59 % of the total variance. The first factor accounted for 37 % of the total variance. Further, we carried out additional common method tests with a marker variable [101]. This test proved to be useful in the testing of CMB by Schwarz et al. [102]. We chose the four-item perceptual Astrology Interest [103] scale and included it in the survey instrument. The correlations between all predictor and criterion vari ables are highly significant. Astrology Interest, in turn, has a nonsig nificant correlation of 0.085 with our DV firm performance. We used this correlation of the marker with the criterion scale to partial out the common method effect from other correlations to evaluate the extent of method bias. The tests showed that the partial correlations between all predictor and criterion variables remain highly significant, suggesting that correlations in the model are not heavily biased by a common method [101]. In sum, CMB is not likely to be a concern.

The three-step MICOM approach was adopted to test for measure ment model invariance between firm groupings with cost leadership versus differentiation strategies [93]. Configural invariance (Step 1) was established by employing identical measurement and structural models for each group that was estimated using identical data treatment and analysis specifications. The permutation test was used to assess compositional invariance (Step 2) and the equality of means values and variance (Step 3). The results are shown in Appendix E. In comparing the two business strategy groups, differentiation and cost leadership, compositional invariance (Step 2) was confirmed except for the first-order composites of Selling and Profitability. Since compositional invariance occurred for their respective higher-order composites MC and Firm Performance and due to the low number of violations (2 out of 25 composites), it was still permissible to proceed to Step 3 [93]. The equality of mean values was established (Step 3a). In the final step, two composites showed inequality of variances (Step 3b). Second-orde composite MC and its first-order composite (indicator) Market Infor mation Management had significantly different variances between groups. Partial measurement invariance was thus confirmed and MGA was permissible in the structural model [91].

## 7. Discussion

## 7.1. Contributions

This study offers numerous novel contributions to the IS, marketing, and data science literatures. These are highlighted and discussed in Table 4.

## 7.2. Research implications

This study builds on RBV theory to answer how and to what extent BDR acts to enhance MC, which, in turn, leads to better firm perfor mance. In so doing, this study makes important theoretical contributions to resource-based research in management, IS, and marketing.

First, our study improves understanding of the ways in which BDR impacts firm performance. Consistent with RBV logic and based on study findings, BDR can be inferred to be a valuable, rare, and costly-to-imitate resource [41,42] that can be a source of competitive advantage when its value-creating potential is capitalized via market-directed processes [27,29]. Specifically, the results show that MC mediates the relationship between BDR and firm performance. This study thus lends support to recent RBV-based marketing/IS studies asserting that strategic IT re sources only have value potential but realizing this value requires alignment with other complementary IT-enabled organizational capa bilities [29,55,72]. We thus confirm the complementarity resources theory approach [29–31]. Rather than separating out IT/BDR, one must understand how these resources impact marketing capabilities first, and then how via this mediation, MCs increase the impact on business value. BDR therefore do not create value in isolation but must be a part of a business value-creating process with other IS and organizational capa bilities $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ customer service, marketing, and analytics) operating in a synergistic manner [32–34]. Even more specifically, the findings show that the indirect effect of BDR on firm performance being leveraged in firm marketing processes is substantial (coefficient = 0.34, p < 0.01). This suggests that an 11–12 % increase in firm performance may be attributed to strategic investments in BDR. By way of contrast, prior IT business value research has found that IT investment into business in telligence resources, before the emergence of big data technologies, improves firm performance by only 5–6% beyond what can be explained by other factors [109]. Our results thus confirm the expectation that BDR has greater potential for differential performance and competitive advantage than prior data-driven IT [3,5].

Second, this study makes a novel theoretical contribution with its parsimonious conceptualization of BDR. Consistent with RBV theory, the results suggest that effective BDR requires a set of critical, comple mentary big data–related IT resources exerting a joint influence on MC. As informed by qualitative big data literature, our empirical analyses indicate that all three dimensions—big data technology resources, big data analytics skills, and organizational big data resources—represent statistically significant, conceptually distinct domains of BDR.

Furthermore, the BDR construct captures the relative importance of BDR dimensions in predicting MC and firm performance. The results suggest that specialized human resources, or big data analytics skills (weight = 0.48, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , is the most important facet of BDR. The empirical results thus confirm concerns raised by scholars that this lack of human talent to develop predictive models and algorithms that sup port corresponding business decisions and organizational processes is the greatest impediment to big data success [5,49]. We emphasize, however, that big data–driven IT infrastructure (weight = 0.39, p < 0.01) (including nonrelational databases and warehousing technologies, which are the analytics platform and applications) and organizational resources (weight = 0.26, p < 0.05) that foster big data use (e.g., top management support and organizational culture) are also key di mensions of strategic BDR. It is worth noting that the relative impor tance and significance of the three BDR dimensions remained unchanged across firm and industry characteristics, lending support for the empirical generalizability of the novel BDR construct.

Third, the findings underline that the business impact of BDR is contingent on firm strategy, that is, whether the firm pursues a cost leadership or a differentiation advantage over its rivals. The multigroup analyses suggest that when mediated by MC (0.62, p < 0.01), BDR’s indirect contribution to the explained variance of firm performance is 13 % (0.36, p < 0.01) in firms doing differentiation. In stark contrast, firms competing based on cost leadership achieve a 3% increase in perfor mance attributable to BDR (indirect effect 0.17, p < 0.01).

The most likely explanation for these highly divergent effects be tween differentiation and low cost leader firms is that the predictive properties of big data analytics outweigh the efficiency advantages achieved through the automation and optimization of marketing pro cesses. A differentiation firm focuses on MC effectiveness by delivering better products and services to their customers. Specifically, big data –driven insights from the marketplace help firms better predict their customers’ and competitors’ behaviors and to innovate by personalizing their products and services as well as other aspects of marketing activ ities [5,9,60]. The findings thus underscore how firms seeking to differentiate themselves from competition need to understand what their customers want and, by offering personalized new products and services accordingly, will then be poised to reap greater rewards from a big data–driven marketing strategy than will cost-focused firms. That is, big data particularly enhances MC by helping them become more adaptive to continuous changes in the market [60]. Firms that empha size a cost leadership strategy are thus less likely to improve MC effec tiveness through market-based product innovation due to their inherent internal orientation.

We offer another possible explanation in that big data may also be used to enhance MCs’ efficiency by differentiating firms to maintain relative cost parity in their quest for competitive advantage [58,110]. Cost leadership firms, in turn, may not be able to exploit big data’s full potential to improve MC effectiveness through a better understanding of customer needs and new product and service innovations. Then cost and time efficiencies in MC may not be sufficiently achieved through the optimization and automation of marketing processes [24,73]. Cost leadership firms that specifically pursue a big data–driven cost advan tage that builds on this inside-out foundation to gain superior perfor mance therefore do not achieve the expected impact of BDR on enhancing MC efficiency.

These findings are also intriguing because prior research has emphasized the critical role of industry rather than firm characteristics in determining the value-creating potential of big data [12,16]. While the results show that industry sector, goods versus services, and envi ronmental turbulence demonstrate minor effects on business impact, our findings strongly suggest that firm strategy, above and beyond industry characteristics, determines BDR success. The multigroup analyses further indicate, however, that the role of industry characteristics may be more important for cost leadership firms. Competitive intensity notably exerts a greater influence on the levels of MC (0.41, p < 0.01) than BDR (0.37, p < 0.01), implying that cost leaders are more likely to build better MC as a response to competitive pressures rather than as a result of big data investment.

## 7.3. Managerial implications

This study has important managerial implications for firms because becoming a big data–driven marketing organization is inhibited by technological, human, and organizational challenges (big data tech nology resources, big data analytics skills, and organizational BDR). Study findings suggest, though, that firms that acquire appropriate BDR can indeed achieve competitive advantages over their rivals.

The key for managers is to understand what they can do to maximize the likelihood that their firms benefit from investments in BDR. With this in mind, we stress that MC are the critical link between BDR (big data technology resources, big data analytics skills, and organizational BDR) and firm performance. Practitioners should ensure that BDR are properly aligned with the firm’s marketing processes. In practice, many marketing organizations fail to make full use of big data–driven insight to guide marketing decisions. We therefore recommend that managers assess the feasibility of BDR in the context of their application to MC. To do this, managers should regularly measure the impact of big data projects on different marketing processes in terms of efficiency, effec tiveness, and innovativeness by identifying the most appropriate customer, market, and financial performance metrics.

Second, we advise managers to direct intensive effort to make sure that all aspects of their firms’ overall big data assets are sufficient, if not perfectly balanced. Firms should not focus solely on their technological big data infrastructure or on their recruitment of data scientists. An organizational culture that discourages big data can seriously under mine its use, and this lack cannot be compensated for by excelling in big data analytics skills. We urge management to take immediate corrective action if inadequacies in any of these dimensions are observed.

Third, we recommend that managers carefully consider whether the potential of big data investment is being fully used in their respective firms. For example, Amazon.com’s greatest competitive advantage is created through their outside-in orientation afforded by big data–driven customer insight [60]. Yet Amazon.com also excels in minimizing the costs and time spent on pricing, distribution, and communications with automated decision support engines. This study’s findings similarly underline the point that managers working with differentiated product and services, regardless of the industry, should consider big data as a key component in building more efficient, effective, and innovative MC to meet customer needs. B2C manufacturing firms should note that they might be in a particularly favorable position compared to other data-driven industry sectors. The advent of sensor-based analytics and the lower adoption of big data may represent opportunities in B2C manufacturing that are less evident in the retail, e-commerce, finance, and banking sectors [10–13].

## 7.4. Limitations

This study has limitations that also present opportunities for future studies. First, the data in this research was cross-sectional and therefore causation cannot be established. Second, the single-informant design with self-reported subjective data is conceivably a potential source of CMB, although our numerous tests show that it should not be an issue. It needs to be noted that prior studies have found that subjective measures can slightly overestimate the MC-firm performance relation ship [25,55]. Third, the generalizability of results is restricted to large, U.S.-based firms/SBUs operating in B2C industries. Future studies might explore big data–driven MC in SMEs, B2B sectors, and other cultural contexts. Furthermore, other studies should confirm our results about the lack of impact of industry characteristics on the BDR, MC firm per formance relationship. Fourth, this study focused solely on MC. Future research may seek to improve understanding about how BDR influence MC once other firm capabilities such as R&D and operational capabil ities are controlled for. An expansive embedded IS resources model might also focus on the increasingly connected business context, recognizing that BDR value might require co-creation by bridging ca pabilities among partners and equitable sharing of disparate resources. Also, once embedded, BDR could also be the source of new value-added customer services. Furthermore, data gathered from the customers warehouse can be analyzed for patterns of usage in relation to other products in order to make personalized product recommendations. Although business executives recognize the importance of information [111], the potential uses of information as a capability-generating resource are yet to be fully exploited.

## 7.5. Conclusion

BDR play a vital role in improving the efficiency and effectiveness of strategic MC to achieve competitive advantage. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first large-scale empirical study to examine the action mechanism and impact of strategic big data investments on firm performance, one that also considers firms’ global strategic orien tation. As a foundation for future research, this study underscores the need to better understand the performance impacts of big data–related resources. We hope that this contribution motivates more academic research to address the complementary, enabling role of BDR to enhance the effectiveness, efficiency, and innovativeness of strategic organiza tional capabilities.

## Appendix C. Explanation of online panel data usage in the research

The professional, permission-based worldwide panel provider. Research Now, administered online questionnaires for data collection for ou research. Their services are largely used by academic marketing and IT researchers. Research Now monitors quality by checking for illogical answer or inconsistent responding, overuse of item nonresponse (e.g., Don’t Know), speeding (overly quick survey completion; responses where the completion time is less than 30 % of the median length of the survey are eliminated). In order to avoid professional panelists, they limit survey participation. This avoids excessive survey participation, which would otherwise create survey fatigue and potential bias. B2B respondents have additional levels of protection from being overused. Research Now offers representative samples by balancing the sample for clients on outbound, inbound, quotas, using a wide range of targeting criteria from demographics to more complex behavioral and attitudinal profiling.

Online panels are one of the most important innovations in management research. In 2017, 14 % of published empirical articles employed online panel data and usage of online panelists from a global marketplace is gaining popularity because of several advantages online panels offer over traditional convenience samples. The main advantage is that online panels provide researchers with a convenient way to reach a potentially unlimited number of representative sample participants (compared to usual student convenience samples) while keeping costs to a minimum [112]. Addi tionally, online panels give researchers access to hard-to-reach targets from across the globe (e.g., in the B2B, such as CEOs, CMOs), facilitating increasingly representative samples [113]. There is academic evidence that the attention levels of, and psychometric data from, online panel par ticipants meet or exceed those from traditional convenience samples [114]. The Walter et al. [115] meta-analysis based on more than 32,121 online panel participants across 90 independent samples shows that online panelists are capable of yielding higher quality data than traditional convenience samples, Therefore by 2011, all major top management journals had published articles based on online panel data [115.116]

## Appendix F. Discussion of the single-informant limitation of the research

The relationships between variables in our model are potentially confounded by the same respondent effect. In the past, diverse measurements were adopted to evaluate firms’ performance [117], and, unfortunately, single informant measurement is a largely adopted in firm performance and management research. Many publications about firm performance in international top journals have this single informant issue where managers respond to both independent variables and dependent variables [29,50,65,69]. As other examples, Hao and Song [70], Akter et al. [65] as well as Fosso Wamba et al. [69] use survey data from (IT) managers and business analysts with only declarative performance data and without any externa performance data. On the other hand, some researchers recommend using self-reported evaluations of financial performance because innovative managerial efforts cannot be measured with solely financial performance indicators [118–120]. We thus position our research in line with these authors.

A weakness of using an online panel provider in our case – beside the advantages outlined in Appendix C – is that for legal and ethical reasons, the panel provider does not offer firm-related information about anonymous respondents. While we believe that this issue presents a limitation in our research and should be addressed in future studies, the single informant issue is a generalized problem in firm performance research. The literature agrees that it is difficult to fully capture and properly attribute the value generated by IT investments and even less for big data investments. The task of obtaining granular data on IT/big data investments, assessing changes in IT functionality, and isolating effects on a value-based variable are onerous [121]. The subjectivity of primary data, inaccessibility of reliable secondary firm data, unavailability of appropriate proxies, and use of cross-sectional designs inhibit the study of IT/big data value. We further believe that the present research is among the first that empirically studies the links between BDR, management, MC, and firm performance, which is a real contribution and includes strong managerial implications.

As we were conscious of potential issues with our single informant approach, we included several procedural and statistical controlling techniques for common method bias (CMB). First, respondent anonymity was protected. Second, as a potential statistical remedy, we used CFA and Harman’s single-factor test to assess common method bias [100]. Eight factors had eigenvalues greater than one, and together they accounted for 59 % of the total variance; the first factor accounted for 37 % of the total variance. However, the Harman’s test does not completely rule out the risk of common method bias, so we carried out a partial correlation test with a theoretically unrelated marker variable [101] that we included in the survey and that was statistically uncorrelated with the other measures in the data.
