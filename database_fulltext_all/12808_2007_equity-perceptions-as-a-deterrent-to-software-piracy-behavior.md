# Equity perceptions as a deterrent to software piracy behavior

David E. Douglas <sup>a,\*</sup>, Timothy Paul Cronan <sup>a</sup>, James D. Behel <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> University of Arkansas, United States <sup>b</sup> Harding University, United States

Received 5 October 2005; received in revised form 14 April 2007; accepted 11 May 2007

## Abstract

Software piracy is a problem for the software industry. An estimated world-wide piracy rate of 35%, representing approximately a \$31 billion dollar loss in 2004 was reported by the second annual Business Software Alliance (BSA) and International Data Corporation (IDC) piracy study. This high rate shows piracy to be a significant global problem. Both BSA and the Software and Information Industry Association consider the impact of piracy to be a critical issue and have established web links to allow anonymous reporting of software piracy. Deterrents have been welcomed by software producers and others. Equity theory offers a possible explanation of software piracy behavior but no models have been shown to link equity constructs as having a direct influence on software piracy. We investigated the use of equity theory (fairness) as a deterrent to software piracy. Our results identified equity components that significantly influenced equity in the context of software piracy and further indicated that equity significantly influenced software piracy. <sup>#</sup> 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved

## 1. Introduction

Software piracy parallels the growth of personal computers and continues at a high rate. There are a number of definitions for it. The Software Publishers Association (SPA) defined software piracy as the unauthorized duplication of computer software. Unauthorized activities involve copying disks, electronic downloads and transfer of programs and data, and purchasing from counterfeit resellers.

Despite innovative efforts to protect against it, use of the internet and better technology has resulted in an increase in software piracy. The annual studies commissioned by Business Software Alliance (BSA), until 2003, estimate the software piracy rate increased to 40% by 2001 after declining from 46% in 1995 and 36% in 1999. Since 2001, the software piracy rate appears to be steady but the estimated dollar loss continues to increase as the software industry grows [23].

Software piracy varies by region. The U.S./Canada has the lowest rate (23%) while Eastern Europe has the highest (71%). The top three software pirating countries are China, Vietnam, and Ukraine all with a rate exceeding 90%. This is likely to change as some countries are joining the World Trade Organization (WTO) and entry requires enforcement of intellectual property rights. The U.S. government has confronted China because of its blatant indifference to the piracy of software products [11].

Software companies are concerned about curbing piracy. Reports of the Microsoft Corporation have outlined how U.S. states are losing millions of dollars and hundreds of jobs each year due to software piracy [47]. From a global perspective, Traphagan and Griffth [46] studied the impact of software piracy on global software competitiveness. They concluded that the more economically developed the nation, the larger its computer infrastructure and market for software but that high personal incomes did not result in low piracy rates; legal protection, effective enforcement, and cultural norms were necessary to produce low software piracy rates.

Marron and Steel [34] examined the economic and cultural institutions that enforce property rights and found that piracy rates were lower in countries that had strong institutions for enforcing property rights. Husted [22] found that software piracy significantly correlated to GNP per capita, income inequity, and to Hofstede’s cultural dimension of individualism. Moores and Dhillion [36] studied the relationship between threat of punishment, availability of pirated software, and cost in Hong Kong. They found significant gender differences and higher piracy rates for the relationship of high availability of pirated software coupled with low censure and high costs of legal software.

Our research takes a different approach by examining the impact of equity theory on software piracy by developing and using an instrument to measure users perceptions of equity (fairness) in a software piracy context. The question guiding this research was:

‘‘Do users of computer systems consider fairness when making decisions involving software piracy?’’

The instrument used closely follows the instrument Joshi [27] developed for the equity component of his user information satisfaction (UIS) instrument.

## 2. Software piracy behavioral model

Prior research has attempted to explain ethical behavior. Jones [26] provides a summary of ethical decision models from which many behavioral and ethical models have been derived. Venkatesh et al. [48] provided a summary of the theory of reasoned action (TRA) and the theory of planned behavior (TPB) (see also [32]) and several software piracy models [16,21,45] were based on the deontological and consequential aspects of ethical evaluation. Banerjee et al. [5] developed a research framework to model the ethical behavior intentions of IS professionals and found that individual and situational characteristics influence ethical behavior. Leonard et al. [29] also studied the influence on ethical behavior of perceived importance, and individual characteristics. Explanations of software piracy behavior have been offered—moral judgment [28,49], context analysis [35], risk [44], attitude [39], codes of ethics [19,24,25,40], and organizational policies [41]. Other research found that females in the United States pirate less software than males [15,42,43] and that 82% of a sample of U.S. students admitted that they would pirate software regardless of the price [38].

## 2.1. Equity theory

In the social psychology literature, ‘‘equity’’ is considered equivalent to fairness and justice. Equity theory specifically addresses human pursuit of fairness in a social exchange. It attempts to consolidate exchange theory, cognitive dissonance theory, reinforcement theory, and psychoanalytic theory into a simple theory about human behavior in social exchange [1]. Other studies have incorporated equity theory into a theory of social behavior [50,51].

Individuals assess equity by comparing their outcomes with their inputs in a social exchange. An individual compares his or her ratio of outcomes to inputs with those of other individuals and, if there is a perceived difference, the individual determines his or her exchange to be inequitable.

The main propositions of equity theory are:

\- individuals will try to maximize their outcomes where outcomes equal rewards minus costs,

\- groups can maximize collective reward by evolving accepted systems for equitably apportioning resources among members,

\- groups will generally reward members who treat others equitably and generally punish (increase the cost for) members who treat others inequitably,

\- when individuals find themselves participating in inequitable relationships, they will become distressed,

\- and individuals who discover they are in an inequitable relationship will attempt to eliminate their distress by restoring equity [8].

Inequity results when an individual is dissatisfied with the allocation of resources. Other researchers have suggested that merit, equality, need, distributive fairness, and procedural fairness should be considered [30,31].

Even if the exchange is deemed equitable as far as the outcomes–input ratio, individuals may evaluate the total exchange as inequitable because it was not seen as procedurally fair. The response to perceived inequity may be altered expectations, revised comparison standards, changed beliefs and attitudes, and overt behavior.

Fig. 1. Adams’s extended equity model.

The perception of equity is one of the factors influencing the attitudes and behavior of people [2]. The consumer behavior model of Fishbein and Ajzen [12] and Ajzen and Fishbein [3,4] incorporated learning theories to explain behavior. Their TRA has been found to be consistent with expectancy value theories and exchange theory [18].

Walster et al. suggested using equity theory to understand the social behavior surrounding software piracy, because it deals with the degree to which agents in an exchange process perceive it to be fair. When people find themselves in an inequitable relationship they will try to eliminate their distress by restoring equity. Moreover, Glass and Wood [14] found that situational variables significantly affected a person’s intention to provide pirated software.

Adams’s equity theory has been used as the basis for the two primary equity dimensions of Eckhoff [9]— reciprocation and allocation. Berger et al. [6] and Leventhal further delineate allocation fairness into procedural fairness and distributive fairness, extending Adams’s model as a framework for the equity construct (Fig. 1).

Joshi included reciprocal fairness, distributive fairness, and procedural fairness and investigated equity using them as a determinant of UIS.

Fig. 2 presents the equity construct in a software piracy context. The perception of reciprocal fairness is derived through buying or pirating the software. The reproduction of pirated software for resale such as occurs in some countries or on the Web, still conforms to the reciprocation model component. Either the retail price of the software or the price associated with it is given by the consumer in exchange for the product and its perceived value. The price of software piracy may include costs associated with being prosecuted as well as the cost of time and media in copying [10]. Another reciprocal fairness issue involves the expectations of the one who pirates as well as others who know of the activity. Do others perceive the theft to affect them or the impact to be limited only to the software producer? Do others treat the piracy behavior as acceptable? Will the one who pirates suffer guilt or shame?

Procedural fairness is represented by the involvement and interaction of the producer with the consumer. In some cases, protection strategies make the use of the products difficult. The use of both hardware and software protection methods have had minimal success in deterring piracy, and have caused significant consumer frustration with the product.

Distributive fairness relates to purchase of software by different groups of consumers. For example, personal productivity software may be purchased at different prices by many user groups, including students, businesses, home-users, and professionals. Are these products perceived to be fairly distributed? For example, is student or educational discounts perceived to be fair?

## 2.2. Research model

Previous work revealed that equity theory impacted intentions to behave as well as actual behavior. Although equity theory has been included in some studies on software piracy, the focus was different than ours; e.g. Glass and Wood focused on the provider of the software pirated. We examined the influence of the equity construct in the software piracy context and its subsequent verification and validation.

Our hypotheses were graphically illustrated in Fig. 3.

H1. Reciprocal fairness significantly influences equity in software piracy.

The following questions were used from [27] to measure reciprocal fairness:

\- Cost—the value of a software product is worth the cost and effort necessary to pirate (copy) the product (question #2).

\- Consequence—the value of a software product is worth the consequences of getting caught pirating (copying) the product (question #3).

\- Awareness—I would not care if others knew that I had pirated a software product (question #9).

\- Impact—if I pirated a software product, it would have no impact on others who purchase the product legally (question #10).

\- Guilt—if I pirated a software product, I would feel guilty for paying less than those purchasing the product (question #12).

H2. Procedural fairness significantly influences equity in software piracy.

The following questions were used to measure procedural fairness:

\- Worth—the value of available software products appears to be worth the retail price charged (question #1).

\- Pricing—most software companies offer a reasonable purchase and pricing alternative for me, considering my use of the product (question #5).

\- Opinion1—software companies value my opinion concerning the pricing of their product (question #6).

\- Opinion2—software companies value my opinion concerning the methods they use to prevent the piracy of their product (question #14).

H3. Distributive fairness significantly influences equity in software piracy.

The following questions were used to measure distributive fairness:

\- Prevent—when using a software product, I become frustrated by the strategies companies use to prevent piracy (question #4).

\- Valuable—a software product may be more valuable to some other users than it is to me (question #7).

\- Affordability—some other users of a software product may be able to afford a different price than I can afford (question #8).

H4. Equity significantly influences software piracy.

## 3. Research methodology

A 16-item questionnaire (see Appendix A) was used to measure the dimensions of the proposed software piracy model. The items utilized a 7-point Likert scale for all the questions except the one that asked respondents to enter the percent of pirated software on their computer (the number of software products pirated divided by the total number of software products). Answers to each scale could be chosen between strongly agree and strongly disagree. Items were either worded or reverse scored so that ‘‘strongly agree’’ indicated an equitable response and ‘‘strongly disagree’’ indicated an inequitable response.

A preliminary list of questions was generated via an iterative process of Churchill [7]. This led to a preliminary instrument which was piloted by groups of IT faculty members (5), students (5), and non-student users (5) of home computers. Each person in the pilot test was asked to complete the questionnaire and include suggestions or questions about its contents. Their suggestions and comments were used to modify the instrument to improve the wording and instructions.

Two samples were used in our study: to develop the model and, later, for model validation. Analyses of the data for both samples utilized a two-step approach— factor analysis followed by structural equation modeling (SEM). Results of the factor analyses produced the model fit values and confirmed convergent and discriminant validity.

SEM was used to evaluate the structural model. Covariance SEM analysis [13] used the AMOS 5.0 software package.

## 4. Analysis and results

The questionnaire was administered voluntarily during class time to a number of undergraduate business classes at different universities. Students had the option to return the questionnaire without completing it. A guarantee of anonymity was stressed in addition to describing the value of their assistance in furthering research. As with most student samples a majority of the students completed and returned the questionnaire. For the sample used to develop the model, of 240 returned questionnaires, 232 were usable. All the students in this sample owned a PC with computer software. This sample was used to validate the equity constructs and develop the SEM structural model. With a maximum of 16 items, the number of responses was adequate based on the heuristic of a minimum of 10 responses per item [37].

Forty-five percent of the respondents in the development sample were female. The average age of the respondents is 20.5 with a standard deviation of 3.92. The sample had a rather large diversity in geographic origin of the respondents and represented several different major disciplines. The self-reported software piracy variable (the approximate percentage of pirated software products) exhibited considerable variation; a mean of 15.7%, a standard deviation of 22.03 and a range of 100%. Therefore, the sample included respondents with varying degrees of software piracy behavior. The sample did not include graduate students.

Three items addressed the dependency or involvement of the respondent with the computer. These included the hours per day in using the computer, the number of years of using a personal computer, and the number of formal university courses taken on computing. The average for each are; 1.9 h using the computer per day, 6.0 years using the computer, and two formal courses in computing. While the sample was restricted to college students, the general diversity of the respondents was considerable. The only predominant characteristic was the small age range.

A second sample (n = 198) was collected at a different university for model validation. The data was comparable to the earlier model development sample. The demographic profile was not different with an average age of 20.1 (with a standard deviation of 2.58), 6 years of experience, 59% male, 11.7 h/week working on the computer and having completed two computer courses.

Factor analysis was conducted on the model development sample using a Varimax rotation. Because of the strength of the theory underlying the proposed model, the number of factors was held to three. Moreover, the Scree plot, Eigen values, and corresponding variance explained were examined to determine whether the data fit three factors. Based on these analyses, a three-factor model was deemed appropriate.

Table 1 shows the factor loadings for the development sample (bold values indicate factor on which variables loaded). Although other rules of thumb regarding alpha coefficients have been proposed, alpha coefficients of 0.50 or higher were considered acceptable in model development. When rounded to two decimal places, all the items loaded at 0.60 or higher, except for prevention, which did not significantly load in any factor (alpha coefficients less than 0.50) and was therefore removed from the model. The model fit with three factors was therefore supported.

The model validation sample (second) produced a similar three factor pattern. The variable, guilt, loaded heavily on two factors in this sample—the reciprocal factor and the procedural factor. Since the second sample was a validation sample, the analysis was conducted with guilt as part of the reciprocal factor as in the first sample used for model development.

SEM was used to evaluate the structural or path model. Covariance SEM analysis was used for confirming theory using the AMOS 5.0 package. Fig. 4 shows the results of the analysis for the model development sample. SEM analysis assumes multivariate normality.

Multivariate normality was assessed by examining the skewness and kurtosis. All the variables except affordability was within the acceptable threshold values [20,33] for skewness (upper limit of 2) and kurtosis (upper limit of 7). The values for variable affordability were close to the threshold; thus, the assumption of multivariate normality was met. Because of well documented problems with the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ measure, Hair et al. suggested using the $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f }$ statistic. In this case, the model $\overline { { \chi ^ { 2 } } } / \mathrm { d f }$ was 1.96 and was less than the threshold value of 2. Four other indices, considered to be robust fit measures – Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) – by Hair et al. and others [17], were included in Fig. 4.

Given these fit indices, the proposed three factor a priori model was a good fit with 68% of the variance explained by software piracy. The coefficients and corresponding t values are also shown for the paths. Two asterisks indicate significance at the .01 level. The Equity to Reciprocal and the Equity to Procedural paths were significant; however, the Equity to Distributive path was not.

Analysis of the validation sample indicated comparable results to those of the model development sample. SEM analysis of the validation sample yields better model fit indices than the model development sample. Although the model fit for the validation sample was better than the development sample, the amount of variance explained (47%) was smaller. An examination of individual items revealed that the response patterns for question 3 for the samples could account for a part of the difference in the variance explained. This addresses the value versus consequences of pirating software. This may reflect the differences in piracy awareness of students in the validation versus the development sample. The validation sample was taken almost 1 year after the development sample. The awareness may therefore have included exposure to codes of conduct as well as business ethics discussions. Given the results of both samples, the proposed equity a priori model has a strong fit that supports the hypothesis H4, H1 and H2.

## 5. Discussion

Our research proposed equity as a determinant of software piracy. The model proposed the constructs of reciprocal fairness, procedural fairness, and distributive fairness to reflect the equity construct. Analysis of the development sample found that reciprocal fairness and procedural fairness were significant determinants of the equity; and that it had a significant effect on software piracy. Support for the model was provided by using a second sample to validate the piracy model. Analysis of the validation sample also found that the constructs of reciprocal fairness and procedural fairness were significant determinants of the equity construct and that equity was a significant determinant of software piracy.

The distributive fairness dimension was not significant in either the development or the validation sample. A probable reason is that students profit from license agreements negotiated for them by their university.

Our results confirm that equity perceptions are one of the factors influencing attitude and behavior. Software vendors should recognize that attitudinal changes are possible and changed attitudes could result in a lower piracy rate. A concentrated effort to change the users view of fairness should result in significant reductions in software piracy. Some software companies seem to have already started moving in the direction of using advertising to address piracy issues.

Based on our results, fairness should be seen in both the exchange (reciprocal) and the procedures used by the vendor. By a wide margin, reciprocal fairness is the strongest equity factor. Thus, efforts should be concentrated to promote fairness via pricing and advertisements. Perhaps the ultimate fair exchange would be a ‘‘pay as you use’’ approach, much like that exercised in long distance phone service.

## 6. Conclusions

The equity construct was shown to be an important determinant in explaining software piracy behavior (68% in the development sample of our study). To better understand ethical/unethical behavior, the results suggested including the equity construct as a component of the ethical behavior model; for example, China is a country that has an overall software piracy rate in the neighborhood of 90% and they are also a collective society. As such, they tend to share, including sharing software illegally.

Perceptions of inequity could be something other than ‘‘fairness’’. Moreover, inequity may be better explained by rationalization (self-serving, exoneration, etc.) or culture.

He teaches in a wide variety of information systems areas including programming, systems development, database management systems, enterprise systems and business intelligence/ knowledge management with emphasis on data mining and data warehouses. He is Chair of the Enterprise Computing Steering Committee at the University of Arkansas. Dr. Douglas is an active member in the Decision Sciences Institute, the Federation of Business Disciplines (FBD) and the International Association of Computing Information Systems.
