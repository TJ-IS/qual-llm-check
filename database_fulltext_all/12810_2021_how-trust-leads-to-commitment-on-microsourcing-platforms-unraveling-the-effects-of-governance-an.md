# HOW TRUST LEADS TO COMMITMENT ON MICROSOURCING PLATFORMS: UNRAVELING THE EFFECTS OF GOVERNANCE AND THIRD-PARTY MECHANISMS ON TRIADIC MICROSOURCING RELATIONSHIPS<sup>1</sup>

Wenbo Guo School of Management, Xi’an Jiaotong University Xi’an, CHINA {guowenbosteven@gmail.com}

Detmar Straub Fox School of Business, Temple University Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A. {straubdetmar@gmail.com}

Pengzhu Zhang Antai College of Economics and Management, Shanghai Jiao Tong University Shanghai, CHINA {pzzhang@sjtu.edu.cn}

Zhao Cai Nottingham University Business School China, University of Nottingham Ningbo China Ningbo, CHINA {zhao.cai@nottingham.edu.cn}

IS research has extensively examined the role of trust in client-vendor relationships, as well as the role of governance in information technology (IT) outsourcing, but little research has been carried out on the latest manifestation of outsourcing—namely, microsourcing, i.e., the sourcing of smaller scale projects. To extend the literature on the traditional IT outsourcing literature—a stream that largely focuses on medium-to-large scale offline projects—we investigate how to develop trust and commitment in a triadic microsourcing relationship which includes the microsourcer, the microsourcee, and the microsourcing platform (MP). We draw on transaction cost economics (TCE) to theorize a model specifically adapted to the microsourcing phenomenon to scrutinize the influences of formal contractual mechanisms, relational mechanisms, and third-party mechanisms. Combining data from a matched sample of microsourcers and microsourcees on the leading Chinese MP, Zbj.com, the paper deploys degree-symmetric modeling (DSM) for construct conceptualization, measurement, and data analysis. DSM is consistent with the holistic view used to develop the research model for triadic relationships. Findings confirm that the MP is critical in delivering governance mechanisms to ensure the development of triadic trust and commitment. The results suggest that researchers and practitioners should pay closer attention to triadic trust and commitment building through proper governance mechanisms in the online microsourcing marketplace. We argue that this work could be extended to other online digital platforms that involve multiple transacting parties.

Keywords: Microsourcing platform, governance mechanisms, third-party mechanisms, triadic relationship, degree-symmetric modeling

## Introduction

Falling under the larger umbrella of crowdsourcing, microsourcing is an increasingly prevalent practice for seeking flexible solutions from the crowd (Deng et al. 2016; Lu et al. 2016). Microsourcers (clients) initiate an open call on a digital platform to seek microsourcees (vendors) from an undefined, albeit implicitly large community to bid on proposed tasks (Deng et al. 2016; Saxton et al. 2013). Microsourcing tasks are well-structured and predefined, requiring a limited skillset for the completion of tasks. The tasks range across a wide set of categories such as letter writing, graphics design, website design, and computer programming (Deng and Joshi 2016; Majchrzak and Malhotra 2016). According to existing industrial reports, the microsourcing market is huge, with numerous digital platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk, Freelancer.com, and Zbj.com, all of which show a steep upward sales trajectory in this two-sided market (Lu et al. 2016).

The online intermediary that handles the interaction of microsourcers and microsourcees for accomplishing these tasks is the microsourcing platform (MP). Ideally, the central role of the MP is to assist both microsourcer and microsourcee in building a sustained collaboration that can capitalize on low transaction costs (Handley and Angst 2015). An effective MP will support the string of ongoing transactions between these parties. In some cases, however, such collaborations turn out to be temporary and short-term because of the high uncertainty perceived by microsourcing participants (Gadiraju et al. 2015).

Based on an underpinning of transaction cost economics (TCE), we theorize that a key task for the MP is to help microsourcers and microsourcees build trust, that is, the willingness of being vulnerable to actions performed by the other party (Chiles and McMackin 1996; Mayer et al. 1995). Trust can greatly reduce perceived uncertainty and ultimately increase commitment—i.e., the willingness to engage in long-term relationships (Kumar et al. 1995; Yuan et al. 2018). As MPs involve multiple entities, all having unique characteristics, a multisided view of trust and commitment is needed. Thus, the opening contention here is that the literature on trust and commitment can be effectively applied to microsourcing contexts, but in very specific ways.

Indeed, the study of trust and commitment in digital platforms is not new (Pavlou and Gefen 2004; Sun 2010; Xiaojuan Ou et al. 2014). However, triadic microsourcerplatform-microsourcee relationships are seldom mentioned in the literature to date. In such a triadic relationship, the MP is an intermediary, governing transactions between microsourcer and microsourcee (Feller et al. 2012; Guo et al. 2017). From a microsourcing relationship standpoint, the trust/commitment enacted by the microsourcer and microsourcee to the triadic relationship has two components: (1) trust/commitment to each other; and (2) trust/commitment to the platform. On the one hand, it is imperative for both microsourcee and microsourcer to mutually trust and commit to their relationship in order for a series of successful transactions to take place (Thomson and Perry 2006). On the other hand, it can be argued that parties will only remain on the microsourcing platform and participate in a string of transactions if they at least have trust in and commitment to the platform (Chumpitaz Caceres and Paparoidamis 2007; Mahmoud et al. 2018). Accordingly, the current study conceptualizes triadic trust/commitment as a perception held by microsourcing participants toward this triadic relationship.

As previously mentioned, MPs are responsible for providing efficient and efficacious governance mechanisms to ensure triadic trust and commitment in microsourcing relationships. Given that microsourcing shares some common features with traditional outsourcing, this study draws from prior outsourcing literature and incorporates two prevailing governance mechanisms: formal contractual mechanisms and relational mechanisms (Handley and Angst 2015; Huber et al. 2013; Lacity et al. 2010). Formal contractual mechanisms are written and procedural-based mechanisms enforced by a platform, while relational mechanisms are unwritten, worker-based mechanisms (Goo et al. 2009; Poppo and Zenger 2002). Additionally, microsourcing is intermediated by digital platforms offering third-party mechanisms, which are structural mechanisms enacted by third parties to safeguard transactions (Fang et al. 2014; Pavlou and Gefen 2004). These three forms of governance mechanisms guide participant behavior throughout the backand-forth transactional processes.

Moreover, effectual deployment of governance mechanisms varies across contexts. For example, formal contracts are deemed to be effective in governing ambiguous transactions while relational mechanisms are preferable in volatile circumstances (Carson et al. 2006). As microsourcing lies at the intersection between outsourcing and platform-based transactions, the incorporation of the three governance mechanisms differs markedly from that of traditional IT outsourcing. Thus, in drawing on a holistic perspective of the microsourcing relationship, it is appropriate to investigate the distinct roles of formal contractual, relational, and thirdparty mechanisms in improving triadic trust, which, in turn, influences triadic commitment. Accordingly, the present study conceptualizes its key constructs from a holistic perspective and aims to contribute to the literature by answering the following questions:

RQ1: How do governance mechanisms (i.e., formal contractual, relational, and third-party mechanisms) influence triadic trust in a microsourcing relationship?

RQ2: How does triadic trust affect triadic commitment in a microsourcing relationship?

To unpack the aforementioned research questions, we conducted a field study with a leading MP in China, Zbj.com. <sup>2</sup> Questionnaire data were collected from 548 microsourcer-microsourcee dyads, who had completed at least one IT-related microsourcing task. To process this dyadic data, degree-symmetric modeling (DSM), a formulation that calculates the average of degree value and symmetric value rated by both sides, was utilized. DSM aligns well with the aforementioned holistic view of microsourcing relationships because it combines the joint extent of an assessment with the degree of agreement between two rater opinions (Klein et al. 2007; Straub et al. 2004). In a microsourcing relationship, for example, a high evaluation of formal contractual mechanisms indicates that both microsourcer and microsourcee greatly value this construct. Indeed, our empirical findings support hypotheses on the positive role of governance mechanisms in improving triadic trust. Consistent with this argument, formal contractual, relational, and third-party mechanisms substantively impact the trust-building process and the analytical results of the present study further validate the positive effect exerted by triadic trust on triadic commitment.

This study, then, makes a threefold theoretical contribution to the extant literature (see Table 1 for a sampling of these contributions and how they fill research gaps). First, this study adopts a holistic view to investigate triadic microsourcing relationships, which require a shared perception of trust and commitment by both participants rather than a single party (Lu et al. 2016). Investigating trust at the relationship level can thus enrich the stream of TCE literature that advocates for the role of trust in reducing transaction costs (Chiles and McMackin 1996; Dyer and Chu 2003; Hosmer 1995). Second, this research adds to the literature on trust and commitment by conceptualizing them as triadic constructs in microsourcing, involving the MP and two sides of market participants (Bateman et al. 2011; Krishnan et al. 2016). In this study, triadic trust and commitment are conceptualized as containing trust/commitment to the MP and mutual client-vendor trust/commitment, as measured by integrating the valuation and the level of agreement of each participant. This approach also has the potential to extend conceptualizations of trust and commitment beyond triadic relationships, provided that such relationships contain one online intermediary and multiple participants. Third, this study enriches the existing IT outsourcing governance literature by proposing formal contractual, relational, and third-party mechanisms as three forms of governance mechanisms in online transactions intermediated by digital platforms (Handley and Angst 2015; Lioliou et al. 2014). As such, we examine the effectuation of different governance mechanisms in improving trusting relationships. Findings of this study are conducive to the redesign of governance mechanisms for MPs and digital platforms involving multiple entities in general.

## Theoretical Background and Literature Review

## Microsourcing Platforms

Microsourcing tasks usually involve a relatively small amount of money and a short-term duration (Lu et al. 2016; Obal 2009). Oftentimes, these tasks are simple in nature and require only a limited skill set on the part of the workers (Deng and Joshi 2016). An ideal microsourcing task should be well-structured, predefined, modularized, decomposed, and self-contained, all of which make it relatively easy for microsourcees as long as they have clear guidelines from microsourcers (Majchrzak and Malhotra 2016). Meanwhile, microsourcers benefit from such well-designed tasks due to the concomitant ease of evaluating and utilizing outcomes (Ye and Kankanhalli 2013). As illustrated in Appendix A, microsourcing has numerous distinctive features, especially compared to the traditional outsourcing approach from which it evolved.

Markedly at variance with traditional IT outsourcing, microsourcers and microsourcees participate in an online ecosystem governed by the MP. In a microsourcing relationship, each participant has concerns. Microsourcees need to pay attention to task descriptions to ensure that task requirements are modularized and explicitly defined (Nevo and Kotlarsky 2014; Ye and Kankanhalli 2017). Similarly, microsourcees are concerned with communication in terms of follow-up instructions, essential support, and rapid feedback (Nevo and Kotlarsky 2014). Finally, microsourcees need to be aware of the possibility of being exploited by unfair payment or poor evaluations from microsourcers (Deng et al. 2016).

## Transaction Cost Economics and Trust in Microsourcing

Transaction cost economics (TCE) has been widely acknowledged as one of the main avenues for research investigating transactions among entities in marketplaces such as those in outsourcing (Handley and Angst 2015), supply chains (Williamson 2008), and joint ventures (Hennart 1991). In general, TCE posits that organizations perform optimally when they are able to minimize transaction costs through the adoption of appropriate governance structures (Williamson 1979; Williamson 1989). With regard to outsourcing success, Lee et al. (2019) determined that TCE was the best theoretical explanation among the three theories investigated.

Given its evidenced success, it is important to understand what TCE is and how it has been defined. To this effect, Williamson (1979) identified three critical dimensions to characterize transactions when determining governance structure: (1) uncertainty, (2) the frequency with which transactions recur, and (3) a varying degree of durable transaction-specific investments. We argue that the microsourcing phenomenon fits these three critical dimensions of TCE. First, the microsourcing marketplace is uncertain because of the large and diverse community of microsourcers and microsourcees (Lu et al. 2016; Ye and Kankanhalli 2017). Second, compared with such routinized trading activities as material purchasing, microsourcing transactions are on-demand with high flexibility; moreover, participants have no obligation to perform recurrent transactions with fixed partners (Deng and Joshi 2016; Wang et al. 2017). Third, similar to traditional outsourcing, microsourcing tasks exhibit different degrees of idiosyncrasy (Deng and Joshi 2016). For idiosyncratic microsourcing transactions, microsourcees need to read task descriptions carefully and communicate with microsourcers when necessary, an interaction that indicates transactionspecific investments that need to be made by both parties as set-up costs (Lu et al. 2016; Williamson 1979). Taken together, the microsourcing marketplace is uncertain, flexible, and idiosyncratic, which calls for a trilateral governance structure that includes the intervention of a third party to help in governing the bilateral transactions. In this way, the MP takes on the responsibility of governing microsourcing transactions between microsourcers and microsourcees, a point that is consistent with one of the basic tenets of TCE.

Early TCE studies have built on this calculative perspective and assume that transacting parties are always opportunistic, that is, inclined to take advantage of partners (Williamson 1985; Williamson 1993). To prevent opportunism, contractual safeguards have been identified as the predominant tool for redirecting profits away from vulnerable partners (Williamson 1985; Williamson 1979). In later works, other scholars have attempted to extend TCE by considering the evolution of the relationships and incorporating the formation of trust along with transaction processes (Beccerra and Gupta 1999; Chiles and McMackin 1996; Hosmer 1995). Trust is thought to effectively economize transaction costs, especially in the long term (Hill 1990).

With trust, contract negotiations and drafting costs can be attenuated because trading partners hold the view that contract clauses are based on principles of fairness and honesty. Likewise, monitoring costs can be decreased due to confidence in each other’s goodwill and integrity (Dyer and Chu 2003). In addition, information can be exchanged in an accurate, comprehensive, and timely way, a sharing that is conducive to mutual understandings of each other’s requirements and concerns (Chiles and McMackin 1996).

For microsourcing transactions, the role of trust is especially salient. Although microsourcing tasks are small, short-term, and well-structured, they can still be diverse and idiosyncratic in nature. They often take place in situations that require transaction-specific investments in activities such as learning-by-doing, bilateral involvement, and mutual adjustment. Trust can significantly facilitate the efficiency of these activities via frequent information exchange, high receptivity to opinions, and the relaxation of controls, thereby reducing transaction costs in microsourcing relationships (Chiles and McMackin 1996).

A logical extension of TCE, the microsourcing marketplace has a trilateral governance structure that includes three entities: microsourcer, microsourcee, and the MP. Such a triadic relationship thus requires a perspective capable of conceptualizing trust beyond the conventional dyadic assumption. Coleman (1984) has argued against this limited, dyadic view of trust, one that only reflects relations between two actors. According to this perspective, trust is dependent on all participants in a relationship, whether those relationships are dyadic, triadic, or even tetradic or beyond. Conceivably, trust in a microsourcing relationship is well described as triadic and comprised of trust in transacting parties (i.e., microsourcers and microsourcees) and trust in the entity enforcing concrete governance mechanisms (i.e., the MP). Given this, incorporating the notion of triadic trust is important for investigating how to bolster MPorchestrated collaborations, where online trading partners have almost never had face-to-face contacts beforehand. Triadic trust effectively reduces concerns held by online participants in terms of the uncertainty resident in both trading partners and the MP, thereby facilitating transactions through the MP’s digitalized procedures, all without extensive back and forth negotiation. In this sense, lower transaction costs are consciously perceived by participants, an awareness that encourages them to engage in more exchanges and gradually develop more commitment to the relationship (Goo and Huang 2008; Yuan et al. 2018).

In fact, trust does not preexist in a new collaborative relationship, thereby requiring a process of interaction to boost shared knowledge, mutual adaptation, and relational bonding (Ring and Van De Ven 1994). A key to cultivating trust, as denoted by Nooteboom (1996) and Dyer (1996), is to provide favorable conditions for collaboration. The TCE literature contains similar assertions. For instance, some have highlighted the necessity of governing calculative relations to breed trust (Williamson 1993). Scholars have also asserted a hybrid composition of governance mechanisms because building trust necessitates complex and extensive interactions that demand a multitude of governance mechanisms to manage potential disputes (Chiles and McMackin 1996; Granovetter 1985; Rai et al. 2012). Therefore, various forms of governance mechanisms are adopted as safeguards to minimize opportunistic behaviors and to help contracting parties build trust (Goo et al. 2009; Zhou and Poppo 2010).

In the context of microsourcing, MPs take on the role of governing transactions between participants. As such, governance mechanisms provided by MPs are critical to the development of triadic trust and commitment in the relationship among the parties.

## Trust and Commitment in Microsourcing Platforms

Prior research on both outsourcing and digital platforms treats trust as an important enabler of effective projects and satisfactory user retention (Gefen et al. 2003; Goo et al. 2009; Pavlou 2002). In these contexts, trust refers to “the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party” (Mayer et al. 1995, p. 712). In relationships involving trust, both parties must be willing to take risks by lowering safeguards, which significantly improves the efficiency of transactions and reduces costs (Das and Teng 2001). Much has been written about the need for trust in outsourcing relationships (Lee and Choi 2011; Lee et al. 2008) and trust-building mechanisms (Lander et al. 2004; Mao et al. 2008). However, there has been little work centered on exploring the multiplicity of trusting relationships in microsourcing (Lu et al. 2016). Specifically, there is no research that has yet examined triadic trusting relationships and their downstream effects on commitment to the MP. Our study is thus among the first to elucidate the triadic nature of trust in the context of microsourcing and to provide empirical evidence of its developmental and influencing mechanisms.

The triadic nature of microsourcing relationships raises challenges for the conceptualization of trust, a construct that is usually investigated through dyadic relationships (Hosmer 1995; Ireland and Webb 2007; Poppo and Zhou 2016). We define triadic trust as the joint confidence between microsourcers and microsourcees in each other and the microsourcing platform, the overall purpose of which is to successfully fulfill a set of microsourcing transactions.<sup>3</sup> This definition is based on seeing the microsourcing relationship as holistic (i.e., joint confidence), rather than confidence from a one-sided perspective of either party. As shown in Figure 1, there are four relationships that need to be measured and integrated to capture triadic trust, namely: (1) the microsourcer’s trust in the MP, (2) the microsourcer’s trust in the microsourcee, (3) the microsourcee’s trust in the MP, and (4) the microsourcee’s trust in the microsourcer. Among these four types of trust, (1) and (3) represent trust in the MP held by microsourcing participants, while (2) and (4) jointly reflect the mutual partner trust between transacting parties. Appendix B provides a detailed explanation of both types of trust.

Commitment has long been thought to be an outcome resulting from a satisfactory level of trust (Morgan and Hunt 1994).

Being future oriented, it refers to a willingness of transacting parties to engage in further business (Goo et al. 2009; Kumar et al. 1995). The outsourcing literature has converged on the stance that commitment can affect the eventual success of a project and the sourcing relationship (Keil et al. 2000). Problems in outsourcing have been shown to arise from low commitment by participants (Goo et al. 2009; Newman and Robey 1992). With respect to microsourcing relationships, we define triadic commitment as the joint belief between microsourcers and microsourcees in each other and the MP that this relationship is important enough to warrant maximum efforts to maintain it. As Figure 2 shows, similar to trust, triadic commitment consists of four components: (1) the microsourcer’s commitment to the MP, (2) the microsourcer’s commitment to the microsourcee, (3) the microsourcee’s commitment to the MP, and (4) the microsourcee’s commitment to the microsourcer. These components can be reduced to two types: commitment to the MP and mutual partner commitment between participants.

Derived from the reasoning above, our fundamental argument is that triadic trust and commitment—jointly held by microsourcer and microsourcee—are necessary for an effective set of interactions. The MP can facilitate microsourcing transactions in many ways, but one of the main roles of the MP is to help build triadic trust and commitment by reducing uncertainty. To achieve this, the MP needs not only to design appropriate functionalities by means of formal regulations and relational bonds but also to sponsor third-party services to protect transactions against misconduct. With little to no triadic trust and commitment, participants in MPs will cease transacting online.

## Governance Mechanisms in Microsourcing Platforms

TCE proposes two major governance mechanisms (i.e., formal contractual mechanisms and relational mechanisms) for regulating external vendor-client relationships, both of which are enacted by the institution safeguarding these transactions (Lioliou et al. 2014; Williamson 1979; Williamson 1996). Formal contractual mechanisms are written contractual and management-initiated mechanisms between a microsourcer and microsourcee that are enforced by the MP to guide behavior toward desired objectives (Cao and Lumineau 2015). They are enacted to avert exchange hazards and reduce transaction costs by offering three types of mechanisms: foundation, change, and metering<sup>4</sup> (Goo et al. 2009).

Because of the bounded rationality assumed by TCE, formal contractual mechanisms are unable to account for all possible contingencies in transactions (Chiles and McMackin 1996; Williamson 1989). To complement formal contractual mechanisms, relational mechanisms are thus deployed. These refer to unwritten mechanisms between a microsourcer and microsourcee enforced by the MP to encourage interactive behaviors by developing social relations and shared norms (Cao and Lumineau 2015; Lacity et al. 2009). Such mechanisms cover soft issues in transactions and contain dimensions of normative relational, conflict resolution, and mutual dependence mechanisms (Goo et al. 2009).

Formal contractual mechanisms and relational mechanisms of governance are designed and enacted by the MP to be an integral part of the triadic relationship. Notably, recent studies have found that online transactions are also governed by mechanisms enacted by third parties rather than the digital platforms themselves (Fang et al. 2014; Pavlou and Gefen 2004). Examples of such third parties include credit card companies, internet security service providers, and online payment intermediaries (Huang et al. 2017; Kim et al. 2008; Kim et al. 2016). Third-party mechanisms are structures enacted by outside parties to safeguard transaction success; in this sense, they are external to the triadic microsourcing relationship. In the context of this study, we deem three mechanisms to be relevant for the study of microsourcing transactions: (1) escrow services, (2) security seals, and (3) privacy seals. ## Degree-Symmetric Constructs in the Model and Degree-Symmetric Formulations

The unit of analysis in our research model is the microsourcing relationship. Assessment of this relationship relies on the degree as well as the consensus of opinion of the parties. Specifically, the evaluation of governance mechanisms should be based on the joint cognition or shared mental model of both microsourcee and microsourcer, meaning that a highly effective mechanism requires both parties to give higher level ratings. For example, conflict resolution mechanisms aim to help both parties attain a satisfactory result, a result that cannot be so auspicious if there is a discrepancy between parties in rating such mechanisms. Similarly, triadic trust/commitment also requires the consensus of opinion of both parties for success. It is intuitive, then, that mutual partner trust/commitment likewise represents high levels of trust/commitment held by both sides; otherwise. it cannot represent the mutual perceptions of one for the other. Trust/commitment to the MP indicates the extent to which the MP attains trust/commitment from both sides of a microsourcing relationship. The lack of a high evaluation by either side would undermine the overall trust/commitment.

As such, this study collected data from both microsourcer and microsourcee to evaluate governance mechanisms, triadic trust, and triadic commitment. In order to capture the degree and consensus of opinion of both sides, DSM was adopted as the best way to measure each triadic construct (Klein et al. 2007; Straub et al. 2004). On the one hand, symmetric value (i.e., metrics assessing consensus between two or more raters) cannot reflect the absolute level of constructs (Ross et al. 1997). With a high symmetric value, for example, it is not possible to differentiate whether two or more raters jointly agree that they have a low, medium, or high level of trust in each other. On the other hand, degree value (i.e., metrics assessed by the average score of two or more raters) cannot reflect how much the two (or more) parties agree. A high evaluation from one party can significantly increase the overall score of the focal construct regardless of how large the discrepancy is, on average. When considered jointly, the degree and symmetric value of the evaluations best captures the construct. Hence, magnitude and consensus need to be considered simultaneously.

DSM is thus appropriate because it captures both the degree and consensus of opinions held by the microsourcer and microsourcee (Straub et al. 2004; Wu et al. 2015). As DSM implies, a governance mechanism is most effective only when it receives the highest levels of evaluation from both microsourcer and microsourcee. If a microsourcing relationship has a high level of triadic trust/commitment, participants hold a shared perception of high-level trust/commitment toward one another and the MP. See Appendix C for further details on how DSM operates and for examples of computations.

## Governance Mechanisms and Triadic Trust

According to TCE, formal contractual mechanisms serve as safeguards against exchange hazards via the implementation of explicitly written rules (Williamson 1985). It is preferable for parties to develop and adhere to detailed formal contracts to reduce ex post disputes caused by ambiguous responsibilities (Williamson 1989). Among these three dimensions, foundation mechanisms can be defined as overarching stipulations serving as a keystone for clarifying objectives, roles, and responsibilities in a transaction set (Goo et al. 2009). The provision of foundational mechanisms eliminates problems such as low task modularity, poor task design, and ambiguous role clarification, thereby reducing microsourcees’ concerns about the quality of released outsourcing tasks (Deng et al. 2016; Nevo and Kotlarsky 2014). From the standpoint of microsourcers, foundation mechanisms enacted by the MP can filter out ineligible microsourcees and cordon off their misinterpretation of tasks (Lu et al. 2016). In this way, the provision of foundational mechanisms serves as the basis of building trusting atmospheres in microsourcing relationships, given that reaching consensus on tasks is the precondition of completing transactions.

Change mechanisms refer to processes of feedback and efficient adjustments in the process of completing tasks (Goo et al. 2009). Through the regulation of feedback and adjustment procedures, change mechanisms such as status reports can be effective in reducing microsourcing participants’ concerns about progress in the execution of tasks (Deng et al. 2016). Solutions to the predictable contingencies in microsourcing transactions are stipulated by change mechanisms, which allow participants to feel protected during the execution of tasks (Goo et al. 2009). The MP offers the provision of change mechanisms to exert a sustainable process control, enabling participants to focus on task-specific issues rather than defending themselves from opportunism. Such a provision gradually cultivates a sense of trust not only in trading partners, but also in the MP, the entity that enacts the provision.

To make a contract implementable, it is imperative to ensure the provision of metering mechanisms which refer to clear statements of measurements, penalties, and rewards (Goo et al. 2009). The clarification of criteria manifested in the provision can create a sense of fairness among microsourcing participants. For example, deliverables can be evaluated by a series of quantitative indicators stipulated in the provision, a procedure that reduces the possibility of disputes raised in the evaluation of task results (Guo et al. 2017; Lu et al. 2016). With the specification of rewards, microsourcees are motivated to engage in ongoing transactions and reach higher performance goals (Zogaj et al.

2014). In response to such engagements, microsourcers should be willing to provide support for their counterparts to better complete tasks (Nevo and Kotlarsky 2014). Penalties are also useful in reducing concerns of misconduct such as data breach, privacy offenses, and deliberate exploitation (Lu et al. 2015; Obal 2009). The provision of metering mechanisms can create a set of quantitative criteria for implementing contracts, which is conducive to aligning participants’ incentives and building trust in microsourcing relationships.

The provision of these three types of formal contractual mechanisms can reduce both microsourcer and microsourcee concerns about whether transactions mediated by the MP can be efficiently completed. After successful back-and-forth regarding formal contractual mechanisms, mutual partner trust can be developed between the parties for a successful collaboration. Through this process, the MP becomes more highly trusted because it provides formal contractual mechanisms. As such, triadic trust in a microsourcing relationship can be improved by the provision of formal contractual mechanisms enacted by the MP.

H1: In a microsourcing relationship, the provision of formal contractual mechanisms positively influences the level of triadic trust in this relationship.

TCE sets forth bounded rationality as a behavioral assumption that calls for mechanisms to complement the gap left by formal contracts (Williamson 1979; Williamson 1989). Relational mechanisms can fill this gap by addressing social actions like open communication and cooperation, which are critical elements in a wide range of transactional relationships (Goo et al. 2009; Lacity et al. 2009). Among three dimensions of relational mechanisms, normative relational mechanisms refer to tools that cultivate patterns of accepted and expected behaviors shared by both parties and directed toward collective goals (Goo et al. 2009; Heide and John 1992). In microsourcing relationships, the purpose of normative relational mechanisms (e.g., communication tools) is to build solidarity in the expectation that behaviors are directed toward relationship maintenance and common goals (Jap and Ganesan 2000). Based on the solidarity facilitated by this provision, both parties develop a shared behavioral pattern of considering each other’s interests when making adaptations and sharing information (Goo et al. 2009). These norms assuage participant concerns regarding partner’s opportunistic behaviors, which makes them more willing to contribute to collaborations. Trust, then, is formed in the process of collaboration guided by the provision of normative relational mechanisms.

In microsourcing relationships, as in perhaps all relationships, conflicts and disputes are inevitable. Thus, it is necessary to have conflict resolution mechanisms, which can be defined as informal mechanisms that help both parties achieve mutually satisfying resolutions through consensus in conflicts (Goo et al. 2009; Dant and Schul 1992). Because of the complexity of disputes in microsourcing, conflict resolution mechanisms are contingent and specialized, requiring intensive interactions among relevant parties for a satisfactory solution. One such manifestation of this would be 24/7 specialist teams and arbitrational mechanisms set up by the MP to conciliate conflicts. For example, task performance evaluation is vulnerable to disputes caused by cognitive dissonance between partners. On the one hand, deliverables often fail to meet microsourcer expectations due to ineffective communication practices regarding task specification (Ye and Kankanhalli 2017). On the other hand, microsourcees often believe their counterparts devalue microsourcees’ task performance to purposely exploit them via lesser payments (Deng et al. 2016; Obal 2009). Such tensions require the MP to step in with the provision of conflict resolution mechanisms. If conflicts can be amicably resolved, partners will feel more secure in the relationship with the belief that their interests will be protected even when unexpected conflict arises. This, in turn, facilitates the development of trust in microsourcing relationships.

Mutual dependence mechanisms refer to procedures that develop a recognition by both parties that they should rely on each other for benefits (Goo et al. 2009). For example, rating systems are a form of mutual dependence mechanisms that help both parties develop a shared cognition that their success relies on the good opinion of the other party. The provision of such mechanisms cultivates a sense of interdependence, which then incentivizes participants to put a high priority on their collaborative relationships (Lusch and Brown 1996). Specifically, these mechanisms encourage microsourcees’ high engagement and work quality by generating satisfactory results, which, in turn, reduces concern on the part of their counterparts (Wang et al. 2017). Alternatively, microsourcers can focus more on building sustained relationships with microsourcees to improve microsourcees’ perceptions of fairness (Deng et al. 2016). In addition, mutual reliance shows that microsourcing activities undertaken by the MP supplement other alternatives, leading to a sense of belonging to the MP on the part of the partners. Conceivably, trust can be easily bred in transactions governed by the provision of mutual dependence mechanisms.

To summarize, the provision of relational mechanisms can enhance triadic trust in a microsourcing relationship by facilitating the social aspects of transaction sets. Specifically, mutual partner trust is more easily built in an atmosphere where partners have fewer concerns and deeper engagement.

Furthermore, pleasant transactions are conducive to developing trust in the MP who enacts the provision of relational mechanisms.

H2: In a microsourcing relationship, the provision of relational mechanisms positively influences the level of triadic trust in this relationship.

Regarding the entity enacting governance mechanisms, TCE puts forward the possibility of multiple institutions providing mechanisms to safeguard transactions (Williamson 1979; Williamson 1999). The emergence of digital platforms renders this commonplace by integrating third-party mechanisms enacted by outside parties rather than by the platforms themselves (Fang et al. 2014; Huang et al. 2017; Kim 2008). Escrow services are one of the thirdparty mechanisms used by most MPs to underwrite the financial aspect of transactions. Escrow services can be defined as services where third parties hold and regulate payment of the task and authorize payment only after the set of transactions is completed (Pavlou and Gefen 2004). In microsourcing, the escrowing institution receives payment from a microsourcer and holds this amount until both parties are satisfied with the results. Conceivably, microsourcees are more willing to engage in tasks when the risk of being unpaid is reduced in this way. At the same time, microsourcers feel more secure due to the low possibility of misconduct sans dispute resolution. Participants can thus concentrate on completing tasks, which makes mutual partner trust more likely to evolve. Due to this financial guarantee, participants are more inclined to trust the MP.

Security seals are another type of third-party mechanism commonly adopted by MPs. By definition, security seals are guarantees provided by third parties that protect information, transactions, and users from being attacked by malicious entities (Kim et al. 2016). By advertising their security seals, MPs ensure that microsourcers are more willing to release tasks online rather than performing offline monitoring activities themselves that are, oftentimes, more uncertain and expensive. Similarly, when the cybersecurity of the site is warrantied, the pool of microsourcees will likely be enlarged. Increasing numbers of both microsourcers and microsourcees create a network externality that ultimately improves the efficiency of microsourcing transactions because the probability of matching suitable microsourcees to tasks is improved (Ye and Kankanhalli 2013). As such, trust can also be built within microsourcing relationships using security seals to make these relationships more secure and successful.

MPs also offer privacy seals to protect information from inappropriate and unauthorized disclosure. Privacy seals are guarantees enacted by third parties through a series of regulations and techniques to ensure the privacy of information transmitted online (Luo 2002; Xu et al. 2011). These can effectively address microsourcer concerns about losing sensitive information (Zogaj et al. 2014). Reassured by privacy seals, microsourcers will be more likely to post more microsourcing tasks without worrying about information disclosure. Additionally, more information can be comfortably shared with microsourcees as they work on tasks, a sharing that reduces concerns about microsourcers’ low levels of involvement and enhances the quality of completed tasks (Nevo and Kotlarsky 2014). The adoption of privacy seals encourages an open and interactive atmosphere for participants to jointly engage in tasks, which fundamentally facilitates the development of trust.

The provision of third-party mechanisms by MPs is conducive to a secure environment for online microsourcing transactions in that it offers financial, technical, and privacy guarantees. As a consequence, participants are more likely to engage in microsourcing transactions, from which mutual partner trust is gradually built. Likewise, trust in the MP can be further boosted by its role in building favorable transacting environments by introducing these third-party mechanisms.

H3: In a microsourcing relationship, the provision of thirdparty mechanisms positively influences the level of triadic trust in this relationship.

## Triadic Trust and Triadic Commitment

In a microsourcing relationship, triadic trust is the joint confidence held by both microsourcer and microsourcee that they can rely on each other and the MP. With a high level of triadic trust, participants believe in the other’s integrity and reliability and are willing to stay in the relationship for additional transactional exchanges (Mayer et al. 1995). According to the basic tenets of TCE, transaction costs should be significantly reduced when collaborating with trustworthy partners supported by a reliable MP because less attention needs to be paid to monitoring, negotiating, and reconciliation (Chiles and McMackin 1996; Dyer and Chu 2003). Concurrently, a long-term relationship is conducive to achieving higher collaborative performance because of the familiarity, common language, and shared knowledge between partners (Kalwani and Narayandas 1995; Prajogo and Olhager 2012). Thus, when participants perceive partners in a microsourcing relationship to be trustworthy, they will prioritize this ongoing relationship and be inclined to put forth greater efforts to maintain it (Morgan and Hunt 1994). While the relationship between trust and commitment has been amply verified in traditional dyadic contexts (Bugg Holloway et al. 2016; Eastlick et al. 2006; Yuan et al. 2018), we posit that this logic is still valid when applied to a triadic online relationship.

H4: In a microsourcing relationship, the level of triadic trust positively influences the level of commitment to this relationship.

## Research Methodology

## Method and Data Collection

To validate our hypotheses, we conducted a field study in a popular MP in China, Zbj.com. As detailed in Appendix E, we posted a job asking microsourcees to participate. The criteria for participation required microsourcees to have at least one previously completed transaction on an IT-related task. Setting this requirement allowed us to contact the designated microsourcer. Also, the focus on IT-related tasks excluded many low idiosyncratic, commodity-like tasks where trust and commitment are presumably not so important. After assembling a list of microsourcees, we visited the homepage of the microsourcees and randomly selected microsourcers who left feedback about the microsourcee’s service. The feedback system is prima facie evidence of sufficiently effective cooperation or that the project was completed. The project feedback also documents that a string of transactions actually took place.

In all, we gathered 548 valid microsourcer-microsourcee dyads for the study, excluding nonpaired data. Appendix F shows the demographic information on the microsourcing tasks, microsourcers, and microsourcees involved in this sampling. Nonrespondent bias was assessed by comparing early and late responses (Armstrong and Overton 1977). The results showed no significant differences across principal constructs.

## Measurement

As in our research model, three types of governance mechanisms, triadic trust, and triadic commitment were measured as formative constructs. The formative approach was applied to formal contractual, relational, and third-party mechanisms because of its ability to capture the bulk of the conceptual domain of these mechanisms (MacKenzie et al. 2011). Based on the unique characteristics of the microsourcing context, formative measures are thus fitting for this research. For instance, formative indicators can measure specific details of these mechanisms rather than general or global reflective perceptions. Given this, we operationalized triadic trust and commitment as formative constructs because of their definitions, which inherently incorporate the two components discussed earlier. Moreover, formative measurements conformed to the decision rules advanced by Jarvis et al. (2003) in terms of: (1) direction of causality, (2) interchangeability of indicators, (3) covariation among indicators, and (4) nomological net of indicators. Taking formal contractual mechanisms as an example, this construct reflects a set of provisions of formal governance that are determined by foundational, change, and metering aspects. It is thus clear that these aspects do not share the same content and are not interchangeable. For this reason, they should not necessarily covary with one other. Likewise, their antecedents and consequences may differ due to their distinctive characteristics.

To validate this judgment, we checked the dataset by employing confirmatory tetrad analysis and assessing variance inflation factors (VIFs) (Bollen and Ting 1993; Gudergan et al. 2008). Appendix G presents results from the CTA-PLS computations with 5,000 bootstrap subsamples, supporting the formative measurement of all constructs. Moreover, multicollinearity was not a threat to our formative approach because no VIF exceeded 10.0 (Dormann et al. 2013). Finally, adequacy coefficients were calculated based on the recommendation of Edwards (2001). Thus, the results in Appendix G show satisfactory support for the choice of formative measurement in measuring these relationships.

Measurement of formal contractual mechanisms, including foundation mechanisms, change mechanisms, and metering mechanisms, were adapted from Goo et al. (2009). Relational mechanisms consisted of normative relational mechanisms, conflict resolution mechanisms, and mutual dependence mechanisms and were based on the scale developed by Goo et al. (2009) and Mohr and Spekman (1994). Operationalization of third-party mechanisms was based on three studies, with Pavlou and Gefen (2004) for escrow services, Kim et al. (2016) for security seals, and Xu et al. (2011) for privacy seals. Due to a dearth of studies on the role of governance mechanisms in microsourcing, our scales were adapted to fit this particular context. Scales used are shown in Appendix D.

In developing the questionnaire, a group of microsourcers and microsourcees were invited to check whether the measurements addressed the characteristics of their microsourcing transactions. The points they raised provided direction for revising the scales. For example, one item among the change mechanisms states that the MP “provides mechanisms that help this microsourcee/microsourcer and us to develop formal contracts that describe how periodical checks of task progress will be conducted.” This adaptation was based on a comment concerning the specific mechanism of the MP in periodical checks to prevent tasks from being misdirected.

Triadic trust comprised two components: mutual partner trust and trust in the MP. We used mutual partner trust as a second-order formative construct measured by three aspects: integrity, benevolence, and competence (McKnight et al. 2002). Trust in the MP was also used as a second-order formative construct using items adapted from Gefen et al. (2003) and McKnight et al. (2002) to measure structural assurance and situational normality. We employed components of triadic commitment that are similar to triadic trust: namely, mutual partner commitment and commitment to the MP. The three dimensions we used to measure mutual partner commitment—durability, input, and consistency— were measured by scales adapted from Goo et al. (2009) and Kumar et al. (1995). Commitment to the MP was measured by adapting items that reflected continuance commitment and affective commitment (Bateman et al. 2011; Malhotra and Galletta 2005).

The instrument had parallel measures for both microsourcees and microsourcers, and we used DSM to calculate all the construct values using data collected from both sides. As previous studies have suggested, a dyadic design is recommended when investigating phenomena associated with relationships involving multiple parties (Klein and Rai 2009; Ross et al. 1997), especially when adopting DSM to evaluate the consensus of both parties (Klein et al. 2007; Straub et al. 2004). The degree-symmetric value of governance mechanisms reflects the joint cognition on how the MP governs transactions. This value can only be high when both sides concur with the provision of certain governance mechanisms. Triadic trust and commitment thus measured the perceptions of microsourcee and microsourcer toward their mutual relationship and that with the MP, which can be high only in situations where both parties agree that they have a high level of trust and commitment. Moreover, in that this research was conducted in China, we employed a back translation approach to ensure semantic consistency between the English and Chinese instruments (Brislin 1970).

## Analysis and Results

## Measurement Model Results

We chose partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) to analyze data for three main reasons. First, PLS-SEM is especially suitable for exploratory work and prediction when theories are in the early stages of development (Hair Jr et al. 2014), as with the development of our microsourcing theory. Second, component-based PLS-SEM readily handles both formative and reflective constructs (Gefen et al. 2011). Third, PLS-SEM is liberal regarding the distribution of data so that a dataset which is not normally distributed can still be readily manipulated in this analytic tool without substantive distortions in parameter estimates (Diamantopoulos and Siguaw 2006; Gefen et al. 2011; Ringle et al. 2012).

As previously mentioned, data were collected from both microsourcers and microsourcees. DSM was employed as a tool to aggregate dyadic items to a score reflecting both degree and consensus (Klein et al. 2007; Straub et al. 2004). As such, we tested reliability and validity of degree-symmetric constructs, details of which are provided in Appendix G.

For the reflectively measured constructs, internal consistency reliability and convergent and discriminant validity were used to assess the measurement model (Chin 1998; Gefen et al. 2000). Appendix G gives a detailed account of the results of Cronbach’s alphas, composite reliabilities, and average variance extracted (AVE). Cronbach’s alphas for all reflective constructs were above the threshold value 0.70, suggesting good reliability (Nunnally 1978). The AVE for each construct was greater than the threshold of 0.50 (Fornell and Larcker 1981). Together with the satisfactory item loadings, all above 0.60, these results demonstrated that the instrument had acceptable convergent validity. To assess discriminant validity, we compared the square root of the AVE of each construct with its correlation with all other constructs (Hair Jr et al. 2014). All AVE square roots were larger than corresponding correlations, suggesting good discriminant validity. As some of the correlations exceeded 0.60, we tested multicollinearity by calculating the VIF of each reflective construct. VIFs ranged from 1.434 to 3.453, all below the threshold of 10.0 (Dormann et al. 2013), suggesting that multicollinearity was not a significant threat.

For formatively measured constructs, we followed the validation steps suggested by Petter et al. (2007) and Cenfetelli and Bassellier (2009). First, the weights and t-statistics of the formative indicators were examined. Table 2 shows the weights and t-statistics of the formatively measured constructs— specifically, formal contractual mechanisms, relational mechanisms, third-party mechanisms, trust in the MP, mutual partner trust, commitment to the MP, mutual partner commitment, triadic trust, and triadic commitment. All indicators were well below the standard community alpha protection level of 0.05. Next, because no indicator weight was negative, suppressor effects were not a concern (Cenfetelli and

Bassellier 2009). Finally, as reported above, multicollinearity was not a serious issue among any of the reflective constructs which made up the first order formative constructs. Thus, the a priori condition for covariation among the formative constructs was satisfied (Diamantopoulos and Siguaw 2006; Petter et al. 2007). As such, formative constructs appear to be properly measured in this research. After validating formative constructs, we calculated DSM scores for the assessment of the structural model (Hair et al. 2011).

Based on these procedural results, we assessed the structural model by examining the magnitudes, path coefficients, and R<sup>2</sup> values, as summarized in Figure 4.

Regressing triadic trust on the three governance mechanisms was found to account for 42.7% of the explained variance, providing strong support for H<sub>1</sub>, H<sub>2</sub>, and H<sub>3</sub>. Furthermore, triadic trust had a positive and significant influence on triadic commitment $( b \ = \ 0 . 5 8 1 , \ t \ = \ 7 . 5 2 3 )$ explaining 34.7% of the variance. Thus, H4 is also supported.

## Post Hoc Analysis

Previous studies have found a complementary or substitutive effect between formal contractual and relational governance mechanisms (Huber et al. 2013; Lioliou et al. 2014; Poppo and Zenger 2002). These mechanisms have typically been tested through statistical interactions. Although interactions among different governance mechanisms were not the focus of this study, a post hoc analysis was performed to consider these effects in our sampled microsourcing setting. The incorporation of third-party mechanisms thus made it a three-way interaction analysis. Results showed that all twoway interactions, including the one between formal contractual mechanisms and relational mechanisms (b = 0.053, t = 0.493), formal contractual mechanisms and thirdparty mechanisms (b = 0.222, t = 1.835), and relational mechanisms and third-party mechanisms (b = -0.028, t = 0.225), were insignificant. The three-way interaction among these governance mechanisms was likewise not significant (b = 0.119, t =0.819). The logical inference here is that microsourcing appears to differ from traditional IT outsourcing in that there are no real substitutive or complementary effects with respect to governance mechanisms.

## Discussion, Implications, and Limitations

The current study holistically investigates triadic relationships in microsourcing. Its first focus was on modeling triadic trusting relationships via the provision of effectual governance mechanisms. The results validate our hypotheses that formal contractual mechanisms, relational mechanisms, and third-party mechanisms are all conducive to a triadic trusting relationship, with formal contractual mechanisms being the strongest of these antecedents. The results are also consistent with the findings of prior studies where some forms of governance mechanisms are necessary to safeguard outsourcing projects (Goo et al. 2009; Lioliou et al. 2014) and online transactions (Fang et al. 2014; Xu et al. 2011). Overall, the positive and evidentiary strong relationship between triadic trust and triadic commitment was supported, a linkage that extends traditional dyadic relationships (Morgan and Hunt 1994) to triadic, online relationships. The results of the proposed model support the validity of our modified TCE model as a theory base for explaining online triadic relationships, highlighting the importance of governance mechanisms for building trust to reduce transaction costs in ongoing collaborations.

In the post hoc analysis, we did not find interaction effects among formal contractual, relational, and third-party mechanisms. This indicates that the roles of the three governance mechanisms in improving triadic trust do not have a multiplicative impact on the DV, a result that differs dramatically from the findings of prior research that has often reported complementary or substitutive relationships between formal contractual and relational governance in traditional dyadic relationships (Cao and Lumineau 2015; Poppo and Zenger 2002; Rai et al. 2012). As such, this finding provides preliminary evidence that, in online triadic relationships, effectuating different governance mechanisms will not undermine the desired outcomes nor result in a trade-off. In particular, this study found that the MP provides functions that enact formal-contractual and relational mechanisms, which, in turn, increases the salience of these distinctions. Microsourcing participants were thus able to see these mechanisms as inherently different and avoid intertwining their roles in improving trust.

With regard to formative second-order results, contractual mechanisms were most critical (path coefficient of 0.338), and significantly more so than relational and third-party mechanisms. It is also the case that the coefficient of thirdparty mechanisms is larger than that of relational mechanisms (0.255 versus 0.226). This result indicates that formal contractual mechanisms aid transactions in moving forward without delay, owing to the structural regulations enacted internally, thus nurturing an interaction that is critical to triadic trust. Third-party mechanisms are enacted by external entities but still apply compulsory rules to transactions, thereby being relatively important to triadic trust. For relational mechanisms, which take longer to evolve, microsourcing participants do become aware of its role in affecting future, repeat transactions, but they are likely not to consider such mechanisms to be as important as the other two mechanisms.

Several formative, first-order components were also noteworthy. According to Cenfetelli and Bassellier (2009), weights reflect the relative importance of indicators. In this sense, foundation mechanisms contribute more to the extent of formal contractual mechanisms than change mechanisms $( \Delta b = 0 . 0 3 5 , t = 2 2 . 0 2 4 )$ and metering mechanisms $( \Delta b =$ $0 . 0 1 5 , t = 9 . 2 3 1 )$ ). This finding echoes the assertion derived from traditional outsourcing literature on the critical role of key principles and agreements that stipulate both parties behaviors before a transaction takes place (Goo et al. 2009). Although microsourcing tasks are short term and earn relatively small amounts of money, this by no means eliminates the importance of developing ex ante consensus as a solid foundation (Lu et al. 2016). Participants are acutely aware of the rules that should be promulgated to facilitate mutual agreement on the obligations and responsibilities of each party.

We found that mutual dependence mechanisms are more important than normative relational mechanisms $( \Delta b \ =$ $0 . 0 1 7 , t = 8 . 6 1 1 )$ and conflict resolution mechanisms $( \Delta b =$ $0 . 0 3 0 , t = 1 5 . 4 8 5 )$ , which contradicts previous results from empirical studies on microsourcing. This finding highlights its arms-length nature, where minimum interaction is deemed to be desirable (Deng et al. 2016). The focus on cultivating mutual reliance between transacting parties reflects concern over the occurrence of opportunism or the carelessness that might be associated with short-term transactions. Although microsourcing tasks are short-term by nature, the relationship between microsourcer and microsourcee could potentially extend to a long-term relationship with recurring transactions. In this sense, transaction costs are further reduced because of the trust and familiarity that develop side-by-side with the collaboration. For this reason, we infer that participants might perceive mechanisms for developing mutual dependence to be crucial.

The results of this study show that privacy seals are more salient in contributing to third-party mechanisms than escrow services $( \Delta b = 0 . 0 3 9 , t = 2 0 . 7 6 6 )$ and security seals $( \Delta b = 0 . 0 2 2 , t = 1 1 . 8 2 8 )$ . This result addresses a certain anxiety about privacy safety in microsourcing transactions. Clearly, microsourcers need to share information with microsourcees for completing certain tasks without having to worry about information loss (Zogaj et al. 2014). Similarly, microsourcees should be concerned about unauthorized disclosure of their information deliverables which, in fact, have become their intellectual property (Ye and Kankanhalli 2017). The participants of this study reported heavy reliance on privacy seals, perhaps indicating a lack of confidence in the MP to fully protect participants privacy, a point that calls for extra attention from MPs to more fully address this issue.

Furthermore, mutual partner trust was found to contribute more than just trust in the MP to triadic trust $( \Delta b = 0 . 0 3 5 ,$ t $\begin{array} { r l } { = { } } & { { } 9 . 6 7 3 ) } \end{array}$ , which, not unsurprisingly, suggests that participants pay more attention to their trading partners when building the cognition of trust in transacting relationships. Among the dimensions of mutual partner trust, competence had the highest statistical weight $( \Delta b = 0 . 1 0 4 , t = 8 6 . 0 7 0$ for integrity; $\Delta b = 0 . 2 3 4 , t = 1 7 6 . 6 2 9$ for benevolence), which highlights participant concerns about whether their counterparts are capable of fulfilling their obligations. This phenomenon is probably due to the misalignment between the low entry barrier into the marketplace and the minimal level of skills required for microsourcing tasks (Saxton et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2017). In terms of trust in the MP, structural assurance was found to be more important than situational normality $( \Delta b = 0 . 4 0 7 , t = 2 5 7 . 0 3 1 )$ . This result supports the view that the participants’ major expectation of the platform is to provide certain guarantees, regulations, and procedures to protect their interests (Feller et al. 2012; Guo et al. 2017). As digital platforms are spread across similar transactions and environments, situational normality appears not to be as key a factor as structural assurance (the latter being nearly twice as strong as the former).

Similar to triadic trust, the weight of mutual partner commitment was statistically higher than commitment to the MP $( \Delta b \ = \ 0 . 0 1 9 , \ t \ = \ 9 . 8 1 1 )$ . Consistency, input, and durability were shown to contribute equally to their secondorder construct, i.e., mutual partner commitment. For commitment to the MP, affective commitment had a higher weight than continuous commitment $( \Delta b \ = \ 0 . 0 9 0 , \ t \ =$ 82.607), which indicates that the economic benefits of microsourcing are less salient than the inherent feeling of satisfaction it brings to participants. For example, microsourcing provides flexible jobs for individuals, thereby offering work-life balance (Deng and Joshi 2016). On the other hand, microsourcers can gather innovative ideas and expand their capabilities by hiring outsourced workers (Blohm et al. 2016; Obal 2009).

## Theoretical Implications

The current study posits that, theoretically, microsourcing is a triadic relationship that requires trust to keep parties committed. This paper thus explores the different roles, obligations, and concerns of each entity in microsourcing relationships. Specifically, the MP is understood to be an intermediary providing a favorable environment for microsourcing transactions. As a two-sided platform, its goal is to maintain the balance between each side of the market while increasing the overall size of the market. Therefore, building a series of mechanisms for safeguarding transactions is a major task for the MP in addition to meeting participant expectations. Microsourcers and microsourcees are understood to have different concerns when transacting under the auspices of the MP. This study identifies concerns that hinder partners from developing trust and continuous engagement in microsourcing relationships based on the governance mechanisms provided by MPs. By investigating triadic relationships, the current research addresses a critical theoretical knowledge gap in the microsourcing and, more broadly, the crowdsourcing literature, both of which tend to stress the interests of only one party (Afuah and Tucci 2012; Leimeister et al. 2009; Lu et al. 2016).

As such, this study enriches a stream of TCE research advocating for the role of trust in eliminating transaction costs. Numerous theoretical works on TCE have been used to ground the role of trust in reducing costs related to contract drafting, negotiating, and monitoring (Beccerra and Gupta 1999; Chiles and McMackin 1996; Hosmer 1995). As a consequence, prior empirical studies have paid little to no attention to conceptualizing trust at the relationship level (Goo et al. 2009; Mao et al. 2008; Yuan et al. 2018). As contended by TCE theorists, trust can only be effective in reducing transaction costs when it is jointly perceived by all parties in a relationship, a point that has not been sufficiently emphasized in prior works (Bromiley and Harris 2006; Chiles and McMackin 1996). The current study, then, is an attempt to address this empirical gap by adopting a holistic view to conceptualize triadic trust to represent the joint perception of each participant. In this sense, this study contributes to the TCE literature not only by offering solid empirical support but also by improving its claims via validating the importance of addressing participants’ joint perception of trust.

Given that microsourcing is a form of outsourcing conducted on digital platforms, this study extends IT outsourcing literature by showing how prior findings can be fitted to the digital platform literature (Lander et al. 2004; Lee and Choi 2011). We posit that governance mechanisms, trust, and commitment, which are well addressed in traditional outsourcing research (Goo et al. 2009), are still vital in this new theoretical context. Importantly, we argue that the lens for understanding them needs to be adjusted to the unique microsourcing context.

In terms of trust and commitment, this study goes beyond the traditional dyadic perspective by conceptualizing them as triadic constructs aligned to triadic microsourcing relationships. Existing studies have been carried out mainly through the study of dyadic relationships via either monadic or dyadic ways of theorizing trust and commitment (Lee and Choi 2011; Yuan et al. 2018). The essential involvement of the platform as an intermediary complicates and, at the same time, eases transactional relationships. A triadic trusting/committed relationship should exhibit trust/commitment toward each other and toward the platform. Concurrently, having trust/commitment to a transactional partner, in addition to an enabling IT platform, makes this context markedly different from traditional IT outsourcing contexts.

It is possible that this triadic approach can be generalized to other forms of platform-mediated, peer-to-peer transactions like e-commerce, crowdfunding, and e-health. Specifically, relevant studies can advance the triadic approach through the development of triadic constructs that contain the two components measured by DSM to capture both degree and consensus between the transacting parties. Furthermore, adopting the holistic view necessitated by this approach can extend beyond triads to online transactions involving a digital intermediary and multiple participants. For example, tetradic trust can be conceptualized in transactions with one intermediary and three parties, which consist of mutual trust among transacting parties and trust in the intermediary. DSM can also be extended to integrate data from multiple (k) sides, based on the seminal work of Straub et al. (2004), which articulates the operationalization of this approach. In a nutshell, the triadic approach of conceptualizing trust and commitment is generalizable to a significant portion of digital platform research.

Governance mechanisms, too, are conceptualized in the present study as the joint perception held by microsourcer and microsourcee in a relationship. Similar to the aforementioned triadic approach, our treatment of governance mechanisms is based on a holistic view that considers the degree and consensus of both parties. It thus advances the current governance mechanism literature that focuses mainly on dyadic relationships where governance is provided by either or both transacting partners (Goo et al. 2009; Oshri et al. 2015). In this study, the MP takes on the responsibility of governance mechanisms, an action that requires joint perceptions from both participants to evaluate its effectiveness.

Furthermore, this study identifies third-party mechanisms as a form of governance mechanisms enacted by entities outside microsourcing relationships. This advances knowledge on vendor-client governance studies, in that such studies have not yet differentiated whether governance mechanisms are enacted by internal transacting parties or external groups. The present study conceptualizes thirdparty mechanisms as a dimension of vendor-client governance mechanisms in order to extend prior research on identifying external assurances against security threats in online transactions (Fang et al. 2014; Kim et al. 2016). This trichotomous categorization of vendor-client governance is generalizable to other online platforms, echoing the call to address the governance issue regarding digital platforms in two-sided markets (Tiwana et al. 2010).

## Practical Implications

The findings of this paper offer ways for microsourcing practitioners to better maintain collaborative relationships. First, MP operators should be aware of their obligation to create a favorable environment for users to transact in by providing safeguards in the form of formal contractual, relational, and third-party mechanisms. This study is highly suggestive that these mechanisms can be effective in promoting the site. Consistent with traditional outsourcing projects, explicit boilerplate contracts are important for setting the baseline for microsourcing transactions. Because of the limited budgets of many of the participants in negotiating contracts, MPs should provide a general framework and assist both parties in customization. Special attention should be paid to contractual clauses related to control and metering during task execution.

At the same time, MPs should also provide functionalities for facilitating relational interactions between transacting parties, a provision that cultivates a sense of community and belonging to the platform. It is worth noting here that, to assist in building trusting relationships, MP practitioners need to develop and disseminate proper norms for participants. Moreover, MP managers need to adopt assurances provided by third parties to protect the payment, information security, and privacy of online transactions. Additional attention might also be paid to introducing assurances that address privacy, the highest concern of microsourcing participants.

Microsourcers must be aware of fulfilling their responsibilities across each stage of the collaboration to foster long-term and repeated relationships with their partners. Specifically, they should ensure the modularity and executability of microsourcing tasks. Before releasing tasks, microsourcers should check the clarity and correctness of task descriptions and contract clauses under MP guidance, lest preconditions for a successful transaction remain unfulfilled. When their counterparts are performing tasks, they should communicate, using the MP’s tools to stay in touch with microsourcees and handle requests for help. After task completion, it is imperative for microsourcers to make a fair assessment of the deliverables and to utilize proffered MP mechanisms for resolving disputes.

Similarly, the results of this study provide guidance for microsourcees to attain trust and commitment from their counterparts. Before deciding to take on a task, microsourcees should exchange messages with their partners to settle contractual issues by utilizing the available tools of the MP. For best results, microsourcees should be fully engaged when undertaking tasks and delivering periodic reports to inform partners about progress. It is also necessary to be open to adjustments, given sufficient communication. After task completion, microsourcees can direct their attention to evaluation; and, in the event of dissatisfaction, they can negotiate or ask for arbitration from the MP.

## Limitations

Our research is by no means definitive, which leaves open opportunities for researchers to further explore this fascinating phenomenon. First, this research adopts a crosssectional approach to collect data at a single point in time. Future research might collect longitudinal data to capture the dynamics of cognitive changes regarding perceptions of trust and commitment. In addition, causality can be strengthened by collecting data for governance mechanisms prior to measures of trust and commitment.

Second, the results of this study are limited in external validity since the sample is restricted to data from a single Chinese MP, albeit a very large one that might be readily generalizable to other large platforms. As researchers have noted, the conceptualization of trust could vary under different cultural conditions and social environments (Doney et al. 1998; Schoorman et al. 2007; Vance et al. 2008). Thus, the importance of different dimensions of trust in the MP and commitment to the MP may vary across different MPs. Therefore, replications of this study and validation of the research model using other data sources would be advisable. In particular, scholars are encouraged to investigate this topic in other countries to highlight the possible impact of cultural differences. Certain contextual factors can be incorporated as moderators in such work, which can enrich existing inferences on triadic trust and commitment in microsourcing relationships.

Third, this research collected data from one MP in order to control the confounds that can be generated by different platforms. As such, this design is a double-edged sword in that it makes it impossible to test the influence of objective platform-based features (e.g., market size, task category, and platform service fee) and to compare different MP strategies. For example, varying platform pricing structures might impact long-term commitment to microsourcing relationships because they directly determine transaction costs. Future studies can potentially investigate these and other factors of MPs that influence the trust and commitment of participants and compare effects across platform features.

Last but not least, the sample selection of this study was only partially randomized, which could lead to bias in the results. Given the random sample pool in which questionnaires were distributed, this approach can only be called partially randomized because the valid responses tended to be from participants who had transactions prior to the one being investigated. In other words, valid respondents were those who were naturally more experienced. Although we conclude, using statistical controls, that the number of prior collaborations had no effect on either triadic trust or triadic commitment, future studies are still encouraged to address this issue by involving more respondents from a diverse range of experience to mitigate this potential threat.

## Conclusion

Microsourcing is among the most popular of recently innovative digital markets. It is therefore attracting increasing attention from IS scholars. The current study takes a holistic theoretical view to consider microsourcing as an essentially triadic relationship that requires trust to foster commitment. Based on TCE, provisions of formal contractual, relational, and third-party mechanisms were all found to be salient antecedents for breeding triadic trust and commitment in the relationship. Data collected from both sides of the relationship supported all hypotheses. Overall, we hope that the results of this study can inform practitioners of each entity involved in a microsourcing relationship in order to jointly build long-term collaborative relationships.

## Supplemental Theoretical Background on Microsourcing

Differing from traditional offline outsourcing practices, microsourcing is a new way to match the supply and demand of labor, a marketplace that takes place through an open, virtual, online intermediary called the microsourcing platform (MP) (Deng et al. 2016a; Lu et al. 2015). Within the confines of the MP, a microsourcer initiates an open call for a task, to which microsourcees submit their qualifications and proposals. The microsourcer then chooses a bid winner.<sup>7</sup> Consequently, the chosen microsourcee undertakes the task according to an agreedupon reward in a specific time frame. As a business model for efficiently matching service supply and demand, MPs have grown rapidly in recent years and boast a large market size. For example, Amazon Mechanical Turk is estimated to have a yearly transactional volume of USD 10 million to 150 million.<sup>8</sup> Similarly, Freelancer.com has more than 35 million registered users and nearly 10 million posted projects, serving people in 247 countries, regions and territories.<sup>9</sup> Zbj.com, the largest MP in China, announced that they had more than 6 million microsourcers and 13 million microsourcees in 2015 and that they are adding several millions of new users per year.<sup>10</sup>

The microsourcing relationship is triadic and consists of three entities: microsourcer, microsourcee, and the MP. The microsourcer is the entity that initiates the sourcing process by submitting a task request and specifying acceptance criteria. The microsourcee is a member of the crowd who undertakes and executes tasks and thereby gains monetary rewards from the microsourcer. The MP is a digital intermediary ensuring that online microsourcees successfully complete task requests and that online microsourcers pay for work that meets the agreedupon standards. The successful completion of a string of transactions taking place under the auspices of an MP entails trust from both microsourcer and microsourcee. Shortcomings on either side will lead to the undermining and/or termination of the relationship. For further clarification, we state the key roles of the MP context in Table A1.

In the variant of microsourcing known as the “contract” model, the microsourcee is selected by the microsourcer based on qualifications and work proposals. Thereafter, the microsourcee undertakes the task according to an agreed-upon reward in a specific time frame. The contract model is well suited for task types such as programming, website design, and software development—tasks to which IS scholars have long paid attention. This contract model, moreover, can be thought of as the traditional IT outsourcing model with the additional defining characteristic of the MP as an intermediary connecting microsourcer and microsourcee. New features compared to traditional features are summarized in Table A2.

There have been numerous studies on crowdsourcing in the existing literature. These include but are not limited to: (1) conceptual studies introducing crowdsourcing variations (Afuah and Tucci 2012; Doan et al. 2011; Estellés-Arolas 2012); (2) crowdsourcing-based open innovation and contest (Dissanayake et al. 2015; Leimeister et al. 2009; Zheng et al. 2011); and (3) literature analysis and reviews in the IS domain (Majchrzak and Malhotra 2013; Pedersen et al. 2013; Zhao and Zhu 2012). However, studies on microsourcing are still in their infancy. Table A3 summarizes relevant, to-date scholarly studies on microsourcing.

## Supplemental Information on Governance Mechanisms, Triadic Trust, and Triadic Commitment

For MPs, there are three types of governance mechanisms: formal contractual, relational, and third-party mechanisms. Formal contractual mechanisms have three subdimensions: foundation, change, and metering mechanisms (Goo et al. 2009). Foundation mechanisms stipulate key guiding principles, obligations, and targeted levels of performance for both parties. To instantiate this mechanism, the MP can provide online consultants to assist both parties in reaching an agreement prior to starting tasks. Change mechanisms focus on feedback processes and adjustment procedures over the duration of microsourcing task completion. Such mechanisms can be realized through status reports that enable both parties to regularly discuss task progress and make necessary adjustments. Metering mechanisms clearly define measurements, rewards, and penalties for each trading party. Such mechanisms can be instrumented by MPs in the step-by-step design of structured guidance for microsourcers in developing their task descriptions via clearly predefined criteria.

Relational mechanisms consist of normative relational, conflict resolution, and mutual dependence mechanisms (Goo et al. 2009). Normative relational mechanisms specify patterns of accepted and expected behaviors shared by both parties that can benefit the attainment of collective goals. Communication tools in the MP are conducive to building up shared norms via frequent interactions between parties. Conflict resolution mechanisms concentrate on arriving at mutually satisfactory resolutions to conflicts whenever they occur. Due to the complexity of disputes in microsourcing, conflict resolution mechanisms are contingent and idiosyncratic and thereby require intensive interactions among relevant parties for a satisfactory solution. One such manifestation of this is 24/7 specialist teams and arbitrational mechanisms set up by the MP to conciliate conflicts. Mutual dependence mechanisms are designed to foster recognition of mutual reliance between microsourcers and microsourcees. An example of this is a rating system tool for cultivating a sense of interdependence because it brings reputational awareness to participants and encourages collaboration with their counterparts to satisfactorily deliver on the assigned task.

Third-party mechanisms include mechanisms like escrow services, security seals, and privacy seals. Escrow services aim to regulate and authorize payment only after the microsourcer receives and approves the deliverables (Pavlou and Gefen 2004). Security seals have also been widely adopted to protect transactions from being attacked by malicious external entities (Kim et al. 2016). In addition, privacy seals can prevent the disclosure of sensitive information during transactions and thus prevent unauthorized use of said information (Xu et al. 2011). Table B1 illustrates the definition and exemplary functionalities of MPs of each subdimension.

There are two major components of triadic trust: trust in the MP and mutual partner trust. Trust in the MP is institutionally based and focuses on the belief that favorable conditions are in place to support success (McKnight and Chervany 2001). Such trust is determined by the MP in its role as an intermediary providing a regulatory, technical, and procedural environment in which to conduct ongoing microsourcing transactions between partners (Luo 2002; McKnight et al. 1998). Scholars have widely claimed that institution-based trust is thought to have two components: structural assurance and situational normality (Gefen et al. 2003; Lowry et al. 2008; McKnight and Chervany 2001). In the context of microsourcing, structural assurance refers to the belief that structures in the platform—regulations, promises, legal recourse, guarantees, and other procedures— are in place to promote the overall success of transactions. Situational normality is defined as the belief that the environment of the platform is ordered and success is more likely when the situation is normal or favorable.

Mutual partner trust is a high priority in building collaborative relationships because it helps participants rapidly achieve ex ante task agreement and efficiently evaluate task performance (Brinkhoff et al. 2015). Mutual trust arises from the trustee’s integrity, benevolence, and competence (Gefen et al. 2003; Mayer et al. 1995; McKnight et al. 2002). Integrity refers to the trustee’s honesty and adherence to keeping promises. Benevolence refers to the trustee’s sense of care and motivation to act in the trustor’s interests. Competence refers to the ability of the trustee to do what the trustor requires. As noted by Mayer et al. (1995), “each contributes a unique perceptual perspective from which to consider the trustee while the set provides a solid and parsimonious foundation for the empirical study of trust for another party” (p. 717). As such, it is essential to consider these three aspects of trust in trading partners.

Likewise, triadic commitment contains two dimensions: commitment to the MP and mutual partner commitment. Commitment to the MP is essentially a manifestation of commitment to a digital platform, a construct that contains two dimensions: continuance of commitment and affective commitment (Bateman et al. 2011; Malhotra and Galletta 2005). Continuance of commitment refers to the desire to persist in utilizing the MP based on the recognition that it delivers net benefits not readily available elsewhere. Affective commitment is the willingness to utilize the MP based on the congruence of values and positive affect. Commitment to the MP is conducive to maintaining a large community of both microsourcers and microsourcees; it is a community attitude that is critical for the sustaining an MP’s operations.

Mutual partner commitment consists of durability, input, and consistency (Goo et al. 2009; Kumar et al. 1995). Durability refers to the desire to continue a relationship based on positive affect toward the partner. Input reflects the willingness to be deeply involved in the relationship through the investment of capital and effort. Consistency represents each party’s confidence in the stability of the relationship. Commitment between participants is critical to reifying rule-binding transactional behavior and the idea of long-term business partnerships.

## Degree Symmetric Modeling

Table C1 presents the derivation formula for creating degree-symmetric scores (Klein et al. 2007; Straub et al. 2004; Wareham et al. 2005; Wu et al. 2015) for dyadic or k-adic data. Traditional monadic models consider either one side of the business relationship or the other— microsourcer or microsourcee. Symmetric models, on the other hand, incorporate the idea of balance. In measuring only symmetry in a business relationship, the exact degree, level, or value of the construct is lost. An example of this is situations where there is a low degree of trust that is still symmetric between partners. Degree-symmetric measures, however, capture both the balance and degree of the relationship. For instance, if a relationship’s symmetric value is only assessed, a microsourcer and microsourcee could conceivably scarcely trust each other, but still have a high symmetric value. In considering the benefits of cooperation within microsourcer-MP-microsourcee relationships, the current study focuses on an examination of both the symmetry and magnitude of perception in key relevant constructs, such as trust and commitment to the relationship.

Table C2 gives two examples of calculating degree-symmetric values. In relationship A, both microsourcer and microsourcee values are higher than average, i.e., they both demonstrate high trust in their relationship. Dividing microsourcer value, 0.80, by the microsourcee value, 0.80, the relationship’s symmetric value can be derived as 1.00. The average of the degree value and the symmetric value is then used to derive the degree-symmetric value according to the DSV formulation shown in Table C1.

Consider, by way of contrast, relationship B, in which both microsourcer and microsourcee values are lower than average, i.e., they show low trust in the relationship. The symmetric value is 0.75 and the degree-symmetric value is 0.55.

Consider relationship C, in which one value is high and one is low, i.e., only one party shows high trust in the relationship. The symmetric value is 0.375 and the degree value is 0.55; therefore, the degree-symmetric value is 0.46. In this case, the asymmetry of the partner value is incorporated into the calculation.

## Appendix E

## Data Collection Procedures

Data Collection from Microsourcees: We posted a job at Zbj.com asking microsourcees to participate in the study with a reward of 20 RMB per questionnaire (Figure E1). We then gathered a list of microsourcees willing to participate in the study. Our requirement for participation is that microsourcees need to have at least one previously completed IT-related transaction.

Data Collection from Microsourcers: After we confirmed the participation of the microsourcees, we contacted the microsourcers by going to the microsourcees’ homepages and randomly selecting from microsourcers who left feedback about the microsourcees’ service. The feedback system is prima facie evidence of sufficiently effective cooperation; in other words, it is evidence that the project was completed. The project feedback also documents that the transaction actually took place. Figure E2 shows a screenshot of a sample microsourcee’s storefront on Zbj.com. In all, we gathered 548 valid microsourcer-microsourcee dyads for the study, excluding non-paired data.
