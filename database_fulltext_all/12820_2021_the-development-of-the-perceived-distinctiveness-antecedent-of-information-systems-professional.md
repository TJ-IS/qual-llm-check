# THE DEVELOPMENT OF THE PERCEIVED DISTINCTIVENESS ANTECEDENT OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS PROFESSIONAL IDENTITY<sup>1</sup>

Cynthia K. Riemenschneider

Information Systems and Business Analytics Department, Hankamer School of Business Baylor University, Waco, TX, U.S.A. {c\_riemenschneider@baylor.edu}

Deborah J. Armstrong

Florida State University

Department of Business Analytics, Information Systems & Supply Chain, College of Business Florida State University, Tallahassee, FL, U.S.A. {djarmstrong@business.fsu.edu}

This study explored the professional identity of information systems (IS) workers and explicated the set of salient characteristics that comprise the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession. We developed a more complete picture of IS workers’ perceived distinctiveness, including its composition and outcomes. The perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession, in turn, contributes to individuals’ professional identity. We employed a mixed methods design (qualitative and quantitative) to leverage the strengths of each method. In Study 1, we analyzed transcripts of focus group interviews, using a robust qualitative method—revealed causal mapping. Utilizing the midrange theory that emerged from Study 1, we further explicated and empirically tested it with a quantitative field survey in Study 2. The meta-inference from these relationships can be stated as follows: The occurrence of change within the profession, the facets of knowledge needed, and the continuous refinement and adaptation of the knowledge base within a mentally demanding work context are what make the IS profession distinctive from other professions. Specifically, our findings indicate that the extent of change; the need for continuous learning; the use of creativity and logic to solve problems; the breadth of knowledge, skills, and abilities required; and the level of technology and business integration, time pressure, and stress compose the perceived distinctiveness of the IS professional. Future research might use our findings to incorporate elements of the IS profession into IS-specific theories.

Keywords: Professional identity, IS workforce, revealed causal mapping, mixed methods

## Introduction

The more organizations invest in information technology (IT) and the more this technology influences their business processes (Kretschmer 2012), the more information systems (IS) professionals are needed. Because of the inherent dynamics of the IT field, organizations have faced difficulty in retaining IS professionals. According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics,<sup>2</sup> the information industry for the month of August

2019 had 130k openings, 97k hires, and 98k separations (net - 1,000), while the business services industry had 1,319k openings, 1,155k hires, and 742 separations (net +413,000). For more industry comparisons see Appendix A, Table A1.

For more than 30 years IS research has relied heavily on a limited set of theories (e.g., organizational equilibrium, unfolding model of turnover, job embeddedness theory) and antecedents (e.g., job satisfaction, affective commitment, age, gender) borrowed from the management discipline to study turnover intention (Joseph et al. 2007; Lo 2015). After an exhaustive search of the literature, we located only one study (Brooks et al. 2015) that looked at the relationship between professional identity and turnaway from the IS profession. In their meta-analysis, Joseph et al. (2007) called for the use of fresh turnover perspectives that “can offer important and new understanding of the IT turnover phenomenon” (p. 564). We answered this call and filled a research gap by applying identity theory to the profession as an innovative and theoretically grounded approach to expand the body of knowledge on IT turnover intention.

This paper is grounded in the assumption that a deeper exploration of the IS professional identity construct could provide additional insights on the IS turnover intention phenomenon. IS professional identity captures how individuals define themselves as members of the IS profession, distinct from other professions, along with some level of value attached to this definition. Researchers have found that professional identity can influence individuals’ career choices (Skorikov and Vondracek 2011). Individuals who identify more strongly as an employee might view their career as situated within the organization and be less inclined to turnover. In contrast, individuals who identify more strongly as a professional might view their career as situated within the profession (across organizations) and be more inclined to turnover.

By enhancing our understanding of IS professional identity, we can expand our knowledge and toolset for addressing problems related to the demand for IT workers. We move beyond the basic individual attributes, job-related and perceived organizational factors that have been exhaustively studied to explicate the potential influence professional identity might have on IS turnover intention. Understanding how IS workers view their profession and themselves within the profession could aid organizations in their development of more finely tuned human-resource initiatives to recruit and retain high-value IS professionals.

Dobrow and Higgins (2005, p. 568) stated, “it is critical that researchers develop a solid understanding of identity, particularly professional identity, and the factors that influence its development.” Exploring the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession as an antecedent to IS professional identity could have theoretical and practical implications not only for research on turnover/retention, but also for research on performance, exhaustion, and social inclusion in IS. For example, questions such as how to increase the retention of key IS talent might be informed by focusing on enhancing (or diminishing) perceptions of distinctiveness. If IS personnel perceive that they are more distinctive from other organizational personnel, this could lead to increased IS professional identity and increased turnover intention. In contrast, if IS personnel perceive they are more like their organizational colleagues, it could lead to decreased IS professional identity and turnover intention. Thus, we are using IS professional identity to address turnover intention while trying to understand what makes IS professionals distinctive.

The objective of this study is to develop a more complete picture of IS workers’ perceived distinctiveness from other business professionals and its influence on their identity with respect to the IS profession. We used a sequential mixed methods approach incorporating a qualitative (Study 1) and a quantitative (Study 2) component because neither study alone could adequately address the research questions (Venkatesh et al. 2016). Referring to Table 1 from Venkatesh et al. (2013, p. 26), the purposes for applying mixed methods in this study are both developmental (questions from one study emerge from the inferences of a previous study) and compensatory (one method compensates for the weakness of another method).

The application of the mixed methods scientific approach to identify the dimensions of the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession, as well as proposing and testing a midrange theoretical model with a parallel sample to formulate metainferences, provides significant robustness to our research. Study 1 aimed at qualitatively developing midrange theory that addressed the research question: What components of the work environment do IS professionals perceive as distinctive to their profession? Study 2 used a quantitative survey to address the research question, “Does a multidimensional view of the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession influence IS professional identity”? Study 2 provided the opportunity to assess the significance of the characteristics composing the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession and test the influence of the perceived distinctiveness on IS professional identity.

Thus, this research explores the professional identity of IS workers and explicates the set of salient characteristics composing perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession. It contributes by developing a more complete picture of IS workers’ perceived distinctiveness, including its composition and outcomes. The perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession, in turn, contributes to individuals’ professional identity. In doing so, this research expands the IS workforce body of knowledge to address the societal issue of meeting the demand for an inclusive global IS workforce. The next section reviews identity theories and the IS literature on identity. The subsequent sections describe our mixed methods approach with Study 1 (exploratory study using interviews) and Study 2 (confirmatory study using surveys). A discussion of the findings, meta-inferences, and implications of this research, complete the study.

## Related Research

## Identity

Social identity theory emphasizes group processes and relations and posits that the groups to which people belong can provide their members with a definition of who they are (Hogg et al. 1995). One type of social identity, collective identity, “helps to create and preserve a system of meaning that binds people together” (Fayard and DeSanctis 2010, p. 384) and is based on group members’ convergent beliefs about the fundamental, enduring, and distinct attributes of the group (Hardy et al. 2005). Within the business environment, individuals might identify with their organization, profession, or job. Although a small number of studies have been conducted on IS work or job-related identity (e.g., Buche 2006), very little research has been published utilizing IS professional identity. Our research focuses on an individual’s professional identity (i.e., occupational identity; Magenau and Hunt 1989), which addresses how individuals define themselves as members of a profession along with some level of value attached to this definition. For example, do IS workers think of themselves primarily as IBMers and secondarily as members of the IS profession, or vice versa? If IS workers view themselves primarily as IBMers, they might suggest modifying an existing application to reduce costs. On the other hand, if IS workers view themselves primarily as IS professionals, they might prescribe developing a new application just to utilize a cutting-edge technology.

Articulating the unique characteristics of the profession is one way in which individuals can identify a profession’s domain (Abbott 1995). If individuals do not perceive that a group has some level of distinctiveness, they are less likely to identify with that group (McGuire 1984). Millward (1995) found that “the interpersonal nature of patient care … is what makes nurses distinct and marks them out from doctors in a particularly positive way” (Millward 1995, p. 312). Thus, we assert that to establish an IS professional identity, not only do individuals need to perceive that their profession is distinctive (Brooks et al. 2011), but there must be some central characteristics around which this perception is focused. Thus, we contend that a necessary (but not sufficient) condition for the development of an IS professional identity involves ascertaining the distinctive characteristics of the profession as perceived by individuals within the profession.

## Identity and IS Professionals

Although the concept of professional identity has been around since the 1970s, IS professional identity has received limited academic attention. Recently, researchers have explored the creation of a group identity for librarians facing a new digitized work environment (Boudreau et al. 2014) and for technology bloggers responding to new media developments (Vaast et al. 2013), and in examining how members of an online occupational community focused on banking responded to identity threats such as the “taint” from financial crises (Vaast and Levina 2015).

Although research has been conducted on IS work or jobrelated identity, which is a socially constructed, employmentbased self-concept that shapes the ways individuals behave when performing IS work (e.g., Buche 2006), as well as on IT identity, which captures the extent to which individuals view the use of an information technology as integral to their sense of self (Carter and Grover 2015), very little work has been done regarding the concept of IS professional identity. See Appendix A, Table A2 for a list of relevant work-related social identities.

In the few studies that directly focus on IS professional identity, findings indicate that the IS professional identity is malleable (Alvarez 2008), there is a strong sense of professional identity for workers within the IS field (e.g., Crepeau et al. 1992; Marks and Scholarios 2007), and IS professionals more strongly identify with their profession than with their organization (Chesebrough and Davis 1983; Tam et al. 2002), potentially leading to greater turnover (Johnson et al. 2006). Finally, Brooks et al. (2011) found that IS professionals identify with the profession based on their need to identify, their perceptions of the profession, their public perceptions of the profession, and their perceived similarity to others.

In our review of the literature, we found that the preponderance of researchers utilized Ashforth and Mael’s (1989) framework of organizational identity (Albert and Whetten 1985), which consists of five antecedents of identity (distinctiveness, prestige, categorization, salience of outgroups, and group formation factors). Validated measures could be adapted to the IS profession for all the antecedents except distinctiveness. We triangulated the measures used by Mael and Ashforth (1992) with those used in one of the few studies of professional identity in IS (Brooks et al. 2011). While informative, Brooks et al. (2011) measured the level of distinctiveness, not the characteristics of the IS profession that are perceived as distinctive.

To identify the set of characteristics that IS professionals perceive as distinct to the IS profession, we employed a mixed methods sequential approach. “Identity is formed and reformed by categorization through social interaction” (Hecht et al. 2005, p. 36). During such social interactions “there is an implication that a profession has an identity—that the professionals see themselves as part of a community of likeminded individuals” (Ford and Gibbs 1996, p. 5). In Study 1 we explored IS professionals’ distinctiveness, those characteristics that differentiate the IS function from other business functions, through focus group interviews.

## Study 1: Theory Building with Qualitative Data

We used a “soft positivism” approach (Kirsch 2004; Ravishankar et al. 2011) and the revealed causal mapping (RCM) data analysis method (Narayanan and Fahey 1990) for Study 1. Soft positivism is a “process designed to reveal preexisting phenomena and relationships among them” (Kirsch 2004, p. 378). This approach “allowed us to conduct the data analysis with certain expectations based on prior theory, while also allowing some unexpected findings and explanations to emerge from the data” (Ravishankar et al. 2011, p. 41). We were primarily engaged in induction (discovery) and thus sought to evoke emerging themes/categories through the perspective of our participants using a soft mode of analysis. The revealed causal mapping data analysis method represents causal assertions as networks of concepts (Axelrod 1976) and embodies the inductively derived theories of the participants (Dutton et al. 1983). The specific genre of qualitative research provides criteria for assessing the “internal consistency between the various elements of the qualitative study” (Sarker et al. 2018, p. 755). See Appendix B for a discussion of soft positivism, revealed causal mapping, and the genres of qualitative research.

## Study 1 Method

The data collection and analysis strategy for Study 1 included collection of data from multiple firms, trained researchers facilitating the focus group interviews, summary information providing validity of the transcripts, trained researchers coding the verbatim transcripts, and documentation of the coded concepts with illustrative participant quotes (Venkatesh et al. 2013). Specifics regarding the research design and data collection are provided in Appendix B.

For each study, we employed purposive sampling using parallel samples (the samples for each study are different but drawn from the same underlying population) and sequential (conducting one strand, then the other strand) mixed methods analysis (Venkatesh et al. 2013; 2016). For Study 1, we recruited participants from three firms within the United States at which the primary organizational output is not information. See Appendix B, Table B1 for details of the participating organizations. Our Study 1 sample was comprised of 45 IS workers, which is consistent with sample sizes in other qualitative studies (e.g., Gershon et al. 2004; Nelson et al. 2000). The demographics for the participants in Study 1 are provided in Appendix B. We used the focus group interview approach because it “provides the opportunity to study how individuals jointly construct and give meaning to phenomena” (Munday 2006, p. 45).

## Study 1 Data Analysis

Collective identity theory provides the general framework for this study because it focuses on the boundaries around and the content of a group-related identity. However, midrange theories are needed when focusing on a specific industry, group, or situation. Midrange theories have a limited scope (Gregor 2006), can be evoked through qualitative methods (Nelson et al. 2000), and are interpreted through the knowledge found in generalized theories (Narayanan and Fahey 1990). Specifically, we develop a midrange theory of the distinctiveness antecedent of IS professional identity and its relationship with IS professional identity.

A qualitative methodology that lends itself to the development of midrange theory is revealed causal mapping (RCM) (Narayanan and Fahey 1990). RCM has been used within the IS field to study topics such as IS development (e.g., Siau and Tan 2005; Tegarden and Sheetz 2001), project failure (Ackermann and Eden 2005), and IS workforce issues (e.g., Deng and Joshi 2016; Reid et al. 2010). Appendix C provides an explanation of the RCM method, a graphic of the RCM process used (Figure C1), and a glossary of key terms (Table C1).

## Study 1 Findings

Our goal was to gather contextualized knowledge and develop a midrange theory considering researcher boundaries (Carter and Grover 2015). We acknowledge that we do not have the interpretation of the data, but rather an interpretation grounded in our understanding of the literature and immersion in the data. Using Carter and Grover (2015) as an exemplar, we identified the definition, scope, and relationship elements considered in our conceptualization of the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession in Appendix D, Table D1. The dimensions of the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession identified in the relationships element of Table D1 are defined along with descriptive phrases from the participants in Table D2. Our task was to elicit the relevant concepts from what participants said regarding the distinct characteristics of the IS profession and place these concepts into a coherent representation scheme (see Figure D1). We center the discussion on the seven concepts that the focus group interview participants directly linked with the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession concept.

Like Luftman et al.’s (2017) formulation of alignment as a multidimensional construct, using the data from Study 1, we formulated the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession construct as a seven-dimensional (formative) construct. These dimensions are (1) extent of change, (2) continuous learning, (3) creativity and logic, (4) breadth of knowledge, skills, and abilities, (5) technology and business integration, (6) time pressure, and (7) stress. We used extant literature to support the findings from Study 1. While we employed a sound qualitative methodology and analysis for Study 1, when using a formative construct, the completeness of the dimensions is a concern (Podsakoff et al. 2012), and it is possible that additional dimensions of perceived distinctiveness were not revealed in Study 1. For a summary of the literature supporting each of the seven dimensions, see Appendix D, Table D3. The research model is shown in Appendix D, Figure D2, and a discussion of each dimension follows.

“Change and uncertainty have undoubtedly characterized the working climate for today’s IS professional” (Kaluzniacky 1999, p. 823). For the extent of change concept, participants talked about change in terms of the temporal aspect of change using phrases like “always,” and “nothing stays the same.” Others commented on the scale of change, with statements such as, “It’s the amount of change” or “things change at an accelerated rate.” The pace of change significantly challenges IS workers (e.g., Luftman and Kempaiah 2007), and the nature of change has evolved as IS professionals change through radical and disruptive transformation (Amis et al. 2004). We define the extent of change as the rate, pace, and amount of change that is experienced in the IS work environment. As one participant (MeatCo, Male 1<sup>3</sup>) stated: “So, for me, it’s the amount of change. If you look at the medical profession, you know, the needle I get a shot with looks the same as it did 20, 30, 40 years ago. But the tools in IT 20, 30 years ago, punch cards. It’s the amount of change and our ability to keep up with it that makes us unique.”

The continuous learning concept is focused on the need to learn and contains comments on the temporal aspect of this learning. Several participants made statements like: “That’s what I like about IT, just always learning new stuff” (WineCo, Female 6). It is well documented that IS personnel have to retrain and relearn as new technologies, methods, and techniques are introduced (e.g., Bettis and Hitt 1995), and our data support this finding. We define continuous learning as the requirement of updating one’s knowledge and skills and learning new tools and techniques on an ongoing basis. One participant (WineCo, Male 5) shared, “I’ve had this conversation with my wife several times over the past 17 years about when are you ever going to be trained? … And the answer is, because by the time I finish training, we’ve changed how we do things and what we used to do and I have to be trained on something else.”

Another dimension identified was creativity and logic. Participants discussed the idea that working in IS requires creativity, a certain level of creative problem-solving, as previously noted by Gallivan et al. (2004) and Stevens et al. (2011). Describing the need for creativity, one participant said, “If you’re not creative, you’re not finding those new solutions and you don’t have that curiosity to look for new ways to do things, then that’s not good” (MeatCo, Male 8). Working in IS also requires logical problem-solving skills, which were described by statements such as “Driving out the solution, the private detective-type work, is very interesting to me” (WineCo, Female 3), illustrating the need to logically solve new and emerging problems. We define the creativity and logic concept as focused on the need to approach tasks with a perspective of analytical idea generation and implementation for solving novel problems.

For more than 30 years, researchers have documented that IS professionals should possess a multifaceted skill set (e.g., Cheney and Lyons 1980; Stevens et al. 2011) and have suggested skills they should possess (e.g., Ferratt et al. 2005; Gallivan et al. 2004; Levina and Xin 2007; Niederman et al. 2016). From the literature and Study 1 data we define breadth of knowledge, skills, and abilities as a distinct set of focused qualifications that are necessary to be an effective IS professional. Referring to this connection, one participant (ShipCo, Female 3) stated: “We are unique people who are technical but yet can communicate, work with people well, and bringing those two together, and it’s very difficult to find that skill set.”

Study 1 findings indicate that participants perceived the need to have a broad understanding of the business domain (e.g., beverage distributor). They must also understand the small elements that collectively constitute the business area (e.g., pursuing a growth business strategy through rapid merger and acquisition integration). In addition, they must have a broad understanding of the available technologies (e.g., data-driven business solutions) and the detailed characteristics of the technology (e.g., how to integrate cloud services with mobile apps on multiple platforms). Consistent with research on the importance of business-IT alignment in the literature (e.g., Jahnke 2004; Luftman and Brier 1999; Tiwana 2012), the participants saw their role as being driven by the technology and the business domain. As one participant (FoodCo, Male 2) stated: “The one thing that we have to know beyond all the technical and all the logic we have in our brain—we have to know the business. We have to know the business almost as well as, and in some cases even better than, the people over in the business.” Triangulating the Study 1 data with the literature, we define technology and business integration as knowledge regarding how the technology and business domain fit together to meet the needs of the organization.

Study 1 participants were very cognizant of the time aspect within the IS work environment. A participant (TransCo, Female 8) stated: “So when people say there’s not enough time, there’s an expectation from the business on how fast you should deliver something without understanding the complexities involved and what it takes to deliver it right.” We define time pressure as the pressure experienced by individuals because of the temporal aspects within which IS professionals work. This pressure can be especially intense for IS professionals because they are often responsible for the success or failure of projects (e.g., Keil et al. 1998), while at the same time having unpredictable milestones and little influence over project due dates. For example, accounting professionals have recurring deadlines that engender predictable processes and schedules. In contrast, an IS professional who has been working on a project for weeks can be suddenly assigned a new project that takes precedence and must be completed immediately. Often, the IS professional has no control over the deadline or the reallocation of effort.

Our participants perceived that the high levels of stress that IS personnel must continually face makes them unique from other professionals. For example, as MeatCo Male 5 shared: “The nature of the project work is much different from people I know in other professions. You know, the stress is very cyclical. There is lots of stress, and the stress can come from a lot of different angles.” We define stress as the demands that the IS environment continually places on individuals, and the feeling that being an IS professional consumes all their time.

The perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession captures the characteristics of the IS work environment that differentiate the IS function from other functional areas. In line with previous research (Ashforth et al. 2013; Ashforth and Mael 1989; Brooks et al. 2011), we confirm the relationship between the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession and IS professional identity, and state the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis: The perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession positively influences IS professional identity.

## Study 2: Theory Testing with Quantitative Data

In Study 2 we used a quantitative approach to test the proposed model by investigating the confirmability of the Study 1 findings (Venkatesh et al. 2013).

## Study 2 Method

For the survey administration, we used Qualtrics, a third-party online survey administration and market research company. IS researchers (e.g., Kayhan and Bhattacherjee 2013; Smith 2011; Yang et al. 2011) have used this approach for data collection to facilitate the process of identifying a sample that is representative of the population, particularly when specific characteristics of the participants are required. The respondents came from a double-verified online research panel managed by the Qualtrics research team. The respondents were sent an email invitation with a link to the survey and were verified for their position within the IS field (e.g., database administrator, IS manager, software developer), age (18-65), and geo-location (U.S. only) to ensure the validity of results. To avoid a potential threat to validity (see Venkatesh et al. 2013, Table 5), the panel participants were incentivized for their time and were run through several checks to ensure the quality of responses (e.g., less than 5-minute completion time). A total of 271 responses were received by the researchers, and 25 were dropped because of illogical responses (e.g., years in IT > age) or outliers (observation greater than 3 standard deviations from the mean for that field; Hair et al. 2006), leaving a total of 246 individuals. A detailed description of the data cleansing process and demographics for the Study 2 participants are provided in Appendix E, Table E1. Appendix F provides details of the measures (Table F1) and the measurement development process utilized when there was no existing measure. Gender, age, education, tenure in the IS field, organization, and job were included as control variables.

## Study 2 Analysis and Results

We used a multistep process to evaluate the measurement model, including a higher-order construct using SmartPLS v2.0 (Ringle et al. 2005; Wetzels et al. 2009; Wright et al. 2012). PLS is well-suited for testing both reflective and formative constructs (Carter et al. 2014), and our sample size exceeded the criteria (presented in Appendix G). To illustrate the vigorous analysis of the quantitative data, we incorporated measures of internal and external validity and statistical validity in our analysis (see Venkatesh et al. 2013, Table 5). In the first stage, we examined the measurement model for proper psychometric properties, while in the second stage we focused on testing the structural model. We documented the statistical tests performed to validate our model in Appendix G. The results demonstrated that our measurement model met the rigorous standards expected in IS research.

The structural research model was tested in SmartPLS using the standard bootstrap resampling procedure (5,000 samples; 245 cases [n-1]) to assess the significance of the paths. Because the controls explained less than 10% of the variance in professional identity, and the full model explained 58% of the variance in IS professional identity, we conclude that the preponderance of the variance is explained by perceived distinctiveness, supporting our hypothesis. The details of the tests and graphical results are presented in Appendix H.

## Discussion and Implications

Recall that the overarching objective of this study was to develop a more complete picture of IS workers’ perceived distinctiveness from other business professionals, and the IS profession’s influence on IS workers’ identity with respect to the profession. A strength of this study emanates from the application of the mixed methods scientific approach to (1) identify the dimensions of the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession, (2) propose a midrange theoretical model, (3) test the midrange theoretical model with a parallel sample, and (4) formulate meta-inferences. The integrative efficacy, inference transferability, and integrative correspondence requirements of a mixed methods study were all met as delineated in Appendix I, Table I1. Applying a mixed methods approach allows researchers to explore, hypothesize, report findings, and infer holistically—both opening and closing the investigative loop.

Following the guidelines for mixed methods research, we present the findings from Study 1 and Study 2 and address the meta-inferences<sup>4</sup> that can be drawn from this analysis. According to Venkatesh et al. (2016, p. 449), “If the quantitative and qualitative methods lead to convergent results … then the integration may provide good arguments for the quality of the inferences.” Referring to Appendix I, Table I2, we address the meta-inference based on the findings of the two studies (i.e., strands). This metainference offers our first theoretical contribution; the inference quality is strong because of the consistent results across the studies. The meta-inference from these relationships can be stated as follows: The occurrence of change within the profession, the facets of knowledge needed, and the continuous refinement and adaptation of the knowledge base within a mentally demanding work context are what make the IS profession distinctive from other professions. Admittedly, IS is not the only profession with time pressure and stress. We argue that the group of these seven dimensions taken together is what individuals perceive makes working in the IS profession different from other professions. Regardless of whether IS is objectively unique, IS workers perceive that there are distinctive characteristics of their work environment that distinguish it from other fields. The current theory provides insight into occupation-specific distinctiveness perceptions as well as IS professional identity. Building on initial work on IS professional identity and the current study, other antecedents to IS professional identity worthy of exploration might include IS profession reputation and person-IS profession fit. Additional outcomes of IS professional identity to explore might include professional citizenship behaviors, turnover, exhaustion, and social inclusion.

## A Research Agenda to Study IS Professional Identity

It is beyond the scope of this study to develop an in-depth research agenda for all potential phenomena, but we developed illustrative, IS-specific, and theoretically grounded research agendas for retention/turnover and social inclusion. Questions such as how to increase the retention of key IS talent can be informed by focusing on enhancing (or diminishing) perceptions of the distinctiveness of the IS profession.

For example, Joseph et al. (2012; 2015) identified three job moves within the IT field: turnover to another IT job in a different firm, turnaway-within to the same firm and a non-IT job, and turnaway-between to a different firm and a non-IT job. As suggested in the introduction, if IS personnel perceive they are distinct from other organizational personnel, this might lead to increased IS professional identity and increased turnover intention. In contrast, if IS personnel perceive they are more like their organizational colleagues (i.e., less distinctive), it could lead to decreased IS professional identity and decreased turnover intention. Another possible outcome of decreased IS professional identity is increased turnaway intention. Although turnaway might not negatively influence the organization (e.g., turnaway-within), the loss of IS personnel to other areas of the organization could negatively affect the IT department. Future research could explore the retention of IS personnel at the intersection of the organization and the IS occupation.

When looking at turnover and IS professional identity, two theories seem especially suited to the context: conservation of resources (COR) theory and job embeddedness theory. COR theory (Hobfoll et al. 2018) could prove interesting by comparing COR theory components at the organization and profession levels to determine whether there are any spillover effects. Similarly, job embeddedness theory could be contextualized to the IS profession. Researchers could assess the embeddedness of IS professionals within their profession via links (e.g., IS professional identity), fit (e.g., person-IS profession fit), and sacrifice (e.g., need for professional identity), and the influence of these factors on turnover intention.

The second area of IS literature that this research is suited to is social inclusion. “In a society with strong occupational ties, the demarcation between members and outsiders, between us and them, is likely to be more pronounced and behaviorally more significant than in a more self-oriented society” (Burrage 1972, p. 246). For example, approaches to management have been found to be quite different based on one’s national culture (i.e., upbringing, background, and experiences); and managerial influence can shape the development of a person’s professional identity (Pratt et al. 2006). Many organizations are global, with IS professionals from all over the world collocated or remotely working together. With global organizations, not only are there potential technical differences to address (e.g., according to Apple, as of 10/28/2019, the iPhone XS eSIM technology was supported in only 45 countries worldwide<sup>5</sup>), but IS professionals might also have a steep learning curve regarding different societal norms and taboos.

The intersectionality of gender and ethnic identities and IS professional identity might provide opportunities to extend social inclusion research. We assert that Trauth’s (2002) individual differences theory of gender and IT provides a framework for this exploration. Trauth (2017) stated that sources of variation are subsumed under three constructs: individual identity, environmental influences, and individual influences. Looking at the intersectionality of an individual’s identities, perceived distinctiveness regarding the profession, and its influence on IS professional identity might provide additional insights regarding recruitment, persistence, and advancement in the IS field. Consider an African American man who has a high level of perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession. Would he experience a more positive relationship between IS professional identity and persistence in the IS field than a Caucasian woman? Or does the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession have more of an influence on the advancement outcome via IS professional identity for him? These questions offer a plethora of research opportunities for deeper exploration of the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession construct and IS professional identity coupled with issues of social inclusion.

Social capital theory (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998) suggests that to maintain social capital, an individual is likely to engage in exchanges (e.g., collaboration) with others (Adler and Kwon 2002). Considering the heavy focus on teamwork within the IS field, one possible research stream could focus on the team aspect of the profession using the salience of out-group and in-group formation factors as antecedents to IS professional identity along with the perceived distinctiveness. Future research on IS professional identity might examine differences resulting from factors such as job roles (e.g., technical vs. professional), the organization (e.g., public vs. private), and cultural influences (U.S. vs. Latin America). This might afford more nuanced insights into the perceived distinctiveness of subgroups of IS professionals as well as potential moderators of the relationship between perceived distinctiveness and IS professional identity.

## Managerial Implications

As previously stated, a meta-inference from our findings indicates that the occurrence of change within the profession, the facets of knowledge needed, and the continuous refinement and adaptation of the knowledge base within a mentally demanding work context are what make the IS profession distinctive. Considering this metainference, managers might want to explore occasions to offer IS professionals the opportunity to increase their knowledge of the business and how current technologies and future technology trends fit with business needs via training, job shadowing, and job rotation. By affording these opportunities to IS personnel, managers could capitalize on the aspects that IS employees perceive as making them distinctive. Stating a counterargument, managers could facilitate IS personnel in training business professionals on how to use technology to solve business problems (e.g., how SharePoint can aid the organization in terms of knowledge management) to increase understanding regarding the strategic integration of technology and the business domain. Managers could foster a win/win situation by allowing IS personnel to share their expertise and experience, while interacting with business professionals (potentially) decreases IS personnel professional distinctiveness, while cross-discipline training increases the knowledge base of the organization.

## Limitations and Future Research

It is important to acknowledge this study’s limitations. One limitation concerns the qualitative data collection. The companies included in the qualitative phase are not primarily technology companies but have IS professionals who support the business. We suspect that IS professionals not working at a technology company might see more distinctiveness in the profession. For example, if you are one of a few in the IT department versus one of a whole firm, it might be more obvious how your skills are distinctive. Future research could explore the boundaries of the construct within various work environments.<sup>6</sup> A limitation from Study 2 might be the gender composition of the sample. Our quantitative sample was 50% male, which differs from recent estimates of the demographic makeup of computing occupations in the U.S. at 74% male (NCWIT 2017). Future research might undertake a replication study with a sample that is closer to a 75/25 male/female representation.

## Conclusion

We used a mixed methods study to develop and test an occupation-specific theory of the dimensions of the perceived distinctiveness of the IS profession and the influence of this distinctiveness on IS professional identity. The metainference from these relationships can be stated as the occurrence of change within the profession, the facets of knowledge needed, and the continuous refinement and adaptation of the knowledge base within a mentally demanding work context are what make the IS profession distinctive from other professions. The theory developed and tested here could be used as a foundation for the exploration of the professional identity of IS personnel, and insights from this study could be used to develop targeted policies and programs related to turnover, retention, and social inclusion.

## Study 1 Methodological Details

The revealed causal mapping method coupled with a soft positivism approach (Kirsch 2004) aligns most closely to the realist interpretive case study genre (i.e., type of qualitative research) identified by Sarker et al. (2018a; 2018b). According to Sarker et al. (2018a, p. 755), each qualitative genre “carries a certain set of underlying philosophical and methodological assumptions, and consequently, specific guidelines, criteria, and references, which, when acknowledged by authors, provide an internal consistency to the study.” Sarker et al. (2018a) identified four key elements that underlay each genre. The first genre element is the conception and use of data. We used creative, focus group interviews (over individual interviews) to understand the participants’ feelings and thoughts, and thus viewed the data source as researcher provoked. The data was assumed to be the socially constructed reality (i.e., shared understanding) of the participants regarding their conceptualization of the IT field. The second genre element is the nature and role of theory. While we were informed by theory prior to the data collection, we developed midrange theory as a product of Study 1. The third genre element is the data analysis strategy. We used revealed causal mapping as our data analysis strategy. It is an inductive strategy as it “moves toward generating concepts and theories from the data” (Sarker et al. 2018a, p. 759). The fourth genre element is the nature of claims regarding findings. The findings of this research were used to develop “a plausible understanding of a poorly understood phenomenon” and generate “new concepts and novel insights” (Sarker et al. 2018a, p. 760).

For Study 1, we recruited participants from the three firms within the United States at which the primary organizational output is not information. Similar to LaPointe and Rivard (2005), the three firms were chosen because of their similarities and differences (see Table B1 for details of the participating organizations). The firms were chosen because they are similar in that they have in-house IS departments located at the corporate headquarters; they differ in terms of industry, geographic location, size, and organizational culture. As this phase of the research was exploratory in nature, and not organization specific (i.e., focused on the IS profession), these organizations provided participants that met the research criteria (i.e., IS professionals).

The primary contact was the chief information officer (CIO) at each company, who identified IS personnel within the respective organization. The CIOs’ endorsement was critical to enhancing employee participation, and we did not view their role in initially identifying our participants as problematic because we were focused on the general IS field rather than the employees’ current job situation. Participants were recruited via email invitation and an administrative person within the organization handled the invitation and scheduling process. We used a convenience expert sample (IS professionals), which was appropriate as the goal was to develop a depth of understanding around a specific phenomenon (Slaughter et al. 2006; Weber 1990).

Our sample was comprised of 45 IS workers (24 men and 21 women). Eight men and nine women participated from ShipCo; seven men and five women participated from MeatCo; and nine men and seven women participated from WineCo. The largest age group was 40-44 with 32% of the participants, and just over 68% of the participants were between the age of 35 and 50. In terms of home life, just over 70% were married, and the number of children ranged from 0 to 4 with a mean of 1.4. In terms of education and compensation, just under 87% had a bachelor’s or a graduate degree, and 62% had an annual salary between \$100k and \$150k. The participants mean organizational tenure was 9.0 years, and their tenure in the IS profession was 16.5 years. In addition, 93% of the participants had more years in the IS field than years in the company, indicating that the majority had worked in IS for more than one organization.

A professional identity reflects what is consensually held to be the social reality of the group (Postmes and Jetten 2006; van Zomeren et al. 2008). Believing that much of our social reality is both constructed and perpetuated through discourse (Berger and Luckmann 1966), using a soft positivist perspective and qualitative data collection (i.e., focus groups) and analysis we sought to elucidate the cognitive representations of individuals and the emergent cognitions evoked from group interaction. A focus group is “a group comprised of individuals with certain characteristics who focus discussions on a given issue or topic” (Anderson, 1990, p.241). The goal of focus group interviews is to collect high-quality data from a roughly homogeneous group in a more natural social context using the viewpoint of the participants (Patton, 2002).

We used the focus group approach (over individual interviews) because focus group interviews have high face validity, capture real-life data, are flexible (Babbie 1995), and provide a more relaxed atmosphere for participants than one-on-one interviews, thereby increasing the opportunity for spontaneous responses (Madriz 2000). At each organization, we conducted two focus group interviews during working hours in on-site conference rooms, for a total of six interviews. To build confidence in the findings and increase the likelihood of discovering new insights, all interviews were conducted by two researchers (Eisenhardt and Graebner 2007). An interview guide was developed to facilitate the semi-structured interview process in which the questions were open-ended and follow-up questions were included as needed.

## Interview Guide

Introduction protocol: Thank you for your willingness to participate in this focus group. We are researchers from \_\_\_ who are conducting a research project investigating the working conditions facing Information Technology professionals. For about 45 minutes we’ll be asking you to share your experiences working in IS. Your participation is voluntary. Anything you tell us will be kept confidential. Thank you in advance for your assistance.

1. Think back to why you left other IS positions in the past and what your colleagues told you when they decided to leave an organization. What caused you, your colleagues, or friends to decide to leave the organization?

2. Did they move to an IS job in another organization, or leave IS completely?

3. Did management provide a public explanation for why that person left?

4. If yes, ask informant to elaborate (e.g., who provided the explanation, did they announce it verbally or in writing, what reason did they give).

## Revealed Causal Mapping Process

First, we selected the source for the data (IS workers) and gathered the narratives emerging in the focus groups. We systematically examined the interview transcripts to identify the causal statements. A total of 219 causal statements were identified in the transcripts, which were separated into “cause” and “effect” phrases. During the data analysis process, the researchers developed a coding scheme inductively as the concepts emerged from the interview transcripts. Similar to Joshi and Kuhn (2007) and Todd et al. (1995), a phrase was the base unit of analysis to develop the coding scheme. While there is a growing body of literature regarding the IS workplace and IS-related skills, th absence of an established framework for the distinct aspects of the IS work environment presented an opportunity. Thus, the emergent coding scheme was not atheoretical, but grounded in theory and organic in nature (Strauss and Corbin 1998).

To develop the coding scheme, words that were frequently mentioned in the causal phrases were grouped together (Narayanan and Fahey 1990), and a word or word group was created to summarize the phrases. With the revealed causal mapping process, applicable statements include both cause and effect phrases. As the participants answered the question “what makes IS unique,” we include the concept labeled Perceived Distinctiveness of the IS Profession to capture the impetus for their responses. The researchers individually coded the phrases, discussed the concept labels and resolved discrepancies to reach 100% agreement. The six maps (one for each focus group) were aggregated (Axelrod 1976). Comparisons were made between the six maps and the findings confirmed a considerable level of concept overlap, with each concept appearing on an average of four maps (min = two, max = five).

In revealed causal mapping research, the point of redundancy, or saturation, among the subjects represents the point at which further data collection would not lead to the identification of additional concepts (Narayanan and Armstrong 2005) and determines the adequacy of the sample size (Axelrod 1976). The difficulty is that the point of redundancy is not calculated until after the interviews have been completed and the coding scheme has been developed. If redundancy is not reached, additional interviews are conducted. For this project, redundancy was reached at the fifth focus group, which suggests that the sample captured the relevant concepts.

Borrowing from social network analysis (Knoke and Kuklinski 1982) as well as classic research on RCM (e.g., Narayanan and Fahey 1990; Nelson et al. 2000) to interpret the map, we focus on the reachability. A reachability matrix indicates the direct and/or indirect effects of one concept on another (see Nelson et al. (2000) for the reachability matrix derivation) and is reported on the line between two concepts on the map. The concepts and relationships in the map are a result of using a 0.2 reachability cutoff value because it allowed a maximum inclusiveness of concepts within the constraint of map readability.

## Study 2 Measurement Development

We triangulated extant literature and our findings from Study 1 to develop an instrument to measure the characteristics perceived as distinctive to the IS profession. As we searched for existing scales, we compared the individual items with the phrases that emerged from the focus group interview participants to verify that the scales were representing the same underlying concept. Through this process, we noted that some of the aspects the participants addressed in Study 1 were not found in the IS literature (e.g., technology and business integration) and thus we needed to develop measures. We followed the procedures suggested by Schwab (2005) to develop measures and to make inference about the measures’ construct validity.

We began the measurement development process with the construct definition developed from the statements provided by the participants in Study 1. We then returned to the literature to review construct definitions and measurement scales. The literature was triangulated with the Study 1 interview data to develop the scale items. According to Mackenzie et al. (2011, p. 306), “we believe it is important to develop items that are conceptually understood by the population of interest.” We had two doctoral students with no relationship to the study review the items for clarity. We then conducted a card sort of the items with doctoral students (different from previous phase) who had IS work experience and no relationship with the study. We were guided to use doctoral students for this phase by Mackenzie et al. (2011, p. 306) who assert, “when selecting people to serve as raters, it is important to make sure that they have sufficient intellectual ability to rate the correspondence between items and the theoretical definitions.”

A pilot study was conducted prior to data collection to determine content validity. A total of 12 individuals with experience in the IS field completed the pilot instrument. The pilot study participants included IS professionals from the Study 1 data collection who were willing to take the survey and provide feedback. Since a significant duration had passed between the focus group interviews and quantitative data collection, and as the interviews covered more content than the survey, we felt these individuals would be appropriate respondents for a pilot study. Based upon feedback from the participants some of the positioning of items was changed and some typographical errors were corrected. The results of the pilot study provided sufficient confirmation to proceed with full data collection. The first round of data collection consisted of 230 IS professionals whose average age was 46.1 years, with 66.1% being male. The participants represented a variety of IS positions, and the average tenure in IS was just over 19 years. The scales presented acceptable psychometric properties—the Cronbach’s alpha for each construct was well above the recommended value of 0.70 (Hair et al. 2006) and each construct’s average variance extracted (AVE) exceeded 0.50 (Chin 1998; Fornell and Larcker 1981) indicating convergent validity; and each construct’s AVE was greater than the squared correlation between each pair of constructs in the model, indicating acceptable discriminant validity. After the first data collection, minor modifications were made to the survey, and four additional IS professionals in different industries and geographic locations reviewed the revised instrument. We incorporated their feedback and proceeded with further data collection (Round 2). Note that the round 1 data was used to establish the psychometric properties of the measures and was not included in the final sample. The details of each measurement scale are provided next, and Table F1 provides the constructs, items, stems, and sources of the measures used in this study.

The extent of change measure was developed by Cunningham (2006), who measured the degree of change using a three-item semantic differential scale. After an exhaustive exploration of the literature, we did not find an extant continuous learning scale that was appropriate for the IS context. Therefore, we developed five items to capture the construct. The creativity and logic measure was adapted to our context with six items based on Zhang and Bartol’s (2010) measure and the Study 1 findings. The breadth of knowledge, skills, and abilities (KSA) measure consisted of 11 items that were developed based on the findings of Lee and Fang (2008) and address the qualifications required within the IS profession. They provide four categories of KSAs—interpersonal, personal, IS, and organizational. We again followed the measurement development process detailed previously. For this measure, we used Lee and Fang’s (2008) statements from each category of KSAs to create the items. Three categories of KSAs (i.e., dimensions) emerged, which we label interpersonal (consisting of Lee and Fang’s interpersonal and personal categories), business/industry, and technology. The personal motivation item was removed from the interpersonal KSA category because it did not conceptually fit with the other items. This resulted in an 11-item scale.

The time pressure measure consisted of four developed items using the process identified above. The technology and business integration consisted of six developed items based on Preston and Karahanna’s (2009) measure of strategic business and IS knowledge which were adapted to our context. Stress was captured using six items from the Cohen et al. (1983) stress scale. After reviewing a wide range of extant literature, we identified the perceived distinctiveness five-item measure developed by Brooks et al. (2011) as a sound representation of an overall (i.e., reflective) measure. The professional identity measure consisted of eight items from Wen et al. (2016) which were adapted to our context.

## Second-Order Constructs

Perceived Distinctiveness of the IS Profession was modeled as a second-order formative construct formed by the seven first-order concepts (five reflective and two formative). To validate the Perceived Distinctiveness of the IS Profession construct, we used a five-stage process. In the first stage, we converted the breadth of knowledge, skills, and abilities second-order formative construct to a first-order formative construct (from Ringle et al. 2012, supplement, page 8, paragraph 2). To accomplish this, we ran the model with breadth of knowledge, skills, and abilities as a second-order formative construct. Next, we used the latent variable scores for the breadth of knowledge, skills, and abilities dimensions (Interpersonal, Business, and Technical) from step one as formative indicators of a first-order breadth of knowledge, skills, and abilities formative construct in the model.

In the second stage (from Lohmoller, 1989; Petter et al. 2007) we used the reused [repeated] indicators procedure to assess the model. This procedure utilized the constructs (e.g., change, stress, etc.) as dimensions of the Perceived Distinctiveness of the IS Profession in a formative second-order model. The items in each of the dimension constructs were also included as reflective indicators of the Perceived Distinctiveness of the IS Profession construct and were thus reused [repeated]. In accordance with guidelines for formative measurement (e.g., Wells et al. 2011), we considered the relative and absolute contributions of the first-order constructs. While the path weight (i.e., relative contribution) for stress was small (.072), the bivariate correlation between stress and Perceived Distinctiveness of the IS Profession demonstrates a stronger absolute relationship (.369, p<.01). Thus, we retained stress in our model.

In the third stage (Wells et al. 2011; Gefen and Straub 2005) we also ran the model using the Perceived Distinctiveness of the IS Profession as a first-order formative construct with the dimensions as indicators. This was accomplished by using the latent variable scores generated in PLS for the dimension indicators. The results were consistent with the repeated measures procedure with a sligh increase in the R<sup>2</sup> for professional identity (change in R<sup>2</sup> = 0.017, f<sup>2</sup> = 0.04).

Finally, we conducted a redundancy analysis (e.g., Wells et al. 2011). We used formative and reflective Perceived Distinctiveness of the IS Profession constructs in PLS and examined the strength of the relationship between them. The formative construct was measured using the latent variable scores for the first-order constructs as formative indicators. The reflective construct was measured using the five perceived distinctiveness reflective items (Brooks et al. 2011) from the data collection. The path weight of 0.720 between the two constructs suggests that the formative items provided good coverage of the Perceived Distinctiveness of the IS Profession. The results support our representation of the seven dimensions forming the Perceived Distinctiveness of the IS Profession.

## Study 2 Measurement Model Assessment

Before examining the path model, we evaluated general information (means, standard deviations, and correlations) about the model constructs in SPSS (see Table G1). The sample size required for analysis using PLS with this model was determined by multiple research guidelines. First, some researchers have suggested that the sample size should be calculated by multiplying the largest number of structural paths directed at a construct by 10 (e.g., Hair et al. 2011) or by 20 (e.g., Willaby et al. 2015). Using either of these criteria would suggest a sample size of 70 or 140 respectively. Second, Hair et al. (2014) suggests including the minimum R<sup>2</sup> value into the calculation and provide some suggested sample size values (see Exhibit 1.7 on page 21 of Hair et al. (2014)). Using this criterion (5% significance level with minimum R<sup>2</sup> of 0.10) would suggest a sample size of 166. Third, Kock and Hadaya (2018) suggest using the inverse square root method, in which the recommended sample size would be 142. Given that our sample consisted of 246 participants (which exceeds each of the four criteria presented), SmartPLS seemed an acceptable data analysis tool for the dataset. The results supported our representation of the seven dimensions forming the Perceived Distinctiveness of the IS Profession. The constructs explained 58.2% percent of the variance in the overall IS professional identity construct.

Data were analyzed for any violations of normality, skewness, and kurtosis; no significant violations were found. A factor analysis was performed to examine the factorial validity of the measures (see Table G2 for factor loadings). SPSS was used for the factor analysis, and a principal components analysis with Varimax rotation was employed, as suggested by Gefen and Straub (2005) and demonstrated in Rai et al.’s (2006) study. An item was retained if its loading was above 0.6 on the latent construct/dimension and below 0.4 on the other constructs/dimensions (Hair et al. 1998; 2006). We followed the approach outlined by Chin (1998b) for reflective measures to evaluate construct reliabilities, convergent and discriminant validity of the model. Evidence of reliability is presented when both the Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability are greater than 0.70 (Bagozzi and Yi 1988; Garver and Mentzer 1999). Cronbach’s alpha for each construct was well above the recommended value of 0.70 (Hair et al. 2006) and ranged from 0.87 (creativity and logic) to 0.94 (perceived distinctiveness). Composite reliability ranged from 0.91 (professional identity) to 0.95 (perceived distinctiveness). Each construct’s average variance extracted (AVE) exceeded 0.50 (Chin 1998b; Fornell and Larcker 1981) and ranged from 0.62 (professional identity) to 0.76 (time pressure), satisfying the requirement for convergent validity. See Table G2 for the composite reliability, AVE, and Cronbach’s alpha for each reflective construct.

To evaluate the discriminant validity of the constructs, we used the approach recommended by Fornell and Larcker (1981). The Fornell-Larcker criterion suggests that discriminant validity is shown when the square root of each AVE is larger than its correlation with any other latent constructs/dimensions (Chin 1998a; Fornell and Larcker 1981). Table G3 provides the data and indicates that the construct’s AVE is greater than the squared correlation between each pair of constructs in the model, indicating discriminant validity. Convergent validity is not required for evaluating the psychometric adequacy of formative construct indices (Jarvis et al. 2003), and composite reliabilities and Cronbach’s alpha are not assessed, because formative indicators are not necessarily internally consistent (Bollen 1984; Chin 1998b). Because the correlations between formative indicators can be positive, negative, or zero (Bollen 1984; Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001), reliability in an internal consistency sense is not meaningful for formative indicators (Bagozzi 1994; Hulland 1999).

Multicollinearity is an undesirable property in formative models because it causes estimation difficulties (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001) The variance inflation factor (VIF) assesses the degree of multicollinearity and is used to determine whether the formative indicators are highly correlated (Petter et al. 2007). A traditional rule of thumb posits that multicollinearity is a concern if the VIF is higher than 10; however, for formative measures, scholars suggest that VIF values greater than 3.3 indicate multicollinearity (Diamantopoulos and Siguaw 2006; Petter el al. 2007). In order to ensure there were no issues with multicollinearity, we calculated the VIF values for the formative constructs and found them to be well below the acceptable threshold of 10.0 (Neter et al. 1990) and the more stringent threshold of 3.3 (Diamantopoulos and Siguaw 2006); our values were 1.28 for extent of change, and 2.18 for breadth of knowledge, skills, and abilities. For a Durbin Watson statistic (d = 2.11) with seven regressors, a sample size of 246 and a p-value of 0.01 we found the values of the lower bound $( \mathrm { d } _ { \mathrm { L } , \mathrm { a } } = 1 . 6 0 )$ and upper bound (d<sub>U,α</sub> = 1.75) Since d > d<sub>L,α</sub> we conclude that the errors are not positively autocorrelated, and since $( 4 \mathrm { - d } ) > \mathrm { d } \mathrm { u } , \mathrm { a }$ we conclude that the errors are not negatively autocorrelated. This indicates that multicollinearity was not an issue for the two formative constructs.

“Common methods bias is the magnitude of the discrepancies between the observed and the true relationships between constructs that results from common methods variance” (Doty and Glick 1998, p. 36). To address potential common-method bias in the survey design, we included reverse-scored items and marker variables to reduce compliance problems (Lindell and Whitney 2001). We assessed the extent of common-method variance (CMV) in the data with three tests. First, we performed Harmon’s one factor test (Podsakoff and Organ 1986) by including all reflective items in a principal components factor analysis. The results revealed eight factors, with no single factor accounting for a majority of variance (i.e., the largest factor variance was 36.1%), suggesting no substantial CMV among the scales. We then followed the procedure recommended by Podsakoff et al. (2003), which specifies that, in addition to theoretical constructs, a common-method construct (that includes all the indicators) be used in the empirical research model. We assessed the variance explained by the common-method construct relative to the variance explained by the substantive constructs. As shown in Table G4, the average variance explained by the substantive constructs is 0.70, while the average variance explained by the common-method construct is 0.25. Finally, we performed a marker variable test as suggested by Lindell and Whitney (2001). We used two variables that should have no relationship with the substantive variables of interest: (1) satisfaction with car insurance company and (2) intention to take a long trip soon. Common method variance “is represented by the smallest observed positive correlation between a substantive variable and an a priori chosen ‘‘marker’’ variable that is believed to be theoretically unrelated to at least one substantive variable, but susceptible to the same causes of CMV … any observed correlation between the two cannot be due to a true relationship and, thus, must be due to something else the variables have in common (i.e., CMV)” (Richardson et al. 2009, p. 767). The smallest correlation was 0.016, suggesting no correction (i.e., removing shared variance) was needed (based on Jayachandran et al. 2005; Richardson et al. 2009). Taken together, the results indicate that common method bias was not a serious concern in this data.

## Study 2 Structural Model Assessment

Prior to evaluating the model hypotheses, we examined the significance of the control variables. We used a two-step approach in which we first ran our model with only the control variables. We then re-ran the model with all the theoretical variables and computed a pseudo F-test (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006; Chin et al. 2003; Mathieson et al. 2001) to test the significance of the change in $\mathbf { R } ^ { 2 } .$ . This will show the variance explained beyond the control variables and whether the change in $\mathbf { R } ^ { 2 }$ is significant. In our analysis we found that using the model with just control variables only gender $( \beta = 0 . 2 0 8 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and organizational tenure $( \beta = 0 . 2 8 4 , p < \dot { 0 } . 0 1 )$ ) were significant predictors of professional identity $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } \mathrm { \ v a l u e } = 0 . 0 6 6 )$ . This suggested that men identify more strongly with the profession, and that perhaps individuals who have been with a particular organization longer have, over time, found the IS work environment more representative of their identity.

The $\mathbf { R } ^ { 2 }$ value with the control variables only was 0.066, the $\mathbf { R } ^ { 2 }$ value for the full model was 0.582. The resulting change in $\mathbf { R } ^ { 2 }$ is 0.516. The effect size $( \hat { f } ^ { 2 } = [ { \bf R } ^ { 2 }$ full model – R<sup>2</sup> controls only] $/ \left[ 1 - { \bf R } ^ { 2 } \right.$ controls only]) indicated a value of 0.55 $( f ^ { 2 } = [ 0 . 5 8 \bar { 2 } - 0 . 0 \bar { 6 } 6 ] / \left[ 1 \bar { . } . 0 6 6 \right] = 0 . 5 5 ) .$ Effect sizes have been documented as small (0.02), medium (0.15), and large (0.35) (per Cohen 1988). To test the significance of $\cdot f ^ { 2 }$ (pseudo F-test) we multiplied f<sup>2</sup> by (n-k-1) where n = sample size, k = number of independent variables and the degrees of freedom = 1, n-k (Mathieson et al. 2001, p. 104). This produced a pseudo F-test of 134.87(1, 245). Thus, the effect of the distinctiveness of the IS profession construct on professional identity was large (0.55) and highly significant $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). As the controls explain less than 10% of the variance in professional identity, the change in $\mathbf { R } ^ { 2 }$ is large, and effect size of the change in R<sup>2</sup> is large, we conclude that the preponderance of the variance is explained by the constructs in the model. The results can be seen graphically in Figure H1.
