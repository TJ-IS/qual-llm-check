## 1 Introduction

The opening quote is from an Instacart worker explaining why she continued working for the food/grocery delivery platform Instacart during the global COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. It is indicative of the advances in digital and network technologies (cf. Brynjolffson & Kahin, 2002) that have given rise to a gig economy where technologyenabled digital platforms such as Instacart, Postmates, Lyft, Uber, and Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) have transformed how work is organized and performed in many service industries. Referred to as digital labor platforms (DLPs), these platforms serve as intermediaries that broker on-demand services using a dispersed workforce of millions of individuals and facilitate transactions between workers and their clients. On these DLPs, a new kind of structure replaces the kind of “fixed”

employer-employee relationships common in “traditional” organizations (Gandini, 2019; Heeks, 2017). Individuals engaging in work on these platforms are considered independent contractors; they are often referred to as crowdworkers or gig workers due to the new work form of crowdsourcing and the nature of the piecework performed by these workers.

Worker experiences and working conditions on DLPs are being debated within the academic community and among industry practitioners. On the one hand, a new structure such as this affords workers with work autonomy and flexibility (e.g., Deng & Joshi, 2016; Kuhn & Maleki, 2015). On the other hand, gig work is generally considered precarious because it offers less of a safety net than more traditional forms of work (Chen, 2018), shifting work-related risks to workers: As platforms do not provide such benefits as training, health insurance, and retirement plans, operational risks tend to be shifted to workers (Bajwa et al., 2018; Weil, 2014). Thus, it is not surprising that concerns about the working conditions on DLPs are rising, including the instability of income and absence of employee benefits. With these increasing concerns concerning DLPs, it is important for both academics and policy makers to gain a richer understanding of working conditions and worker experiences. With this in mind, we offer some insights into such risks in this article.

Notwithstanding the many calls over recent decades for information systems (IS) researchers to devote more attention to studies that have real impacts on societal issues (e.g., Davison & Bjørn‐Andersen, 2019; Desouza et al., 2006), there are limited examples of research concerning the impacts of digitization on work-related issues concerning risk. The focus tends to be more closely related to the influences of technologies on professional identities and organizational transformation (e.g., Baptista et al., 2020; Stein et al., 2013). IS researchers have examined the construction of various preferred identities by professionals through the use and influence of information technology (IT) artifacts in organizational workplaces (Stein et al., 2013). More recently, a study of worker voicing behaviors in the microtask crowdworking environment showed that gig workers often attempt to report their gig work-related problems and make suggestions to three different recipients (job requester, platform, and worker community), but, unfortunately, their voices are not always heard, depending on the recipients (Deng et al., 2023). Despite media interest and increasing research in other disciplines (e.g., management, labor relations, organization science, sociology), IS research on gig work risks is still relatively scarce, possibly due to the recent development of the gig economy’s current digital form (Kane & Josserand, 2019).

Digital labor platforms vary in the degree to which work elements are digitized (e.g., digitization of tasks, assets, governance, and support services), and such differences delineate the boundaries of gig work in time and space (Joshi et al., 2022). In this paper, we examine workers’ perceptions of gig work and work-related risks. While prior research has paid increasing attention to platforms’ attempts to control labor, develop markets, and engage workers as contractors, little work has been devoted to the exercise of labor agency. This study compares workers’ risk perceptions and coping behaviors on multiple platforms. In doing so, we respond to the calls for further research on gig work across platforms (Heeks, 2017; Kane & Josserand, 2019) and on labor agency in the various forms of gig work (Barratt et al., 2020). In particular, this paper aims to answer two questions:

RQ1: What risks do workers perceive when engaging in different types of gig work?

## RQ2: How is labor agency exercised on different digital labor platforms?

To provide some insight into these questions, we conducted a comparative qualitive case study and focused on gig workers in the US participating on three different types of platforms: delivery, ridesharing, and microtask crowdworking. We use the term “gig workers” because of our focus on the DLP work context and on the human interactions with digital technologies in work processes. We define gig workers as individuals who use an app (such as Uber, Instacart) or a website (such as Amazon

Mechanical Turk) to match themselves with clients in order to provide services in return for piece-rate payment, consistent with the definition by Rosenblat and Stark (2016). In terms of risk, prior IS research has examined how IT might support (and often automate) and streamline key business processes and customer requirements, with a view to improving efficiency and effectiveness, and cutting costs, which involved quite some risk (Galliers, 1997). The research predates the gig economy but starts us on the path of thinking about risk in more general terms regarding IT and change. In this study, we define risk broadly as “a situation or event where something of human value (including humans themselves) has been put at stake and where the outcome is uncertain” (Rosa, 1998, p. 28), consistent with prior gig economy research (Bieber & Moggia, 2021).

## 2 Literature Review

## 2.1 Digital Labor Platforms

Digital labor platforms such as Uber, Instacart, and MTurk have transformed how work is organized, assigned, and executed. The platforms connect and match buyers (clients) and sellers (workers), thus playing the role of market intermediary (Harmon & Silberman, 2019). Depending on the location requirements for work (or services), DLPs enable both digitized work and location-specific work. Some platforms, such as MTurk and Upwork, enable the transaction of digitized work, including microtasks (Deng & Joshi, 2016) and IT freelance work (Taylor & Joshi, 2018). Meanwhile, other platforms facilitate the transactions of location-specific work, including ridesharing through Uber and Lyft and food/grocery delivery via Instacart and DoorDash. Because of their role in the online matching of the supply and demand for labor, these platforms are often viewed as online labor markets by IS researchers who study the platforms from an economics perspective regarding, for example, pricing (Chen & Horton, 2016), auction processes (Hong et al., 2016), and the effect on local entrepreneurial activity (Burtch et al., 2018).

Prior IS research has also examined such platforms from the perspective of organizing work. For example, the platforms are viewed as technology-enabled metaorganizations that resemble both a market and an organization: They serve as a market when the app performs market functions such as matching supply and demand; they serve as an organization when the app exerts managerial control on gig workers (Möhlmann et al., 2020). In other words, DLPs are viewed as organizations because the platforms monitor and control individuals who register with a platform to undertake work and provide services for compensation by relying on machine-learning algorithms (Newell & Marabelli, 2015; Rosenblat & Stark, 2016). From a worker perspective, the platforms provide workers with a technology-enabled work environment that can afford considerable job autonomy and work flexibility (e.g., Deng & Joshi, 2016; Kuhn & Maleki, 2015). However, the platforms also create opportunities for power asymmetry (Kingsley et al., 2015) and worker exploitation (Deng et al., 2016). In this regard, IS researchers have paid increasing attention to the role of technology in such work and the implementation of algorithmic management by such platforms.

Studies of such DLPs have examined the role of technology in the platform governance of work processes by focusing on two domains: algorithmic management and platform design. For example, a study of Uber drivers’ perceptions revealed that DLPs such as Uber employ algorithms for two purposes—matching and control—to optimize matching and meet market needs. In this case, the dual purposes of algorithmic management have led to workers experiencing tensions relating to execution, compensation, and belonging (Möhlmann et al., 2020). In addition to the algorithm embedded in the work processes, the platform design (e.g., MTurk) has been found to generate a greater amount of market power for clients (requesters who post tasks) than for the individuals performing tasks for payment (crowdworkers), who are generally subject to uncompetitive wage posting and incomplete information (Kingsley et al., 2015). The power of the platform in terms of its design (both technical and processual) affects workers’ perception of workplace fairness (Fieseler et al., 2019). Based on a qualitative survey of 203 US workers active on MTurk, Fieseler et al. (2019) found that biases embedded in the institutional setting impacted the platform; the triadic relationship between clients, workers, and platform providers; and the power of platform providers to design settings and processes that affect workers’ fairness perceptions. In addition to the platform’s institutional setting, the interactions between workers and platforms are also affected by government regulations—direct regulation implies more mutual dependence, while indirect regulation accentuates the power imbalance between the platform and its workers (Karanovic et al., 2021). Complex interdependencies have been shown to arise in the interactions of the multiple participants (clients, workers, platform administrators) on the various DLPs. Bearing these prior studies in mind— particularly those by Bellesia et al. (2019), Fieseler et al. (2019), and Kuhn and Maleki (2015)—in this study, we focus on the experiences and behaviors of workers on these different platforms.

Gig work varies in terms of its services and the associated work-related risks. Prior research (e.g., Graham & Anwar, 2018; Heeks, 2017) distinguishes between two types of gig work: One is related to “remote work” or “click work” and the other to “placebased work” or “location-based work.” As these terms imply, the former is digitized work that is not geographically constrained (i.e., software coding and graphic design via platforms such as Upwork or transcription and writing via MTurk), while the latter is geographically specific and performed locally by workers but may be less digitally intensive, such as the work on ridesharing platforms like Uber and Lyft or delivery platforms such as Instacart and Postmates. Participants in both types of gig work are expected to invest in their work operations—for example, by equipping themselves with the tools and devices needed to perform the work. On place-based work platforms for ridesharing and delivery, workers provide the required transportation tools or equipment (i.e., car, fuel, GPS, mobile app) to transport passengers or deliver meals and groceries. Similarly, on remote-work platforms (MTurk, Upwork), workers are expected to provide their own computing devices (i.e., a computer, internet connection). Since the gig economy encompasses a broad range of services, workers on different types of platforms are likely to experience different types of risks and perform their gig work differently. In this regard, prior studies have called for further research comparing place-based and remote gig work—in particular, regarding their “commonalities and differences” (Heeks, 2017, p. 44). A key theme of the extant gig work literature concerns the working conditions of gig workers, including contract, risk, income, regulation, and resistance (Kane & Josserand, 2019). Given our focus on work-related risks and worker behaviors, we focus our literature review on two themes: gig working conditions and the risk perceptions of workers.

## 2.2 Working Conditions and Perceived Risks on Digital Labor Platforms

Research on gig work has examined concerns related to the working conditions on DLPs and has shown that such concerns fall into five categories: earnings, work process, working hours, health, and safety. First, gig workers often complain about their earnings from gig work (e.g., low, unfair rates of pay compared to effort) and frequently report earning less than minimum wage (Deng et al., 2016). This is of growing concern, as an increasing portion of gig workers consider their gig work to be their full-time employment. For example, Margaryan (2016) reported that 25% of gig workers are working more than 40 hours per week. However, even though gig workers report long working hours, some of those hours are idle time when workers are not generating income (Carmody & Fortuin, 2019). Additionally, it has been noted that workers in certain geographical areas may be disadvantaged. For example, on the Upwork platform, workers located in Asia are at a disadvantage because bidding begins when a new job is posted—which is frequently during the early hours of the morning in North America (D’Cruz & Noronha 2016; Martin et al., 2016).

Moreover, gig workers have increasingly raised concerns about work processes on digital platforms, including a lack of communication and a lack of channels for conflict resolution in the assignment and performance of gig work. For example, MTurk workers have reported a lack of information and poor communication with those posting jobs (job requesters) (e.g., Bergvall-Kareborn & Howcroft, 2014). In addition, gig workers are increasingly reporting problems associated with the overall organization of gig work, including complaints about the lack of reasons for work rejection by job requesters, the suspension or termination of a worker’s account by platforms, and the lack of transparency in how to obtain a more preferable worker status on a platform (e.g., Deng et al., 2016; Martin et al., 2016). In these circumstances, procedural injustice has emerged as a key problem in the gig work process.

Prior studies have also examined the impact of gig work on workers’ experiences. Gig work is commonly viewed as precarious not only by those in academia but by practitioners as well. Prior research has revealed risks to workers in different contexts. Two commonly reported risks are financial risk and physical health risk. Financial risk, also called “income risk,” is associated with the unpredictable income from gig work due to the absence of a safety net (Chen, 2018; Doucette & Brandford, 2019). Uncertainty in income generation comes from different factors. For example, studies of crowdwork on MTurk and Upwork have shown that workers do not have a guaranteed weekly or monthly income: Their capacity to earn money depends on their platformspecific work history and reputation (Deng et al., 2016; Taylor & Joshi, 2018). Additional causes of financial risk are uncertain project frequency and uncertain work hours (Gandhi et al., 2018).

Another common risk is the physical health risk arising from performing gig work. Place-based work, such as delivery and transportation work, exposes workers to risks related to driving safety, poor weather conditions, and poorly maintained roads. Christie and Ward (2019) explored the road safety risks that workers in placebased work are exposed to, revealing that drivers experience fatigue, pressure to violate traffic regulations, and distraction from their phones, all of which are major risk factors for vehicle collisions and bodily harm. These factors are intrinsic to the piecework nature of gig work and often result from the self-induced pressure to earn more income. Mitigating such risk factors requires removing pressure from the work design and offering protections such as a guaranteed minimum wage and a social safety net (Bieber & Moggia, 2021).

In addition, the crowdsourcing model of gig work defines workers as independent contractors and forces them to bear the risks of the business operation. In this regard, two risks—employment risk and entrepreneurial risk—are inherent to gig work. An example of employment risk is the lack of employment benefits (such as health insurance, retirement benefits). To avoid employmentrelated costs such as health insurance, retirement benefits, and professional training, platforms make contractual arrangements that shift these costs onto workers (Bajwa et al., 2018; Cherry, 2016; Fox et al., 2018). Grocery and food delivery platforms such as Instacart and Postmates provide access to a scalable local workforce while avoiding the costs and risks associated with direct employment (Coe et al., 2007). Workers are also exposed to entrepreneurial risk—the risks arising in the operation of a business, such as the need to invest in the necessary tools and equipment (i.e., car, bicycle, computer) (Stewart & Stanford, 2017). In traditional employment, this type of risk is referred to as a business risk because it is a risk assumed by the business (Rosa, 1998). However, since platform workers are typically considered to be independent contractors, they become personally liable for such operational risks. Therefore, business operation risks are shifted from the employer to the worker (Bajwa et al., 2018). Table 1 summarizes the gig work risks identified in prior research.

As discussed above, various studies have addressed concerns emerging from the working conditions on platforms but few have further investigated the problems arising from these working conditions or compared and contrasted worker behaviors across different DLPs (Heeks, 2017; Kane & Josserand, 2019). Such problems may not only cause job dissatisfaction and stress but may also lead to suboptimal task performance. Thus, it is important for platforms and clients to understand the problems that gig workers face as well as their coping strategies. To address this research gap, this study examines the risks that gig workers are exposed to across different DLPs and investigates if and how the workers on these different platforms cope differently. To inform our study, we draw on labor agency theory.

## 2.3 Labor Agency

Labor agency is defined as “both the intention and practice of taking action for one’s own self-interests and the interests of others” (Rogaly, 2009, p. 1975). It is expressed in response to production relations under capitalism. Labor agency can be conceptualized in different ways, including classifications based on intended outcomes (Katz, 2004) and institutional constraints (Cumbers et al., 2016). Consistent with prior studies on gig workers (Anwar & Graham, 2020; Barratt et al., 2020), we use Katz’s (2004) classification of resistance, reworking, and resilience as a starting point for categorizing labor agency actions. Resistance describes attempts by labor to transcend capitalist social relations (Cumbers et al., 2016). Thus, workers’ direct and indirect confrontations with their clients can be seen as resistance, even when confronting unfair practices in the work system. For example, on the remote-work platform Upwork, workers have exhibited individual acts of resistance, such as filtering clients and jobs as well as leaving negative feedback about clients (Anwar & Graham, 2020).

Reworking involves workers’ attempts to improve their working conditions, both at and outside of work, while operating within existing social relations (Coe & Jordhus-Lier, 2011). Workers can attempt to increase their income by maximizing their access to work, thus demonstrating reworking agency. For example, a study on gig work on food delivery platforms in Australia (e.g., Deliveroo and Uber Eats) found that workers often work on multiple platforms simultaneously or log in to a single platform using multiple accounts simultaneously (Barratt et al., 2020). This limited, individual, form of reworking can increase workers access to work without challenging the work assignment processes on the platform.

Resilience consists of small acts of coping under often adverse socioeconomic circumstances (Warren, 2019). While these acts may be necessary for workers to sustain themselves, they can also reinforce the trajectory of the capitalist sociospatial relations that created the adverse circumstances in the first place (Katz, 2004; Warren, 2019). One such resilient act is the entrepreneurial behavior of workers: the willingness to invest in upgrading equipment (e.g., upgrading their mode of transport from bicycle to scooter) so that they can work longer and faster, thus enhancing their earning capacity (Barratt et al., 2020). This individual act of resilience provides benefits to the platform. For food delivery platforms, such asset upgrades have been found to sustain the platforms’ ability to acquire more markets by increasing the reliability and speed of deliveries while expanding the viable range between restaurants and customers and enticing workers to work for longer time periods (Barratt et al., 2020).

Prior research has considered collective as well as individual expressions of labor agency. In traditional employer-employee relations, collective agency arises in the form of actions taken by trade unions (Coe & Jordhus-Lier, 2011). Yet with declining union membership and influence, union actions now represent an increasingly minor proportion of collective agency (especially in the US).<sup>1</sup> As such, collective agency needs to be considered more widely (Barrett et al., 2020). In this regard, scholars have suggested additional factors that can influence workers’ agency. These factors include gender, age, class, migrant status, and social networks (Carswell & De Neve, 2013). While frequently invoked, labor agency remains an understudied topic in empirical research (Coe & Jordhus-Lier, 2011). There have been calls to delineate the difference between the agency potential of deliberate acts and those of routine daily worker activities, as both have the capacity to shape the economic geography (Agar & Bohm, 2018; Jordhus-Lier et al., 2018).

On digital labor platforms, workers have demonstrated workaround solutions to resolve work-related problems. For example, to cope with the financial risk, gig workers make themselves “available” by creating an account on a remote (e.g., MTurk, Upwork) or place-based (e.g., Instacart, Postmates, Uber, Lyft) platform to indicate their willingness to sell their labor. Due to workers’ contractor classification, platforms do not compensate workers for their idle time. If idle time becomes too high, workers could, in theory, restrict their availability or exit the online labor market. In reality, however, worker choice is limited, making such exit behavior uncommon. For example, in Barratt et al.’s (2020) study, the labor market in Australia is characterized by persistent under- and unemployment, growing precarity, and the increasing availability of temporary migrant labor, making gig worker exit strategies less likely. There is a need for further research on labor agency in the various forms of gig work (Barratt et al., 2020). Similarly, in the US, unemployment is an ongoing concern that was exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic (Schwartz, 2020). It is within this economic context that we explore how workers in the US cope with gig work-related risks in multiple types of app-based gig work.

## 3 Research Method

## 3.1 Research Design

Prior research has called for a considered approach to adopting appropriate methodologies in conducting IS research (e.g., Galliers, 1992; Galliers & Land, 1987). In this study, we chose a comparative qualitative casestudy research design (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007; Yin, 2014) because we study worker behaviors on multiple digital platforms and thus need to delve more deeply into workers’ perceptions of risk in different contexts. Interpretive IS researchers (e.g., Walsham, 1993) and, more broadly, qualitative approaches (e.g., Kaplan, 2004; Myers & Avison, 2002; and, most recently, Simeonova & Galliers, 2023) have argued that in the digital age, qualitative approaches need to be used alongside quantitative methods, given the need to provide critical reflection on the unquestioned outcomes that can arise from, for example, algorithmic decision-making.

For remote gig work, we chose the microtask crowdsourcing platform, Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk), because it is one of the popular microtask crowdsourcing platforms open to individuals with different levels of skills to perform digital work remotely. For place-based gig work, we selected ridesharing (Uber, Lyft) and grocery/food delivery (e.g., Instacart, Postmates) because such gig work requires physical labor and is a location-based service. Our choice of the three different types of DLPs (delivery, ridesharing, and microtask crowdworking) allowed us to observe a variety of lived experiences across different platforms. Our research design of studying three platforms responds to the editorial call to pay attention to a variety of digital capabilities across platforms and to recognize that such variability may cause differences in worker perceptions and their adaptation to technology use (Joshi et al., 2022).

In line with our earlier comments, we believe that an interpretivist philosophy is required for our research purposes in terms of understanding how groups of platform workers adopt and adapt to the use of digital platforms in their daily work routines (cf. Nandakumar & Jones, 1997).

## 3.2 Data Collection

We collected narratives of workers through semistructured surveys between June and December 2020 in accordance with the guidelines of the first author’s university Institutional Review Board (IRB). Narratives provided study participants with a channel to reflect on their own lived experiences and make sense of their own behaviors to imbue their experiences with meaning (Cinque et al., 2021). Our data collection included 102 semi-structured surveys with gig workers located in California. The 102 workers were recruited from three types of DLPs, including 38 food/grocery delivery couriers, 31 ridesharing drivers, and 33 MTurk microtask workers.

Qualitative data were collected from open-ended questions in online surveys (cf. Myers, 1997). In these semi-structured surveys, we asked respondents about their gig work experiences (both satisfying and frustrating), motivation to engage in gig work, working conditions, perception of work risks and coping strategies. Informed by prior gig work studies (e.g., Deng & Joshi, 2016; Rosenblat & Stark, 2016), we asked the following openended questions: “Why did you decide to work on this app/platform?” “What is your most satisfying experience on the app/platform?” “What is your most frustrating experience on the app/platform? How did you handle it?” “In your opinion, what potential risks and concerns are individuals exposed to while working on (through) the app?” and “How are you dealing with the potential risks and concerns you have on the job due to COVID-19?” The respondents were asked to provide one or more examples to support their responses.

For place-based work experiences, we collected narratives from gig workers participating on ridesharing platforms (Uber and Lyft) and food/grocery delivery platforms (Instacart, Postmates, and DoorDash, etc.) between June and October 2020. We posted an ad targeted at Facebook groups for gig workers located in California. To encourage gig workers to share their experiences, we offered them the opportunity to be entered into a raffle for a \$50 Amazon gift card as a reward for completing the survey. On average, completing the survey took 18 minutes.

For remote work experiences, we collected data from a sample of MTurk workers in December 2020. Since MTurk is the largest platform for microtask crowdsourcing, it provides an appropriate site for collecting rich text narratives regarding individuals participation and experiences. Workers log on to the platform to view and accept microtasks (called HITs) and receive pay for completed HITs. The platform records each approval or rejection in the worker’s performance history and uses the metrics as a basis for determining the worker’s eligibility for its Master status designation. For our data collection, we set up a survey HIT and specified participants must have a total HIT approval rate of greater than 80%, be a US high school graduate, have a HIT approval number of greater than 500, and be located in California. With an average completion time of 20 minutes, each participant received \$5 in compensation, the equivalent of approximately \$15/per hour.

A total of 102 gig workers completed the survey: 38 (37.3%) for food/grocery delivery, 31 (30.4%) for ridesharing, and 33 (32.4%) for microtask crowdworking. The majority of delivery workers (84.2%) were female, while the majority of Uber/Lyft drivers (74.2%) were male. Among the participating

MTurk workers, the proportion of males and females was relatively even: 51.5% males and 48.5% females (see Appendix Table A1).

Almost half of the platform workers (49%) in the sample had no college degree. However, the proportion of high school graduates with at least some college education varied among the three groups of gig workers: 27.3% (microtask), 63.2% (food/grocery delivery), and 54.8% (ridesharing) respectively. The microtask workers were mostly White/Caucasian (87.9%) and were employed full-time elsewhere (69.7%), whereas workers on the delivery and ridesharing platforms were more diverse in terms of ethnicity and employment status. The demographic distribution of the workers shows some differences among gig workers across the three types of platforms (see Appendix Table B1).

On average, 40.7% of the respondents’ total household income came from their platform work; workers spent on average 28.4 hours a week on their gig work and had an average of 32 months (2.7 years) of platform tenure. Among the three types of platform workers, the microtask workers reported the longest tenure, with an average of 48 months, followed by 31 months for the ridesharing workers, and 16 months for the food/ grocery delivery workers. The ridesharing workers worked the most hours per week (34.5 hours) and relied on their gig work for the highest proportion (48%) of household income, while the delivery workers spent the fewest hours per week working (21.7 hours) and relied on the platform for the lowest proportion (35%) of household income.

## 3.3 Data Analysis

We used thematic analysis to interpret the narrative data. Thematic analysis is a qualitative method for identifying, analyzing, and reporting certain themes or patterns across an entire data set (Braun & Clark, 2006; Clarke et al., 2015). Following the guidelines articulated by Braun and Clark (2006), we conducted thematic analysis to interpret worker responses to the open-ended questions on their perceptions of workrelated risks and their coping behaviors on the three types of DLPs. First, informed by prior research on risks and agencies (see Table 1), we performed an open coding of worker narratives to identify an initial pool of codes, referred to as first-order codes. Second, we sorted and grouped the codes in higher-order themes based on the shared commonalities and collated each code’s data extracts to its corresponding theme. Next, we iteratively refined the codes of risks and coping behaviors by screening all collated extracts for each theme and made the necessary revisions. Finally, we checked for missing codes and organized the subgroup codes in a hierarchical structure. To ensure the reliability of our thematic analysis, two independent coders (the first author and another researcher)

followed the coding scheme, coded the data, and compared and discussed the coding. We finalized the results of the thematic analysis by resolving the discrepancies between the results of the two coders.

## 4 Findings

As noted, the objective of the study was to reveal the risks and concerns of those working on different types of DLP and to examine their coping behaviors. Our data coding and analysis revealed six major work risks and identified three types of labor agency on the three types of DLPs: delivery, ridesharing, and microtask crowdworking platforms. Our data analysis shows that workers on the different platforms perceived different risks and exercised their agency differently in coping with these risks. We first present the risks perceived by workers on all DLPs and a comparison of the risks across the three different types of DLPs. This is followed by workers’ enactment of labor agency and a comparison of coping behaviors across the DLPs. This section ends with a presentation of the association between risks and labor agencies on DLPs.

## 4.1 Gig Work Risks on DLPs

Our data coding and analysis revealed six major work risks: employment; entrepreneurial; financial; mental health; physical health, and technological.

## 4.1.1 Employment Risk

Employment risk is referred to as concerns and problems arising from the contractual arrangements associated with work on DLPs. This risk is rooted in the worker categorization of independent contractor. Using contractual arrangements, DLPs avoid offering employment-related benefits such as health insurance, retirement benefits, and professional training, shifting these costs onto workers (Bajwa et al. 2018; Cherry, 2016; Davis, 2015; Fox et al. 2018). Workers in our study expressed their concerns about employment risk:

I am worried about lack of health insurance. I could not afford the money to purchase medical insurance for myself and my family. (ID 63; delivery worker)

Although workers on all the DLPs perceived risks associated with the independent contractor status (i.e., lack of benefits and training), workers on the remotework platform (MTurk) expressed greater frustration and concern about losing income by being treated unfairly by certain job requesters (i.e., work rejection) and lacking channels for voice and dispute resolution. For example, a MTurk worker elaborated on this type of employment risk:

[Platform should] Have a process to go through to get help when a requester won’t overturn a rejection, when we truly believe it was not our fault for receiving it. I once received 17 rejections from one requester after working on their batch of HITs. They ended up admitting it was their mistake but did not do this before the 30-day time allotted for rejection reversal and so I am stuck with 17 unfair rejections. There’s nothing I can do about them. (ID 68; MTurk worker)

As indicated by the above remark, worker perception of work-related risk is rooted in the work process governed by the platform. Such employment risk is different from that identified in prior research, which is associated with employer-employee contracts and employment benefits such as health insurance, retirement plans, and professional development (Bajwa et al., 2018; Cherry, 2016; Fox et al., 2018). In this regard, the employment risk expressed by our study participants reveals a new dimension of employment risk regarding gig work in the form of unjust processes such as payment rejection and the lack of dispute channels.

## 4.1.2 Entrepreneurial Risk

Entrepreneurial risk concerns the risks assumed by gig workers regarding their classification as independent contractors. For example, gig workers must assume risks associated with the capital investment needed to perform their work, including the purchase of new equipment for work or the maintenance of existing equipment (e.g., car, computer, internet service, etc.). Likewise, gig workers may have concerns about low or no return on their capital investment if, for example, they fail to earn enough money to cover the capital investments needed to do the work. Consistent with prior research (Bajwa et al., 2018), we consider these types of risk to be entrepreneurial risk. Many of the workers in our study reported feeling helpless regarding the entrepreneurial risks the platforms had shifted onto them, as expressed by the following quotes:

I don’t think I know exactly what my losses are. I feel like I have more to lose for quick money. I had a car repair that was 500 dollars. That was luckily my paycheck for 3 weeks. (ID 70; Postmates worker)

They [the platforms] don’t care about my expenses as car repair, tires and gas. (ID 80; Uber driver).

## 4.1.3 Financial Risk

Financial risk was the most prevalent risk reported by the DLP workers we surveyed. Consistent with prior studies (i.e., Chen, 2018; Doucette & Brandford, 2019), we define financial risk as arising from the unpredictable income from gig work due to the absence of a safety net. In our study, workers aired their frustrations about the variability and unpredictability of their income from platform work:

I began to work for Instacart when the pandemic started as my primary source of income began to dry up. I work 8 am-5 pm.

Most of the jobs tend to be first thing in the morning and early afternoon. Never really know how much money you are going to make. Some days I make \$200. Other days \$30. (ID 46; Instacart shopper)

[On the MTurk platform] You have to be available pretty much 24/7. Batches and highpaying studies can appear any time of the day. I hate that about MTurk. Nothing is ever consistent, either. (ID 96; MTurk worker)

In many cases, workers also reported that the uncertain and unpredictable income was related to the low pay of the gig work, as expressed in the following remarks:

The risk is not being paid a fair rate and that a lot of times the company takes larger percentage of my pay. (ID 91; Uber driver)

I am frustrated about unfair pay. MTurk should provide a fair wage for all workers and punish/ban requesters [who] don’t pay a fair living wage. (ID 96; MTurk worker)

The workers in our study reported that income uncertainty is also associated with unpaid labor, which is a common feature of platform work. Workers reported unpaid labor in the form of the time and effort spent searching for jobs, waiting for a delivery item to be available, communicating with clients, or disputing rejected submissions of digital work, as well as problems occurring at the pick-up location (grocery store or restaurant) that are beyond their control. As a Postmates worker explained:

I wish it accurately paid you for the waiting times and if you drive to a restaurant and it’s closed or there is a problem, they should still pay you for your time. (ID 85; Postmates worker)

## 4.1.4 Mental Health Risk

Mental health risk refers to concerns and threats associated with workers’ emotional and mental wellbeing while performing gig work and can result from working long hours and/or dealing with unreasonable customers. Location-based gig workers complained about the mental stress induced by juggling multiple parties and devices or dealing with technological malfunctions. As one Postmates worker explained:

I am exposed to risks of traffic collision, phone damage, stress. Being rushed … in navigation ... overheated phone ... stacked orders that were unexpected on a time frame. (ID 70; Postmates worker).

The remote microtask workers also considered their work to be “emotionally and mentally draining” because of the lack of high-paying HITs and competition from a large pool of workers on the platform. In addition, constantly watching for newly posted HITs can cause burnout, and while joining online forums was perceived to be beneficial by microtask workers (see Section 4.3), some workers felt that listening to others’ success stories negatively affected their self-esteem and confidence:

The greatest risk that workers face is likely a mental risk. Even the most proficient workers are prone to burn-out quick easily due to the nature of how to find and finish work on MTurk. In order to be really proficient, a worker needs to be vigilant all of the time. Sometimes there will be a “flash batch” of HITs which are short lived bu can be completed at a rate of \$40-\$70/hour. This needs to be constantly tethered or tuned-in to the platform in order to “catch” HITs is exhausting. (ID 15; MTurk worker)

Mental risk is the biggest thing. If you belong to a forum watching others make money can make you feel like you are failing. Not everyone has the same qualifications so you can’t compare yourself to others but it is still hard not to. That make work on your feelings about how successful you are. (ID 72; MTurk worker)

## 4.1.5 Physical Health Risk

Physical health risk is related to threats to personal safety and physical injuries while performing platform work, which affects the worker’s health of that of their family members. Workers on the ridesharing and delivery platforms frequently expressed concerns about physical health risks caused by the physical labor required by such work, such as driving passengers, shopping, and delivering groceries and food:

Physical risks are risk of lifting bulky items, risk of tripping/falling when walking upstairs with lots of bags/boxes, walking over uneven ground or cluttered yards with bags/boxes (ID 46; Instacart worker)

I am worried about being robbed or assaulted by a passenger, especially when conducting a drop off in a remote location in which I lose cell service. Another safety concern is being hit on and having unwanted sexual advances, especially when the passenger is inebriated. (ID 03; Lyf driver)

Thus, threats to physical safety arise from multiple sources—including physical factors, such as lifting heavy or bulky items to provide delivery services, and human factors, such as fears of being robbed or assaulted by a ridesharing passenger.

In addition, environmental factors such as poor weather and road conditions also negatively affect the working conditions for ridesharing and delivery workers and increase the likelihood of car accidents. In 2020, the global COVID-19 pandemic also emerged as a major environmental factor that exposed workers on ridesharing and delivery platforms to severe health hazards. Since the nature of place-based gig work requires workers to interact with other people in crowded places such as grocery stores and restaurants, the workers in our study expressed concerns about contracting the coronavirus and infecting their family members. In this regard, the physical health risk of gig work during COVID-19 emerged as the dominant risk facing the place-based workers in the study. Workers’ concerns are evident in the following remarks:

We risk getting infected even if we are taking all proper precautions simply by being indoors at grocery stores where other people congregate. Other shoppers in the grocery stores do not always practice social distancing. (ID 46; Instacart worker)

Right now the spread of COVID-19 is a great risk for all gig economy workers as we don’t really know if we’re exposed and we don’t have a choice not to work. (ID 65; Grubhub delivery worker)

Finally, physical health risk is also associated with a lack of physical mobility incurred during extended sitting and driving during long work shifts. As expressed by a ridesharing worker:

[Physical] Health risks are that you are sedentary for so long. Low-paying jobs pushed workers to take more rides and drive longer, potentially leading to higher physical health risks (e.g., fatigue, car accidents, etc). (ID 93; Lyft driver)

## 4.1.6 Technological Risk

Technological risk relates to problems and concerns arising in technology use while performing platform work, including apps, devices, and information posted and stored on the apps. Performing gig work through computer networks and apps exposes workers to risks associated with technical malfunctions, including information security incidents, cyberattacks, and password theft. One such technological risk concerns security and information privacy on digital platforms, as reflected in the following quotes:

Threats on the app are Bots stealing best paying batches, identity theft, shoppers stealing card numbers, phone numbers and have a person deactivated. (ID 71; Instacart worker)

Some HITs will ask for personally identifying information, which could potentially put workers at risk for having their accounts hacked or their identity stolen. (ID 41; MTurk worker)

Some workers reported server connection problems or problems with communication features on an app, which sometimes prevented workers from completing their work—e.g., processing a HIT or completing an order delivery. The two quotes below illustrate such technological risks:

The frustrating experience is when the server is down for a survey or any game, and when the link is blocked. This becomes frustrating when we accept the HIT and start proceeding when the link becomes blocked or when the HIT has reached the maximum number of participants. (ID 20; MTurk worker)

One time, I showed up at Walmart to pick up an order. but the Walmart lady told me that somebody already came and picked it up. But the order was still shown on my phone and I was supposed to pick it up. I couldn’t clear it from my job list. (ID 61; Postmates worker)

In the first quote, the worker described how a blocked survey link prevented a MTurk worker from accepting a job. In the second quote, a Postmates worker described how a pick-up order was shown as incomplete on the app but the order had been picked up at the store, which prevented the worker from moving to the next work assignment. In this case, the worker had to improvise by driving to the delivery address and clicking the order completion button to work around the technical glitch.

While in the cases above, the technical glitch occurred during the worker’s use of an app function, in other cases, the technical risk is inherent to the design and implementation of a platform and its apps. As shown below, workers raised concerns about missing app features, which, for example, made it impossible to easily update orders and worker schedules or caused the platform to fail to deliver promised worker benefits:

I wish there was an option to transparently give updates to orders as well as have a safeguard in the system against people who want to keep giving bad ratings. (ID 08; Postmates worker)

[There is] The lack of consistency. The app will send really poor paying batches but inform you that if you maintain a 5-star rating you will see good batches but it don’t happen. The support has no clue how to help with issues. They don’t understand distance and can show you baches that are deliverable up to 60 miles away. (ID 71; Instacart worker)

## 4.2 Comparing the Perceived Risks across the DLPs

Six risks were perceived by workers on the three types of platforms. Workers on the three types of DLPs perceived four common risks: employment, financial, mental health, and technological. Despite the common concerns across DLPs, workers on the different platforms reported different risks. Two risks—entrepreneurial and physical health risks—were perceived by workers engaging in delivery and ridesharing but not by workers in microtask crowdworking. Appendix Table E1 summarizes the different risks across the platforms.

Unlike remote microtask work, location-based delivery and ridesharing work requires workers to invest in cars and bear the cost of gas and car maintenance/insurance. In performing location-based gig work, workers shared perceptions of entrepreneurial risk, such as ridesharing workers not being compensated enough to pay for car repairs or struggling to pay high insurance deductibles following a car accident. Moreover, location-based work increases risks to physical health. As one Instacart worker put it:

Aside from being exposed to the public right now, during a pandemic, I am also approaching people’s homes alone at a variety of hours during the day. It can also be physically strenuous work, as people often order very heavy or bulky items. (ID 50; Instacart worker)

Differences in risk perception were also revealed between the two types of place-based DLP. For example, financial and technological risks were bigger concerns for workers on delivery DLPs compared to workers on ridesharing DLPs. Delivery DLP workers struggled with poorly paid jobs and uncertain income. This was made worse by a problem called tip baiting, which was only reported by workers on delivery platforms. Tip baiting occurs when customers are allowed to take back a portion of the tip paid. One Instacart worker expressed their frustration and concern in the following quote:

There should never be a way to tip bait. If they change the tip after delivery, it should only be to tip more, or have some real solid complaint prompting wanting to tip less. Some customers have abused workers by bribing them to take orders with high tips then lowering them. (ID 101; Instacart worker)

In addition, the quasi-party interactions on delivery platforms cause workers to perceive technological risk. As discussed in 4.1.6, delivery workers encountered technical problems while completing orders in a grocery store or attempting to cancel a customer order on the app. Thus, technological risk includes both problems occurring during the utilization of an app feature as well as problems embedded in the platform design. The technological problems unique to delivery apps are reflected below:

App doesn’t always work in stores. [I hope the platform provides] better assistance with app issues and crashes. (ID 71; Instacart worker)

It’d be nice that if you had a legitimate reason for canceling shift, like being sick, you’d be allowed to drop a block without fear of being penalized. (ID 68; Postmates worker)

Our comparison of risk perceptions by workers on the three types of platforms suggests that the workers shared perceptions of most of the six risks while raising concerns about some risks at different levels. In contrast to workers on place-based platforms who expressed concerns about physical safety and medical health, workers on remote-work platforms were more likely to express concerns about mental health risks.

## 4.3 Coping Behaviors on the DLPs

Our analysis revealed three types of labor agency— resilience, reworking, and resistance—but the extent to which each agency was enacted differed. First, gig workers in our study demonstrated resilience in coping with perceived risks on DLPs. Referred to as resilience agency, this is the lowest level of agency, consisting of small acts of coping under often adverse socioeconomic circumstances (Warren, 2019).

Workers coped by complying with company policy, following government regulations, resolving issues with clients, or reporting concerns to the platform. These small acts of coping were found among workers on both place-based and remote-work platforms. On placebased platforms, workers encountered higher than usual levels of physical health risks during the COVID-19 pandemic. As a result, these groups of platform workers, including ridesharing and food/grocery delivery workers on multiple platforms, demonstrated worker agency acts of resilience by exercising caution and modifying their daily work routines to protect their health and that of others. They cleaned their vehicles more often, reduced physical interactions with others, and maintained social distance (i.e., six feet) when delivering food and grocery orders.

Doordash provides masks, hand sanitizer, and gloves. I keep bleach wipes in my car to wipe down steering wheel and keys. I take off shoes before entering my home and wash hands and arms immediately. (ID 55; DoorDash worker)

I have concern of COVID-19 and no healthcare. I clean [my] car more frequently [and] only accept trips with riders who wear masks. (ID 70; Lyft driver)

By contrast, workers on the remote-work platform (MTurk) perceived minimal work-related physical health impact from COVID-19, as they were distanced from inperson interactions. However, they felt threats to their mental health more strongly, and expressed their exhaustion and frustration in searching for and watching for new job posts (sometimes 24/7). Their individual coping acts were limited to contacting requesters for explanations about rejected work and reporting problems to the platform owner (Amazon). The following remarks reflect the small coping acts performed by the remote microtask workers in our study:

Getting rejected for dumb reasons (cancelled study, took survey too quickly, wrong code, etc). I complain to the requester and ask for them to please reverse it. I try several times to email, but after a few times, I just give up. It’s not worth the hassle (unless it’s a lot of money). (ID 96; MTurk worker)

Dealing with requesters who just blatantly commit fraud by refusing to pay after work is done. Perhaps even more frustrating has been trying to report this behavior to Amazon, and hearing Amazon’s customer service openly admit that they are not willing to do anything about criminal behavior on their own platform. (ID 45; MTurk worker)

Reworking agency involves worker attempts to improve their working conditions, both at and outside of work, while operating within existing social relations (Coe & Jordhus-Lier, 2011). In this study, workers expressed reworking agency by improving work efficiency, using multiple platforms/accounts, and joining online worker communities. For example, on the remote-work platform, workers attempted to mitigate financial risk by attempting to generate more income on the platform. One frequently mentioned strategy was joining online forums for MTurk workers to learn from the community. This is reflected in the following remark:

I joined because I wanted to connect with others doing the same work I was doing. It helps to motivate you and gives you tips on how to make more money. The other people who are on the forum post good hits which help you make more money. (ID 72; MTurk worker)

The third type of worker agency is resistance agency. Only a small percentage of workers showed resistance agency. Resistance is referred to as worker attempts to transcend capitalist social relations (Cumbers et al., 2016). The workers revealed three resistance behaviors, including declining a poorly paid job, avoiding a bad client, and quitting a platform. For example, during the pandemic when the use of face masks was mandated, workers on ridesharing platforms (Uber, Lyft) could refuse to take passengers who failed to maintain COVID-19 safety protocols (e.g., wearing face masks). Similar coping behaviors were also observed on other platforms. The following remarks reflect worker the coping behaviors of avoiding certain clients and failing to complete an order:

Anytime I receive an unfair rejection. there is no way to reverse other than if the requester decides to. This happens about once a month which makes me wary to take chances on new unreviewed requesters. (ID 05; MTurk worker)

I stopped after grocery store lines got long and items were out of stock, which cuts into how much I’d make. The platform should consider minimum profit margins for workers. No one wants to risk COVID or waste their time making low amounts on small orders. (ID 01; Instacart worker)

Although three types of labor agency were demonstrated by the workers in our study, in most cases of perceived risk, no act of worker agency was identified. Workers often had no idea how to address problems or threats arising from their work or were afraid to do so. For example, in the quotes below two workers on Postmates stated that they did not report issues to the company or decline work assignments out of fear of being penalized by the platform:

Honestly, I don’t (report to the platform). I feel like they are kind of shady and would deactivate you for complaining. (ID 85;Postmates worker)

It’d be nice that if you had a legitimate reason for canceling shift, like being sick, you’d be allowed to drop a block without fear of being penalized. (ID 68; Postmates worker)

## 4.4 Comparing Worker Coping Behaviors across the DLPs

Workers in the three types of gig work (delivery, ridesharing, and microtask crowdworking) examined here employed similar coping strategies. First, to minimize the financial risk and maximize their income-earning opportunities, platform workers on the different platforms reported employing similar reworking strategies, such as employing multiple accounts for work assignments. On MTurk, some workers reported using multiple computer screens or creating multiple accounts in order to quickly capture well-paid HITs. On the ridesharing and delivery platforms, workers reported signing up to work on two or more platforms to minimize their idle time and access more ride orders. For example, a delivery worker explained how they managed to work on two delivery platforms—Grubhub and Shipt—simultaneously:

I have jumped from one app to another to figure out the best one for me and what works on my schedule and financially. Now, I only do Shipt 8am-6pm then Grubhub 6-8pm. Shipt is busy during daytime while Grubhub is busier on dinner hours. (ID 65; delivery worker)

Even workers who chose to stay on one platform shared a common reworking agency to some extent. For example, they reported taking breaks during their work or selecting their work hours and pick-up locations to fit their needs or maximize their earnings. Three workers on the three different platforms—MTurk, Uber, and Grubhub—explained their reworking strategies in the following quotes:

My most frustrating experience on MTurk is slow times and not being able to find good HITs to work on and wasting time. I step away and clear my head and come back to it at a better time. (ID 05; MTurk worker)

I choose to drive 7 am to 2 pm. I just seem to make good money during those times, plus I’m a morning person and like to get off work early. (ID 25; Uber driver)

I choose to work 11 am to 3:00 pm and 5:30 pm to 9:30 pm. These are the peak lunch and dinner hours. I also make sure I have a big enough break in the middle of the day to ensure that wherever I am after the early shift, I’ll always have enough time to go home for lunch and rest for a bit before going out again for the later shift. (ID 68; Grubhub delivery worker)

To a limited extent, workers demonstrated resistance behavior in adverse gig working conditions, such as refusing extremely low-pay work or deciding to quit a platform. A few delivery workers shared experiences about switching between platforms for better income and tips. One worker switched from UberEats and Postmates to Grubhub and explained:

I’ve worked for UberEats and Postmates previously. I feel like customers on Grubhub tip better than customers on the other platforms. I like that Grubhub assigns you a region based on where you live. So I put much fewer miles on my car than [with] other apps, and have a lot of repeat restaurants in areas I’m very familiar with, which makes the whole pick up process go smoother. (ID 68; Grubhub delivery worker)

The resistance act illustrated above was mainly reported by workers on place-based platforms. The existence of multiple platforms providing similar services and the platforms’ reliance on local physical labor affords placebased gig workers some opportunities to exercise their agency in resisting unfair, low-paying assignments or even quitting one platform in favor of joining another. However, this resistance act was rarely reported by the remote-work platform workers. This may be explained by the fact that MTurk is the only major platform available for microtask crowdwork and that access to such gig work is not geographically or temporally constrained.

Workers in precarious jobs or those who are unlikely to remain at a workplace are less likely to share in the rewards of successful reworking or resistance (Coe & Jordhus-Lier, 2011), which creates an incentive for “short-term” workers to accept current arrangements and which may explain the low level of labor agency (i.e., resilience acts) primarily expressed by the gig workers in our study.

## 4.5 The Association between Perceived Risks and Worker Coping Behaviors on DLPs

In summary, the platform workers participating in our study expressed six major types of work-related risks and three main types of worker agency. Reworking strategies such as creating and employing multiple accounts were commonly adopted by workers on different platforms to mitigate the financial risk of platform work. However, worker strategies to cope with employment and technological risks remained limited. Table 3 summarizes the common risks and associated coping strategies revealed in this study.

As shown in the summary table, worker agency in managing employment, entrepreneurial, and financial risks remained limited among the workers in our study. To cope with the employment risk of work rejection, workers revealed limited strategies. Examples included avoiding “bad” clients before accepting a job on the platform and communicating with clients to resolve disputes about rejected work (e.g., rejections on MTurk). However, for the commonly perceived employment risk of the lack of employee benefits, no coping behaviors were identified. When asked about how they coped with entrepreneurial risk, workers did not seem to have a clear answer. A few drivers mentioned that they rented their vehicle from the platform because the mileage on their personal vehicles accumulated very quickly. When one Uber driver needed to fix car damage incurred by riders, they reported going to an Uber Facebook group for suggestions. Strategies to cope with financial risk were similarly limited, with some workers revealing reworking (e.g., using multiple accounts or platforms) or resisting (avoiding poorly paid jobs or bad clients) behaviors.

To cope with the physical health risk, place-based gig workers showed some resilience behaviors—the minimum level of agency. They explained that they tried to protect themselves from the risk of contracting COVID-19 by maintaining the safety protocols recommended by government and health organizations such as the CDC (the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention). In doing so, they hoped that they would be able to continue to work during the pandemic. Some workers also revealed reworking strategies, such as modifying their routine procedures in handling groceries at a client’s house or interacting with passengers during a ride. The COVID-19 pandemic made one Lyft driver (ID 86) change his work behavior, as he “tried not to talk or have less conversation with passengers.” Similar reworking acts were shared by a Postmates worker:

[My new delivery routine is] Setting the item safely on an elevated chair, table or box, knocking and then stepping back and waiting for the door to be opened. (ID 57; Postmates worker)

To cope with mental health risks, MTurk workers demonstrated some resilience behaviors. They explained that they took the initiative to email job requesters to ask for reasons for a job rejection or to ask for the reversal of a rejection, or they reached out to a university IRB office if the HITs were for survey studies. Moreover, they expressed their desire for the platform owner (Amazon) to improve the work processes and customer support:

Amazon needs to provide actual customer support for both workers and requesters and stop pretending MTurk exists in a black box that nobody should be able to get help or talk to the company about. (ID 06; MTurk worker)

A lot of new requesters have a hard time determining whether or not their work is fair, and many don’t understand the detrimental impact of blocks and rejections. Therefore, it would be helpful for there to be a “guide” to help new requesters understand the MTurk ecosystem better. By ecosystem, I mean the MTurk platform itself, the forums, and the HIT/Requester rating tools (like TurkOpticon & Turkerview). (ID 15; MTurk worker)

MTurk workers’ resistance agency was rarely observed. As remote gig work is open to people without restriction regarding their locality and nationality, workers on the MTurk platform face competition from a large, global supply of digital labor. The limited resistance acts they expressed were refusing to accept tasks from job requesters with whom they had previously had a bad experience and attempting to avoid a “bad” job requester once they learned about a requester’s poor reputation from fellow workers in online forums.

To cope with technological risk, workers exhibited some resilience behaviors, such as reporting problems to the platform. Facing the above technological risks, workers mostly reported the issues to the platform through phone calls or messaging. However, the workers were largely disappointed with the technical support by the platform— partly due to the difficulties workers encountered in explaining the technical glitches occurring locally to technical support persons located remotely. As one Instacart shopper explained,

It’s really hard to get to talk to someone from the United States. When we message or call Instacart support it’s an out of the country person who many times doesn’t understand what we mean. (ID 02; Instacart worker).

The workers expressed a desire for easier ways to request technical assistance with problems such as order updates or cancelations, as reflected in the above remarks. In addition, workers reported using workaround solutions, such as fooling the app by driving to a delivery address in the case of duplicate orders for delivery.

## 5 Discussion

The objectives of this study were to compare the workrelated risks perceived by platform workers across DLPs and to investigate the presence of worker agency in online labor markets. Our analysis shows that workers on the three different types of platforms (delivery, ridesharing, microtask) shared four types of risk—employment, financial, mental health, and technological—but the frequencies of these risks differed across the platforms. For example, workers on both delivery and microtask platforms were more likely to be exposed to technological risk, but they differed in their exposure to financial risk. Moreover, place-based platforms for ridesharing and food/grocery delivery were associated more strongly with physical health risk and entrepreneurial risk, while the remote work (microtask) platform was associated more strongly with mental health risk. To cope with the workrelated risks, the DLP workers in our study enacted three types of labor agency—resilience, reworking, and resistance—but their enactment of labor agency remained limited. No coping behaviors were reported for most of the work-related risks on the DLPs, especially for delivery and ridesharing.

## 5.1 Worker Agency on Different Labor Platforms

Gig workers demonstrated individual forms of labor agency, similar to employees in traditional work organizations. That said, the contractor status of gig workers profoundly shapes their agency potential because contractors are self-employed, placed in market competition with each other, paid by piecework arrangements, and managed and controlled by the algorithms that the platforms deploy for the allocation of each piece of work (Richardson, 2020). Moreover, our study shows that the workers’ ability to express agency in the labor process was actively constrained by the technological features deployed by the platforms. For example, the delivery app restricted workers’ ability to modify a delivery work order and scheduled and monitored worker performance (e.g., customer ratings), reducing workers’ ability to express agency. Nonetheless, the workers engaged in reworking and resilience to make their work more bearable. For example, workers reported signing up on two platforms simultaneously to locate work orders closest to their home or logging onto both Uber and Lyft to find rides and reduce idle time. This individual form of reworking enabled the workers to increase access to work and earning potential, thus mitigating the income risks intrinsic to platform work.

Not all the workers in our study demonstrated agency. Those who did showed resilience and reworking behaviors to different extents. Nevertheless, the workers narratives concerning their work-related frustration and coping behaviors revealed the coping strategies of reaching out to other workers through online forums (e.g., MTurk forums) or Facebook groups (ridesharing/delivery workers). Without traditional trade unions, MTurk workers demonstrated a trial-and-error approach to negotiating and defending the payment they deserved, revealing that they were in the process of learning to claim their agency. This process aspect of labor agency is echoed in a prior study of the labor agency of hotel workers (largely migrants, students, and part-time workers) at the Dorchester Hotel, where individual acts of agency were part of a learning process (Wills, 2005).

Additionally, the enactment of labor agency was not always similar on the different platforms. For certain strategies, such as reworking, workers behaved similarly across different DLPs. Common reworking strategies across all platforms included registering on multiple platforms, using multiple accounts on the same platform, and modifying work schedules. However, resistance strategies were far different on the remote-work platform compared to the place-based platforms. On MTurk, workers showed resistance behaviors through avoiding “bad” job requesters on the remote platform, while workers on place-based platforms (e.g., Uber, Lyft) exhibited resistance by canceling a ride, refusing to drive a customer who did not wear a mask, or simply quitting the platform.

The limited, individualized acts of agency revealed in our study are echoed in prior studies on the gig economy. For example, Barratt et al. (2020) characterized gig work as a departure from socially protected work; although individual workers express locational agency through attempts to maximize their income and cope with market relations, workers’ desire to express agency beyond individual forms of resilience is often constrained by workers’ temporary migrant worker status or transient attachment. In another study, Anwar and Graham (2020) studied gig workers on Upwork, a remote-work platform for IT services, and found that resilience and reworking were the most common individual labor agency practices, particularly among new and less-educated workers. They found that because the less-educated workers were more likely to come from low-income backgrounds, they were more likely to find the remote work on the Upwork platform to be an attractive livelihood opportunity and were therefore less likely to be involved in resistance acts such as declining or canceling work contracts.

Regardless of the types of risks, our study of workers across multiple platforms suggests that the root cause of the risks is a lack of transparency about the platform algorithm. For example, financial and physical health risks are generated when workers lack critical information about the frequency and organization of their work orders and how their work data are collected and used in the algorithm. This is echoed in a study of the Australian Deliveroo platform (Gregory, 2021): “the conditions of algorithmically management platform work fall short of enabling individuals to ‘be their own boss’ and to at the minimum—ensure their own physical safety” (p. 328). The algorithm obscures the organization of the work and evaluation of worker performance, thus limiting the worker’s ability to assess and manage the financial and physical risks on the ridesharing platform.

Prior research has shed light on the risk management and coping behaviors of platform workers. One suggestion is to provide workers with clear and accessible insight into the algorithmic organization of their work so that they can enhance their understanding of the work organization and can better manage their daily work to mitigate financial and physical health risks (Gregory, 2021). However, algorithm management is foundational to gig work and workers remain subject to the control imposed by the algorithm (Möhlmann et al., 2020). Our research on the agency of gig workers shows that workers have limited power to cope with the various risks that are deeply rooted in the fundamental design of platform-based work. For gig workers to manage the risks and improve their well-being, formal labor regulations are essential to protect financial benefits as well as physical and mental health in the long run.

## 5.2 The Interplay between Worker Agency and Platform Intermediation

Our analysis suggests that labor agency on digital labor platforms is impacted by two important factors: the locality of physical requirements and the level of platform intermediation. On the place-based platforms (delivery and ridesharing), we found that gig workers were more likely to demonstrate resistance (e.g., quit a platform) and reworking agency (e.g., sign up with multiple platforms or choose different work hours). By contrast, on the remote-work platforms (microtasks), we found that workers’ resistance agency was constrained by the large supply of crowdworkers worldwide and the high competition for digital gig work. In the latter case, we found that workers exercised a lower level of agency, resilience agency (e.g., attempting to resolve work rejection problems with job requesters and seeking tips and solutions from online forums).

In addition, we found that platform workers’ ability to exercise agency is affected by the different levels of platform intermediation—a triple as compared to a quadrangular relationship—in the platform work. Gig work platforms are commonly viewed as a marketmaker, matching clients that have needs for services with on-demand gig workers. However, the degree of market-making differs across the three types of platforms. Microtask crowdworking platforms (such as MTurk) and ridesharing platforms (such as Uber and Lyft) engage a three-way relationship and create an online market for workers and clients to make transactions, thus serving as a mediator in an online labor market. Meanwhile, food-delivery and grocerydelivery platforms (e.g., Instacart and Postmates) act as “double” market-makers, seeking to match restaurants and grocery stores that sell food and groceries with consumers while also facilitating the transport of such goods from the restaurant or grocery store to the consumer. In this regard, these delivery platforms are effectively transforming both product and labor markets, and the double market-making in app-based food and grocery delivery thus involves a quadrangular relationship. As described in Barrett et al. (2020, p. 1647), food-delivery platforms need to simultaneously make and coordinate (1) a product market, intermediating between consumers and restaurants, and (2) a labor market, matching labor supply with the demand generated by product markets. This represents an extension of traditional labor market intermediation into both product and labor markets mediated by technologies.

As a result of the differences in triple compared to quadruple relationships in platform intermediation, we found that workers exercised their agencies differently. The workers’ ability to exercise agency was weakened due to more constraints imposed by the quadruple relationship compared to those on microtask and ridesharing platforms. As discussed above, the delivery workers did not have a guaranteed minimum pay on each delivery order, and their pay was often reduced due to the unavailability of an item. Workers’ frustration and helplessness regarding the compensation structure on the delivery platforms is reflected in the following:

If customers won’t take replacements for outof-stock items we should still be paid for the missing item since we looked for it and spent even longer trying to replace. Our profit margin shouldn’t be affected by store availability or a customer who is simply picky. We’re in a pandemic. Just accept the other kind of almond milk... or charge them regardless vs lowering the % we earn based on the total bill now being lower. We accept the job based on the pay and when pay gets lowered it’s not cool. (ID 40; Instacart worker)

On DLPs, workers’ potential agency is affected by factors such as physical requirements and platform intermediation. As independent contractors, gig workers’ acts of agency must be conceptualized differently from those of employees (Barrett et al., 2020).

The differences in worker experiences and risk perceptions were related to the different platform designs: ridesharing and delivery require physical labor and occur in local areas, while microtask crowed work is open to a virtual workforce not limited to local geography. In particular, food/delivery platforms coordinate the relationship among four parties: customer, restaurant (or grocery store), worker, and platform (Barratt et al., 2020). Accounting for the differences in platform design and intermediation, our study provides a nuanced understanding of worker perceptions and behaviors across digital labor platforms.

## 6 Contributions and Implications

As has been noted, our study adopts a comparative qualitative case-study research design (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007; Yin, 2014) to examine worker experiences across multiple DLPs. While researchers have paid increasing attention to platforms’ attempts to control labor, make markets, and engage workers as contractors, they have devoted limited attention to the exercise of labor agency. Among the limited number of past studies, Barratt et al. (2020) examined the exercise of labor agency by Australian workers on two food-delivery platforms, while Anwar and Graham (2020) focused on the forms of agency demonstrated by African gig workers on the Upwork platform. In contrast to these two studies, which examined gig workers in one sector of the gig economy, our study explores worker agency behaviors and the perception of risk in three sectors—food/grocery delivery, ridesharing, and microtask crowdworking. In Table 4, we compare the findings of this study with those of the two cited gig economy studies on labor agency.

In this study, we provide a more nuanced understanding of labor agency based on gig workers’ lived experiences, how they adapted to changing working conditions (e.g., COVID-19), and how they defended their right to payment for completed jobs (e.g., work rejections on MTurk). Based on our data analysis and findings, we developed a conceptual framework of labor agency in the gig economy. Conceptualizing labor agency requires considering multiple and varied dimensions (Coe & Jordhus-Lier, 2011). We also considered the dimensions of work locality and task digitalization. As gig work is a constantly evolving economic landscape that transcends national and geographic boundaries, our engagement with the labor agency phenomenon in the gig economy advances knowledge regarding the new era of technology use at work. Thus, our work here seeks to address important societal problems to develop knowledge to benefit business and the society (Davison & Bjørn‐Andersen, 2019).

## 6.1 Theoretical Contribution

We contribute to gig work and labor agency research by revealing nuances in the multi-stakeholder gig work environments consisting of platform, workers, clients, and the online worker community. For example, to adapt to the multifaceted nature of the gig work environment, the reworking agency revealed among gig workers extends beyond the working conditions within the same company; it is demonstrated in work interactions with other companies (e.g., platforms). Likewise, worker resistance agency is enacted in relation to different recipients, e.g., client (job requester) and company (platform). Our study contributes to research on gig work and labor agency in several ways.

First, the study contributes to gig work research by investigating the variety of work-related risks that workers face in their daily work routines. Prior studies of gig workers have identified the challenges and problems that gig workers encounter as independent contractors in this new form of work, including the lack of predictable income and employee benefits. For example, workers on MTurk and Upwork have expressed frustration concerning the lack of a guaranteed weekly or monthly income (Deng et al., 2016; Taylor & Joshi, 2018). Uncertain project frequency and work hours have led to unpredictable incomes for gig workers (Gandhi et al., 2018). Our study presented six risks faced by workers on three types of platforms and identified associated coping strategies that some workers enact in the face of each risk. The findings regarding the different risks and worker coping behaviors provide us with a more comprehensive understanding of the complex platformbased work environment.

Moreover, this study contributes to gig economy research by revealing the root causes of the risks in platform-based gig work. On the surface, gig workers have the freedom to self-regulate their workload to prevent fatigue—by, for example, taking breaks when needed. However, for those who are dependent on gig economy work as their main source of income, their ability to put food on the table is dependent on the number of deliveries and rides they complete in a day. As a result, it was not surprising to find that the Postmates delivery workers and Instacart shoppers in our study experienced time pressure with each order completion and delivery. Such intrinsic pressure often caused them to work long hours and feel exhausted, leading to higher safety risks. Christie et al. (2019) explored the road safety risks that gig economy workers in place-based work (such as transporting or delivery) are exposed to. The study revealed four major risk factors for vehicle collisions, including fatigue, distraction, speeding, and the lack of risk management. However, such factors are intrinsic to piece-rate gig work and tied to workers’ pressure to make more income. Government regulation has thus far done little to help reduce the safety risks encountered by such gig workers. In Great Britain, for example, laws limit weekly driving hours for workers to 50 hours, but many couriers working in the gig economy regularly exceed that weekly driving hour limit (Christie et al., 2019). Mitigating such risk factors would require removing the pressure from the work design and providing protections that, for example, guarantee a minimum wage and provide insurance and other worker protections (Bieber & Moggia, 2021).

This study contributes to work extending the construct of risk to the gig economy context. In the context of standard employment, there are two distinct types of risk—personal risk arising in the private lives of individuals and business risk related to the business operations of companies (Bieber & Moggia, 2021). However, the boundary between private life and business operations is blurred in the gig economy: Gig workers act as independent contractors and freelancers; thus, all risks experienced in their work lives are related both to their business and their personal lives. As shown in our study, gig workers may only exercise agency at a minimum level because of the lack of insurance and other worker protections to help them reduce risk.

To mitigate the financial (income) risk in gig work, Bieber and Moggia (2021) introduced the idea of a principle of inverse coverage that would require platforms (considered to be employers of short-term contract workers) to contribute more to insurance and worker protection programs because they use more gig workers for short, variable and less predictable work forms. This proposed tax would not be feasible unless platforms were to consider gig workers to be their employees. Currently, companies like Uber, Lyft, and DoorDash in the US do not contribute to social security for workers whom they don’t consider to be employees. Gig workers must typically fund their own Medicare and Social Security payments by paying selfemployment tax at a rate of 15.3% of their income.

## 6.2 Practical Implications

In order for an economy to maintain and sustain a healthy and productive workforce in the gig labor market, it is important for both platform organizations and policy makers to understand gig workforce needs, working conditions, and gig worker agency and to develop policies and regulations to protect gig workers’ rights and benefits. Our study has shown limited, individualized forms of agency employed by gig workers, but, in the long term, collective agency is needed in the gig work environment. However, gig work platforms intermediate both labor and product markets and limit workers’ agency potential by creating a disconnect between workers and job requesters, fragmenting the workforce, and reducing the possibility of trade unions (Coe & Jordhus-Lier, 2011). These factors discourage collective agency in online labor markets. Moreover, the technologically enabled work intermediation performed by the platforms shifts economic work-related risks onto workers, thereby reinforcing and even driving precarity in gig work and undermining workers’ ability to make a decent living in online labor markets.

One potential strategy to promote labor agency on digital platforms is to examine the IS capabilities of the platforms and adopt a platform development model that enhances interactions between workers and platforms (Tan et al., 2015). As workers, clients, and platform owners are all key stakeholders in the gig economy, it is important to implement platform governance practices to align the values and goals of multiple stakeholders to maintain and sustain the growth of platform-wide ecosystems (Huber et al., 2017).

## 7 Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Research

Our study examines gig worker experiences and agency by comparing worker experiences and behaviors across different types of gig work platforms, including delivery, ridesharing, and microtask crowdworking. It adds value to existing research by showing that both the digital intermediation by a platform and the physical labor requirement of gig work influence workers’ perceptions of risk regarding their work and their enactment of labor agency. Our study found that the workers across these platforms shared some risk perceptions but differed regarding others, depending on the nature of work (digitized or place-based). Moreover, this study revealed that workers’ exercising the three types of labor agency (resistance, reworking, and resilience) were affected by the nature of the platform (i.e., place-based or remote). The dynamics of gig worker agency on different platforms extend the findings from prior research on a single type of platform (i.e., Anwar & Graham, 2020; Barrett et al., 2020).

We acknowledge the evident limitations of the study regarding the generalizability of the findings. As noted, this study focuses on gig workers located in the US state of California. A promising direction for future research would be to examine the differences in how workers exercise agency on the same platform but under different regulatory environments. In other words, do the Instacart shoppers, delivery workers, and MTurk workers in other locations exercise labor agency similar to or different from the gig workers in this study? A study of Uber drivers across different cities in the US and Europe (Karanovic et al., 2021) showed that workers respond differently to problems arising in work organizing on digital platforms. In other parts of the US that impose stricter regulations on all stakeholders of the platform economy, such as New York City, where drivers supplement Uber’s insufficient or unavailable solutions by providing task-related information to one another, organizing meetings, and sharing advice. By contrast, in places that offer platform owners more discretion, the power imbalance between the platform owner and workers may be accentuated: Drivers are increasingly opposing Uber’s organizing solutions and expressing dissatisfaction related to wage insecurity, ratings, and compensation.

From a social perspective, research has highlighted the concern that gig workers do not benefit from the customary worker protections (Bieber & Moggia, 2021). Unlike workers in traditional employment, gig workers do not receive unemployment, nor can they claim unemployment benefits—although they are often underemployed (e.g., have less work or work at a lower rate). Our study provides empirical evidence on the financial risk associated with underemployment and lower wages that workers on delivery and grocery shopping platforms are subjected to on a daily basis.

Gig workers’ independent contractor status profoundly shapes their agency potential, as they are selfemployed, subject to market competition with other workers, and rely on platforms and algorithms for each job (Richardson, 2020). This agency potential is further limited by the economic stresses resulting from the global pandemic. In our study, almost half of delivery workers joined the gig workforce in 2020 because of reduced income or lost employment in the traditional labor market during the pandemic. Although they were deeply concerned about contracting the coronavirus, they could not afford to not drive passengers or deliver food/groceries because they needed the income, as reflected in the quote of the Instacart worker in the opening vignette.

Important questions still need to be answered in the space of gig work platform ecosystems. Senior scholars (e.g., Desouza et al., 2006, p. 345) in the IS field have encouraged more research that has “reverberation” potential (i.e., papers that provoke or stimulate others to push that line of scholarly inquiry further). Thus, to realize the reverberation potential of IS research, we as a research community need to adopt a broader view of IS research to include work environments that are transformed by IS and, in the context of this study, worker emancipation (or

marginalization) that is shaped by technology—given the view of IS as a sociomaterial (e.g., Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014) or sociotechnical subject, “the boundary of IS has to include human beings” (Galliers in Desouza et al., 2006, p. 346). We believe that the widespread adoption of the gig economy and the rapidly growing labor relation concerns arising on digital labor platforms are important matters that reverberate not only through the communities of social work and public policy but also through the IS community. As IS researchers who care not only about rigor and relevance but also about the social reverberations and responsibility of our research, we have a moral responsibility to address important societal problems in order to develop knowledge that benefits both business and society (Davison & Bjørn‐Andersen, 2019). In this regard, we trust that this multi-platform case study on gig work risks and labor agency will serve its purpose of attracting and exciting more scholars to conduct this line of research that matters to the stakeholders in technology-mediated and -enabled gig work environments.

She received her PhD in information systems from Carnegie Mellon University. Nancy’s research interests include crowdsourcing, digital and social media, information technology workforce, STEM careers, first-generation college students, and social inclusion. She is the co-PI of a grant totaling \$5.3M grant from the Connecting Minority Communities pilot program of the US National Telecommunications and Information Administration. Her research work has been published in journals such as MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, and Information Technology & People, among others. She serves as an associate editor for Information Systems Journal, Information and Organization, and ACM Transactions on Social Computing. Currently, she serves as the coeditor-in-chief of The Data Base for Advances in Information Systems.

Bob Galliers is The University Distinguished Professor Emeritus and former provost at Bentley University and Professor Emeritus at Warwick University, where he served as the dean of Warwick Business School. Additionally, he has held professorial appointments as research director in the Information Systems Department at the LSE, and earlier, as head of the School of Information Systems at Curtin University, Western Australia. He was the founding editor-in-chief of The Journal of Strategic Information Systems and also served as the fifth president of the Association for Information Systems (AIS). He received the AIS LEO Award in 2012 and became an AIS Fellow in 2003.
