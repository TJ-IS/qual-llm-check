# Digital Natives or Digital Immigrants? The Impact of User Characteristics on Online Trust

CHRISTIAN PIETER HOFFMANN, CHRISTOPH LUTZ, AND MIRIAM MECKEL

CHRISTIAN PIETER HOFFMANN is an assistant professor at the Institute for Media and Communications Management, University of St. Gallen, Switzerland. He received his Ph.D. in business administration (communications management) at the University of St. Gallen. His research is focused on online communication, social media, and political communication.

CHRISTOPH LUTZ is a Ph.D. student in media and communication at the University of St. Gallen, Switzerland. His dissertation focuses on online participation in various social domains, online privacy and trust, and digital serendipity.

MIRIAM MECKEL is a professor of communication management and director of the Institute for Media and Communications Management, University of St. Gallen, Switzerland. She received her Ph.D. from the Westfälische Wilhelms-Universität Münster, Germany. Her research focuses on online communication, social media, and the transformation of journalism.

ABSTRACT: Previous research suggests that user characteristics such as web experience and demographics may affect online trust. Drawing on social cognitive theory, we explore the moderating effect of user characteristics on online trust. Based on a survey of German Internet users, we differentiate three groups by age, web experience, and education. We term these groups digital natives, digital immigrants, and naturalized digitals. A multiple-group analysis reveals significant differences in trust formation, particularly in the cues considered in the evaluation of online services. Whereas a large user base inspires confidence in digital natives, naturalized digitals are more geared toward familiar brands and recommendations. Digital immigrants most critically weigh the risks of a transaction against its benefits. We argue that specific user characteristics are associated with distinct cognitive schemata, implying distinct interests and evaluations in online transactions. Online services should differentiate their signaling efforts according to the targeted customer group.

KEY WORDS: e-business, online trust, trust cues, web user characteristics, website signaling.

Trust has been identified as a key prerequisite for the flourishing of online businesses. Users must have sufficient faith in the integrity, competence, and benevolence of a business partner to engage in an online transaction [12, 25, 45, 47, 48]. A widely accepted definition describes trust as “a psychological state comprising the intention to accept vulnerability based upon positive expectations of the intentions or behavior of another” [60, p. 395]. Trust is most relevant when social settings are characterized by uncertainty, risk, and interdependence [26, 46]. In these instances, users must rely on their expectation that the transaction partner will fulfill commitments and refrain from opportunistic behavior [28, 32].

Trust serves to reduce perceived risks and social uncertainty [26, 25]. By trusting in an online service, users engage in complexity reduction and render their social surroundings more navigable [28, 42]. Internet users strive to judge the trustworthiness of a service provider by considering a number of trust cues or signals. These signals permit an estimation of the service’s competence, benevolence, and integrity (i.e., its trustworthiness) [7, 71]. Accordingly, online services are eager to signal their trustworthiness to potential users/customers [37, 46].

Researchers have come a long way in analyzing the multifaceted trust construct and identifying antecedents of online trust [7, 19, 33, 45, 47, 70]. Overwhelmingly, however, studies in online trust tend to treat users as a homogeneous group. This begs the question, Is it really all the same if an e-commerce provider wants to instill trust in a male or a female, a younger or an older user? Do more or less educated, experienced, or wealthy customers react to identical signals of trustworthiness? Our study addresses the following research question: Are there differences in online trust formation based on sociodemographic user characteristics?

Some previous studies have indicated that user characteristics do, in fact, affect online trust formation. Lee and Turban [36] suggested, but did not test, an influence of gender on online trust formation. Chen and Rea [16] studied the effect of demographics on the choice of online privacy controls and identified differences between genders and among races. Sheehan [61] observed that although women appear more concerned about their privacy online, men are more likely to adapt their online behavior to protect their privacy. Awad and Ragowsky [4] showed that women more often look at different elements of word-of-mouth recommendations than men do when building online trust. Bhatnagar et al. [11] analyzed the influence of demographics on online-shopping behavior and observed that age increases openness to online shopping.

To systematically analyze the effect of user characteristics on online trust, however, a theoretical framework is required that helps explain why characteristics such as gender, age, and education should lead to differences in trust formation. We propose that social cognitive theory provides such a framework. The next section introduces a social cognitive perspective of online trust. We then outline and slightly extend a well-established model of online trust upon which our examination is based [47]. Finally, we develop hypotheses regarding the effects of user characteristics on online trust. These hypotheses are tested using a survey of German Internet users. We conduct a latent class analysis to differentiate groups with distinct user characteristics. The resulting groups provide the foundation for a multiple-group analysis of the proposed model to test for group variances in online trust formation.

## Literature Review and Research Model

## A Social Cognitive Perspective on Online Trust

Why should user characteristics affect online trust formation? Is online trust a question of nature rather than nurture [59]? Research has shown that each individual has an inherent propensity to trust [45]. Internet users are more or less prone to trust an online party because of personality traits such as extraversion or conscientiousness [3, 25, 36, 70]. McKnight et al. [47, p. 339] described this propensity as a “disposition to trust” (i.e., “a tendency to depend on others across a broad spectrum of situations and persons”).

At the same time, Luhmann [41] stressed that trust formation constitutes a form of mental calculation. When building trusting beliefs, those who trust evaluate a trustee’s ability, benevolence, and integrity based on the available cues or information [12, 25, 45, 47]. Although affective predispositions play a role in this calculation, an evaluation is largely based on cognitive deliberations: Internet users must weigh the benefits of a transaction against its risks when deciding whether to rely on a transaction partner [21, 34, 37, 39, 64]. Social cognitive theory (SCT) provides a helpful foundation for analyzing systematic group differences in such mental calculations.

Social cognitive theory posits that environmental factors, personal factors, and behavior form a causal model of “triadic reciprocity.” Within this model, (a) an individual’s environment influences personal factors (such as cognition and affect), which in turn shape the individual’s choice of environment; (b) personal factors influence behavior, which in turn influences these personal factors; and (c) behavior affects the environment, which in turn affects behavior [5]. This framework has been applied to information and communication technology (ICT) use, showing that environmental factors such as schooling and ICT access affect ICT self-efficacy, which in turn affects use behavior [18, 73]. A key insight of SCT is that learning experiences, such as formal training, use experience or social influence, drive knowledge and skills development, and thereby affect cognitive and affective predispositions [5, 6].

These predispositions can be understood as users’ cognitive structures: Cognitive structures constitute networks of information, affective evaluations, and associations that are stored in the user’s mind and used to interpret perceptions, integrate new information (learning), recall knowledge, and apply knowledge to judgment, decision making, and action [39]. Cognitive structures allow for orientation in complex and potentially overwhelming decision-making situations [74]. Certainly, the online environment with its myriad participants and complex technological underpinnings provides countless such situations. Internet users must learn how to quickly evaluate the particular circumstances of a potential online transaction (i.e., build reliable cognitive structures). Cognitive schemata or scripts facilitate the speedy evaluation of the benefits and risks of a transaction [23, 29, 44].

Social interactions are formative in the development of cognitive structures. Users learn what to consider and how to evaluate cues when pondering an online transaction based on shared learning experiences [5, 10]. In other words, shared learning experiences lead to common cognitive structures upon which trusting deliberations are based. Information systems research has variously stressed the importance of social influence on ICT attitudes and use [2, 68]. We therefore propose the following: Whenever membership in a specific sex, race, or age group is affiliated with distinct learning experiences regarding the exposure to and use of ICT, distinct patterns in trust formation are to be expected. In addition to demographic user characteristics, socioeconomic characteristics should affect online trust formation whenever they denote distinct learning experiences regarding ICT use.

Whether membership in a sociodemographic group signifies a distinct learning experience is largely dependent on environmental factors such as economic (e.g., access), institutional (e.g., educational quality), technological (e.g., affordances), or cultural (e.g., discrimination) conditions. For example, the effect of gender on online trust formation should be dependent on the cultural context because it may affect women’s exposure to ICT relative to men’s exposure [24, 73]. Before we explicate hypotheses for the moderating effects of socioeconomic user characteristics on trust formation, we introduce the model of online trust upon which our empirical analysis is based.

## Research Model

We based our analysis on a well-established model of online trust proposed by McKnight et al. [47], which we adjusted slightly to adapt to our specific research focus. We chose this model because it accounts for the complexity of the trust construct by differentiating trusting beliefs (i.e., perceptions of a trustee’s attributes that are deemed beneficial to the truster) from trusting intentions (i.e., the truster’s willingness to depend on the trustee). Trusting beliefs are conceptualized to promote trusting intentions. Users’ “belief that needed structural conditions are present (e.g., in the Internet) to enhance the probability of achieving a successful outcome in an endeavor like e-commerce” [47, p. 339], or institution-based trust, is proposed to increase both trusting beliefs and trusting intentions. Finally, users’ disposition to trust is hypothesized to bolster trusting beliefs (H3a), trusting intentions (H3b), and institution-based trust (H3c).

Hypothesis 1: Trusting beliefs will have a positive effect on trusting intentions.

Hypotheses 2a and 2b: Institution-based trust will have a positive effect on trusting beliefs and trusting intentions.

Hypotheses 3a–3c: Disposition to trust will have a positive effect on trusting beliefs, trusting intentions, and institution-based trust.

We extend McKnight et al.’s [47] model by including a number of so-called trust cues. Trust cues are the features of an online service considered by users when judging the service’s trustworthiness [22, 25, 33, 72]. Online transactions are mediated by information technology, resulting in information asymmetries: users have no access to physical informational cues regarding the quality and trustworthiness of a service [75]. However, a range of website and service properties do provide informational cues that allow users to make judgments and build trust [7, 71]. Trust cues are conceptualized and have been shown to directly affect both trusting beliefs and trusting intentions [44, 45, 46, 72].

The importance of trust cues in online transactions can, again, be explained by SCT. Perception of a trust cue triggers preestablished cognitive and affective associations in the user’s mind, which allows for speedy information processing. Trust cues render it easy for users to retrieve stored information and apply that information to judgments and decisions. Given that users must learn what constitutes a reliable trust cue, distinct shared learning experiences regarding ICT use should also result in group patterns with regard to the effect of trust cues on trusting beliefs and intentions [6].

Among the most commonly discussed cues in the literature are the reciprocity or relative benefit of a service, the service’s brand and reputation, user control over personal data submitted to the service, third-party endorsements of the service, and whether the service has an offline presence.

Reciprocity. Previous studies have posited that a fair degree of reciprocity in the exchange of data, money, products, and services reduces users’ concerns and induces trust [62]. Ashworth and Free [3, p. 110] observed that “consumers perceive the information that is collected about themselves to be an input into an exchange with the online agent. In return, they expect to receive outcomes such as online services, goods, monetary compensation, or something else of value.” In other words, users are willing to rely on another party if the purpose is to gain a clear benefit [35]. Fair information practices (i.e., the proactive communication of security and privacy policies, guarantees, and further customer services) are crucial signals of reciprocity [19, 71]. Fair information practices allow users to judge the trade-off between the risk they are willing to take and the expected benefits, thereby facilitating trusting intentions [3, 35, 62]. The mere presence of textual information strengthens trusting beliefs by signaling a willingness to create transparency [46, 59]. Warranties, in particular, signal trustworthiness, because opportunistic behavior will entail expenses for the transaction partner [75].

Hypotheses 4a and 4b: Reciprocity will have a positive effect on trusting beliefs and trusting intentions.

Brand and reputation. Brands have long been understood to be a signal to customers that conveys a specific service quality. This signaling effect has also been termed “reputation” in the online trust literature [37, 48]. Previous studies have observed that in regard to trusting an online service, consumers are more willing to rely on large and well-established providers [33]. Companies attempt to foster their reputations and enhance their brand recognition via the design of their web presence [20, 22, 47]. Lowry et al. [39] theorized that brand awareness, brand image, and website design bolster trusting beliefs because they serve as cues that trigger associations stored in the users’ cognitive structures, prompting a feeling of familiarity. Both high visibility and professional design are associated with significant resource requirements—the necessary investments render opportunism more unlikely and therefore induce trust [42].

Hypotheses 5a and 5b: Brand and reputation will have a positive effect on trusting beliefs and trusting intentions.

User control over personal data. When users fear losing control over the spread and use of their personal data, they lose trust in a transaction partner [30]. Businesses, in turn, can garner trust by providing users control over the users’ own personal information [19]. Sheehan and Hoy [62] posited that user control comprises the two dimensions of “awareness of information collection” and “information usage.” For users, uncertainty regarding the loss of control over personal data increases the cost of a transaction, rendering it less attractive and thereby reducing trust [35]. Measures of user control over personal data, in turn, signal a service’s willingness to “empower” the user. These measures serve as cues of fairness and trustworthiness [64].

Hypotheses 6a and 6b: User control will have a positive effect on trusting beliefs and trusting intentions.

Third-party endorsements. Third-party endorsements encompass independent statements of experts such as certificates or seals as well as statements of other users, which attest to the trustworthiness of a business [71]. Some studies have observed that seals of approval bolster online trust and may even generate price premiums [43]. Other studies, however, have not been able to identify a significant direct effect [35, 49]; some stress that the effects of third-party endorsements depend on contextual factors such as product price and category or motivational factors such as user involvement [9, 34, 40, 63]. There are several reasons why third-party endorsements should bolster trust. First, professional seals can be quite costly similar to well-established brands; they therefore signal previous investments, which users interpret as a decreased likelihood of opportunistic behavior. Second, as shown in the context of cobranding, endorsements associate two previously unrelated entities in the customers’ perception. Endorsements thus facilitate the transfer of positive cognitive associations with the endorser to the (frequently less familiar) endorsee [1, 39]. Finally, endorsements that are provided by other users showcase the previous use of the service and signal a social norm in the sense of the service’s general acceptability to users [55, 58].

Hypotheses 7a and 7b: Third-party endorsements will have a positive effect on trusting beliefs and trusting intentions.

Offline presence. The physical appearance of a business–its offices, stores or branches–provides users with a range of cues regarding its quality. Pure online businesses are at a disadvantage in that respect: they must rely exclusively on the appearance of their web presence to signal their trustworthiness [75]. The existence of a physical store plays a mediating role in trust formation [32]. Companies that are well established in the offline world are also perceived as more “real” online. A strong physical presence increases the accountability of a service. It signals significant investments, which, again, can be interpreted as a reduced likelihood of opportunistic behavior [42]. Although a dishonest online service could theoretically just disband and disappear in no time, brick-and-mortar shops are tied down by their physical assets [35, 38].

Hypotheses 8a and 8b: Offline presence will have a positive effect on trusting beliefs and trusting intentions.

## Effects of User Characteristics

Drawing on SCT, we argue that sociodemographic user characteristics can be expected to affect online trust whenever they denote a distinct learning experience regarding exposure to and use of ICT. Most evidence regarding sociodemographic distinctions in ICT use has been collected in relation to age or generational differences. Prensky [57] and Palfrey and Gasser [53] proposed that those generations born after 1980 constitute a distinct generation of digital natives because they grew up immersed in digital technology. Older generations of Internet users who had to adopt digital technologies later in life have been termed digital immigrants. Digital natives are suggested to have experienced a specific technological socialization shaped by a distinct ICT environment, which can be assumed to result in characteristic cognitive and behavioral patterns [66, 69]. Accordingly, digital natives exhibit distinct usage patterns (i.e., personal social media use, synchronous written communication, experimental ICT use) [69].

A number of studies have addressed gender distinctions in Internet use, indicating that ICT remains a predominantly male domain [24]. In Germany, a higher percentage of the male population use the Internet [65]. Female students have been shown to exhibit lower computer self-efficacy and exposure to ICT compared with their male counterparts [73]. Among grown women, distinct attitudinal patterns have been identified in the literature, including lower ICT self-efficacy and higher computer anxiety [27, 59, 61, 67]. ICT learning and use among women has been shown to be more peer oriented than among men [15]. In some cases, female access to ICT is inhibited by socioeconomic boundaries [2].

Previous studies have also provided evidence that educational attainment and use experience indicate learning experience affecting ICT use. In the digital divide literature, education has been shown to be a strong predictor of ICT use [2]. Education can also be interpreted as a predictor of socioeconomic status, which affects both ICT perceptions and adoption [31]. McKnight et al. [47, p. 340] suggested that general web experience should have a positive effect on trust formation “because experience provides the consumer with a feeling that dealing with the Web is proper and normal.” Walczuch and Lundgren [70] observed that experience with e-commerce in general increases trust in e-retailers. It should be noted that the digital natives/immigrants’ generational distinction incorporates the notion of web experience because digital natives are considered to have been socialized in a technological environment that facilitates familiarity with online services, particularly social media [69].

Turning to the research model introduced in the previous segment, we propose a number of moderating effects of user age, gender, education, and web experience on online trust. Because the core trust model has been tested and confirmed in a range of studies covering a variety of contexts and samples, we expect the core trust model as proposed by McKnight et al. [47] to remain robust across user groups. Nevertheless, some variations of the hypothesized effects are likely. The effect of trusting beliefs on trusting intentions (H1) should be stronger among more educated, experienced, and male users because these users exhibit higher computer selfefficacy [59, 73]. This heightened level of confidence should make it easier for users to adapt behavioral intentions according to subjective judgments [61].

Hypotheses $9 a { - } 9 c { \mathrm { : } }$ Education, web experience, and gender will moderate the effect of trusting beliefs on trusting intentions.

The effect of institution-based trust on trusting beliefs (H2a) and intentions (H2b) can be expected to be stronger among younger and more experienced users because they more readily transfer the perceived situational normality of the Internet to individual online services [47, 69]. Older, less experienced users, in turn, may be more skeptical of an online service, even if they consider the Internet a safe environment, because online services are not as familiar to them as offline alternatives. McKnight et al. [47] hypothesized that the direct effect of disposition to trust on trusting beliefs (H3a) and intention to trust (H3b) will be weaker for more experienced users because it will largely be mediated by institution-based trust. We conclude that the effect of disposition to trust on institution-based trust (H3c) will, in turn, be stronger for more experienced and younger users.

Hypotheses 10a and 10b: Age and web experience will moderate the effect of institution-based trust on trusting beliefs and trusting intentions.

Hypotheses 11a-11c: Web experience will moderate the effect of disposition to trust on trusting beliefs, trusting intentions, and institution-based trust.

Of particular interest in actual practice should be group differences in the effects of trust cues on trust because services can most easily affect user trust by managing these cues [35, 46].

Reciprocity (H4a and H4b). We expect the effect of reciprocity on both trusting beliefs and intentions to be stronger among more educated and experienced users. Hoffman et al. [30] proposed that experienced Internet users more critically consider an online retailer’s privacy and security standards. Younger, experienced users are more used to the online environment; these users more easily choose an online service as opposed to an offline solution. Therefore, younger, experienced users will concentrate less on signals of situation normality [47, 70] and base their judgments on the specific conditions of a transaction.

Hypotheses 12a and 12b: Education, web experience, and age will moderate the effect of reciprocity on trusting beliefs and trusting intentions.

Brand and reputation (H5a and H5b). The effect of brand and reputation on trusting beliefs and intentions can be expected to mirror the effect of reciprocity. Strong brands should be especially attractive to users who are having a more difficult time choosing an online service [11]. Strong brands reduce risk perceptions because they signal significant investments on the part of the service. They should therefore be particularly reassuring to less experienced, older, and female users. Users with lower levels of self-efficacy should find it comforting to rely on large, established, and well-known providers [72, 70]. Younger and more experienced users have also been shown to be more experimental in their web use [69]. They should therefore be more willing to give smaller, less established services a chance.

Hypotheses 13a and 13b: Gender, web experience, and age will moderate the effect of brand and reputation on trusting beliefs and trusting intentions.

User control (H6a and H6b). A range of studies have addressed sociodemographic differences in privacy concerns. A literature review by Smith et al. [64] showed that younger, poorer, and less educated users are less concerned about their privacy. Control mechanisms should therefore be less important to these groups. Milne et al. [50] showed that use of privacy controls decreases with age; age may therefore have a curvilinear effect on the consideration of user control, with very young and older users being less interested in controls and medium-aged users more interested. Male users have been shown to be more restrictive in their choice of privacy controls than females, with restrictions increasing with years of schooling [16, 50, 61]. Again, more experienced users are generally more critically interested in privacy and security standards [30]. Therefore, we expect user control to have a stronger effect on trusting beliefs and intentions among more educated and experienced, male, and middle-aged users.

Hypotheses 14a and 14b: Gender, education, web experience, and age will moderate the effect of user control on trusting beliefs and trusting intentions.

Third-party endorsements (H7a and H7b). Professional seals and prices should be especially reassuring for less experienced users still hesitating to choose an online service. Such endorsements can strengthen the impression of situational normality. In addition, positive associations with the endorsing institution can be transferred to the online service [39]. We therefore expect this cue to more strongly affect trusting beliefs and intentions among older and less experienced users. In the case of female users, studies have shown that social influence and recommendations are especially effective in driving web use [4, 59]. Therefore, female users should also be more receptive to third-party endorsements. Age effects, conversely, could be moderated by the more pronounced “social” use of the web by young digital natives [69]. This could render them more receptive to peer ratings and recommendations.

Hypotheses 15a and 15b: Gender and web experience will moderate the effect of third-party endorsements on trusting beliefs and trusting intentions.

Offline presence (H8a and H8b). Finally, older and less experienced users, who are more used to brick-and-mortar services, should be particularly reassured by a service’s offline presence. As in the case of brand and reputation, offline facilities signal significant investments and a decreased incentive for opportunistic behavior [42]. Digital natives, conversely, are more used to the online environment; offline presence should therefore be less of a factor for them [47, 69, 70]. Female users have been shown to exhibit lower computer self-efficacy; the availability of offline outlets could therefore encourage them to try out a new service, because mistakes can always be corrected by visiting a physical store.

Hypotheses 16a and 16b: Gender, web experience, and age will moderate the effect of offline presence on trusting beliefs and trusting intentions.

## Methodology and Results

## Sample and Measures

The survey sample was recruited from a pool of German Internet users who were demographically representative of the German population and wasprovided by a leading market research institute. During October 2013, 5,328 users were invited to participate in the survey by e-mail. Participants were offered a small monetary incentive. The response rate was 31.27 percent, or 1,666 users, 122 of whom did not complete the entire questionnaire and were thus excluded from the final analysis (N = 1,544). The questionnaire employed in the survey questioned participants on demographics such as age, gender, education, and employment as well as on their Internet usage habits, such as length of experience, usage context (private or professional), and frequency (including frequency of use of specific online applications). The questionnaire also asked participants to judge their own Internet skills based on a few standardized items [7, 47]. Subjects were then requested to think back to the last time that they signed up for a new online service by opening an account that required providing some personal data—and to name the chosen provider. Subsequently, all questions regarding trusting beliefs, trusting intentions, and trust cues referred to the chosen provider. The scales for the four core trust constructs were derived from the works of McKnight et al. [47], Jarvenpaa et al. [33], Bart et al. [7], Bhattacherjee [12], and Gefen [25]. The scales for the trust cues were derived from a comprehensive literature review (all scales are listed in Appendix A). The precise phrasing of all items had been adjusted based on a pretest conducted among 34 users who were requested to comment on the wording of the listed items. Each item was rated by the survey participants based on a five-point Likert scale.

## Measurement Model

The measurement model was evaluated based on a confirmatory factor analysis. We tested for unidimensionality and scale reliability on the item and construct levels (see Appendix B). On the construct level, we used Cronbach’s alpha (α), composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE) to assess the internal consistency of the scales (Table 2). All measures except third-party endorsements (AVE = 0.46) were above the required criterion values. We retained that factor because of its theoretical importance. Overall, we can assume scale reliability. In addition to the confirmatory factor analysis, $R ^ { 2 }$ was calculated. No item fell below the threshold of 0.40 [13]. As shown in Table 2, discriminant validity of the constructs can be assumed, except for third-party endorsements and user control, in which slight overlaps exist. Because we assessed dependent and independent constructs in a single cross-sectional questionnaire, common methods variance (CMV) could distort our results [56]. We applied some ex ante measures in the survey design such as item randomization, separating dependent and independent variables, and reverse coding of some items. CMV was tested based on Harman’s single-factor test and the more comprehensive CFA marker technique [76]. We observed no indication of significant CMV issues in our data. We also tested for but identified no indication of mulitcollinearity.

## Results

To test the hypothesized model and answer the research question, we took a threestep analysis approach. First, we tested the hypothesized structural model for all respondents. In a second step, we statistically tested participants’ invariance across different sociodemographic characteristics using latent class analysis. This technique defines distinct user groups (classes) that provide the basis for a subsequent multiple-group analysis (MGA). Third, we performed a multiple-group analysis to analyze differences in the structural models between the identified user groups, thereby answering the research question.

Our analysis confirms the strong effect of users’ trusting beliefs on their trusting intentions (H1). Disposition to trust and institution-based trust do affect trusting beliefs (H2a, H3a); however, we could not find a significant direct influence on trusting intentions (H2b, H3b). In general, the proposed trust cues influence trusting beliefs more strongly than trusting intentions and, with the exception of brand and reputation, the effect of trust cues on intentions is heavily moderated by trusting beliefs (Appendix C). Reciprocity and third-party endorsements strongly affect trusting beliefs (H4a, H7a) but only indirectly affect trusting intentions (H4b, H7b). Conversely, brand and reputation have no significant effect on trusting beliefs (H5a) but have a strong direct effect on trusting intentions (H5b).

Counter to our hypotheses, user control and offline presence both have a negative effect on trusting beliefs (H6a, H8a) and no significant effect on trusting intentions (H6b, H8b). This result suggests that a strong consideration of privacy control mechanisms negatively affects trust. Awareness of information collection may possibly trigger privacy concerns, thereby undermining a general reliance on the benevolence and integrity of a provider [62]. In addition, service providers that are well established in the offline world appear less trustworthy online, which may be due to a perceived lack of competence compared to specialized online providers. Nearly 50 percent $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 4 8 2 )$ of the total variance of trusting intentions and 40 percent of trusting beliefs can be explained by the other trust constructs and cues, showing a good overall explanatory power. This finding is consistent with previous empirical applications of the underlying trust model [28, 47, 54].

## Latent Class Clustering

To analyze the effect of user characteristics on trust formation, we must differentiate among distinct user groups. We therefore conducted a latent class analysis (LCA) based on the hypothesized distinctive characteristics (age, gender, education, and Internet use). Analysis of latent classes reduces the response patterns of all individuals represented in a sample to a number of classes. Within each class, the probability of item response is estimated for all members. In our case, membership in a class represents the likelihood of a specific pattern of sociodemographic characteristics and web use. For each class, the probabilities of falling into a certain (response) category are estimated based on maximum likelihood estimation. Thus, the profiles of the classes can be evaluated post hoc. Because LCA is based on statistical modeling, goodness-of-fit measures such as the likelihood ratio or the chisquared test help determine the accurate number of classes.

To identify the optimal number of classes, we tested different latent class solutions ranging from two classes to four classes using MPlus and bootstrapping with 500 resamplings. The analysis provided substantial evidence for a 3-class separation. The quality of latent class assignment can be evaluated using the entropy measure. Values close to 1 indicate a high quality of membership classification. Given an entropy value of 0.849, we assumed good precision of the model (Table 4). As shown in Table 5, the p-values of 1.000 for both the Pearson chi-squared and the likelihood ratio chi-squared indicate that the difference between the observed and implied response patterns was insignificant. Given our large sample size and relatively few items, we can conclude that both the Pearson and likelihood ratio tests are asymptotically chi-square distributed and that the tests are applicable and meaningful.

## Interpretation of Latent Class Analysis

The three identified user classes are characterized by differences in age, education, and use behavior (Table 6). Participants differ notably in how they use the Internet; members of Classes 2 and 3 use all applications covered in the survey more frequently than members of Class 1, most significantly in the case of social media. Members of Classes 2 and 3 are much more active in their use of social networking sites, blogs, and Twitter. Online dating and gaming are also more frequent in Classes 2 and 3. All three classes are most similar in their use of more traditional applications such as online banking, online shopping, reading online news, and e-mailing. Accordingly, when asked for their Internet skills, members of Classes 2 and 3 are significantly more likely to judge themselves as able to create a profile on a social networking site, publish a video online, or publish information on a blog or microblog. These class distinctions allow us to address hypotheses regarding the moderating effect of use experience.

Although both Classes 2 and 3 are similar in terms of use behavior and experience, they differ in terms of their demographics, specifically age, education, and work experience. We do find some slight gender differences, with a relatively high proportion of female users in Class 2, but judge these differences to be too small to analyze for the moderating effect of gender on trust formation. As to age, we find that all members of Class 2 are 25 years and younger, whereas 75.8 percent of Class 1 are older than 45. Accordingly, retirees are significantly more likely to belong to Class 1, whereas students largely belong to Class 2. Class 3 represents middle-aged professional men and women. These age differences allow us to address hypotheses regarding the moderating effects of age on trust formation. Finally, education also differentiates the three classes: members of Class 3 tend to exhibit the highest educational levels, with members of Class 1 being substantially less educated. Members of Class 2 are by and large still in school.

The identified classes strongly resemble the digital natives/immigrants distinction suggested by Prensky [57] and Palfrey and Gasser [53]—but not in the sense of a mere generational differentiation. As Vodanovich et al. [69] noted, a user’s “nativity” actually describes the user’s attitude, experience, and skills with respect to an active and creative use of digital technologies. Accordingly, the concept of “nativity” extends beyond a mere age distinction, describing more of a continuum; user categorizations may shift based on learning experiences [69]. In the case of our analysis, this becomes most apparent in the case of the Internet-savvy Class 3. Class 2, which is composed of young, competent Internet users who are geared toward social media use, is closely in line with the traditional understanding of digital natives. Class 1, however, comprises older, less experimental Internet users who are geared toward traditional online applications. These users can fittingly be described as digital immigrants. Class 3, however, includes middle-aged professionals who are active and competent Internet users. These users are too old to qualify as digital natives but too heavily engaged in online media to be considered digital immigrants. We therefore refer to members of this class as “naturalized” digitals: former digital immigrants who, because of their open attitude toward new media and presumably also for professional reasons, were quick to adopt new media and have become as avid and creative in their Internet use as those socialized in the new media environment.

Finally, to answer our research question regarding differences in online trust formation based on users’ sociodemographic characteristics, we conducted a multiple-group SEM (MGA) for the three classes identified in the LCA. The aim of an MGA is to compare a structural model for different groups. Thus, an MGA is similar to an ANOVA; however, instead of comparing groups in terms of their means, the groups are compared in terms of a system of relations between several latent variables. To assess the assumptions to conduct an MGA for the measurement model, we followed the procedure suggested in the literature [13, 17, 51]. The first step was to test the model for configural invariance. In this model, only the factor structure is constrained to be equal across groups, whereas all other parameters can be estimated freely [13]. Thus, the configural model uses identical items to measure identical constructs in all groups. As shown in Table 7, the configural model $\left( \mathbf { M } _ { 1 } \right)$ fits well.

In a second step, we tested the model for metric invariance, in which both the factor structure and the factor loadings are held as equal between the groups. This is a necessary condition when comparing the structural paths between the groups and implies equal measurements for all groups. The $\mathbf { M } _ { 2 }$ model fit indices were slightly worse than those of $\mathbf { M } _ { 1 }$ , which shows that the factor loadings are in fact not equal across the three groups. At the same time, all the fit values for the metric invariance test are above the threshold. Based on these results, we can assume configural and metric invariance and can therefore proceed to compare the structural models.

Similarly, the effect of the third-party endorsement cue, although differing significantly between groups, is difficult to evaluate because there are so few significant effects. The only significant effect on trusting beliefs is among the middle-aged naturalized digitals group, whereas thirdparty endorsements negatively affect trusting intentions among the young digital natives group. These findings indicate that seals and assurances appear reassuring to professionals, whereas younger users are actually less likely to rely on a tested and scrutinized service. Nevertheless, we find only weak support for Hypotheses 15a and 15b.

Finally, we find the negative effect of the offline presence cue on trusting beliefs to be stable across all groups, but it is most pronounced among digital natives. Digital immigrants, in turn, appear somewhat more open to this cue. These findings support Hypotheses 16a and 16b. Overall, however, an offline presence does not (or no longer does) constitute an advantage to online services. We observe that the explained variance $( R ^ { 2 } )$ in the dependent constructs differs among the three groups. The considered antecedents and cues better explain trusting beliefs and intentions among the older groups (digital immigrants and naturalized digital) than the beliefs and intentions of the younger digital natives. This finding indicates that there are additional antecedents or cues that influence trust formation among the younger and social- media-savvy digital natives that have received too little attention in previous research.

## Discussion

## Implications for Online Trust Research

Today, online trust can be considered a mature strand of research. Refined and welltested models of trust formation have been established and cover a range of important trust antecedents [12, 25, 33, 36, 47, 62]. Studies have shown that in settings characterized by uncertainty, risk, and interdependence, Internet users engage in complexity reduction by forming trusting beliefs and intentions [26, 46]. In other words, users reduce perceived risk and social uncertainty by relying on their expectations regarding their transaction partner’s behavior [25, 26, 25, 32]. Although some studies have proposed that user characteristics such as demographics or web experience may influence trust formation, little research has been focused on this question [7].

Based on SCT, this study provides a theoretical argument and empirical evidence for systematic differences in trust formation based on user characteristics such as age, education, and experience. More specifically, we propose that trust formation, as a mental calculation, is driven by cognitive structures (schemata, scripts) that are shaped by learning experiences. Insofar as user characteristics signify distinct learning experiences regarding the use of ICT, differing cognitive structures and, accordingly, differences in online trust formation can be expected. We found that this is especially true for the cues considered by users when forming expectations regarding the interaction partner’s behavior. Therefore, although all Internet users engage in complexity reduction and the mitigation of social uncertainty when forming trusting beliefs and intentions, distinct user groups tend to consider different signals of trustworthiness because of shared learning experiences regarding ICT use (Table 9).

Our analysis identified only a few differences among user groups in the core trust model proposed by McKnight et al. [47]. We did, however, observe that disposition to trust has a stronger effect on institution-based trust among more experienced and better-educated users. These users thereby find it easier to transfer an overall trusting stance to the specific social environment of the Internet [28]. This finding is consistent with the more experienced and active user groups’ judging themselves as more open to exploring new online offers or applications.

Regarding the trust cues considered, we observed a significant effect of the analyzed user characteristics: older, less experienced, and more passive Internet users appear to more critically examine the specific risks and benefits associated with an online transaction. Because these users are less familiar with the brands or reputation of online providers, they appear to have a more difficult time developing simplified cognitive scripts and therefore focus more on the specific benefit provided by the service. Among younger and more active users, the reciprocity cue does not significantly affect trusting beliefs or intentions.

Younger and savvier users, because of their media socialization, appear to have established cognitive schemata and scripts well suited to the online environment. In the case of digital natives, these scripts appear to be driven by perceived situation normality; therefore, established brands and large user bases are considered important trust cues. The older, more experimental naturalized digital group considers a wider variety of trust cues. These well-educated, experienced users exhibit significant flexibility in adopting new media and services. They establish relatively complex cognitive schemata allowing for easy adaptation to new sociotechnical environments.

Consistent with our hypotheses, we observed that younger users who have socialized in a new media environment are less geared toward service providers with an offline presence or third-party endorsements such as privacy seals. Overall, the offline presence cue appears to be more of a hindrance in the online world than an advantage. However, older, less experienced users are more open to service providers familiar from the offline world. Specialized online services may either be less burdened by past critical experiences or simply appear more competent and therefore trustworthy, particularly to young, savvy users. Middle-aged professionals also find third-party endorsements trust inducing. This may be because these users are attracted to professional recommendations; however, these experimental Internet users may also be geared toward more recent and emerging services that tend to be recommended by word of mouth.

## Implications for Practice

Notably, it is not sufficient to differentiate Internet users by age alone [53, 57]. We confirm that the notion of digital “nativity” is more than just a matter of generation: it is an attitude toward new media and usage habits. We identified a group of young, active, and savvy Internet and social media users, many of whom are still in school, whom we call digital natives. We also found a group of significantly older, more skeptical, or passive Internet users—many of them retirees or homemakers—whom we categorize as digital immigrants. However, we also identified a third group of middle-aged, highly educated professionals, many of whom are active in the services sector, who are nearly as savvy and active in their Internet use as the “natives.” We refer to this group of actively adopting former “natives” as the naturalized digitals.

Most significant for online services attempting to establish or foster trust among customers is our finding that these three user groups differ in their consideration of trust cues. For digital immigrants, choosing an online service is all about risks and benefits—they pay relatively little attention to brands or recommendations, presumably because they are less familiar with online services, but pay close attention to the terms and conditions of a service as well as its privacy policies [19]. To reach this user group, providers should clearly explain the risk/return profile of their service [3]. The middle-aged, professional naturalized digitals are more willing to rely on familiar brands and reputable providers. This group is also influenced by quality seals and assurances. The reciprocity of a service is of interest to this group but less so than to the “immigrants.” Finally, digital natives rely nearly exclusively on large, established, and familiar brands with a large customer base, preferably specialized online providers because an offline presence negatively affects the trusting beliefs of this group. Quality seals and assurances also negatively affect trusting intentions, although this effect is only barely significant. We speculate that endorsements are a remnant of the offline world and clearly do not attract digital natives.

## Limitations and Future Research

Inevitably, our study is subject to a number of limitations. Trust formation is context specific and strongly dependent on cultural and institutional influences [20, 36, 47, 70]. The analysis is based on a survey conducted among German Internet users.

Based on sociodemographic similarities, the results should be applicable to Western markets, including the United States, but may not be transferable to low-trust environments or emerging economies. The results could also be further differentiated by business model or the benefits and risks associated with a transaction [34].

Moreover, we described a sample bias toward frequent and experienced Internet users. Although we did analyze for differences among more or less experienced users, our results will not shed much light on the considerations of representatives of “the other side” of the digital divide: nonusers or infrequent users.

These limitations provide avenues for future research. SCT has proven helpful in digital divide and technology adoption research [18]. We utilized SCT to differentiate Internet users’ habits by sociodemographic characteristics (i.e., digital natives compared with immigrants). Given that cognitive structures encompass both factual information and affective evaluations, SCT could also further the analysis of emotional judgments or influences in trust formation such as anxiety or enjoyment [8]. Research has turned to neuroimaging to examine the interplay of cognitive and affective processes in trust formation [21, 59]. SCT can serve to explore the social embeddedness of users’ reliance on either cognitive or affective scripts in the processing of social uncertainty—and thereby group differences.

Given that trust and the effect of trust cues are context specific and dependent on cultural setting [63], the theory presented in this study may provide a foundation for the analysis of such contextual influences, because these influences clearly constitute environmental settings that shape distinct learning experiences. This approach may also be applied to studies of digital divides because macroeconomic and cultural settings should influence ICT learning/socialization and capability development [2, 73]. Such a broader perspective may also serve to test the applicability of the natives vis-à-vis immigrants distinction in various cultural settings.

Furthermore, we propose that the theory presented in this study may serve to further explore gender differences in ICT use [27, 59, 61, 67]. We observed that gender did not significantly differentiate the user groups identified in our analysis. The more gender implies distinct learning experiences in ICT use, however, the more we expect it to signify distinct patterns in Internet use. Such a perspective may help explain why the generational differences implied in the natives/immigrants dichotomy appear to overshadow gender differences—at least in the West.

Finally, because we argue that socialization and group differences affect Internet use, a structural perspective may be helpful in explaining online trust and service adoption. Social network analyses may prove helpful in characterizing user groups and these users’ shared learning experiences, the relative importance of social normality, and the role of recommendations in service adoption. It appears plausible that the reluctant digital immigrants would constitute a less dense social network, reducing the effect of social normality as a trust cue, whereas the reverse may hold true for digital natives. Naturalized digitals, in turn, may share close ties to both younger and older user groups, leading to a differentiated perception of trust cues and stronger reliance on recommendations.

## Conclusion

Researchers have come a long way in identifying antecedents and outcomes of online trust. A number of studies have suggested that user characteristics may moderate trust formation on the Internet. This study turned to SCT to explain why that may be the case: depending on the social, cultural, and economic environment, sociodemographic characteristics may indicate distinct learning experiences with respect to information and communication technology. Such shared learning experiences can result in shared cognitive schemata that, in turn, lead to group-specific attitudes or approaches to ICT. Applied to user trust in online services, SCT implies that distinct sociodemographic groups may have distinct interests or perspectives when evaluating the trustworthiness of an online service. Based on a survey of German Internet users, we differentiated three user groups by age, education, gender, and web use habits. Conducting a multiple-group analysis, we tested for significant differences based on a well-established model of trust formation that we complement with a number of trust cues (i.e., signals of trustworthiness considered by users when evaluating the service). We identified significant differences in that the three groups (1) more or less readily transfer their trusting disposition to their trust in the institutional environment of the Internet and (2) focus on different trust cues when evaluating the trustworthiness of a transaction partner. These findings should be of interest to online services that are vying for the trust of Internet users, because the signals of trustworthiness employed by a service should be differentiated according to the targeted user demographics. Researchers are provided with further evidence of the importance of context with regard to the trust in and use of ICT. We conclude that established findings in ICT use may have to be revisited as the social, economic, cultural, and technological environment of users evolves, because these contextual developments leave their marks on user attitudes and behavior. Because of their socialization, the “digital natives” of the future (whatever their label may be) can be expected to exhibit cognitive and behavioral patterns that are distinct from today’s “natives” or “immigrants.”
