# KNOWLEDGE EXCHANGE AND SYMBOLIC ACTION IN SOCIAL MEDIA-ENABLED ELECTRONIC NETWORKS OF PRACTICE: A MULTILEVEL PERSPECTIVE ON KNOWLEDGE SEEKERS AND CONTRIBUTORS<sup>1</sup>

Roman Beck IT University of Copenhagen, Rued Langgaards Vej 7, DK-2300 Copenhagen S, DENMARK {beck@itu.dk}

Immanuel Pahlke and Christoph Seebach Goethe University Frankfurt, Grüneburgplatz 1, 60323 Frankfurt, GERMANY {pahlke@wiwi.uni-frankfurt.de} {cseebach@wiwi.uni-frankfurt.de}

Organizational knowledge is one of the most important assets of an enterprise. Therefore, many organizations invest in enterprise social media (ESM) to establish electronic networks of practice and to foster knowledge exchange among employees. ESM improves interaction transparency and can be regarded as a sociotechnical system that provides a language for communication and symbolic action as well as a better sense of others’ social identity. Accordingly, the individual characteristics of knowledge seekers and contributors determine why and how interactions occur. However, existing studies tend to focus only on knowledge contributors’ characteristics and to treat knowledge as an object that needs to be transferred. To address this gap, this study conceptualizes and empirically tests a multilevel model of knowledge exchange in electronic networks of practice (ENoP) that includes the characteristics of knowledge seekers and knowledge contributors as well a their dyadic relationship from an activity-centered language/action point of view. A dataset of 15,505 enterprise microblogging messages reveals that knowledge seekers’ characteristics and relational factors drive knowledge exchanges in social media-enabled ENoP. Focusing on organizations with knowledge exchange supported by information technology, our research extends prior findings by providing the first evidence that the communicative act expressed by question–answer pairs impacts the quality of knowledge exchanged.

Keywords: Knowledge exchange, enterprise social media, language action view, electronic networks of practice, hierarchical linear modeling

## Introduction

Knowledge is an organization’s most important resource for staying competitive. Thus, the effective exchange, reconfiguration, and interpretation of knowledge among employees are of vital importance (Tsai 2001). Building on the concept of electronic networks of practice (ENoP) (Wasko and Faraj 2005), enterprise social media (ESM) are expected to foster interpersonal knowledge exchanges among an organization’s employees (DiMicco et al. 2008). An ESM-enabled ENoP can be regarded as a symbolic social interaction system (Ågerfalk 2010) that is technically implemented (Goldkuhl and Lyytinen 1982) and allows members to build virtual identities, cooperate in networks, and cognitively rationalize within their shared frames of understanding (Orlikowski and Gash 1994). Prior studies have illustrated that ESM-enabled ENoP effectively bridge the gap between knowledge demand and supply across organizational and geographic boundaries (Kankanhalli et al. 2005; O’Dell and Hubert 2011). However, ESM-enabled ENoP not only reflect but also change the underlying human interaction in a social system. This phenomenon requires a fundamental assessment of the assumptions about how communication and collaboration within an ENoP take place when designing ESM. While the primary goal might be to facilitate knowledge exchange from a functional point of view (Goldkuhl 2006), the ESM-based communication discourses embrace new forms of individual representations and identity creation in the virtual space (Aakhus 2007).

However, even with capable ESM platforms in place, social issues might hinder members from collaborating and exchanging knowledge in ENoP (Kankanhalli et al. 2005) because they do not know each other personally and thus have to rely on the kindness of strangers (Ma and Agarwal 2007; Wasko et al. 2009). Since ESM-enabled ENoP offer innovative functions (e.g., follower features, tagging, personal profiles) that provide network members a lot of information about others’ social identity (Stuart et al. 2012), we posit that knowledge seekers’ and contributors’ individual characteristics may help clarify our understanding of the knowledgeexchange process in ENoP. Moreover, beyond individuallevel characteristics, the relationship between a knowledge seeker and a contributor may influence their knowledge exchange in ESM-enabled ENoP, whereby ESM provide a language for both to communicate. That language comprises symbols and functions that allow for communication in the form of dialogues, through which knowledge contributors can adjust their messages in response to the relational and individual aspects they observe from the knowledge seeker’s logs and profiles in the network (Beynon-Davies 2010). However, when exchanging knowledge in ESM-enabled ENoP, individuals also engage in symbolic action through the additional functions of language provided by the ESM (e.g., emoticons), thereby constructing social realities. Thus, the quality of the knowledge exchanged depends on shared common frames of reference since the specific knowledge as well as its interpretation manifest forms of alternative socially constructed realities (Orlikowski and Gash 1994). As such, the knowledge exchanged in ENoP is actually knowledge that the recipient mentally reconstructs, which contains a socially constructed meaning of actions from the dialogue or communication act through which it is exchanged. Here, the communication not only depends on the identities and characteristics of the individuals constructing the messages, but also on their relation and interaction level—namely, where the speech acts and symbolic representations in work cultures take place (Swidler 1986).

This research proposes a different perspective on knowledge exchange, one that posits that the quality of knowledge exchanged is not assessed in a quasi-objective way. Instead, we define the quality of knowledge as a cognitively perceived pragmatic value from the recipient’s perspective based on the communication acts as well as the exchanged information. In other words, we fully acknowledge the value of social interaction between knowledge seekers and contributors, which leads to the development of knowledge for, about, and through action (Ågerfalk 2010). For example, a statement suggesting that the knowledge content quality “includes the quality of documents, reports, lessons learned, and so forth, in structured and unstructured formats” (Kulkarni et al. 2006, p. 314) may not fully reflect the quality of knowledge exchanged. On the contrary, we illustrate that the communication activity-based interpretations the knowledge seeker makes define the quality of knowledge, and these interpretations are driven by individual and relational characteristics.

Accordingly, we theoretically develop a multilevel model of knowledge exchange in ENoP focusing on the individual characteristics of knowledge seekers (e.g., social presence) and contributors (e.g., reputation) as well as the traits embodied in their dyadic relationship (e.g., relationship strength) from a language/action point of view. We claim that the quality of knowledge exchanged is closely linked to the question–answer pairs, which represent a symbolic unit of interaction explained by multilevel characteristics residing at the individual level and at the conversational level of ENoP members´ dyadic relationship. To test that empirically, we focus on question–answer pairs exchanged on an ESM platform as our unit of analysis (Kearsley 1976; Ram 1991).

## Theoretical Background and Model Development

## Knowledge Management and ENoP

According to the knowledge-based view of the firm, knowledge is a scarce resource, and the ability to manage it determines an organization’s competitiveness (Grant 1996). Hence, organizations invest in knowledge management initiatives to improve their access to the dispersed knowledge of their employees (Kankanhalli et al. 2005). Alavi and Leidner (2001) define knowledge management as a systemic, organizationally specified process to acquire, organize, and communicate individual knowledge so that others may make use of it. In this traditional view, knowledge-management systems support processes for creating, storing, and exchanging knowledge in organizations. Scholars often distinguish two forms of knowledge management systems: repository-based and network-based approaches (Kankanhalli et al. 2005). The former relies on the storage of knowledge in databases, such as searchable document repositories (Grover and Davenport 2001), and mechanisms for acquiring, controlling, and publishing knowledge (Phang et al. 2009). The latter approach focuses on systems to foster social connections among people (Kankanhalli et al. 2005) such that users gain access to the resources of colleagues in their practice-related social networks (Brown and Duguid 2001; Wasko and Faraj 2005). These social networks can further be broken down into two categories. On the one hand, there are communities of practice (Wenger 1998), which are tightly interlinked groups of people who know one another, communicate face to face, and coordinate among themselves (Brown and Duguid 2001). On the other hand, there are networks of practice, which consist of larger, more loosely interlinked, geographically distributed groups of people engaged in shared frames and practices who do not necessarily know one another or meet in person.

Based on the notion of networks of practice and reflecting technical advances in web technologies, Wasko and Faraj (2005) define an ENoP as a special form of network of practice in which members primarily interact through electronic communication technologies. An example is the emerging ESM-enabled ENoP, which can be regarded as a sociotechnical system that provides a language comprising emoticons, abbreviations, short messages, and hyperlinks for symbolic action. Based on that language, communicative actions occur in the form of question–answer speech acts (Goldkuhl and Ågerfalk 2005) through which actors adjust their responses to a history of previous communication acts (Beynon-Davies 2010). However, although ESM-enabled ENoP offer a social infrastructure for connecting people, whether employees can access the desired knowledge depends on others’ willingness to contribute to the collective (Wasko et al. 2004).

## Knowledge Exchange and Symbolic Action in ESM-Enabled ENoP

Focusing on interactions between members of an ENoP, we define knowledge exchange as a dyadic, linguistic, speech acts-driven communication process between two individuals, a knowledge seeker and a knowledge contributor (Hansen 1999; He and Wei 2009). However, as is known from prior studies (Wasko and Faraj 2000, 2005), individuals frequently resist or feel unable to exchange knowledge with others, especially in voluntary settings. To understand knowledge exchange better, Szulanski (1996) suggests categorizing the antecedents of knowledge exchange into five groups: type of knowledge, characteristics of the knowledge seeker, of the contributor, of their relationship, and of the network that connects them. As Figure 1 depicts, four of these groups are embedded in a hierarchical structure, thereby constituting a multilevel system of factors that influence knowledge exchange (Klein and Kozlowski 2000). That is, the phenomenon of knowledge exchange in ENoP depends on specific factors at a higher level as well as at the same level (from the top down): the network level and the individual level (both of which are higher levels) and the dyadic interaction (or relational) level, which is the level at which knowledge exchange occurs (Cross and Sproull 2004). For example, knowledge exchange in an ENoP occurs between seeker A and contributor B. From prior interactions, A has built relationships with B, C, and D, and B has developed relationships with A, C, and E. In this hierarchical structure, knowledge exchange between A and B depends on their individual characteristics, the characteristics of their relationship, and network-level factors. In this study, we focus on factors at the individual level and at the dyadic interaction level. Hence, we follow a cross-level approach to analyze knowledge exchange as an interpersonal phenomenon that is embedded in a higherlevel context (Klein and Kozlowski 2000).

Although knowledge exchange may depend on factors at different levels, empirical research has thus far adopted a single-level approach to ENoP, converting factors from different levels to either the dyadic interaction level or individual level. Moreover, these studies have mainly focused on the knowledge contributor, suggesting that altruism (Wasko and Faraj 2005), personal expectations of economic rewards (Constant et al. 1996), and reputation benefits (Chiu et al. 2006; Wasko et al. 2004) determine a person’s motivation to contribute knowledge to others. Research based on social capital theory (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998) has also cited the influence of knowledge contributors’ structural, relational, and cognitive characteristics on knowledge exchange. For example, a contributor’s centrality in the structural network (Robert et al. 2008; Wasko and Faraj 2005) and the strength of his or her relationships with others (Chiu et al. 2006; Wasko et al. 2004) affect contribution quality. Similarly, cognitive characteristics, such as shared language and vision (Lu and Yang 2011; Robert et al. 2008) or tenure (Ma and Agarwal 2007; Wasko and Faraj 2005), can enhance knowledge exchange. Finally, relational factors, such as norms of reciprocity (Lu and Yang 2011) and group identification (Chiu et al. 2006; Ma and Agarwal 2007) have also been analyzed.

These studies provide rich insights into knowledge exchanges in ENoP, but somewhat neglect the role of knowledge seekers’ individual characteristics (He and Wei 2009) and the importance of the conversation for action itself, which requires analyses based on ENoP members’ interactions at the dyadic interaction level. In response, we propose a model that includes the characteristics of both parties in dyadic knowledge exchanges at the individual level (Level 1) together with relational factors at the dyadic interaction level (Level 0), where symbolic action takes place. In this regard, our model overcomes structural shortcomings by incorporating the multilevel characteristics of knowledge exchange without aggregating lower-level variables to a higher level, for example, tie strength on the individual level (Chiu et al. 2006; Wasko and Faraj 2005), or disaggregating higher-level variables to a lower level. On the one hand, aggregating factors from the dyadic interaction level to the individual level (e.g., computing a single average tie strength for each ENoP member) excludes meaningful variations of relational factors, hence making it impossible to study knowledge exchange as dyadic interactions between knowledge seekers and contributors (Hofmann 1997). On the other hand, disaggregation of higher level variables to a lower level—that is, assigning the individual characteristics of knowledge seekers and contributors to each dyadic interaction—exaggerates the sample sizes of the higher-level variables, yields wrong standard errors, and makes the influence of higher level variables seem more significant than they actually are.

Therefore, in the following, we adopt a hierarchical approach, which lets us examine the influence of the antecedents of knowledge exchange simultaneously at two levels. We depart from prior research on knowledge management that examines the quality of knowledge from an economic transactional perspective and is solely based on the credibility of the source (Grover and Davenport 2001) as well as from research that conceptualizes DeLone and Mclean’s (2003) information quality construct as quality of knowledge based on quasifactual assessments. Instead, we define quality of knowledge as a cognitively perceived representation of the pragmatic value of knowledge from the recipients’ perspective that is observed at the dyadic interaction level. That is, we fully acknowledge the social interaction between knowledge seekers and contributors, which leads to the development of knowledge for, about, and through action (Ågerfalk 2010), and thus focus on question–answer pairs as the unit of analy sis. With ESM-typical functions for expressing symbolic action, ENoP members can learn not only from the answers given but also from the interaction itself, which is in turn useful to adjust their actions. Furthermore, ESM allow knowledge seekers to vote on how useful the knowledge they gained from dialogue with other people is in supporting their actions.

With such an activity-centered focus, this so-called language/ action view (Goldkuhl and Lyytinen 1982) recognizes the intersubjectivity of formal symbols and language and how they are used in communication acts. Winograd (1987) uses dance as a metaphor for communication acts, “in which the conversation steps proceed toward mutual recognition” (p. 8). In such communication acts, during an ESM-enabled knowledge exchange, the recipient can acknowledge that his or her condition has been satisfied, for example, through voting that the knowledge he or she understood and interpreted was useful for future action. While the questions and answers are linguistic acts and are representations of the utterances of those involved, using ESM as language allows for sharing links or media that can support real actions. In other words, ESM affect not only how we communicate knowledge but also what kind of knowledge is communicated (Te’eni 2001).

In many ways, ESM support cooperation principles that are fundamental for the language/action view: user participation is necessary to achieve intersubjectivity or, in other words, to contextualize knowledge and to communicate it to the knowledge seeker (i.e., the interpreter). Furthermore, knowledge exchange is controlled by the ENoP, which also maintains the linguistic and “symbolic universe” available for its members (Goldkuhl and Lyytinen 1982). In accordance with the language/action and conversations-for-action view (Winograd and Flores 1995), the pragmatic value of knowledge interpreted and gained to satisfy the recipient’s needs is highly interpretive and does not resemble objective reality like in the knowledge-as-object view. Instead, the quality of knowledge is embodied in practical use, satisfying knowledge seekers immediate needs as they perform certain activities within their work environment. ESM allow for embedding these activitycentered question–answer speech acts in ENoP to express symbolic action in knowledge exchanges.

In this regard, high-quality knowledge is not necessarily factual or objective but socially constructed, residing within human minds. Thus, Krogstie et al. (2006) stress that highquality knowledge cannot be defined by quasi-objective codified representations in information systems but must be specified by its ability to support actions (Goldkuhl 2006). They also criticize the knowledge-as-object view as a misinterpretation of explicit and tacit knowledge (Krogstie et al. 2006). Instead, what can be seen in terms of symbolic interaction is how individual and social factors predict the quality of knowledge exchanged, which is expressed in linguistic moves constituted by question–answer pairs. Question– answer pairs in ESM-enabled ENoP can be defined as wellformed utterances aiming at certain purposes and can thus be regarded as equivalent to natural language speech acts. Here, directives in the form of questions are expressed and responded to by individuals using the specific language available in ESM. Since people may use ESM to ask questions for supporting their actions and because they are able to rate the usefulness of the obtained knowledge, our conceptualization of the quality of knowledge exchanged is a pragmatic one.

## A Multilevel Model of Knowledge Exchange in ENoP

Human interaction is driven by individuals’ interest to gain knowledge that delivers practical usefulness and enables actions that are better grounded (Goles and Hirschheim 2000). With the goal of ESM to support human collaboration, such technologies provide a language that contains symbols for expression in the language/action view (Goldkuhl and Lyytinen 1982). Furthermore, ESM can present visualizations of the discourse between members of an ENoP and allow for judgments about the quality or usefulness of the knowledge exchanged based on question–answer pairs, as illustrated in Figure 2. The knowledge seeker will perceive the reconstructed knowledge to be valuable for future action depending on how satisfying the question–answer speech act was as well as how useful he or she finds the knowledge exchanged to be (Goldkuhl 2008).

The model in Figure 3 highlights the specific factors of each group (i.e., knowledge seekers, knowledge contributors, and the relationship between them) as independent variables at both levels. To analyze the influence of these factors on knowledge exchange as the dependent variable, we chose question–answer pairs sent by the members of an ENoP as the unit of analysis (Kearsley 1976; Ram 1991). As illustrated in the example provided in Figure 2, receiver A of the knowledge exchanged with B has to determine if the utterance of B provides pragmatic value for future action or not. The question–answer pair also illustrates how emoticons or contextual insights are exchanged, which provides qualitative insights for A. Thus, we argue that knowledge exchange, specifically the quality of the knowledge exchanged in ENoP, depends on the characteristics of the knowledge seeker and the contributor at the individual level as well as on characteristics of their relationship at the dyadic conversational level. Therefore, we define the dependent variable “quality of knowledge exchanged” as a representation of the pragmatic value of knowledge, cognitively perceived as helpful or not helpful for future action from the recipient’s perspective. Moreover, we include control variables (i.e., language, type of knowledge, type of request, and cultural closeness) that prior research has identified as influential with respect to the quality of knowledge exchanged in ENoP.

## Individual Level (Level 1): Knowledge Contributor Characteristics

Interpersonal knowledge exchange requires the contributor to dedicate time and effort to codify his or her knowledge in conversations (Goodman and Darr 1998), which creates opportunity costs (Kankanhalli et al. 2005). According to social exchange theory (Blau 1964), people maximize their benefits and minimize their costs; they are motivated to do others a favor if they expect future returns will exceed their efforts. Thus, members of a social network must perceive that the effort required to contribute knowledge to others will be rewarded (Kankanhalli et al. 2005; Wasko and Faraj 2005). For example, people exchange knowledge in ENoP, because they believe they can establish and improve their reputation in the network (Ba et al. 2001; Davenport and Prusak 1998). Empirical studies confirm that reputation significantly affects the quality of the knowledge exchanged (Wasko and Faraj 2005). We propose that people who have achieved a high reputation by contributing knowledge in the past believe their efforts are rewarded by others in the network. As a result, they become more motivated to provide others with highquality knowledge for action to improve or maintain their reputation. Accordingly, we put forth the follow hypothesis:

## H1: The better a knowledge contributor’s reputation in the network, the better the quality of the knowledge exchanged in an ENoP.

Alternatively, theories of collective action and social capital propose that people who are centrally embedded in a network come in regular contact with many people and are thus more likely to help others voluntarily (Burt 1995; Putnam 1995). This rationale is based on the argument that people develop a habit of cooperation when they interact more with others (Marwell et al. 1988). That is, experiencing a successful interaction with others helps a person develop communication skills for framing and translating knowledge for others (Reagans and McEvily 2003). From a social exchange theory perspective, the costs of knowledge exchange diminish, increasing the likelihood that a person will believe the expected benefits will exceed his or her effort (Blau 1964). Moreover, those with experience in codifying knowledge may be able to exchange more comprehensible knowledge with others, and the quality of knowledge exchange increases when the habit of cooperation is stronger on the contributor side. This positive influence has been confirmed previously (Wasko and Faraj 2005), specifically with respect to ENoP. Thus, we propose the following:

H2: The stronger a knowledge contributor’s habit of cooperation, the better the quality of the knowledge exchanged in an ENoP.

According to social identity theory, people also tend to classify themselves and others into social categories based on individual characteristics such as personal interests (Tajfel 1978; Tajfel and Turner 1985). In this regard group identification is an individual’s perception of oneness with a group based on his or her relative closeness to other members’ characteristics (Ashforth and Mael 1989; Tajfel and Turner 1985). Group identification leads to cooperative and altruistic actions in organizations (Ashforth and Mael 1989) and is particularly important in online groups where the depersonalization phenomenon may cause a reduced sense of others’ personal identity, thereby enhancing the importance of grouplevel social identity (Bargh and McKenna 2004). Thus, members with stronger group identification may engage in more prosocial behavior, such as knowledge exchange, because they personally experience the successes and failures of the collective (Constant et al. 1996; Ellemers et al. 2004). From a social exchange perspective, concern about the group could even override the cost effects of exchanging knowledge, thereby enhancing people’s motivation to dedicate their personal resources to the collective (Constant et al. 1996). Indeed, this positive influence of group identification on knowledge exchange appears in several prior studies (Chiu et al. 2006; Ma and Agarwal 2007). Accordingly, we propose the following:

H3: The stronger a knowledge contributor’s group identification, the better the quality of the knowledge exchanged in an ENoP.

## Individual Level (Level 1): Knowledge Seeker Characteristics

Social status refers specifically to a person’s position of interpersonal influence and elevated standing in a group (Bourdieu 1984; Sorokin 1998) and accrues for people who possess certain attributes (e.g., competence or knowledge) valued by the members of a collective (Bourdieu 1984; Flynn 2003). A person’s ability to claim social status in a group is thus facilitated by other members’ beliefs that he or she possesses or provides something of unique value (Sutton and Hargadon 1996). For instance, social exchange behavior, such as giving and receiving help, can confer social status because people tend to be held in higher esteem if they appear to provide more or superior help than others (Flynn et al. 2006). Furthermore, lower-status people are attracted to persons of higher status (Kahn and Alexander 1971) and may strive to impress those with higher status to obtain recognition (Dino et al. 2008). The theory of power reduction also proposes that people seek to reduce their interpersonal status differences (Mulder 1971; Tajfel 1982). Because helping in general is strongly associated with higher status, lower-status members within a group might seek to improve their positions by pro viding knowledge to higher-status individuals (Van Leeuwen and Täuber 2011). In line with this reasoning, a request for knowledge from a high-status knowledge seeker creates an opportunity—particularly for members with lower status—to attract attention from higher-status members. In addition, in striving to improve their relative status position, knowledge contributors dedicate more effort to exchanging knowledge with high-status members, which results in higher-quality knowledge being exchanged. Thus, we propose:

H4: The higher a knowledge seeker’s social status in the network, the better the quality of the knowledge exchanged in an ENoP.

Furthermore, social capital literature reveals that an actor’s centrality in a network influences his or her ability to effectively share resources with others (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998; Tsai 2001; Uzzi 1997). Network centrality reflects the amount of interactions an actor has with others and determines the potential channels available for accessing knowledge in the future. This channel variety influences the availability and quality of information access and enhances the actor’s likelihood of obtaining desired knowledge (Desouza 2003; Hansen 1999). Tsai (2001) finds a significant positive link between an actor’s centrality and knowledgeenabled innovative capability. Furthermore, Cross and Cummings (2004) provide empirical evidence that centrality in a network increases a person’s ability to access knowledge from others. Thus, on the individual level and with respect to knowledge exchange in ENoP, we argue that more centrally embedded knowledge seekers have more variety in the channels available to request knowledge. As a result, they can obtain the requested knowledge from a relevant expert, which should increase the quality of the knowledge exchanged. Hence,

H5: The higher a knowledge seeker’s channel variety in the network, the better the quality of the knowledge exchanged in an ENoP.

Communication behavior in conventional face-to-face settings relies on uncertainty reduction and trust building through nonverbal cues as a means for interpersonal evaluations of others (Burgoon and Hale 1988; Burgoon and Walthers 1990). More information about partners reduces discomfort, and raises the level of affection (Walther et al. 2001). These effects are particularly relevant for impression formation among individuals and their decision to invest effort in communication and knowledge exchange (Berger and Calabrese 1975). A subjective sense of an interaction partner and his or her personality can be captured by the concept of presence, which “refers to the sense of existing or of ‘being there’ in a virtual space” (Schultze 2010, p. 437). In social media, social presence is of particular relevance; it refers to the “illusion of access to a remote or distant other, that is, being with” (Schultze 2010, p. 438). Because computer-mediated communications typically lack the symbols and cues available in face-to-face settings, social presence should be rather low, which likely leads to more uncertainty and less trust than traditional communications offer (Daft et al. 1987; Markus 1994). Therefore, various tactics exist to foster social presence in computer-mediated communication, including avatars (Bente et al. 2008; Schultze 2010), personal profiles (Ma and Agarwal 2007), and photographs (Walther et al. 2001). However, using such self-presentation functionalities that help others develop a sense of social presence is a voluntary activity in ENoP. Therefore, with respect to knowledge exchange, we argue that the more a knowledge seeker engages in activities to establish a social presence that others can perceive, the more trust and the less uncertainty the knowledge contributor will sense, which leads to a higher willingness to share and exchange higher-quality knowledge. Accordingly, we propose the following:

H6: The higher a knowledge seeker’s social presence in the network, the better the quality of the knowledge exchanged in an ENoP.

## Dyadic Interaction Level (Level 0): Relationship Characteristics

The norm of reciprocity is one of the most important moral rules in human value systems (Gouldner 1960; Ouchi 1980). In its most general form, it is based on two minimal demands: “(1) people should help those who helped them, (2) and people should not injure those who have helped them” (Gouldner 1960, p. 161). In organizational research, the norm of reciprocity has been applied extensively to explain individual behavior within exchange relationships (Robinson et al. 1994; Wayne et al. 1997). Social exchange theory argues that such reciprocity produces diffuse obligations among the parties involved in an exchange (Blau 1964) such that a person’s motivation to help others reflects a psychological contract that states that others will fairly discharge their own obligations in the future (Robinson and Rousseau 1994). If such contracts are not violated, reciprocal relationships help build trust, thereby stabilizing social interaction and resolving the problems of collective action (Blau 1964; Robinson and Rousseau 1994). The positive effects on trust due to intra-organizational resource exchanges and production innovation have been confirmed (Tsai and Ghoshal 1998). Moreover, existing studies on ENoP verify that knowledge exchange is facilitated by a strong sense of reciprocity (Chiu et al. 2006; Wasko and Faraj 2005). Therefore, people contribute knowledge to others in an ENoP based on trust that the favor will be reciprocated in the future or on a perceived moral obligation to give back in the context of an existing interactive relationship (Wasko and Faraj 2000). Accordingly, we hypothesize the following:

H7: The stronger the norm of reciprocity in a dyadic relationship between a knowledge seeker and a knowledge contributor, the better the quality of the knowledge exchanged in an ENoP.

Regarding the norm of reciprocity, prior research has also highlighted the importance of balance between all actors obligations in an exchange situation (Rousseau 1995; Shore and Barksdale 1998). In a balanced relationship, both participants are similarly obligated. In an unbalanced relationship, however, one partner is substantially more obligated than the other. Blau (1964) asserts that exchange partners strive for balance, which is evidenced by studies in the organizational domain (Tsui et al. 1997; Wayne et al. 1997). In addition, social psychology research indicates that the effect of balance on social exchanges is particularly relevant for relationships among peers, such as in ENoP (Clark 1981). For dyadic knowledge exchanges in ENoP, we argue that when the knowledge contributor’s obligations toward the knowledge seeker are higher than the knowledge seeker’s obligations toward the knowledge contributor, the contributor feels indebted for knowledge received in the past. In recurrent interactions, the knowledge contributor thus seeks to maximize the value of his or her contribution as a means to restore balance in the relationship. If instead the knowledge seeker’s obligations exceed those of the knowledge contributor, the contributor may perceive that the seeker has not reciprocated as expected and may be less motivated to spend time helping this knowledge seeker. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H8: The higher a knowledge contributor’s indebtedness in a dyadic relationship with a knowledge seeker, the better the quality of the knowledge exchanged in an ENoP.

Furthermore, many studies have found relationship strength to have a positive influence on the ease of knowledge exchange (Hansen 1999; McFadyen et al. 2009). Specifically, people with strong emotional attachments or those who communicate more frequently are more likely to engage in social exchanges than those who communicate infrequently or are not emotionally attached. The reason for this is that emotionally involved people are willing to exert more effort on behalf of another person (Lawler and Thye 1999; McEvily et al. 2003). Moreover, frequent communication can lead to more effective communication, such as through the development of relationship-specific heuristics (Uzzi 1997). In contrast, Granovetter (1973) theorizes that people close to a knowledge seeker (i.e., strong ties) might provide knowledge that the seeker already possesses, whereas those who are not close (i.e., weak ties) are more likely to offer novel information, suggesting the latter might be more important for knowledge exchange. Several studies have confirmed this so-called strength of weak ties phenomenon (Levin and Cross 2004; McFadyen et al. 2009), whereas others have demonstrated the significant impact of knowledge types on the influence of relationship strength for knowledge exchange (Hansen 1999; Reagans and McEvily 2003). With respect to ENoP, studies have shown strong ties to have a positive effect on the number of contributions (Chiu et al. 2006; Kang et al. 2010). Wasko et al. (2004) also predict that strong relationships have positive influences on contribution quality in ENoP. Controlling for the type of knowledge requested by the knowledge seeker in our model, we propose the following:

H9: The stronger the dyadic relationship between a knowledge seeker and a knowledge contributor in the network, the better the quality of the knowledge exchanged in an ENoP.

Sociological researchers have also observed that people tend to associate with others who are similar to themselves. Along these lines, the concept of homophily is defined as the degree to which interacting individuals are similar in certain attributes, such as their beliefs, opinions, or interests (Lazardsfeld and Merton 1954; Rogers 1995). As experiments in the psychology domain demonstrate, such similarities foster trust and improve behavioral predictability. As a result, ease of communication increases, thus smoothing the coordination of joint activities and interaction, particularly in voluntary settings (Levin et al. 2002). With respect to knowledge exchange through computer-mediated communication, Makela et al. (2007) empirically demonstrate that interpersonal similarities foster knowledge sharing in a multinational context. Moreover, studies of research collaborations have indicated that intellectual similarities significantly improve interpersonal interactions (Kraut et al. 1988; Landry et al. 1996). Therefore, we propose that intellectual closeness, as manifested in shared interests, communication frames, or opinions, positively influences the exchange of knowledge in ENoP and thus propose the following:

H10: The higher the intellectual closeness in a dyadic relationship between a knowledge seeker and a knowledge contributor, the better the quality of the knowledge exchanged in an ENoP.

## Method

## Case Description and Data Collection

Enterprise microblogging (EMB) offers a promising research context in that this technology can establish widespread ENoP for knowledge exchange in organizations. To test our hypotheses, we collected data from a leading multinational financial services provider that implemented an EMB platform to support communication and collaborative activities among employees in different departments, countries, and time zones. Participation in the network was limited to employees only, and access to the platform was possible through a specific web front end. Similar to Twitter, users voluntarily posted short messages that appeared in a chronological stream on users’ EMB interface. All posts were visible to everyone but could be tagged and filtered by keywords, users, and groups. Furthermore, customization of incoming message streams was enabled by a follower feature such that each user could define specific followers and could restrict incoming messages to these members. Each person’s name and profile picture were visible as part of message headers, so participation was not anonymous. Moreover, the EMB platform enabled users to organize into specific interest groups. In our case, the users had organized more than 300 interest groups related to their occupation, hobbies, or interests.

The data sample consisted of all messages posted on the EMB platform during the second half of 2010. Because the platform had been implemented in the second quarter of 2009, characteristics associated with the initial assimilation stage, such as use lags and unstable utilization (Rogers 1995), should not have affected user behavior in our sample. The dataset comprised 15,505 message postings by 1,921 different users who posted at least one message during the investigation period. Each user posted about eight messages on average, with a highly skewed distribution in which only 20 percent of users posted more than 83 percent of all messages. Approximately 80 percent of these users worked in the IT department, one of the largest internal departments of this financial institution. Moreover, across all age groups, participants ranged from new university hires to directors. The total posts numbered 9,623 initial messages and 5,882 replies. Furthermore, 4,361 initial messages sent by 559 users indicated the users’ intention to seek knowledge. Overall, 3,898 of these messages were addressed by 4,887 replies sent by 518 users, while the remaining 463 initial messages seeking knowledge received no reply. A closer analysis revealed that 137 users only sought knowledge without ever helping anyone in return, and 92 users only contributed without ever seeking knowledge. In addition, the dataset contained meta-information about the underlying group and network structures, user profile information, and user-specific settings, which we used to derive appropriate measures for our explanatory variables.

## Measurement Model

On the basis of our theoretical development, we refer to the individual characteristics of knowledge seekers and knowledge contributors and factors residing in their dyadic relationship as important antecedents of successful knowledge exchange and reconstruction in ESM-enabled ENoP.

As illustrated in Figure 4, we analyzed the content of all EMB messages sent during our main analysis period (August– December 2010) to gain insights about knowledge exchanges between knowledge seekers and contributors. Because direct questions signal explicit requests for knowledge from a seeker (Ram 1991), we focused on question–answer pairs. From a pragmatic linguistic point of view, the first and second part of the question–answer sequence are part of a socially agreedupon script for which an expected sequence of actions take place (Yule 1996). To measure the explanatory variables, such as the norm of reciprocity and social status, we considered activity-centered conversations for interactions on the EMB platform. These interactions require time to emerge, so we started collecting these data during a separate preanalysis period (May–July 2010).

In addition, we differentiated two types of explanatory variables: those that develop and vary over time, such as the norm of reciprocity and social status, and those that measure latent constructs that remain relatively stable over (a longer period of) time, such as habit of cooperation. To account for the time-dependent effects of the first type of variable, for every incoming answer A (where t indicates a point in time), we derived corresponding measures from the data collected between May (starting point of the pre-analysis phase) and the timestamp t. For the second type of explanatory variables, we employed all of the data available in our data sample (May– December) to extract representations that were as complete as possible for these latent constructs. That is, we temporally separated our assessment of the explanatory variables from the assessment of the dependent variables when conceptualizing our measurement model. As a result, we were able to address concerns of endogeneity and substantiate claims of causality (Granger 1969; Greene 2000).

## Dependent Variable at the Dyadic Interaction Level: Quality of Knowledge Exchanged

To assess the quality of the exchanged knowledge, we conducted a manual content analysis, which enabled us to make systematic inferences from the data to the context. In our directed content analysis (Hsieh and Shannon 2005), we predefined categories prior to the data investigation by following an established five-step process suggested by Morris (1994). First, we selected the unit of analysis, which determined the segments for the coding process. We identified question– answer pairs as an appropriate unit of analysis because they are elementary in conversation analysis and are identifiable by independent coders (Harwood and Garry 2003; Rourke et al. 2001). Second, based on Wasko and Faraj’s (2005) work, we developed our coding scheme to assess the quality of knowledge exchanged based on the quality of each response to a specific question in terms of its pragmatic value for subsequent action. To fully acknowledge social interaction and symbolic action using EMB as language, we also used an existing feedback mechanism that allowed users of the EMB platform to explicitly rate the quality of other members’ contributions. Third, because training improves coders familiarity with the scheme and reveals their reliability (Kolbe and Burnett 1991), two of the authors independently coded a training subset of 100 posted messages. We discussed the results and revised the coding scheme accordingly (Lombard et al. 2002) until we derived an acceptable final version (see Table 1; for details on the coding process see Appendix A). Fourth, we initiated an iterative process of sample coding and revisions and computed the Krippendorff’s alpha and Cohen’s kappa to assess the reliability of our coding scheme and the validity of our results (Lombard et al. 2002). As suggested in the literature, for both measures, we considered a value of more than .70 as evidence of the reliability of our measures (Dewever 2006, Neuendorf 2002). The whole process was repeated twice until an acceptable level of inter-coder agreement was reached (Morris 1994). Then, after the reliability of the coding scheme was approved, one author processed all posts in our dataset. Although reliability is sometimes computed for the coding of the complete dataset, it is an established approach to rely on a subset of data when the dataset is as large as in our case (Lombard et al. 2002).

Because we were interested in the quality of knowledge exchanged on the EMB platform, we considered all of the messages sent by knowledge seekers during the main analysis phase—namely, 2,005 question candidates. The answers and their assessed quality provided the observations for our dependent variable. On the individual level, we identified 302 unique knowledge seekers who sent at least one request and 265 different knowledge contributors who provided at least one answer.

## Explanatory Variables at the Dyadic Interaction Level (Level0): Relationship

Norm of Reciprocity. In ENoP, a dyadic interaction occurs when an actor responds to another’s posting (Ahuja et al. 2003). Such interactions form a social network, which comprises all conversations among participants on the EMB platform. In this network, we investigated norms of reciprocity as a result of reciprocal interactions between knowledge seeker A and knowledge contributor B. We analyzed whether members of the ENoP helped those who had helped them previously (Wasko et al. 2009) by looking for instances when actor A posted a question to which actor B replied, and then B posted a question that actor A answered. Thus, we defined reciprocity between a knowledge seeker A and a knowledge contributor B at time t as the count of all reciprocal interactions between A and B logged by the EMB platform before timestamp t.

Indebtedness. An indebtedness relationship exists when prior knowledge exchanges between two members of an ENoP cause one of the two participants to be substantially more obligated than the other (Rousseau 1995). From a knowledge seeker’s perspective, we defined the indebtedness of a knowledge seeker A toward a knowledge contributor B at time t as the evaluated contributions of A to B minus the evaluated contributions of B to A before t. To assess the contributions, we weighted each reply by the assessed quality of the knowledge exchanged.

Relationship Strength. In our measure of relationship strength we relied on Granovetter’s (1973) argument that strong relationships are characterized by a higher frequency of contact and friendship compared to weak relationships. Thus, we used the EMB platform’s follower feature to determine relationship strength for several reasons. First, if person A follows person B, the messages posted by person B implicitly receive more priority in A’s stream than those of others, which should increase the frequency of contact between A and B because A is more likely to read the messages of B and is thus more likely to respond. Second, if A voluntarily decides to follow B, it suggests that A has ideas or interests similar to those expressed by B (Fazeen et al. 2011). The asymmetrical structure of the follower feature led us to measure relationship strength between a knowledge seeker A and a knowledge contributor B at time t on a scale from 0 (weak) to 2 (strong) as follows: A does not follow B and B does not follow A (0), A follows B or vice versa (1), A and B follow each other (2).

Intellectual Closeness. To measure the degree to which knowledge seekers and knowledge contributors are close with respect to beliefs, opinions, and interests (Rogers and Kincaid 1981), we employed the EMB platform’s grouping function, which allows every ENoP member to organize themselves into interest groups. Membership in each group indicates some personal information that can be treated as social attributes visible to all members of the ENoP. Whenever two actors are members of the same group, we assume they share some underlying social attributes. Therefore, we used this information to compute a degree of intellectual closeness, which we assessed with a so-called affinity score. The affinity score corresponds to the number of overlapping group memberships between two individuals. Accordingly, we measured the intellectual closeness of a knowledge seeker and a knowledge contributor by the number of groups they share.

## Explanatory Variables at the Individual Level: Knowledge Seeker

Social Status. Especially in ESM-enabled ENoP, social status may be visible to other members of the network through features that automatically aggregate information about each user’s past behavior (Stuart et al. 2012). On the EMB platform we studied, the history of a user’s past contributions, the number of reposted messages, and message quality (assessed by users through “like” and “tagging” features) together provide specific information about an individual’s social status among the collective. Thus, we measured a knowledge seeker’s social status in the network according to the quality of his or her prior contributions on the platform (Rosenbaum and Shachaf 2010). Low-quality answers could also negatively influence social status, so we computed the average quality score of the replies sent by the focal actor prior to timestamp t (Chen et al. 2011).

Channel Variety. The variety of channels on which a knowledge seeker might rely was assessed by a centrality measure based on all dyadic interactions and relationships established through the follower feature. We computed a square network matrix to determine the number of different users with whom a knowledge seeker had interacted in the past independent of the total number of interactions (Ahuja et al. 2003; Izquierdo and Hanneman 2006). For example, a person who interacts 30 times with 20 individuals has a channel variety score of 20, while an individual exchanging 20 interactions with a single individual has channel variety of 1. In addition, we added the number of strong relationships a knowledge seeker possesses with others that were established through the follower feature even if the seeker had not actively interacted with those users in the past. Of the 302 knowledge seekers, only 46 had a centrality score greater than 5, which implies a high dispersion of channel variety among seekers. To account for this skewness, we log-transformed the variable.

Social Presence. The analyzed EMB platform offered several features that allowed users to receive additional information about other members’ personalities. Some information, such as age, gender, and profession, were automatically imported from the firms’ employee repository. However, information about members’ locations and interests depended on their voluntary disclosure. The information provided reflected members’ desire for closeness and connection with others (Biocca 1997; Schultze 2010). For each participant of the ENoP, we set an indicator variable equal to the number of features used to portray themselves: profile photo, interests, and location details. Thus, each member’s social presence score ranged between 0 (no features used) and 3 (all features used).

## Explanatory Variables at the Individual Level: Knowledge Contributor

Reputation. In a social media setting, reputation refers to the extent to which users can identify the standing of others based on certain features that automatically aggregate usergenerated information and signal trustworthiness. Informal and self-regulated feedback mechanisms can describe the past behaviors of specific community members. These mechanisms are usually based on peer evaluations, reviews, and ratings of past contributions (Stuart et al. 2012), and they help improve transparency about past behaviors and influence individual decisions about whether to exchange knowledge with strangers. For the EMB platform we analyzed, the history of contributions and the number of reposted messages were available to the collective, and the quality of knowledge exchanged also was made public by recipients who used the “like” or “tagging” features. Knowledge contributors with above-average scores enjoyed a higher reputation than others, which served as a reputation indicator. To assess reputation according to the trace and evaluation criteria of past actions and thereby determine a knowledge contributor’s future behavior, we calculated the score for the average quality of knowledge exchanged for each contributor (Chen et al. 2011).

Habit of Cooperation. To determine users’ habit of cooperation, we assumed that historical contributions helped each person build his or her skills for framing and translating knowledge for others over the EMB platform. Accordingly, we measured a knowledge contributor’s habit of cooperation by assessing the number of replies he or she made to others in the network. The descriptive analysis revealed a high dispersion in the number of messages sent among contributors, so we analyzed log-transformed observations to check for the effects of this skewness.

Group Identification. Social identity theory suggests that developing a group-level social identity is important for knowledge exchange, which is more difficult to establish in depersonalized computer-mediated communication environments (Bargh and McKenna 2004). Providing information within a group supports social classifications, which then enable people to develop a feeling of belongingness to the group (Ashforth and Mael 1989). Because the analyzed EMB platform allowed for the formation of groups, we assessed the group classification behavior of the participants in the ENoP to obtain a measure of each user’s group identification on the basis of his or her idiosyncratic characteristics. That is, for each member, we computed pairwise social affinity scores according to the underlying social attributes (e.g., interests, occupation) of each group. We calculated the overall group identification of a knowledge contributor by taking the average of the social affinity scores between the contributor and all other actors in the ENoP.

## Control Factors

To account for other influences on the quality of the knowledge exchanged, we included a robust set of controls: the specific type of knowledge exchanged, the type of request, the cultural closeness between the knowledge seeker and knowledge contributor, and the mother language of the two. The measurement of these additional factors is described in the following.

Type of Knowledge. Variance in the quality of knowledge exchanged may result from different types of questions, which can be traced back to the complexity and codifiability of the underlying types of knowledge (Hansen 1999). For example, questions that ask for an expert are easier to codify than questions asking for others’ opinions. In a second content analysis, we included dummy variables for the different types of questions to control for the type of knowledge exchanged. Analogous with the procedure applied to assess answer quality, we followed Morris’s (1994) content analysis approach and manually assigned each request to a predefined category derived from extant research (Morris et al. 2010). Then, we defined dummy variables for the four question types that occurred with highest frequency in our dataset: factual knowledge questions $( \mathrm { D y } _ { \mathrm { f a c t u a l \_ k n o w l e d g e } } ) .$ requests for others opinions $( \mathrm { D y } _ { \mathrm { o p i n i o n } } ) _ { : }$ requests for recommendations $( \mathrm { D y } _ { \mathrm { r e c o m m e n d a t i o n } } ) .$ , and questions asking for knowledge about people with specific characteristics $( \mathrm { D y } _ { \mathrm { s o c i a l \_ c o n n e c t i o n } } ) .$

Type of Request. Prior research has shown that whether a question targets a specific person might influence knowledge exchange (Evans et al. 2010). Directing a question to a specific member of an ENoP may discourage others from responding, which might restrict the number of potential contributors. However, targeted questions also can spur knowledge exchanges, because they highlight clear responsibilities and exert pressure on the target to provide an appropriate answer. All questions that included an “@” tag (e.g., @JaneDoe) were labeled as targeted questions, and we derived a relevant dummy variable $( \mathrm { D y } _ { \mathrm { t a r g e t e d \_ q u e s t i o n } } )$ for the type of request.

Cultural Closeness. The influence of cultural differences between people on social behavior has been confirmed by many studies from several domains. Despite the ongoing debate regarding Hofstede’s (1980) work, many studies on knowledge exchange behavior have produced statistically significant values for his dimensions (e.g., Jarvenpaa and Staples 2001; Leidner and Kayworth 2006). Therefore, to safeguard our results against misleading conclusions, we controlled for the influence of Hofstede’s cultural dimensions in our study. Accordingly, we measured cultural closeness (CC) between knowledge seekers and contributors based on Hofstede’s (1983) power distance (PD), individualism (ID), uncertainty avoidance (UA), and masculinity (MAS) dimensions. We assigned corresponding values to each member of the ENoP depending on his or her country of origin. To evaluate the cultural distance for each dyadic relationship between a knowledge seeker i and a knowledge contributor j, we then computed a Euclidean distance index reflecting the assigned cultural characteristics:

$$
C C _ {i j} = \sqrt {\left(P D _ {i} - P D _ {j}\right) ^ {2} + \left(I D _ {i} - I D _ {j}\right) ^ {2} + \left(U A _ {i} - U A _ {j}\right) ^ {2} + \left(M A S _ {i} - M A S _ {j}\right) ^ {2}}
$$

Using this Euclidian distance, we computed the cultural closeness between seeker i and contributor j in a fourdimensional space using the square root of the sum of the squared differences in the scores on each cultural dimension (Vermeulen and Barkema 2001).

Language. Finally, the most common language spoken by the members of the ENoP was English. Nonnative Englishspeaking knowledge seekers may be restricted in their ability to participate effectively (Carlile and Rebentisch 2003), so we controlled for this effect by including a dummy variable $\mathrm { ( D y _ { e n g } ) }$ equal to 1 for people from an English-speaking country and 0 otherwise.

## Empirical Analysis

We applied hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) as it takes the multilevel structure of our model into consideration (Klein and Kozlowski 2000; Raudenbush and Bryk 2002). Moreover, HLM allows for unbalanced data and does not require observation independence on different levels. This requirement is important for the approach we applied because our dataset contained multiple observations for some actors, and the number of observations among actors differed. Partitioning the variance of a dependent variable into multiple levels, HLM addresses the potential confounding variable interpretation by decomposing the effects on different levels. Moreover, this approach enabled us to control for levelspecific unobserved heterogeneity by including random-effect (or fixed-effect) terms as stand-ins for unobserved individual characteristics (Wooldridge 2002).

## Hypothesis Testing

Focusing on question–answer pairs as our unit of analysis and the respective quality of the knowledge exchanged, we estimated our theoretical model and the hypothesized relationships based on five nested models that reflect the underlying hierarchical structure of knowledge exchange in ENoP. Our basic motivation for estimating five different models was that we were not only interested in simultaneously investigating the influence of all factors on different levels in one model, but we were also interested in investigating which factors exert the greatest influence on our dependent variable: the factors of knowledge seekers or contributors, the factors related to their relationship, or even the factors related to the controls. Therefore, we stepwise-amended a simple baseline model (Model 1) with the specific factor groups defined in our theoretical development section (i.e., controls, relationship, knowledge seeker, knowledge contributor) to analyze how model fit would differ between them (Raudenbush and Bryk 2002). Accordingly, the baseline Model 1 included only random intercepts. Next, we obtained Model 2 by adding our control variables: type of knowledge, type of request, cultural closeness, and language. In Model 3, we regressed the quality of knowledge exchanged on the proposed dyadic-level antecedents, such as the norm of reciprocity and relationship strength, to verify our hypotheses about the dyadic interaction level (Level0). In Models 4 and 5, the intercept estimate obtained from the dyadic interactionlevel analysis served as the outcome variable regressed on individual-level (Level1) antecedents to assess the effects of the individual-level factors of the knowledge seeker (Model 4) and the knowledge contributor (Model 5) on the quality of knowledge exchanged. Moreover, to check the robustness of our results, we conducted an additional estimation including dummy variables for every calendar week to control for confounding time-dependent effects during our period of investigation (Wooldridge 2002). However, the estimates of this model showed no significant differences for the coefficients of our explanatory variables, thus providing further evidence for our empirical findings.

The parameters were estimated on the basis of a mixed-effects maximum likelihood regression procedure. We assessed tstatistics and calculated corresponding p-values for the path coefficients (Rabe-Hesketh and Skrondal 2008). Moreover, we evaluated and compared the five different regression models using established goodness-of-fit test statistics (McCullagh and Nelder 1989). Specifically, we computed log-likelihood values, Akaike’s information criterion (AIC) (Box et al. 1994), and the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) (Schwarz 1978). We also conducted likelihood ratio tests to compare the mixed models with the standard regression models without cross-classified random effects (Rabe-Hesketh and Skrondal 2008). On the basis of the results of these tests, we rejected the null hypothesis at the .01 significance level for all models. The mixed models provided better fit than the standard regression models without random effects. Furthermore, the significantly decreasing goodnessof-fit measures confirmed the validity of our proposed model (Model 5) in comparison with the baseline (Model 1) and control (Model 2) models because the goodness-of-fit measures address not only the models’ accuracy but also their complexity.

The incorporation of the control variables (Model 2) already explained about 14 percent of the total variance of the quality of knowledge exchanged. As Table 3 depicts, the results clearly indicated that the type of question accounted for a significant amount of variation in the quality of knowledge exchanged. Moreover, the high negative and significant coefficients of $\mathrm { \Delta D y _ { t a r g e t e d \_ q u e s t i o n } }$ showed that implicitly restricting the number of contributors by asking targeted questions had a strong negative effect on the resulting quality of knowledge exchanged.

Adding dyadic-level antecedents to our model (Model 3) improves the model fit and significantly increases the explained portion of the total variance (from 14% to 19%), which can be attributed to the increasing explained variance at the dyadic level (from 13% to 19%). ## Discussion and Implications

Although previous research in this area has focused on individuals’ knowledge-contribution behavior (e.g., Chiu et al. 2006; Lee et al. 2011; Ma and Agarwal 2007), existing research approaches fall short when it comes to incorporating the perspectives of both knowledge seekers and contributors as well as the relationship between them in communication acts. Furthermore, they often treat knowledge as an objectlike entity that can be transferred independently from the communication act itself. However, ESM-enabled ENoP are socially constructed linguistic entities based on an intersubjective understanding about conditions and rules that allow for effective communication. Thus, communication “is no longer an act of making something common between people” (Goldkuhl and Lyytinen, 1982, p. 14) but consists of speech acts and interpretations of the knowledge exchanged. That is, in a question–answer dialogue, knowledge seekers and knowledge contributors interpret the messages in this act and assign meaning and value to the knowledge in a sensemaking process (e.g., what the knowledge seeker understood as evolving in the communication act, which can then be used for future action) (Goldkuhl and Lyytinen 1982). Thus, our conceptualization of the quality of knowledge exchanged is different from the knowledge-as-object view, which argues that knowledge can be observed and transferred. Rather, an individual’s willingness to respond to knowledge requests in ESM-enabled ENoP is based on intersubjective perceptions about the knowledge seeker’s characteristics, and the communication activity-based interpretations the knowledge seeker makes define the quality of knowledge for future actions from a pragmatic point of view. Thus, the objective of this study was to identify and understand the social antecedents of knowledge exchange in ENoP on the individual and dyadic interaction levels.

Accordingly, we developed and empirically tested a multilevel model of knowledge exchange in an ENoP, focusing on characteristics of the knowledge seeker, the knowledge contributor, and their dyadic relationship. We applied an HLM approach, which enabled us to analyze variations on the individual and dyadic levels separately in a single research model. Our results provide support for the cross-classified model, in which the dyadic knowledge exchanges are nested in individual-level factors from the knowledge seeker’s and the knowledge contributor’s perspective as well as relational characteristics on the dyadic interaction level. Thus, this work derives several theoretical and practical implications for new forms of symbolic action and their impact on the knowledge-exchange process in virtual settings. In particular, we gained new insights into the exchange process within ENoP, which helps to improve the design of ESM platforms in virtual work environments.

In particular, and contrary to the focus of prior research (e.g., Chiu et al. 2006; Lee et al. 2011; Ma and Agarwal 2007; Wasko et al. 2009), according to our study, the knowledge contributor’s characteristics seem to play a subordinate role with respect to knowledge exchange in ENoP. In particular, our results illustrate that a knowledge contributor’s identification with the collective as well as his or her motivational factors (e.g., reputation) do not significantly influence the quality of knowledge exchanged. Perhaps interpersonal knowledge exchange is primarily triggered by knowledge seekers who submit requests to the network. Potential knowledge contributors then voluntarily determine how much time and effort they are willing to spend to fulfill this knowledge request. It is reasonable to assume that this decision depends on the contributor’s social relationship with the knowledge seeker; that is, this relationship depends on previous experiences and the personal information available to the knowledge contributor. Accordingly, we found that the knowledge contributor’s dyadic relationship with a knowledge seeker is more important than individual-level factors, such as identification and closeness with the community. Nevertheless, ENoP members are still motivated by self-interest, such as seeking to enhance their reputation. Our empirical results confirm that contributors select the recipients to whom they transfer knowledge to improve their reputation in ENoP (Constant et al. 1996).

Furthermore, our study provides empirical evidence that knowledge seekers’ characteristics might be more important in determining knowledge exchanges than the individual characteristics of knowledge contributors. In particular, our results yield new insights into how innovative features, such as the follower, tagging, rating, or profile functionalities, could be used by members of an ESM-enabled ENoP to express additional information about whom they are interacting with (Stuart et al. 2012) or how useful the knowledge transferred actually was. In this regard, ESM technologies provide new forms of symbolic action to express knowledge seekers’ individual factors, such as social presence, social status, and the belief that one has access to another’s intelligence (Biocca et al. 2003). Although, these factors are important for understanding the antecedents of successful knowledge exchange in ENoP, corresponding effects have largely been neglected so far, so our study makes several important contributions.

First, we applied the concept of social presence to the specific case of ESM-enabled ENoP and investigated the influence of knowledge seekers’ self-presentation within computermediated knowledge exchange. In particular, we have conceived an individual’s self-presentation as the voluntary use of specific features, such as tagging or profile functionalities, to provide members of an ENoP with valuable information about whom they are interacting with (Stuart et al. 2012). As our empirical results show, knowledge seekers’ social presence is a critical factor in knowledge exchange: our findings demonstrate that the more a knowledge seeker engages in symbolic action to establish his or her social presence in the network, the more trust and the less uncertainty there is for the knowledge contributor, thus improving the perceived quality of the knowledge exchanged.

Second, based on the level of participation and the quality of past contributions, a knowledge seeker’s reputation seems to provide important information for knowledge contributors, thereby amplifying the chance of future reciprocation (Donath 1999). Indeed, our results confirm that reputation is an important asset that a network member can leverage to achieve social status. In turn, as knowledge contributors strive to improve their social status, they dedicate more effort to sharing knowledge with high-status others, leading to higher-quality knowledge being exchanged. By highlighting the key role of knowledge seekers’ social status in ESMenabled ENoP, this study suggests that communication technologies that offer effective language for self-presentation as well as reputation features can promote higher-quality knowledge exchanged within the community.

Beyond the individual level, we also analyzed how the characteristics of the relationship between a knowledge seeker and a knowledge contributor influence knowledge-exchange processes in ENoP. As our empirical results demonstrate, norms of reciprocity are central for sustaining and regulating cooperative behavior in ENoP. The questions of whether reciprocity matters in professional interorganizational communities or if such environments are characterized by utilitarian structures (Faraj and Johnson 2010) have remained unanswered. One empirical study of users of a firm-hosted forum shows that forum members are often more interested in firm recognition than in individual recognition or reciprocity (Jeppesen and Frederiksen 2006). Similarly, Wasko and Faraj (2005) confirm that contributors in professional online forums have little expectation of their actions being reciprocated. In contrast, our results indicate that an individual’s motivation to exchange knowledge depends on trustworthiness (Blau 1964) and the assumption that the other party will discharge his or her reciprocal obligations in the future (Robinson and Rousseau 1994). Thus, our findings mirror results from studies in face-to-face settings (Putnam 1995) and research on public online communities (Faraj and Johnson 2010) that suggest reciprocity is critical for sustaining supportive relationships and collective action. By demonstrating the positive effect of dyadic reciprocity (Coleman 1990; Putnam 2000) on the quality of knowledge exchanged in ENoP, we extend previous reflections on the importance of generalized reciprocity, which suggest that individual contributions can be reciprocated not by the recipient directly but by a third party (Flynn 2005).

The importance of finding a balance between the obligations of actors in an ENoP can be further highlighted by the significant positive effect of the seeker’s indebtedness on the quality of the knowledge exchanged. Our findings verify the assumption that exchange partners strive for balance in their relationship and that an imbalance leads to efforts to restore that balance. Regarding the norm of reciprocity, obligations are important for binding people together, especially in voluntary contexts without formal contractual relationships (Tsui et al. 1997). That is, people participate because of their perceived moral obligation to give something back to the network (Wasko and Faraj 2005). Our findings on the dyadic interaction level confirm that this principle of obligation applies to the community as a whole and is also relevant for each specific relationship. A lack of balance in fulfilling obligations might lead to indebtedness, with negative longterm consequences (Blau 1964). For example, in nonanonymous internal enterprise contexts, members can count on those whom they have helped before when they request knowledge from the collective (Cross and Sproull 2004). Since knowledge work requires joint problem solving and sensemaking, providing knowledge to others may be a valuable investment to meet one’s own future needs (Brown and Duguid 2001).

In our analysis of the tie strength concept for relationships between individuals who exchange knowledge in ENoP, we found evidence of the strength of weak ties (Granovetter 1973) in terms of the quality of knowledge exchanged over the EMB platform. That is, we uncovered a significant negative influence of relationship strength and intellectual closeness on the quality of knowledge exchanged. These results match previous work proposing that social distance and infrequent relationships lead to valuable knowledge because they provide more access to novel nonredundant information (Granovetter 1973; Krackhardt 1992). However, and contrary to our expectations, our empirical results did not confirm claims that strong ties can improve knowledge exchanges (e.g., Hansen 1999). Previous investigations analyzing knowledge exchanges in ENoP have produced mixed results (Chiu et al. 2006; Kang et al. 2010). For example, Wasko et al. (2004) propose that strong relationships might have positive influences on contribution quality at the network level. With respect to knowledge exchange in computer-mediated environments, Makela et al. (2007) empirically demonstrate that interpersonal similarities based on interests, activities, beliefs, and opinions foster knowledge sharing in professional contexts. Our findings might have arisen because strongly tied coworkers could be aware of their mutual knowledge and thus request knowledge from others more directly, such as by phone or through e-mail conversations, rather than through community-based ESM tools. In this case, knowledge seekers predominantly use ESM-enabled ENoP to find answers to questions for which the potential knowledge contributor is unknown.

Finally, from a practical perspective, our empirical investigation provides notable implications for practitioners that regard ESM platforms as a possible solution for improving knowledge-exchange processes in organizations. Organizations invest significant resources to develop platforms to improve collaboration between colocated employees. Because ENoP facilitate knowledge exchange among distributed coworkers (Constant et al. 1996), which can foster new ideas and innovation (Teigland and Wasko 2003), EMB platforms are a promising means to improve information sharing in organizations. However, prior work on ENoP has focused on factors like group identification and reciprocity as drivers of knowledge exchange, which fall outside the control of system designers or administrators. In contrast, the specific features described and analyzed in this study are widely available on most ESM platforms, so these findings provide valuable insights for the development of systems that further support detailed virtual self-presentations. As we have highlighted, members of an ENoP form impressions about other members’ personalities using various information, such as the quality of their contributions, profile information, and group affiliations. However, conventional reputation or ranking systems cannot capture such multifaceted information about others’ personalities, which indicates the need for new tools that can facilitate richer, easier impression formations in computermediated environments. For example, reputationmanagement mechanisms should not only focus on the number of messages a community member has posted to the collective but should also address the quality of these posts. The latter measure has been established in public questionand-answer portals, such as Yahoo! Answers, and provides an effective means to enhance knowledge exchanges (Su et al. 2007). With our results, we suggest implementing such features in ESM platforms to improve the quality of knowledge exchanged in ENoP.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

Our research has some limitations that should be addressed in further work. First, although we deliberately chose a single study site to gain a comprehensive view of knowledge exchange on a single ESM platform, our theoretical insights about the nature of knowledge exchange in ENoP may apply only to established technology-oriented intra-organizational ENoP. As such, additional research should test the generalizability of our findings beyond our specific study context. For example, studies might explicitly investigate ENoP with respect to intra- and interorganizational ties, noting that internal ties may help build group solidarity, whereas external ties might better enhance the number of knowledge channels available (Hansen 1999). Replicating this study in different departments or industries could also help reveal the generalizability of our results.

Second, we intentionally limited our focus to factors at the individual and dyadic interaction levels. In line with our theoretical discussion of the research gap and our illustration in Figure 1, we did not analyze the influence of factors at the network level, such as information intensity (Glazer 1991), innovativeness (Hurley and Hult 1998), or organization culture (Szulanski 1996). By providing new insights into knowledge exchange in ENoP on the two lower levels, the results of our work should provide a solid basis for further research in this area that includes network-level variables as well.

Third, our empirical investigation was based on archival data enriched by qualitative data that we obtained through a comprehensive content analysis. While existing research has shown that this approach offers several benefits compared to other data collection techniques (e.g., Boyd et al. 1993; Calantone and Vickery 2009), it also logically follows that all constructs and measurements are solely based on the data available on the platform. A further limitation, then, is that we were not able to control for factors in participants’ lives outside the EMB platform. It is reasonable to assume, for example, that at least some of the ENoP platform members already had social connections with others before the ENoP was established. Future researchers should thus consider extending our research by including additional research methods, such as participant surveys or interviews. That way, controls could be included, for example, for ENoP members offline connections or for social ties developed through other electronic communication channels, such as e-mail. Moreover, other additional variables that are difficult to measure based on archival data (e.g., individual levels of altruism) and need to be measured differently may contribute to our research model.

Fourth, although we took into account the principle of temporal separation when operationalizing our variables, the extraction of the latent variables was relatively static, so we cannot really theorize about how these characteristics emerged. Studies based on a longitudinal research design could help confirm the directionality of the influences we identified. Moreover, an investigation with a longer preanalysis period could expand the stability of our measurement model and therefore further enrich our findings.

## Conclusion

Innovative technologies like ESM offer a promising solution to improving knowledge exchange among employees, particularly in distributed work environments. However, even as many organizations implement ESM platforms in their communication infrastructure, they continue to struggle to establish fully active ENoP (O’Dell and Hubert 2011). Consequently, we need a deeper understanding of the microfoundations of user behavior in ENoP.

To capture social antecedents of knowledge exchange in ENoP, we conceptualized and empirically tested a multilevel model of the characteristics of knowledge seekers, knowledge contributors, and the relationship between them. In contrast to prior findings, our empirical results reject the notion that effective knowledge exchange in ENoP depends solely on the knowledge contributor. Rather, we found that knowledge seekers’ characteristics and relational factors are crucial in driving knowledge exchanges in ESM-enabled ENoP. Therefore, our results augment and extend prior research in an important information systems field-namely, computermediated knowledge exchange and collaboration in distributed work environments. The rapid growth of ESM has not gone unnoticed by communication or IS scholars, yet most prior work relies on student populations or considers an advertising or marketing context rather than focusing on ESM that establish ENoP in a professional context. This study contributes to theory and practice by focusing on intraorganizational knowledge exchange as an important organizational capability. Our results deepen our understanding of the interrelated multilevel antecedents of knowledge exchange in ENoP from a pragmatic point of view.

## About the Authors

Roman Beck is Full Professor for IT management and leadership at IT University of Copenhagen, Denmark. His research focuses on the role of IT services sourcing, services management, and services engineering with a special focus on IS outsourcing, social media, and virtualization. He is interested in institutional logics of organizations, organizational mindfulness, and awareness. Roman serves as a senior editor for Journal of Information Technology Theory and Application and The Data Base for Advances in Information Systems, and as an associate editor for Business and Information Systems Engineering, and has published over 100 peerreviewed journal and conference articles in outlets including MIS Quarterly, Journal of Information Technology, Communications of the AIS, Information Technology and People, Business and Information Systems Engineering, the proceedings of the International Conference on Information Systems.

Immanuel Pahlke is a professional management consultant working for a leading consultancy firm in the field of risk and finance. Previously, Immanuel worked as a researcher at the E-Finance Lab and received his Ph.D. from Goethe University Frankfurt, Germany. His research interests are in information and knowledge management as well as financial risk management. His articles have been published in journals such as Journal of Information Technology, Business and Information Systems Engineering, and Journal of Business Economics, as well as several conference proceedings such as the International Conference on Information Systems and the European Conference on Information Systems.

Christoph Seebach is manager of advisory financial services at PricewaterhouseCoopers. He received his Ph.D. from Goethe University Frankfurt, Germany. His research interests are in the areas of social media, business agility, and knowledge management, focusing specifically on social behavior in virtual communities. Christoph’s articles have been published in journals such as International Journal of Business Intelligence as well as in several conference proceedings such as the International Conference on Information Systems, the European Conference on Information Systems, and the Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences.
