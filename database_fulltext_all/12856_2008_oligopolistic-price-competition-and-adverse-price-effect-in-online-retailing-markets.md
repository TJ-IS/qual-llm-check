## a b s t r a c t

The majority of theoretical vertical differentiation models in the literature derive equilibrium prices that exhibit what we call an “adverse price effect:” for a low quality <sup>fi</sup>rm, the equilibrium price may decrease when the product quality increases. This seemingly counterintuitive theoretical result has received little attention in the literature. In order to check whether this result is simply an artifact of model assumptions (such as a duopoly under absence of differentiation costs), we present an oligopoly model with a general cost structure, and derive closed-form solutions for two special cases. Our model shows that the adverse price effect continues to hold in a more general setting and we derive conditions under which such an adverse price effect occurs. We then attempt to <sup>fi</sup>nd empirical evidence of this phenomenon using data from the online retailing (e-tailing) industry where e-tailers selling identical products mainly differentiate with their service offerings. Our analytical and empirical results offer guidelines to e-tailers on how to price their products and decide their service offerings. The results may also have implications for <sup>fi</sup>rms in other industries that are characterized by vertical differentiation.

## 1. Introduction

The last three decades have seen a proliferation of research in the area of vertical differentiation. Starting with the seminal paper of Gabszewicz and Thisse [8], vertical differentiation models have been developed and applied in many different situations. The theoretical models all share the following common features: the industry is characterized by duopolistic competition and <sup>fi</sup>rms can differentiate their product in terms of one or more “vertical dimensions.” A vertical dimension is an attribute for which every consumer prefers to have more rather than less of it, a common example being “product quality.” As the consumer derives a positive utility from an increased presence of the attribute, she is also willing to pay more for it. The majority of the models derive equilibrium prices that exhibit a feature which has received very little attention: when the lower quality <sup>fi</sup>rm increases its product quality, it may have to lower its price. When this happens, we say that an adverse price effect occurs. Hence, when the <sup>fi</sup>rm incurs an additional cost for improving the quality, the <sup>fi</sup>nal pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin will get squeezed hard. Such phenomenon runs counter to popular belief that a better product will improve the pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin because one can charge a higher price.

A close examination of the model setups in the existing literature reveals that some of the simplifying assumptions of the models may explain why the adverse price effect is always present in those models. For example, in Tirole [22, p. 296], the differentiation cost is assumed to be zero. Relaxing the most common assumptions of the existing models, we present a general vertical differentiation model. Consistent with early <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that prices depend on the competitive intensity (i.e., quality differentiation) between <sup>fi</sup>rms. However, the high and low quality <sup>fi</sup>rm's quality levels have different impacts on the prices, even though they may cause the same amount of change in the quality differentiation. Under certain conditions, an increase in the quality for the low quality <sup>fi</sup>rm can result in a decrease in its price. Our model also predicts that in certain situations, prices will go up when the low quality <sup>fi</sup>rm improves its quality, con<sup>fi</sup>rming what one would normally expect. The richness of the results of our model seems to better correspond to what one observes in practice for vertically differentiated products.

This paper seeks to systematically derive conditions under which the adverse price effect occurs. We extend the analysis of the adverse price effect from two perspectives. First, we investigate whether there is a theoretical basis for the adverse price effect in a competitive situation that is less restrictive than the models in the literature. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we extend the model of duopoly that is mainly used in the literature to an oligopoly, and rather than ignoring costs of differentiation (or assuming a very simple form of the cost function), we incorporate a general cost function in our model. In this setting we derive conditions for the existence of the adverse price effect and show in which situations one is likely to encounter this effect. For the duopoly and triopoly cases, explicit conditions are obtained, whereas in the general oligopoly case, we derive an implicit condition for the existence of the adverse price effect.

Second, we empirically test whether there is evidence of such an adverse price effect. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we test for the existence of the effect in the online retailing (e-tailing) industry, which is clearly an oligopolistic setting. In addition, retailers (etailers) in the e-tailing industry mainly differentiate along the dimension of service quality [10,18,26]. This service differentiation is believed to affect a consumer's purchase decision and lead to willingness to pay a price premium [17].

The main contribution of this research is two folds. First, from the theoretical perspective, by presenting a general model, we explicitly identify the conditions under which the adverse price effect will occur. The conditions identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this general setting makes the research model more relevant for practice. The second contribution is that the research empirically con<sup>fi</sup>rms that the adverse price effect indeed exists in the e-tailing industry. Although online retailing has been around for a decade, the competitive landscape is continuously changing, suggesting that the industry has not reached a long term equilibrium. Therefore, e-tailers need to position themselves accordingly. The conditions we identify help e-tailers better understand the competitive situation they are in, which can guide them to tailor their strategies. The combination of our theoretical results and the empirical evidence could help <sup>fi</sup>rms decide whether or not improving their product quality will be pro<sup>fi</sup>table by evaluating whether the conditions for an adverse price effect are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. After reviewing the literature on (mainly vertical) product differentiation in Section 2, we present an oligopolistic model with a general cost structure and discuss the conditions of the adverse price effects in Section 3. Section 4 discusses the implications for a special case (duopoly) and formulates a set of hypotheses that will be tested in a more general setting. Section 5 conducts empirical analyses using cross-sectional data collected from online retailers. Finally, Section 6 concludes and offers directions for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background

The theoretical model of competition we develop is based on the vertical differentiation model in a one-dimensional setting. Some of the classical work include Gabszewicz and Thisse [8] and Shaked and Sutton [20], who show that competing <sup>fi</sup>rms will choose to locate at the extreme ends of the quality spectrum to reduce price competition. Moorthy [13] extends the basic model by incorporating a quadratic differentiation cost and demonstrates that in equilibrium <sup>fi</sup>rms choose products that are differentiated.

Many researchers (for example, [7,14,5,23,25]) have subsequently studied this game under different settings. In the Economides [7] and Neven and Thisse [14] models of twodimensional differentiation, <sup>fi</sup>rms <sup>fi</sup>rst choose product (consisting of two characteristics with one horizontal dimension and one vertical) and subsequently choose price. Both models show that differentiation will still only occur in one dimension; however, under different conditions, it can occur in the horizontal or vertical dimension. Horizontal-vertical differentiation models have also been used to analyze retail channels. Coughlan and Soberman [4] use such a model to analyze under which conditions a manufacturer should establish outlet stores, where the vertical dimension measures the service a consumer enjoys from the retailer and the horizontal dimension represents location.

Besides establishing long-term differentiation strategies along non-price dimensions, the above product differentiation models also imply the adverse price effect: in the short term, prices may drop if the low quality <sup>fi</sup>rm increases its quality level. More formally, we say that an adverse price effect occurs whenever $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial p _ { i } ^ { * } < 0 , } { \partial s _ { i } } < 0 , } \end{array}$ where $p _ { i } { ^ { * } }$ is the equilibrium price and $s _ { i }$ the quality level of a <sup>fi</sup>rm i. For example, in models with zero differentiation cost and a covered market (such as [22,2]), the adverse price effect seems always present. In models with zero differentiation cost but where the market is not covered (such as [5,25]), the adverse price effect exists for the low quality <sup>fi</sup>rm when the quality differentiation between the two <sup>fi</sup>rms is small. In Moorthy's model with a quadratic cost structure, the adverse price effect is present when both the differentiation cost and the quality differentiation between the two <sup>fi</sup>rms are small. In the model by Vandenbosch and Weinberg [23], price always decreases with the increased quality under both the asymmetric and the dominated characteristics competition. Table 1 summarizes some of the representative differentiation models as well as the model assumptions. Except for Bresnahan [3], all models listed in Table 1 study a duopoly setting without empirical tests. Bresnahan [3] studied a general oligopoly model with non-zero differentiation costs, but failed to derive explicit expressions for the equilibrium prices. In the empirical part, however, he did show a negative coef<sup>fi</sup>cient (which resembles the adverse price effect) for the quality variables in his econometric analyses. He interpreted this counterintuitive sign as a result of collinearity with another quality variable. Although the models in Table 1 can be proven to exhibit what we call the adverse price effect, there has been little systematic research in the literature so far about this phenomenon, with the exception of Doraszelski and Draganska [6] who report on a similar phenomenon in a model more closely related to a horizontal than vertical differentiation model.

## 3. Theory of the adverse price effect

In order to investigate the conditions for the existence of the adverse price effect, we present a general vertical differentiation model in an oligopolistic setting where etailers compete on price as well as the positioning of their quality offerings. We model the decision choice of the competitors using a two-stage sequential game, in which <sup>fi</sup>rms <sup>fi</sup>rst decide the quality they will provide, then their product prices. We view such decisions as sequential because price changes can be made relatively easily, whereas changing (perceived) service quality involves more operational adjustments that may take a little longer. Since the purpose of this paper is to understand how a quality choice affects price, our equilibrium analysis focuses on the price competition stage. In other words, an e-tailer's product position with regard to service quality is assumed to be <sup>fi</sup>xed.

We assume there are n <sup>fi</sup>rms who supply a product that differs only in the quality dimension; e.g., in online markets many retailers sell an identical product, but differ in the retail services they offer bundled with the purchase. A <sup>fi</sup>rm is indexed by $i ( i { = } 1 , 2 , . . . . , n )$ and its service quality is denoted by $s _ { i \cdot }$ Without loss of generality, we assume $\scriptstyle { s _ { 1 } < s _ { 2 } < \ldots ~ s _ { n } } .$ . The cost of supplying a product with quality s is the same for all <sup>fi</sup>rms and is denoted by c(s). Customers have perfect information about <sup>fi</sup>rms' qualities and are segmented by their willingness to pay for quality, which we denote by θ, assumed to be uniformly distributed between \_ andθ θ̄. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne $( { \bar { \theta } } - { \underline { { \theta } } } )$ as the range of consumer quality sensitivity. We also refer to this range as consumer heterogeneity with respect to quality. The utility function of a type θ customer is linear and expressed as $U _ { \theta } ( s ) \mathop { = } \theta s - p ,$ , where $p$ is the price of the product with a quality s.

## 4. Hypotheses from the Duopoly case

We now present the closed-form equilibrium prices for the duopoly model. We investigate how these equilibrium prices will change for different levels of quality. Based on the insights obtained from the duopoly model, we propose a set of hypotheses that will be validated in a more general oligopolistic setting in the next section.

The duopoly model turns out to be a direct extension of Tirole [22] by adding variable differentiation costs. Similar to Tirole, we make the following two assumptions:

Assumption 1. $\begin{array} { r } { 2 \underline { { \theta } } - \overline { { \theta } } < \frac { c ( s _ { 2 } ) - c ( s _ { 1 } ) } { s _ { 2 } - s _ { 1 } } < 2 \overline { { \theta } } - \underline { { \theta } } . } \end{array}$ . This assumption <sup>- -</sup> <sup>-</sup>says, roughly, that the marginal cost of quality is bounded from below and above. Otherwise, in the price equilibrium, one of the <sup>fi</sup>rms faces no demand and charges the marginal cost $c ( s _ { i } )$ , which is not an interesting case.

Given the above two assumptions, we can solve the equilibrium prices from Eq. (7) explicitly.

As in other prior vertical differentiation models, undifferentiated <sup>fi</sup>rms $\left( s _ { 1 } = s _ { 2 } \right)$ charge their marginal cost and make no pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Hence, in the long term, <sup>fi</sup>rms will choose different levels of service quality. Previous research (for example, [13,20,22]) have shown that <sup>fi</sup>rms can exhibit maximum or partial differentiation along the quality dimension depending on the cost structure of providing quality. In this paper, we are not interested in deriving the explicit long-term quality equilibrium; rather we seek to understand the effects of the quality on <sup>fi</sup>rm's pricing strategies, and whether these effects are empirically supported.

From Eq. (9), we observe that a <sup>fi</sup>rm's price choice depends on its own quality offering and on the quality difference $\left( s _ { 1 } - s _ { 2 } \right)$ with its competitor. Hence we can formulate our <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis:

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 } } .$ There is a relationship between a <sup>fi</sup>rm's price and quality level.

Hypothesis $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 }$ is a rather general hypothesis and could be tested by models other than the one developed in this paper. Formal testing of this hypothesis is necessary since, if not supported by the data, there would be no support for the theory developed analytically and the more speci<sup>fi</sup>c hypotheses below become irrelevant.

We now examine the effects of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's quality position on its price choices. It can be seen that

$$
\frac {\partial p _ {2} ^ {*}}{\partial s _ {2}} = \frac {2}{3} c ^ {\prime} (s _ {2}) + \frac {2 \overline {{\theta}} - \underline {{\theta}}}{3} \neq \frac {\partial p _ {1} ^ {*}}{\partial s _ {1}} = \frac {2}{3} c ^ {\prime} (s _ {1}) - \frac {\overline {{\theta}} - 2 \underline {{\theta}}}{3}.
$$

Therefore, we formulate our second hypothesis as:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } .$ The relationship between price and quality is different depending on whether a <sup>fi</sup>rm provides a high or low quality product.

This is expected because a <sup>fi</sup>rm should be able to charge more for a “better product,” especially since the <sup>fi</sup>rm will incur a higher cost for providing the extra quality. Note also that $\frac { \partial p _ { 2 } ^ { * } } { \partial s _ { 1 } } \neq - \frac { \partial p _ { 2 } ^ { * } } { \partial s _ { 2 } } ;$ : in both cases the degree of differentiation $\left( { { s _ { 1 } } - { s _ { 2 } } } \right)$ changes by the same amount, but the overall effect on price is different depending on whether the change is caused by an increase in $s _ { 1 }$ or $s _ { 2 } .$ The same holds true for ${ p _ { 1 } } ^ { * }$ . We call Eq. (10) the adverse price effect in a duopoly environment. The adverse price effect will occur when the marginal cost of providing service by the low service provider is small compared to the amount of consumer heterogeneity and its existence can be explained as follows. When the consumer heterogeneity is large (for example θ̄–2θN0) and the marginal cost of quality does not increase too quickly with quality,<sup>5</sup> both <sup>fi</sup>rms will have positive demand (i.e., Assumption 1 is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed). By improving its service quality, <sup>fi</sup>rm 1 threatens to take away <sup>fi</sup>rm $2 ^ { \prime } s$ most price sensitive customers. In order to maintain its market share, <sup>fi</sup>rm 2 then cuts its price. Since <sup>fi</sup>rm 2 has a much higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin, it can do so much more aggressively, hence triggering intense price competition. In order to protect its existing market share, <sup>fi</sup>rm 1 will be forced to cut its own price as well. A similar phenomenon was observed in a segmented duopoly market with specialty and general-purpose products [6]. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 3 } .$ For product categories where consumer heterogeneity with respect to service is high, the price of the low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm will decrease when it increases its service level.

Finally, we notice that

$$
\frac {\partial p _ {2} ^ {*}}{\partial s _ {2}} - \frac {\partial p _ {1} ^ {*}}{\partial s _ {1}} = \frac {2}{3} [ c ^ {\prime} (s _ {2}) - c ^ {\prime} (s _ {1}) ] + (\overline {{\theta}} - \underline {{\theta}}).\tag{11}
$$

This means that the additional service premium a high service e-tailer can expect (relative to the low service e-tailer) increases as the consumer heterogeneity with respect to service $\left( \mathrm { i . e . , } \bar { \theta } - \underline { { { \theta } } } \right)$ increases. So we arrive at our last hypothesis: ce sensitivity.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 4 } .$ The additional service premium charged by high quality <sup>fi</sup>rms increases when the range of consumer heterogeneity increases.

## 5. Empirical evidence

We are interested in <sup>fi</sup>nding empirical support for the hypotheses presented in the previous section, especially the adverse price effect. From our theoretical results, the adverse price effect is most likely to occur in situations where the cost of differentiation is not increasing very fast and when consumer heterogeneity with respect to quality is high. We would like to <sup>fi</sup>nd an industry where the product supplied is identical except in one vertical dimension and the product is available from multiple sources. This excludes product markets such as collectibles and one-of-a-kind items, since in such markets the producer/retailer has more than one dimension in which he can differentiate his products. For example, the “look” of a Tiffany lamp (a horizontal dimension) may play as important a role as its quality.

We chose the online retailing industry to carry out our empirical tests. In the retailing industry, when products are identical, a consumer's retailer choice is mainly driven by non-product attributes. As is customary in the marketing literature (see, e.g., [12]), we view the online purchase of a product as a bundle of utilities consisting of the satisfaction with the product itself and the service received from the purchase experience. We view the services and/or service quality of an e-tailer as the main dimension of differentiation. In contrast to the physical retailing industry, all e-tailers are equally “distant,” that is, the choice of an e-tailer would not be affected by its physical distance.

Consequently, we collected data from the online retailing market where an exactly identical product is offered by several e-tailers. E-tailers mainly differentiate by bundling their product with a certain level of service, so the vertical dimension of “quality” in the previous section should now be understood as “service quality.” Comparison shopping sites

Online services such as expediency of shipping, ability to track the order online, the possibility of talking to a person over the phone (about product information, ordering questions, etc.) have been shown to be important in a customer's online purchase decision [1]. It is therefore reasonable to assume that customers are willing to pay different premiums for such services.

## 5.1. Data collection

We use cross sectional data to test the adverse price effects derived from our theoretical model. Alternatively, we could use longitudinal data for a <sup>fi</sup>xed set of e-tailers whose levels of service change over time. Such data would face other challenges, including but not limited to: (i) the assumption that online customers' heterogeneity and willingness to pay for service remain constant over time; and (ii) entrance and exit of e-tailers in the market over time causing changing patterns of product positionings. In order to control for a change in willingness to pay by consumers, as well as a changing competitive landscape in the e-tailing industry, we opted to use cross sectional data where such factors remain constant. We selected several different product categories with different price levels and different service requirements to test the adverse price effect of service quality.

Two considerations were taken into account when selecting the product categories on which to collect data. First, it is clear from our theoretic analysis that the existence of the adverse price effect depends on the range of consumer heterogeneity with respect to service. Hence we choose several product categories that possibly differ in the extent of consumer service sensitivity. Second, the products from each product category should be available from multiple merchants. Six product categories that met these requirements were chosen: DVDs, video games, external hard drives, high de<sup>fi</sup>nition TVs (HDTV), projector replacement bulbs, and global positioning systems (GPS). We believe DVDs and video games belong to the low service sensitivity category. For these products, a consumer would expect a reasonable level of service, such as ship the DVD or video game on time and make sure it does not get damaged in transit. But additional level of service provision may not provide extra value to the consumer, consequently, may not lead to additional price premiums. In these cases, we expect (θ̄ – θ) to be relatively small.

The other four product categories require a greater level of service compared to DVDs and video games. These products are quite technical in nature, and it may be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for a consumer to <sup>fi</sup>nd exactly what he needs. For example, when a projector bulb burns out, <sup>fi</sup>nding a compatible replacement can be tricky, especially if the projector model has been discontinued. Not only does the replacement bulb requires a <sup>fi</sup>t in the socket, it should have the same brightness, light temperature, amps, heating characteristics, etc. as the original bulb. GPS can also be a service sensitive item for consumers. For example, a consumer can only <sup>fi</sup>nd out about the quality of the GPS after he has used the product: whether the GPS gives correct directions, whether it is easy to install updated version of the map data, etc. Consequently, service provided, such as phone calls for product information to the e-tailer and a good return policy become increasingly important. From an e-tailer's perspective, accepting returns and training people with technical expertise to answer phone calls add to the cost of doing business. From a customer's perspective, it is reasonable to assume that there are customers who are willing to pay extra to receive this range of service provision, i.e., when the range of service provision increases it is natural that some customers will be more willing to pay for those than others which translates into a bigger range for (θ̄ – θ).

Data was collected in May to June 2006. In order to test our analytical model, two pieces of data were needed for each product category: (i) the product price; and (ii) the perceived service level an e-tailer provides. Product prices were collected from BizRate.com, a well-known price comparison site. We used the sum of the base price plus shipping and handling costs as the total product price for each product. In each product category, a random sample of products was selected among the products that were available from at least three merchants listed on BizRate. We made sure that the products offered by the different e-tailers were identical, i.e., the product should be made by the same manufacturer and have the same manufacturer SKU or model number.

In addition to the price information, BizRate also carries customer feedback about the e-tailers. BizRate collects quality ratings at “checkout” by asking a store's customers to evaluate their purchase experiences immediately after completing the online transaction, as well as “after delivery” when the purchase is expected to have been received. The 15 ratings collected by BizRate for each merchant are explained in Appendix B and are based upon hundreds (in some cases thousands) of individual customer ratings. These ratings measure different aspects of a transaction, such as product price, customer support, and overall look and design of the website. Clearly, customer support would belong to service, whereas overall look is about website design. Therefore, as a <sup>fi</sup>rst step, a factor analysis was conducted to try to <sup>fi</sup>nd the common factors. Although the factor loadings for the e-tailers differ from one product category to the next, we can still see basically the same pattern across product categories. That is, the results of the factor analysis for each product category indicated the existence of three underlying common factors, which accounted for about 80%–93% of the variance. They can be labeled service, website design, and price. The factor loadings for the merchant ratings in the six product categories are displayed in Appendix C.

## 5.2. The empirical model

If we assume that the cost structure is close to linear, then it is reasonable to approximate the relationship between price and service in our theoretical model as linear. Therefore our hypotheses can be tested using linear models. Given that our data set contains product prices from multiple merchants, and those from the same merchants are most likely not independent, an ordinary linear model which assumes that the observations are independent would not be appropriate here. Therefore, a linear mixed model [9] that allows for dependent observations is used for our estimation. Linear mixed models allow the modeling of random and <sup>fi</sup>xed effects correctly [16,9,24].

The price functions in the duopoly model differ for the high and low service e-tailers, and we can rewrite them as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p _ {1} ^ {*} = \frac {2 \underline {{\theta}} - \overline {{\theta}}}{3} s _ {1} + \frac {2}{3} c (s _ {1}) + \left[ \frac {1}{3} c (s _ {2}) - \frac {2 \underline {{\theta}} - \overline {{\theta}}}{3} s _ {2} \right], \\ p _ {2} ^ {*} = \left[ \frac {2 \underline {{\theta}} - \overline {{\theta}}}{3} + (\overline {{\theta}} - \underline {{\theta}}) \right] s _ {2} + \frac {2}{3} c (s _ {2}) + \left[ \frac {1}{3} c (s _ {1}) - \frac {2 \overline {{\theta}} - \underline {{\theta}}}{3} s _ {1} \right]. \end{array}\tag{12}
$$

As can be seen from the above price functions, there is a direct effect of an e-tailer's service level on its price (the <sup>fi</sup>rst two parts in the above price functions), as well as an indirect effect caused by the service level of the competing e-tailer (the last part in the above price functions). Generalizing this to an oligopoly with n <sup>fi</sup>rms, and writing $( - \mathrm { \Delta } i )$ for <sup>fi</sup>rms other than i, we arrive at the following price functions:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p _ {i} ^ {L} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} s _ {i} + f ^ {L} (s _ {- i}), \\ p _ {i} ^ {H} = \beta_ {0} + (\beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2}) s _ {i} + f ^ {H} (s _ {- i}). \end{array}\tag{13}
$$

The price function $p _ { i } ( p _ { i } ^ { H } )$ is the price function for the low (high) service e-tailer, and the function $f ^ { L } ( S _ { - i } ) ( f ^ { H } ( S _ { - i } ) )$ captures all the indirect effects of i's competitors when i is a low (high) service e-tailer.

## 5.3. Discussions and hypothesis tests

From Table 3, we can see that the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimate for $\beta _ { 1 }$ is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from zero for all product categories. This demonstrates that merchant service levels indeed have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on prices. In other words, our hypothesis $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 }$ which states that e-tailer's service quality affects price is supported.

Hypothesis $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 }$ deals with the relationship between price and service and says that the effect of service on price could be different depending on whether an e-tailer is high or low service provider. For the DVD and Video Games category, where we were expecting the range of consumer service sensitivity to be small, we see that the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates $( \beta _ { 2 } )$ for the additional service premium are statistically not different from zero. On the other hand, for the Hard Drive, Projector Bulb, and the GPS product categories, $\beta _ { 2 }$ is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly non-zero, indicating that for these product categories, merchants will get a service premium by providing additional services. This is intuitive as the consumer heterogeneity with respect to service is large for these products. By providing high service, merchants are able to extract additional surplus from high service-sensitive consumers. However, for the HDTV product category where we would also expect consumer service heterogeneity to be large, we fail to <sup>fi</sup>nd a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant service premium for the high service e-tailers. Overall, our hypothesis $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 }$ is supported, and the different relationship between price and service is more likely to occur for service-intensive products.

The adverse price effect is found in two product categories (Projector Bulb and GPS), as evidenced by the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative estimates for $\beta _ { 1 }$ in these two categories. Our analytical model prescribes that when the consumer heterogeneity with respect to service is relatively larger than the marginal cost of service quality, the adverse price effect will likely happen for low service e-tailers. In the online retailing industry, the cost of marginal service provision is usually low [21]. In addition, projector bulbs and GPS are service intensive products and providing technical support on bulb compatibility and return policies is important to some of the consumers. Hence it is reasonable that the condition for the adverse price effect is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed for these product categories. The negative coef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates for $\beta _ { 1 }$ indicate that, for low service etailers, increasing the service level will not pay off in terms of prices because of the competitive nature from the high service e-tailers. Hence hypothesis $\mathrm { H } _ { 3 }$ is supported in the two service sensitive product categories.

Finally, our hypothesis $\mathrm { H } _ { 4 }$ posits that the additional service premium charged by a high service e-tailer wil increase as the range of consumer service sensitivity increases. This is evidenced by the relatively bigger coef<sup>fi</sup>- cient estimates for $\beta _ { 2 }$ for the high service sensitive products (Hard Drive, Projector Bulb, GPS) as opposed to those for the low service sensitive products (DVD and Video Game). The HDTV product category did not demonstrate the increased service premium for increasing service. One possible explanation is that HDTVs are very expensive—the average price in our data set is \$3211.29. When consumers buy such expensive products online, they invariably desire to buy from an e-tailer who has a good service reputation. Therefore, although their service sensitivity might be high, the heterogeneity with respect to service might be low, that is, the range between θ and θ̄ may be small. Therefore, the ̄additional service premium for providing high service might not be large.

## 6. Conclusions

In this paper we present a general competitive model to investigate the existence of the adverse price effect. We seek to understand the theoretical conditions under which the adverse price effect occurs. Through our general oligopoly model, we found that this price effect, contrary to many previous models in the literature, only exists in speci<sup>fi</sup>c situations. We further show in this paper that our theoretical results have some empirical support in online retailing markets.

There are several directions for future research. In the paper, we have several restrictive assumptions. For example, we implicitly assumed that e-tailers all have an identical cost structure. This may be an over-simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation. Although it would be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to obtain data about e-tailer's cost functions for service, future research should consider different cost structures. Another extension of the present research is to <sup>fi</sup>nd a method to quantify customer heterogeneity. In our empirical testing, we made some assumptions regarding customer heterogeneity in certain product categories, without actually measuring it. This might contribute to the fact that in some product categories where the adverse price effect was expected, it did not occur.

Another possible extension is to study whether such an adverse price effect would generalize to other markets. If one can <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence of the adverse price effect in other industries, it would have far reaching consequences. Conventional wisdom, often repeated by industry consultants, echoes the sentiment that a struggling company should try to improve the quality of its products, so it can increase its pro<sup>fi</sup>t margins and improve pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. Our current research casts some doubt on this supposed automatic link between quality and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. The adverse price effect states that improving quality may not only deteriorate pro<sup>fi</sup>t margins, but can force a company to even lower its price! In this paper, such conventional wisdom is questioned and we suggest that <sup>fi</sup>nding a unique product positioning — be it at the low boundary of the quality spectrum — might be a more pro<sup>fi</sup>table strategy.

## Appendix A. The triopoly model

The triopoly case can be solved analytically, since $A ^ { - 1 }$ can be written out explicitly. Using matrix notation, the equilibrium prices for a covered market can be expressed as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p ^ {*} = \frac {s _ {1} - s _ {3}}{3} \left[ \begin{array}{c c c} \frac {1}{2} [ 3 (s _ {1} - s _ {3}) + (s _ {2} - s _ {3}) ] & s _ {1} - s _ {3} & \frac {1}{2} (s _ {1} - s _ {2}) \\ s _ {2} - s _ {3} & 2 (s _ {1} - s _ {3}) & s _ {1} - s _ {2} \\ \frac {1}{2} (s _ {2} - s _ {3}) & s _ {1} - s _ {3} & \frac {1}{2} [ 3 (s _ {1} - s _ {3}) + (s _ {1} - s _ {2}) ] \end{array} \right] \\ \times \left[ \begin{array}{c} c (s _ {1}) - \frac {\theta}{c (s _ {2})} (s _ {2} - s _ {1}) \\ c (s _ {2}) + \theta (s _ {3} - s _ {2}) \end{array} \right] \end{array} \tag {14}
$$

With these expressions, it can be checked that the adverse price effect for the lowest quality <sup>fi</sup>rm still exists under similar conditions as in the duopoly case. Simplifying the expressions by introducing the notation $0 { \le } \lambda { \equiv } \frac { s _ { 2 } { - } s _ { 1 } } { s _ { 3 } { - } s _ { 1 } } { \le } 1$ and $\begin{array} { r } { \varDelta \equiv \frac { c ( s _ { 3 } ^ { \star } ) - c ( s _ { 1 } ) } { s _ { 3 } - s _ { 1 } } { \geq } 0 , } \end{array}$ we see that

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\partial p _ {1} ^ {*}}{\partial s _ {1}} <   0 \text {   iff   } [ 3 + (1 - \lambda) ] c ^ {\prime} (s _ {1}) <   - 3 \underline {{\theta}} + (1 - \lambda) ^ {2} (\overline {{\theta}} - \underline {{\theta}}) \\ \quad + (1 - \lambda) \varDelta . \end{array}\tag{15}
$$

Again, the condition of the adverse price effect in the triopoly case seems to require a differentiation cost that is not increasing too rapidly at the low quality level s and/or a wide customer heterogeneity, or more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the existence of customers with a high willingness to pay for quality (high θ̄). The strategic explanation from the duopoly case carries over to the triopoly. Now, the increase in quality from <sup>fi</sup>rm 1 will cause a chain reaction on the prices for <sup>fi</sup>rms 2 and 3, with the biggest reaction from <sup>fi</sup>rm 2 which directly competes with <sup>fi</sup>rm 1. Firm 2's price decrease will then cause a price decrease for <sup>fi</sup>rm 3 as well. It can be easily checked that

$$
\frac {\partial p _ {3} ^ {*}}{\partial s _ {1}} = 1 / 2 \frac {\partial p _ {2} ^ {*}}{\partial s _ {1}} <   0 \text {   iff   } c ^ {\prime} (s _ {1}) <   \Delta + (1 - \lambda) (\overline {{\theta}} - \underline {{\theta}}).\tag{16}
$$

Similar to the duopoly model, we always have that $\frac { \partial p _ { 3 } ^ { * } } { s _ { 3 } } { > } 0$ when c(·)is convex. If c(·) is concave, there can be instances where $\frac { \partial \mathbf { \dot { p } } _ { 3 } ^ { * } } { s _ { 3 } } < 0$ in a situation where customers are not willing to pay much for quality (i.e., θ̄ is small). It is also interesting to note that an adverse price effect in a triopoly can also exist for <sup>fi</sup>rm 2. A necessary condition for this is that $\lambda { > } 1 / 2$ , i.e., <sup>fi</sup>rm 2's product is closer to <sup>fi</sup>rm 3's than to <sup>fi</sup>rm 1's in the quality dimension and

$$
\frac {\partial p _ {2} ^ {*}}{\partial s _ {2}} <   0 \text {   iff   } \Delta + 2 c ^ {\prime} (s _ {2}) <   (2 \lambda - 1) (\overline {{\theta}} - \underline {{\theta}})\tag{17}
$$

It is easy to see that the conditions for the existence of an adverse price effect for <sup>fi</sup>rm 2 are more restrictive than for <sup>fi</sup>rm 1, and the relative magnitude of the effect is smaller for

<sup>fi</sup>rm 2 than for <sup>fi</sup>rm 1. So, we would expect to see an adverse price effect for <sup>fi</sup>rm 1 more often than for <sup>fi</sup>rm 2. When the market is not covered, the expression for A<sup>-</sup> <sup>1</sup> changes somewhat, and again it can be proven that the adverse price effect exists for the low quality <sup>fi</sup>rm. Similarly, the existence of the adverse price effect hinges on the presence of high customer heterogeneity and/or a differentiation cost that does not increase too rapidly. The precise expression of the condition for the existence of the adverse price effect is slightly different from the one in Eq. (15).
