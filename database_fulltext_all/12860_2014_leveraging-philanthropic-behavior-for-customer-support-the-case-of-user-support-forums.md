# LEVERAGING PHILANTHROPIC BEHAVIOR FOR CUSTOMER SUPPORT: THE CASE OF USER SUPPORT FORUMS<sup>1</sup>

Wael Jabr Haskayne School of Business, University of Calgary, Calgary, AB, T2N 1N4 CANADA {wjabr@ucalgary.ca}

Radha Mookerjee Naveen Jindal School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX 75080 U.S.A. {radham@utdallas.edu}

Yong Tan Michael G. Foster School of Business, University of Washington, Seattle, WA 98195 U.S.A. {ytan@uw.edu}

Vijay S. Mookerjee Naveen Jindal School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX 75080 U.S.A. {vijaym@utdallas.edu}

Online user forums for technical support are being widely adopted by IT firms to supplement traditional customer support channels. Customers benefit from having an additional means of product support, while firms benefit by lowering the costs of supporting a large customer base. Typically these forums are populated with content generated by users, consisting of questioners (solution seekers) and solvers (solution providers). While questioners can be expected to keep returning as long as they can find answers, firms must employ different means in order to recognize and encourage the contributions of solvers. We identify and compare the impact of two widely adopted recognition mechanisms on the philanthropic behavior of solvers. In the first mechanism, feedback-based recognition, solver contribution is evaluated by questioners. In the second mechanism, quantity-based recognition, all contributions are weighted equally regardless of questioner feedback. We draw on the pro-social behavior literature to identify four drivers of solver contribution: (1) peer recognition, (2) image motivation, (3) social comparison, and (4) social exposure. We show that the choice of recognition mechanism strongly influences a solver’s problem-solving behavior, highlighting the importance of the firm’s decision in this regard. We address issues of solvers self-selecting a type of recognition mechanism by using propensity score analysis in order to show that solver behavior is a result of forum conditioning. We also study the impact of the recognition mechanism on forum quality and the effectiveness of support to draw comparative analytics.

Keywords: User support forum, text mining, pro-social behavior, recognition mechanism, feedback

## Introduction

A large number of software and hardware firms, including Apple, Oracle, SAP, and SUN, complement their traditional models of customer support (e.g., help desks, product documentation, FAQ lists, etc.) by enabling user-generated support in the form of online technical forums. These user support forums allow threaded communication between users who can post questions and provide solutions at their own convenience.

Such forums help firms support a large community of users at a relatively low cost. In an interview by The Wall Street Journal (Cheng 2009), Hewlett Packard’s community director stressed these cost savings by stating that user forums have saved the company millions of dollars in deflected calls. Academic researchers (Kumar and Telang 2012) as well as industry professionals (Yankee Group 2006) have reported that firms are increasingly using self-service channels in order to cut back on costly agent-based customer support that accounts for approximately 70 percent of their total call center expenses. In addition to cost savings, user forums help firms establish a network of loyal users who help each other troubleshoot, identify and replicate bugs, assess product functionality and usability, and propose feature enhancements. Users also benefit from these forums by having an additional means of product support. They can now solicit help from a large group of volunteers and address their technical challenges in a way that results in quicker (Kumar et al. 2008) and cheaper feedback (typically these forums are free to use). Finally, this user interaction often leads to the development of strong relationships among users (Cross and Cummings 2004).

It therefore becomes clear that firms have a vested interest in maintaining the viability of user support forums. We aim at examining this role of firms in promoting the growth of their forums.

## Problem and Motivation

User support forums have two types of users: those who post questions (hereafter called questioners) and those who solve them (hereafter called solvers). Questioners have an inherent incentive to facilitate the growth of such forums since these represent an additional means of achieving problem resolution. On the other hand, sustaining the interest of solvers often requires various external incentives in order to ensure continuous voluntary contribution to the forum. Indeed, the forum managers we polled (via personal communication) identified the under-provision of solutions as their primary concern. Firms address this concern by employing different means to recognize the contributions of solvers and therefore encourage their sustained participation.

The firms in our study acknowledge user contribution by computing and posting one or two scores (depending on the forum type) that summarize a user’s forum contribution to date. The first score is simply a count of the total number of posts made by a user during her membership in the forum. This score is incremented by one each time she makes an additional post. The second score is more evolved and is based on the number of peer-recognized posts a user makes. This relies on a feature that enables questioners to label posts responding to their questions as being either helpful or correct. The score of the solver who contributed the post is updated only after it has been labeled, making this second score based on peer evaluation.

While similar in most aspects, the forums in our study differ in one distinctive feature: the manner in which user contribution is acknowledged. We refer to this feature as the recognition mechanism. In the first type (hereafter referred to as feedback-based recognition) both scores are posted next to the user’s profile. Forum participants, therefore, not only come to know how much a user contributes, but also how useful her contributions are. In the second type (hereafter referred to as quantity-based recognition) only the volume score is posted next to the user’s profile, providing other participants no (easy) way to evaluate the quality of this user’s posts. Points accumulated in both recognition mechanisms can lead to noneconomic rewards such as status in the community as well as more tangible incentives such as free software, free membership to developer networks, free registration to user conferences, etc.<sup>2</sup>

We study the impact of the recognition mechanism at both the individual and the aggregate levels. At the individual level we study how the contribution of solvers is affected by the recognition mechanism. At the aggregate level we identify how the recognition mechanism can have a significant impact on various outcomes of interest in the forum, namely, the quality and efficiency of problem solving.

## Contribution and Findings

The key goal of this study is to provide insights for firms seeking to leverage the philanthropic behavior of customers in order to enhance customer support. We investigate how the firm’s choice of recognition mechanism drives user contributions (specifically solvers) in technical support forums and adjusts the performance of such forums. The forums we study reflect this objective. We consider forums hosted by information technology (IT) vendors and designed to solve technical problems related to their products (e.g., SAP’s support forum). These forums are manned by volunteer customers who come together for mutual benefit; yet by helping each other, they help the firm as well. This context is quite different from intra-firm communities of practice consisting only of paid employees (Ahuja et al. 2003; Constant et al. 1996; Kankanhalli et al. 2005; Wasko and Faraj 2005).

Our context is also different from previous work studying virtual communities consisting of a software product or technology (such as a programming language) (Chiu et al. 2006; Lin et al. 2007; Moon and Sproull 2008; Raban 2008) that develop organically with no central governing entity. The focus while studying such communities has been on the drivers of knowledge sharing among members. Typical identified drivers include individual characteristics such as experience, values, and beliefs, as well as individual motivators such as the willingness to help others. While we acknowledge the pivotal role of the behavioral drivers of contribution, we focus on the active role that the IT vendor can play in shaping philanthropic behavior. Finally, rather than relying on surveys to study online support communities (Constant et al. 1996; Wasko and Faraj 2005), our study uses secondary data collected on users in a natural setting (Chiu et al. 2006; Singh et al. 2006). This is made possible by the fact that many forums make all interactions between questioners and solvers publically available, allowing researchers to observe the pattern and quality of such communication. There are a few studies like ours that consider vendor-hosted forums (Gu and Jarvenpaa 2003; Jeppesen and Frederiksen 2006; Wiertz and de Ruyter 2007). Moon and Sproull (2008), whose focus is the most closely related to ours, compare contributions in two types of forums: one where feedback is allowed systematically via explicit rating systems and one where such feedback among peers is not allowed. In our setup, feedback is always provided; however, forums differ in the way this feedback is integrated into the recognition mechanism. This allows us to study the role that the designer can play in influencing the dynamics of online communities. The crucial role of the recognition mechanism employed by the firm has not been the focus of previous studies (details in Table 1). These distinguishing factors, namely, the presence of a firm with a vested interest, the leverage of philanthropic participants, and the use of secondary data, make our study unique in relation to previous work.

This study makes the following specific contributions to the literature. We draw from the pro-social behavior literature in order to analyze the contributions of solvers in the context of two different but widely adopted forum recognition mechanisms. In the first mechanism (i.e., feedback-based), the solver’s contribution is evaluated by the level of approval received from the community. In the second mechanism (i.e., quantity-based), all contributions are weighted equally regardless of quality. We show that a solver’s contribution toward problem resolution is influenced differently depending on the recognition mechanism in place, highlighting the importance of the firm’s choice in this regard. Next, we analyze the implications of the recognition mechanism on community building within forums. We find that a feedback-based recognition mechanism fosters inter-user interactions more than a quantity-based mechanism does. We also conduct a causality analysis in order to ensure that our results are not spurious. Finally, we study the impact of the recognition mechanism on the forum quality and effectiveness of technical support, showing that both metrics improve when using a feedbackbased recognition mechanism.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We formulate our hypotheses after considering the relevant theoretical background. We next describe our process of data collection and text mining, build a model of solver contribution, and analyze the estimation results. We also perform a causality analysis on the impact of the recognition mechanism. We then analyze the performance at the forum level before concluding with relevant managerial implications.

## Theory Building and Hypotheses

After introducing relevant theoretical foundations, we present two sets of hypotheses related to the role of the recognition mechanism, one investigating the impact it has on the performance of individual solvers and the other investigating the impact on forum performance.

## Current Literature on Online Communities

Interest in the dynamics of online support communities has generated a fairly rich literature, with the term online community being loosely used to refer to various types of computermediated human interaction. One research stream emphasizes professional communities of practice formed by firm employees (Kankanhalli et al. 2005; Wasko and Faraj 2005). Constant et al. (1996), for instance, show that for distant employees in a large organization, motivation for contribution can be based on self-esteem, personal identification with the organization, organizational citizenship, and norms of generalized reciprocity. Ahuja et al. (2003) study the determinants of individual performance in virtual research and development groups by analyzing the e-mail communication of a group of researchers. They attribute the performance of such group members to direct effects including their functional roles, communication roles, and status, as well as indirect effects such as their network centrality.

Another research stream investigates online communities consisting of product (e.g., software) or technology users (e.g., programming) (Jeppesen and Frederiksen 2006; Lin et al. 2007; Raban 2008). Chiu et al. (2006), for instance, study a professional virtual community of practice and analyze structural (namely, social interaction ties), relational (namely, trust and norms of reciprocity), and cognitive (namely, shared vision and shared language) dimensions. Furthermore, in open source software communities, researchers analyze the drivers of participation. Studies argue that factors fueling open source software development include user satisfaction (Franke and von Hippel 2003), career concerns (Lerner and Tirole 2002), and reputation and status (Raymond 1999). Lakhani and von Hippel (2003) survey participants in the Apache user forum and conclude that solvers incur little cost to answer questions, while they benefit from the amount of knowledge that is gained. Singh et al. (2006) study discussion boards for open source software and investigate the patterns of communication that lead to efficient problem resolution. They suggest features that designers and developers of online communities must have for supporting the cultivation of different community roles. Brush et al. (2005) use survey and usage log data from Usenet to show that the highly interconnected nature of Usenet accounts for its robustness.

Finally, a few studies examine customer-based firm-hosted forums. For instance, Gu and Jarvenpaa (2003) provide an empirical study of sharing behavior among users on peer-topeer technical support forums. They show that sharing is driven by altruistic motivations. Wiertz and de Ruyter (2007) explore the use of the theories of collective action and social capital along with individual attributes as drivers of knowledge contribution in the Hewlett Packard technical support forum. Raban (2008) researches a mixed economic and social market for information and concludes that after accounting for monetary rewards, it is social incentives such as comments and ratings that best induce persistent participation.

Our research differs from the first stream in that members of technical support forums are in their vast majority not company employees and accordingly have no specific commitments. It differs from the second stream in that the forum is hosted by the software vendor and not a third party company, implying that the vendor has incentives to reduce the burden on more expensive help desks. Finally, our research differs from the last stream in that we are primarily interested in analyzing the role of incentive mechanisms in shaping user behavior.

In the following sections, we begin by reviewing what have been typically identified as drivers of user pro-social contribution and then proceed to investigate how users are impacted differently under different recognition mechanisms. We then investigate the role of the recognition mechanism on forum performance.

## Drivers of Pro-Social Contribution

Our first set of hypotheses is motivated by four streams of literature on pro-social behavior: roles of peer recognition, image motivation, social comparison, and social exposure. These are typically used to incentivize individuals into contributing to public goods (Meier 2007; Olson 1971).

## Peer Recognition

Peer recognition typically encompasses community-based inducement mechanisms that celebrate the participation of community members. Recognition can be either a simple acknowledgment of a user contribution or a more elaborate response appreciating this contribution. Various studies have identified peer feedback as one such inducement approach (Forman et al. 2008; Ghose 2009; Resnick et al. 2000; Thomas-Hunt et al. 2003). Moon and Sproull (2008) identify systematic feedback as an effective motivator. Ryan et al. (1983) and Deci et al. (1999) study recognition of individuals in return for their activities. This recognition can entice intrinsic stimuli whenever it is perceived as either informational or motivational and has differential values. Eisenberger and Cameron (1996) conclude that when used appropriately, reward has a highly favorable effect on task interest and creativity. Others (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998; Wasko and Faraj 2005) have found that individuals must think that their contributions will be worth the effort and that some new value will be created in order to contribute knowledge. Yet whether and how feedback affects creativity remains unclear (Byron and Khazanchi 2012), particularly because it is case-specific and improved performance depends on more positive and contingent feedback. Ryan et al. (1983) introduce a typology of rewards given for either starting or completing a target activity in order to determine what is expected when a person is working on a task. We build on this typology to identify two formal measures of rewards enabled by forum design, namely, completion reward and engagement reward. Completion-contingent rewards are explicitly dependent on completing the target task. Such rewards indicate that the person performing the activity has finished it to the satisfaction of the task initiator. On the other hand, engagementcontingent rewards are dependent only on the user engaging in an activity with no requirement to complete it. They are, therefore, awarded for mere participation in an activity. We also identify personalized recognition (such as a thank-you message from a questioner to a solver) as an informal measure of feedback, sometimes referred to as verbal recognition. These three types of performance-contingent rewards can convey substantial positive competence information in cases where the person does well enough to achieve a level of reward that signifies excellent performance (Deci et al. 1999). The above discussion leads us to develop the following hypotheses concerning peer feedback:

Hypothesis 1a: Solvers react positively to an engagement reward.

Hypothesis 1b: Solvers react positively to a completion reward.

Hypothesis 1c: Solvers react positively to personalized recognition.

## Image Motivation

Image motivation is typically defined as the desire to be liked and well-regarded by one’s self and others (Ariely et al. 2009). Theoretical (Benabou and Tirole 2006) as well as empirical (Heffetz and Frank 2010; Neckermann and Frey 2007) studies have shown that image motivation can explain the performance of pro-social activities and the provision of public goods. Striving for image has long been recognized in sociology (e.g., Lin 1990; Thye 2000) and economics (e.g., Congleton 1989). Olson (1971) notes that people are sometimes motivated to contribute by a desire to win prestige and respect. Recent studies show that individuals who are awarded higher status by their teammates respond by contributing even more to the group than they had in the past (Ariely et al. 2009; Willer 2009). Economists have also explained pro-social behavior by noting that the benefits accrued in the form of improved image can often outweigh the costs of such actions (Wichardt 2009). Furthermore, the literature supports the argument that individuals care about their status and are willing to trade this against economic benefits. Wasko and Faraj (2005) propose developing techniques that help build an individual’s reputation as a method to promote individual participation in the online environment. One such example they recommend is to assign status to individuals and make this status apparent within the electronic network. We therefore hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2: Solvers react positively to observable signals of improved image.

## Social Comparison

The theory of social comparison (commonly referred to as peer pressure) rests on the notion that people are driven to compare themselves with their peers. Research in this area has shown that even when effort is unobservable, people work harder when they are provided with a social comparison criterion (Goethals 1986; White et al.1995). In our setting, forum users are able to view the performance of their peers (both through the size of the pool of peers and their levels of productivity). Individuals are willing to exert more costly effort and expand resources in order to avoid falling behind the average performer or to improve an attribute not because they directly value that attribute, but because they wish to rank highly within their perceived peer groups (Ball and Eckel 1998). Within the context of an organization, Ang and Slaughter (2001) apply social comparison to understand peer effects and analyze the performance of contract versus permanent information system professionals on software development teams. Social comparison theory has also been applied to explain the phenomenon of “conditional cooperation” in pro-social behavior. Here, people are more willing to contribute to a social cause when others also contribute (Fischbacher et al. 2002; Frey and Meier 2004); participants try to match the allocations of their counterparts (Croson et al. 2005). Furthermore, when performance is being evaluated relative to others, then an essential question is whether or not the size of the competing crowd is influential. Studies have indicated that restricting the number of participants or allowing free entry should yield better outcomes (Boudreau et al. 2011; Casas-Arce and Martínez- Jerez 2009). Based on the above discussion, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3: Solvers are positively impacted by increased performance and the increased number of other solvers in the forum.

## Social Exposure

The degree to which an individual connects with others within a group is a multifaceted phenomenon (O’Reilly et al. 1989) that reflects attraction to the group and interaction among the group members (Katz and Kahn 1978). This interaction is a significant determinant of group work outcomes since it leads to higher member involvement, coordination, and satisfaction (Hogg and Terry 2000). Numerous descriptive measures have evolved from this field of research with the aim of mapping the conversational pattern among users (Morrison 2002), one of which is ties. In technical support forums, a tie is simply defined as a relationship between two users who contribute to the same thread (Petrovcic et al. 2012). Typically, these ties might not be real social interactions, but instead some form of exposure and user involvement in a group. The extant literature has examined the effectiveness of such linkages on the performance of individuals (Bouty 2000; Wasko et al. 2009) and groups (Guimerà et al. 2005; Nerkar and Paruchuri 2005; Tsai and Goshal 1998). Marwell et al. (1988) conclude that the overall concentration of ties within a group improves its prospects for collective action. Previous research has also studied the value of members’ contacts in sustaining and strengthening the cohesion of communities (e.g., Friedkin 2004; Moody and White 2003), in turn influencing members’ behaviors by strengthening their participation as well as other behavioral indicators of commitment and attachment to the group. In collaborative networks, the existence of a tie is found to be associated with a greater probability of knowledge flow (Singh 2005). In support forums, users have two primary roles, namely, solver and questioner, and can establish contacts with either type of communities. In their roles as solvers, users can freely contribute solutions without these being necessarily labeled as helpful or correct. Our discussion above leads to the following two hypotheses:

Hypothesis 4a: The solver’s increased exposure within the solver community will lead to increased solution contributions from the solver.

Hypothesis 4b: The solver’s increased exposure within the questioner community will lead to increased solution contributions from the solver.

## Recognition Mechanisms

Having explored the behavior of individual solvers in technical support user forums, we next address how manipulating this behavior through recognition mechanisms (namely, feedback-based and quantity-based) leads to variation in forum performance. Since the measurement of such performance has not yet been formalized in the literature, we borrow performance measures adopted in call centers, settings that have very similar characteristics.

## Response to Reward

The provision and consumption of help in user support forums suffer from the risk of under-provision. In order to mitigate this risk, researchers have argued that it is necessary to design these environments so that participants cooperate (Bapna 2003; Zghaibeh and Anagnostakis 2007), increasing the system’s value to its users (Golle et al. 2001). Others have stressed the importance of motivating users to contribute their knowledge to the system (King et al. 2002) via the right set of incentives. These incentives are typically in the form of a reward for either the quality or quantity of contributions. Previous research has also studied the importance of aligning these incentives with the system’s objectives (Ba et al. 2001; Raghu et al. 2004) via essential design adjustments. This literature has shown that the recognition mechanism plays a central role in shaping individual behavior. However, the important question of identifying how system designers can influence user behavior through these recognition mechanisms is still under-researched (Krishnan et al. 2003).

In the context of electronic knowledge repositories, the evidence shows that reward systems are useful for knowledgesharing environments (Orlikowski 1993), and that these systems constitute a good investment for organizations (Bartol and Srivastava 2002). For instance, Voelpel et al. (2005) report on the huge success of Siemens’ ShareNet knowledge repository, a success in part attributable to an appropriate reward system. Nee and Ingram (1998) state that the structure of incentives—material and nonmaterial—facilitates the behavior of individuals situated within a group. Nam et al. (2009) suggest that design features in the context of social question and answer sites must include a point-reward system in order to motivate participation.

However, past research has shown that the mere use of rewards is not consistently effective. Moon and Park (2002) find that while reward systems motivate knowledge contribution, these contributions are not guaranteed to be of good quality. Furthermore, certain rewards could lead participants to concentrate only on the quantity of knowledge contributed, resulting in low knowledge quality (Zhang et al. 2006). Even when monetary rewards are used, Mason and Watts (2009) find that increased financial incentives increase the quantity but not the quality of the work performed by participants. Finally, some studies have found that improperly designed reward systems could discourage knowledge sharing (Bock and Kim 2002; Bock et al. 2005).

## Impact of Recognition Mechanisms

For the firms in our context, it is important that both solvers and questioners return to the forum; this will happen only if the forum remains attractive. In this section, we aim to test the role that the two recognition mechanisms, namely, feedback-based (where rewards received from peers are showcased along with the volume of posts made) and quantity-based (where only a raw count of all posts made is showcased) play in shaping the forum attractiveness. Unfortunately, no measure is currently available to assess forum attractiveness and performance. However, given the striking similarities between forums and call centers, we can safely borrow well-established performance measures used in that context to apply to forums. As at call centers, on a support forum, a customer with a troubleshooting request initiates a thread, then another user with better expertise (solver) takes charge of the thread and begins communicating with the initiator attempting to address the request. The prevalent performance metrics have been classified into one of two groups: quality and efficiency (Gilmore and Moreland 2000).

Call centers measure service quality using wait-related measures such as average call waiting time. However, while these measures evaluate service accessibility, they do not assess the actual service encounter and ensuing customer satisfaction. To that end, de Véricourt and Zhou (2005) and Ren and Zhou (2008) use call resolution to model quality in customer service oriented call centers. We borrow similar metrics and hypothesize that the type of recognition mechanism adopted will have a strong influence on the performance of the user forum:

Hypothesis 5a: The quality of a user forum with a feedback-based recognition mechanism is superior to the quality of a user forum with a quantity-based recognition mechanism.

Hypothesis 5b: The efficiency of a user forum with a feedback-based recognition mechanism is superior to the efficiency of a user forum with a quantity-based recognition mechanism.

## Model at the User Level

## Model Derivation

We test our hypotheses by analyzing the user support forums of four major information technology firms: Apple, SAP, Oracle, and SUN. Two of these IT vendors, namely, Apple and SAP, employ a feedback-based recognition mechanism; the other two, namely, Oracle and SUN,<sup>3</sup> employ a quantitybased recognition mechanism. All four forums share the following common characteristics:

They are well-established and belong to well-established IT vendors.<sup>4</sup>

They have a threaded interface where users post and reply to each other within a thread.

They allow the thread initiator to mark posts as helpful or correct as appropriate.

• They report the user’s cumulative number of postings in the form of a score.<sup>5</sup>

They reward users for their participation using nonmonetary and symbolic giveaways<sup>6</sup> once they reach a certain score level.

The forums in our study differ in only one significant aspect: their choice of the recognition mechanism. The two forums employing the feedback-based mechanism display a score based on the total number of correct posts and helpful posts made by the user. On the other hand, the two forums employing a quantity-based recognition mechanism do not display this user score even though they allow the thread initiator to mark posts as helpful or correct as appropriate. Thus, the company hosting the quantity-based forum opts to not calculate and display this composite score next to the user’s postings.

We include two forums for each type of recognition mechanism in order to ensure that our findings are neither spurious nor related to features of the IT vendor host, but instead are attributable to the recognition mechanism (we discuss this further in the subsection “Causality Analysis”).

The data set consists of all posts made to the user support forums (Figures1 and 2) for the period between February 2006 and May 2009, including poster information, the date and time of posting, and content of the post (Table 2).

Typically, communication in a forum begins with a questioner initiating a thread that details a problem. This usually triggers requests for clarification and possible solutions from solvers. Questioners can acknowledge a solver’s contribution using one or both of the following: labeling a post as helpful or correct (using the forum’s interface), or sending a personal thank-you note to the solver. A solver might post intermediate solutions prior to arriving at the correct post that solves the thread. While only that final post is labeled by the questioner as correct, we identify as solution posts all solvers postings to a thread that attempt to solve it. Some of these posts are recognized by the questioner as helpful by marking them with a helpful star. Others are recognized by the questioner as correct by marking them with a correct star (Figure 3). A final type of solution post includes those that provide solutions but do not meet the needs of the questioner.

We harvest all content of the four forums identified earlier and collect all post-related information. We use query analysis, text mining, and network analysis to derive the variables of interest. The steps followed for the data collection are summarized in Figure 4 and detailed in later sections.

We process the data to create a separate panel data set for each forum where the cross-section is the solver and the time variable is the month. The choice of a month as the time interval is in line with previous studies on voluntary usergenerated content (Forman et al. 2008; Ghose 2009). Each observation corresponds to a unique solver for a given month in the study period. For the purpose of this study, we define a solver as a user who contributed at least one solution per month for at least three months during the study period.<sup>7</sup> We provide a detailed description of what qualifies as a solution in the following section. Since solvers initially join the forum at different times, we do not have the same number of observations for each solver.<sup>8</sup>

## Model Variables

## Dependent Variable: Contribution Level

A sustained contribution by solvers is crucial from the standpoint of the IT vendor hosting the forum. We therefore define the dependent variable Contribution Level as the count of the total number of solution posts made by a solver during a onemonth time period. We define a solution post as one that contributes to problem resolution; we therefore include posts with instructions for solving the problem, identifying sources of error, or suggesting alternative approaches, regardless of whether these posts have been marked as helpful or correct by the questioner. Contribution, therefore, refers to all posts that attempt to resolve questioner problems. We use text mining to identify such posts (details on the text mining method are provided in the following sections).

## Independent Variables

We identify independent variables corresponding to each of the four drivers of pro-social behavior mentioned earlier in line with the related literature: (1) peer recognition, (2) image motivation, (3) social comparison, and (4) social exposure.

Peer Recognition Variables. We collect data on three variables related to peer recognition. Before we introduce these variables we provide a simple intuitive illustration of their connotation. In a classroom setting, an instructor assigns a project to her students that is due by the end of the semester. This project consists of a collection of small tasks. Students submit the tasks in a piecemeal approach during the semester. The instructor announces in class the names of those who completed each task successfully in order to encourage students to work hard on each of the tasks. The instructor engages the students in the process by rewarding them for their progress toward the target. Furthermore, after completing the project, students who complete all the tasks are again acknowledged in class as a reward.

We now formally define each of the variables. The first variable, Helpful Posts, is a proxy for the engagement reward. This variable is computed as the total number of helpful posts made by a solver divided by her total number of non-question posts. Helpful posts, used as the numerator, are posts labeled as helpful by the questioner who initiated the thread. Nonquestion posts, used as the denominator, are posts that may or may not contribute to a solution but are not questions.

The second variable, Correct Posts, is a proxy for the completion reward. This variable is computed as the total number of correct posts made by a solver divided by her total number of non-question posts. The numerator reflects the number of posts labeled as correct by the questioner who initiated the thread. Non-question posts are as defined earlier.

The third variable, Thanks Posts, is a proxy for a personalized reward. This variable is computed as the total number of thank-you posts received by a solver divided by her total number of non-question posts. Within a thread a questioner might post a message appreciating a solver’s support. The way the forum is designed helps track which user is receiving such posts.

All three variables are calculated as percentages where the denominator is the total number of non-question posts produced by the solver during a given time period. The percentage formulation allows interpreting these variables as a weighted contribution (of correct, helpful, and thanks posts) out of the total monthly net output of non-question posts. Percentages are commonly used to signal how resources are allocated (as in financial ratios).

Image Motivation Variables. We collect data on three variables related to image motivation. The first variable, Post Score, contributes to the image of a solver since it measures her overall input to the forum. This variable is computed as the log-transformed total number of posts made by a solver from the time of member enrollment to the current time period regardless of the content of these posts (i.e., these posts might be solutions, questions, etc.). Each time a user makes a post, an up-to-date score showing her tally of posts is displayed, making this information public. The Post Score for a solver can be in the tens of thousands, reflecting the long standing of users in the community and the level of involvement. The second variable, Solution Score, also contributes to a solver’s image. This variable is computed as the total number of solution posts made by a solver from the time of member enrollment to the current time period. We use text mining to identify the solver posts that qualify as solutions. This variable is a refined version of the Post Score since it excludes all posts that are questions or those that are simply chatter. It therefore indicates a solver’s effective productivity and shows the level of refined involvement. For the third variable, Institutional Recognition, we rely on a forum feature whereby forum moderators may assign threads as correct when they deem these threads as solved and when question initiators have not done so. We therefore compute this third variable as the total number of such threads to which a solver posted divided by the total number of threads to which the solver made a post. This is a way for solvers to receive additional recognition contributing to their image.

Social Comparison Variables. We collect data on two variables related to social comparison. The first variable, Others’ Solution Posts, is computed as the total number of solution posts made by other solvers divided by these solvers’ total number of non-question posts. This variable provides a solver with a measure of the productivity of other solvers in the forum, motivating the solver to contribute. The second variable, Distinct Solvers, is the total number of distinct solvers present in the forum for the current time period. This variable provides a solver with a sense of her level of competition within the “solver market.”

Social Exposure Variables. We collect data on two variables related to social exposure. The first variable, Solver with Solver Exposure, measures the connectedness of the solver within the solver community. For a given solver, this variable reflects the strength of contact with each of the other solvers. Solver i makes a contact with solver j if solver j has posted before solver i within the same thread and during the same time period. Here the definition of contact makes no assumption concerning the relationship among those involved in the contact (Petrovcic et al. 2012). It is simply an indication of how solvers who arrive later in the lifespan of a thread are exposed to the postings of earlier solvers. The frequency of a contact within a thread is the number of posts made by solver j before the last post of solver i (also called co-replies). These frequencies are added across threads to obtain the strength of the contact:

$$
\text { Solver   with   Solver   Exposure } _ {i} = \frac {\sum_ {j} (\text { Co - reply }) _ {i j}}{A g e _ {i}}
$$

where i is the focal solver and j represents another solver with whom the focal solver interacted during a given time period.

The second variable, Solver with Questioner Exposure, measures the connectedness of a solver with questioners. This connectedness helps a solver gain visibility with questioners in need of support. This variable is computed as the total number of contacts a solver has with questioners during a time period. A contact with a questioner occurs when a solver provides a solution post in a thread where the questioner is present. A questioner is someone who has asked a question in the thread and can be one of two types: initiated the thread or joined an existing thread afterward, indicating that she is facing similar problems:

$$
\text { Solver   with   Questioner   Exposure } _ {i} = \frac {\sum_ {k} (\text { Co - reply }) _ {i k}}{A g e _ {i}}
$$

where i is the focal solver and k represents the questioners with whom the focal solver interacted during a given time period.

Control Variables. We use four control variables calculated at the solver level. The first variable, Non Question Posts, is a measure of the solution-related activity of a solver during a time period as counted using the number of non-question posts made by a solver during a time period. The second variable, Subforums, is a measure of the breadth of a solver’s involvement within a forum. Subforums are subsections within a forum dealing with specific products or services offered by the firm. For example, some of the subforums in SUN’s forum include Java, Solaris operating systems, etc. This variable is computed as the total number of subforums a solver contributes to during a time period. The third variable, Role, is a measure of the diversity of duties a solver can perform. A role can be one of two types: solver only, or solver and questioner. This variable takes a value of 1 if the solver posted questions (i.e., she is both solver and questioner) during a time period, and a value of 0 otherwise (solver only). The final variable, Age, is a measure of the solvers longevity within a forum as the cumulative number of months since the user first registered in the forum.

Table 3 lists the variables used along with their descriptions. Appendix A shows the variable summary statistics for each forum.

## Content Analysis Using Text Mining

We use text mining to analyze user posts and classify them as solutions, questions, or thank-you posts. Once classified, posts are then used to derive the following variables for each time period: the count of solutions made by a solver (Contribution Level), the count of recognition posts made to a solver (Thanks Posts), and the count of non-question posts (Non Question Posts).

Text mining is widely used to analyze and classify the text of user-generated content via automated means (Forman et al. 2008; Ghose 2009). Text mining classification typically involves assigning a document (a text fragment) to a particular class (Manning et al. 2008; Sebastiani 2002) by building a classifier and then using it to analyze and classify the data set. Building the classifier consists of constructing training and test data sets on which the classifier is repeatedly trained, and then validating the performance of the classifier by running it on a separate sample of the complete data set.

We follow this process to build three different classifiers for the three different variables of interest. Our objective is to first classify each post as either a question or a non-question post. This will give us the variable Non Question Post. The non-question posts then are further split into thanks or nonthank-you posts, giving us our second variable, Thanks Post. Finally, the non-thank-you posts are classified as solution posts or not, giving us our third variable, Contribution Level (detailed steps provided in Appendix B).

The panel data we constructed allows us to better disentangle the individual-specific effects. Both fixed effects (FE) and random effects (RE) models provide a viable means to control for all time-invariant (observed or unobserved) latent variables that may influence the dependent variable. These two models differ in that the random effects model assumes that the idiosyncratic error term is uncorrelated with the included regressors, while the fixed effects model does not rely on this restrictive assumption (Mundlak 1978). Hausman (1978) provided a specification test that is commonly used as a criterion for choosing between these two models. In our case, the null hypothesis that both FE and RE produce consistent estimators is rejected and we therefore resort to the less restrictive fixed effects model. The major feature of fixed effects estimation is the ability to control for all stable characteristics of the individuals in the study, even without measuring them. This approach eliminates potentially large sources of bias, particularly those due to omitted variables (such as altruism in this context). We account for this serial correlation in the model since the results are marginally significant for all four forums.

## Results

It is remarkable to find that the regression estimates for the two forum groups, feedback-based recognition (Apple and SAP) versus quantity-based recognition (SUN and Oracle), are strikingly different with regard to the effects of peer recognition and social exposure. We describe the results below. Hypotheses 1a, 1b, and 1c are confirmed for both forums based on feedback recognition (Apple and SAP). The solvers in these two forums respond positively to all kinds of feedback from the community including engagement, completion, or personalized feedback. These findings are upheld for multiple lag periods (one, two, and three months earlier) to estimate the impact of peer feedback (except for the third lag of the variable Thanks Posts in both the Apple and SAP forums). Solvers are accordingly responsive to all forms of feedback. Maintaining a viable forum with rich solver contribution, therefore, depends on the consistent presence of forum feedback.

On the other hand, solvers in forums that employ quantitybased recognition (SUN and Oracle) display a completely different behavior. None of the coefficients associated with peer recognition (helpful, correct, and thanks) are significant motivators for contribution. Therefore, solvers are not affected by the feedback provided by questioners with quantitybased recognition.

Hypothesis 2 investigates the impact of image related variables on solvers’ contribution levels. This hypothesis is confirmed for forums based on feedback recognition: solvers are motivated by all three types of image indicators, and the image of being a good solver motivates solvers to sustain the contribution of solution posts during subsequent time periods. The effect of cumulative posts (log-transformed Post Score) has a positive impact on contribution. The coefficient for Solution Score is significant and positive; solvers with more solution posts also contribute more. Finally, Institutional Recognition, the number of threads that forum moderators assign as solved on behalf of the solver (signaling the ability of a solver to provide solutions), is also significant and positive, promoting higher levels of contribution.

On the other hand, only the variable for the cumulative number of posts (i.e., Post Score) is significant and positive for both forums that employ quantity-based recognition, indicating that solvers are motivated by a high Post Score. The variable for institutional recognition is significant and positive only in Oracle’s forum, suggesting that solvers sometimes use institutional recognition as an approval of their capabilities. Finally, the coefficient for Solution Score is insignificant in both quantity-based forums. These results reject our hypotheses related to image motivation. Since these forums do not use feedback-based mechanisms, their solution scores do not play a larger role in their overall scores. The only measure of relevance in computing solvers’ scores is the number of posts made, and that is accordingly the only variable that motivates the solver to post more.

Hypothesis 3 studies the effect of social comparison (for any given time period, social comparison is measured by the count of other solvers as well as the number of solution posts provided by these solvers). We find that the effect of social comparison varies by forum. In the SAP forum, the contribution of solvers is neither influenced by the number of other solvers nor by the number of solution posts provided by these solvers. While solvers are not influenced by the number of other solvers in Apple’s forum, they are positively motivated by the level of activity of other forum solvers. This positive effect indicates that solvers swarm to threads where earlier solvers have been involved in resolution in order to potentially snatch some points from later assignment of their posts as helpful or correct. On the other hand, while solvers in SUN’s forum react negatively to both indicators of social comparison, those in Oracle’s forum react positively to the number of solvers present but negatively to the number of solutions these solvers provide. The negative effect of the number of solutions on contribution suggests that solvers might reduce their contribution levels if they feel that other solvers are already putting effort into solving a problem.

Finally, the social exposure variables for the forums using feedback-based recognition are significant and positive, confirming Hypotheses 4a and 4b that a solver’s exposure to the questioner as well as the solver communities increases her contributions. For both quantity-based forums the effect of social exposure (both with solvers and questioners) is not significant.

## Comparative Analysis

The feedback-based recognition mechanism is designed in such a way that the solver’s score is not simply computed as a raw score of the number of posts made, but instead consists of points earned by solvers based on peer recognition. This score captures the ability or reputation that a solver accumulates from forum activities. After analyzing four user support forums, our study regression results indicate that the recognition mechanism strongly influences solvers’ behaviors toward higher contribution levels. Feedback-based recognition makes solvers become responsive to peer evaluation. On the other hand, solvers are not responsive to questioner feedback in forums with a quantity-based recognition mechanism.

The impact of a recognition mechanism seems to extend beyond systematic recognition (helpful and correct labeling). With feedback-based recognition, solvers are also responsive to the personalized feedback (thanks posts) of questioners, but this is not the case for quantity-based recognition. Receiving personalized feedback signals to a solver that a questioner appreciated a solution post; however, its impact is broader. After observing the pattern of communication in the forums, we find that solvers who receive a thank-you post without any of their posts being assigned as helpful or correct typically request the initiator to revisit the thread and make such an assignment. This would directly contribute to a solver’s recognition score.

Another implication of the recognition mechanism is that it impacts community building between solvers and questioners, rather than among solvers only. Our results show that a feedback-based recognition mechanism prompts solvers to interact with other solvers in various threads. These interactions help solvers in various ways, including opportunistic behavior in posting to threads where a solution has not yet been found by a fellow solver with the hope of collecting reward points that are still up for grabs. These interactions also improve forum performance regardless of the interpretation.

In quantity-based recognition, forums maintain a raw recognition score that only accounts for the volume of posts regardless of the quality. Not surprisingly, solvers respond to such incentives by worrying little about any questioner feedback they may receive since such feedback is not reflected in their recognition scores in any way. They also lose any incentive to interact with other members of the forum community. Furthermore, while systematic feedback is still possible in the forum and questioners may continue to use it, such feedback has no impact on solver productivity. The literature on reward systems asserts that an external incentive system is effective only if it can be adapted in order to reap benefits (Olson 1971; Willer 2009). It is possible that because solvers do not care about questioner feedback, quantity-based recognition may lead to the reduced or erratic use of such feedback over time.

Our findings have several implications for the effectiveness of the feedback-based recognition mechanism. First, solvers benefit by improving their image and signaling their high quality type. Second, subsequent potential questioners who search the forum for threads that match their problems can now easily identify which posts are of value without having to read through the entire thread. Finally, the firm benefits by reducing the clutter in a thread.

## Causality Analysis

While the earlier analysis revealed the impact of the recognition mechanism on user behavior, it might be argued that this behavior is an inherent user characteristic rather than a result of some kind of forum conditioning. Accordingly, users selfselect into their choice of forum recognition mechanism. It might be the case that users who are both more responsive to their communities and more social tend to opt for forums with a feedback-based recognition mechanism, while those who are not tend to opt for forums with a quantity-based recognition mechanism. This issue of self-selection typically arises in program evaluation where researchers are concerned with threats to internal validity that include factors other than the intervention being studied (Guo and Fraser 2010). In our context, the self-selection problem seems less probable since forum users are typically customers of the IT product in the first place (in our case Apple, Oracle, SAP, and SUN products) who later on decide to use the forum as a help resource. Furthermore, when running our regression, the results from each of the two forums within a recognition type display the same general pattern. Yet in order to completely rule out this case, we resort to counterfactual analysis using a propensity score analysis (Becker and Ichino 2002; Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983). The counterfactual framework emphasizes that individuals selecting into either the treatment or the nontreatment group have potential outcomes in both states, the one in which they are observed and the one in which they are not. One approach to solving this issue is by the use of the propensity score matching model. The propensity score approach has been used extensively in economics, statistics, and information systems to overcome the problem of selection bias (Dehejia and Waba 2002; Mithas et al. 2006; Smith and Telang 2009).

In our context, we implement the propensity score analysis as follows: With the concern for self-selection bias arising from the inherent characteristics of forum users as related to their responsiveness to the community and social attitudes, we select observable variables related to these two characteristics in order to predict membership in any one forum type. We specifically include three variables related to responsiveness (namely, Helpful Posts, Correct Posts, and Thanks Posts), two variables related to social exposure (namely, number of contacts with solvers and number of contacts with questioners), and control variables (namely, duration of forum membership, number of question posts made, number of subforums contributed to, whether that solver acts sometimes as a questioner, and total posts submitted) to predict the probability of a user joining a forum with a feedback-based recognition mechanism (FBRM = 1) versus joining the quantitybased recognition mechanism (FBRM = 0, typically referred to as the control). Propensity scores are then calculated using the standard probit function with these observed explanatory variables (see Table E1 in the Appendix E for the probit results). We then run a test ensuring that the balancing property of the propensity score is satisfied. The purpose behind this test is to check whether or not observations with the same propensity score have the same distribution of observable explanatory variables independent of treatment status. Once we achieve this, we proceed to matching between groups where one group of users belongs to FBRM = 1 and the other to the control. Identifying clones in both groups and then comparing the contribution of clones with similar characteristics should substantially reduce any remaining selection bias issues. Instead of simply matching users based on propensity scores (as is typically done when only small sample sizes are available), we make use of the wealth of clones in the data set and match users based on the actual values of the observed explanatory variables from the probit model. As additional checks, we derive distributional parameters (mean, median, standard deviation, and histogram) of the variables used in the propensity score estimation in order to ensure that they are similar for clones in both the treatment and control groups. Once this is achieved, the analysis is reduced to comparing the contribution of cloned users across recognition mechanisms. For each set of clones, we derive the difference in contribution between a member of the feedback-based mechanism and her clone in the quantitybased mechanism. We then perform comparisons at two levels. We first run a simple hypothesis to check whether or not the difference in contribution is equal to zero (H<sub>0</sub>: μ<sub>FBRM=1</sub> $- \mu _ { \mathrm { F B R M } = 0 } = 0$ , where $\mu _ { \mathrm { F B R M = 1 } }$ is the mean contribution for members of the feedback-based recognition mechanism and $\mu _ { \mathrm { F B R M = 0 } }$ is the mean contribution for members of the quantitybased recognition mechanism), and reject the null hypothesis to conclude that the contribution of members is not equal across recognition mechanisms. We then run the same type of regression analysis conducted earlier, except that the dependent variable is now the difference in contribution between clones across treatments. The purpose of this analysis is to identify whether or not the difference in contribution across clones in the two types of forums is indeed driven by the same type of pro-social variable. We observe the same pattern (Table E2) with all variables related to peer feedback and social networks being significant and positive. This implies that the difference in contribution from user clones across forums is explained by the variables identified earlier. We can, therefore, safely claim that the recognition mechanism is indeed causing the difference in behavior.

## Model at the Forum Level

We next explore how two different recognition mechanisms influence the user forum’s performance. The value of user support forums lies in having questioner issues addressed, investigated, and ultimately resolved instead of using other costly resources from the standpoint of both the questioners and the firm. We assess the value of forums by drawing on their similarities with call centers. In the latter context, quality is traditionally evaluated by analyzing the successful resolution rate and calculated as the percentage of calls resolved out of the total calls received. This metric evaluates the center’s ability to address callers’ needs. The other metric, efficiency, is evaluated by analyzing the average handle time for a service and is calculated as the average duration of a phone conversation with the call center from the customer’s initiation of the call until the end of that call. We therefore develop comparable measures to characterize quality and efficiency in forums. With these measures, we can analyze the improvements of these performance metrics across recognition mechanisms, testing our Hypothesis 5.

## Forum Quality

We use the assigned resolution rate of threads as a proxy for forum quality. All four forums we study allow thread initiators to assign a post as correct and signal that the thread is resolved. For each time period, and for each of the four forums, we compute the assigned resolution rate as the number of threads labeled correct divided by the number of threads initiated. This rate is a conservative estimate of the successful resolution rate because it does not include threads with a post that solves the problem without being assigned as correct by the thread initiator.

The assigned resolution rate is then compared across the four forums using an analysis of variance experiment. We find that this rate is significantly higher for forums using the feedback-based recognition mechanism compared to forums using the quantity-based recognition mechanism with $F _ { ( 1 , 1 0 8 ) } = 1 6 3 9 . 2 2$ at $\mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1$ , confirming Hypothesis 5a. This result implies one of two findings for feedback-based recognition: either the resolution rate is higher, or questioners are more willing and, therefore, more likely to make the effort to label posts as correct when their threads are resolved, or both. While we cannot make a definitive claim concerning the true resolution rate, we can still safely conclude that feedback-based recognition is of greater value to both current and future forum members, and accordingly the firm. Current forum members (specifically solvers) benefit from increased recognition given that questioners are more likely to assign correct posts.

For feedback-based recognition forums, one can expect that labeling posts as correct will be done more diligently by questioners.<sup>9</sup> Future forum visitors who use the forum as a knowledge repository (searching for a resolution to a previously solved problem) will need to spend less effort to sift through threads for a solution. Forums attract many views for a thread during the time it is being solved as well as after it is concluded. It is helpful for forum users to have resolved threads assigned as correct.

## Forum Efficiency

We first derive a measure that can be likened to the average handle time defined earlier. We derive the time between the initiation of the thread and the posting of the correct solution (as assigned by the questioner) and average this number over all threads with correct posts using the pool of threads assigned as correct. We then compare the average time to resolution (or average handle time) across recognition mechanisms using an analysis of variance experiment. We find that the recognition mechanism is statistically insignificant, indicating that it has no significant impact on handle time with $F _ { ( 1 , 2 8 3 7 9 8 ) } { = } 0 . 0 2 \ \mathrm { a t } \ \mathrm { p } { = } 0 . 7 9$ . When using the number of posts in a thread up to the correct post (or average handle post count) rather than time to correct post, we find that the recognition mechanism is statistically significant with $F _ { ( 1 , 2 8 3 7 9 8 ) } = 1 9 8 3 9 . 4 9 \mathrm { a t p } < 0 . 0 0 1$ , confirming Hypothesis 5b. We find that this measure is on average smaller for feedbackbased recognition. This indicates that while the time to obtain a correct response is not different across mechanisms, feedback-based mechanisms favor a leaner thread with fewer posts needed to arrive at the correct solution (i.e., less clutter). A questioner is, therefore, required to spend less effort to find a solution. It might be that solvers thrive for completeness of their solution so as to maximize the chances having them assigned as correct. If, on the other hand, solvers were to use a piecemeal approach to providing solutions, then they run the risk of letting another solver expand on their partial solution and usurp the recognition. Since the wait for a solution is typically asynchronous, we expect that questioners are likely to be more sensitive to clutter than to time delay.

We next turn to the average resolution rate of support agents in a forum (i.e., solvers). For each time period, we develop a measure that is the ratio of the number of solution posts provided by a solver to the total number of non-question posts she makes. This measure corresponds to the efficiency of solution effort for a solver during a given time period. We then compare this value across the two recognition mechanisms using an analysis of variance experiment. We observe that this ratio is significantly higher for the feedback-based recognition mechanism $F _ { ( 1 , 2 7 9 1 8 ) } = 1 2 1 . 3 5 \mathrm { a t p } < 0 . 0 0 1$ , confirming Hypothesis 5b. This suggests that solvers tend to be more selective concerning the posts they make to a feedbackbased recognition forum type. Since a post improves the recognition score only if it is deemed helpful or correct by questioners, solvers are more sensitive to the quality of their posts. The firm benefits by having a more productive volunteer force as well as less forum clutter.

## Conclusions and Future Work

We show that the recognition mechanism in a user support forum strongly influences solvers’ pro-social behaviors toward increasing their contributions; this in turn affects both forum quality and efficiency. A feedback-based recognition mechanism promotes solver forum contributions, further improving its overall quality and efficiency. Specifically, with feedback-based recognition, solvers become more responsive to the needs of questioners and make a greater effort to connect with both solver and questioner communities. Both of these effects ultimately stem from the fact that, under feedback-based recognition, questioners evaluate the contributions of a solver. Furthermore, because solvers compete to earn recognition, they have an incentive to provide the correct answer with fewer posts. This leads to improved quality and efficiency through increased resolution rates, reduced handle post counts, and increased solver productivity. Finally, feedback-based recognition helps improve the overall forum usability because users with questions similar to the ones already solved (and assigned as such) require less search effort to find a solution.

These findings allow us to make some recommendations to forum hosts. We anticipate that the appropriate recognition mechanism is related to the maturity of the forum. Early in the existence of a forum when activity has not yet gained sufficient momentum, quantity-based recognition mechanisms may work best for ensuring a sustained level of contribution regardless of quality. Users will be increasingly attracted the more they find the forum bustling with activity. Once a critical mass is reached (and sustained), then feedback-based recognition can be introduced in order to foster contribution quality (rather than quantity), thus better meeting the needs of users who arrive at forums to find useful solutions.

It is worth noting that the implications that we derive are not necessarily applicable to all types of support forums. The ones we study have a large user base (several hundred thousand); users are well beyond the stage of getting acquainted with the mechanics of the recognition system and are well accustomed to its functioning. Forums that are in a transient stage might have different dynamics.

Furthermore, implementing a feedback-based recognition mechanism could have some limitations. The mechanism as currently implemented in most forums relies solely on the willingness of questioners to assign a solution post as helpful or correct. Some safeguards need to be set in place to ensure that questioners reward solvers in order for this mechanism to achieve its targets. In a case where solvers do not fully assume this role, forum moderators might be needed to fill the void.

However, there are some limitations to the generalizability of our conclusions. First is the issue of unobserved phenomena, which might confound the results (Christakis and Fowler 2011). While we have no data concerning such underlying phenomena in user support forums, the firm might be actively soliciting user contribution from a select group. There might also be a sudden surge in interest to contribute at any one point in time. Our only safeguard to ensure the validity of our results is that we deal with a large user sample size (all solvers during the study period) and that we add multiple controls. The use of individual-level data also adds to the analysis validity (Van Den Bulte and Stremersch 2004). Second is the issue of product and behavioral characteristics and their impacts on causality (Aral 2011). Our safeguard for confounding effects due to product characteristics is that we replicate our findings for each of the two recognition mechanisms in two forums. Our safeguard for confounding effects due to user-inherent behavioral characteristics is that we use a propensity score analysis. Third is the issue of life cycle dynamics for the forums being researched. In our setup, forums are at a fairly mature stage (at least five years old) and are, therefore, less prone to transient effects.

Future work based on this study could emerge on two fronts: analytical and empirical. We mention two analytical studies that are possible extensions to the present work. First, there is an opportunity to study the equilibrium behavior of questioners and solvers in the presence of a given recognition mechanism, and to design a mechanism that achieves the firm’s objectives. The ability to collect data in natural settings provides an excellent prospect for validating equilibrium predictions. Second, while our study emphasized user forums for customer support, these forums represent only one among the various channels used to support customers. Since customers have a choice concerning which channels they use, there is a need to balance a firm’s resources across various support channels including user support forums, manual help desks, FAQs, etc. This optimal allocation model could be the topic of future work.

We mention several possibilities for further empirical work that could emerge from the current study. We see forum designers continually adjusting their approach for building more elaborate recognition models. Recently, Symantec<sup>10</sup> implemented a recognition mechanism that awards points based on participation as well as problem resolution, representing a hybrid of feedback-based and quantity-based recognition. This company also allows exchanging recognition points for monetary reward (e.g., gift cards). It would be interesting to study the role of monetary versus nonmonetary incentives on solver behavior. A related issue is whether recognition points should be awarded by questioners or moderators. For instance, Adobe<sup>11</sup> recently adopted a recognition mechanism where administrators and moderators alone control the awarding of recognition points. In general, there is a considerable amount of data that can be harnessed from forums where a sharp change to the recognition mechanism was made at a discrete point in time. This provides the possibility of studying and contrasting the before and after effects of a given mechanism.

## Appendix B

## Text Mining Performance in Each of the Four Forums for Each of the Three Classifiers

The text mining approach we adopt is a classification one that assigns a post to one of two categories. The classifier takes posts as input and returns a classified output based on the first-best guess for the category of each input item.

Specifically, and for each of our three variables, the text mining approach is implemented as follows. First, we construct a training data set of labeled posts by manually labeling each post in the data set with its appropriate class. In our setup, this is a binary classification (e.g., a post is a solution or not). To derive the Contribution Level variable, which is a count of the number of solutions, we compile a set of 6,000 posts (equally drawn from each of the four forums) that have been manually labeled as solutions. We then manually label another 6,000 posts as non-solution (equally drawn from each of the four forums as well). We can now use all 12,000 posts to classify posts as a solution or not. We follow the same approach for the other two variables by manually labeling posts as Thanks Posts or not (12,000 training set in total), and Non Question posts or not (12,000 training set in total). Second, we use the LingPipe toolkit (Alias-i 2009) to create a Dynamic Language Model classifier. LingPipe provides a classification facility that takes training sets and learns how to classify further documents (posts) using what it learned with such language models. We use 10-fold cross-validation to train and validate the classifier. Third, after adjusting relevant parameters to ensure that each of the three classifiers (for the three variables) have reasonably high accuracy (ranging between 88.9% and 91.3%), we evaluate the classifiers by running them on a completely different validation set of data. Our classification of the full data set is then done in a sequential manner. We begin with a data set of 12,000 posts, each of which has been manually pre-labeled as Question or Non-Question, Solution or Non-Solution, and Thanks or Non-Thanks. We then run our first classifier on the entire data set and classify the data set as one of two types—one containing Non-Question posts and the other Question posts. We then run the second classifier on the data set with Non-Question posts and classify the set into Solution posts and Non-Solution posts. We finally run the last classifier to classify the Non-Solution posts into Thanks and Non-Thanks posts. Table B1 details the performance of the text mining approach along with additional robustness checks.

Further, we ensure that each of the classifiers is unbiased. An unbiased classifier is one that has the same error rate in both classes. Since we use the text miner to derive the dependent variable as well as some of the independent variables used in the regression analysis, ensuring that the classifier is unbiased avoids the problem of measurement error. This also enables us to satisfy regression assumptions and avoid inflation in the overall regression error as well as the variance of estimators.

## Negative Binomial Model Selection

The dependent variable is a count variable. The Poisson regression model implements the canonical regression specification for a variable Y that is a count of events:

$$
\operatorname{Prob} \left[ Y = y _ {i t} \mid x _ {i t} \right] = \frac {\exp \left(- \lambda_ {i}\right) \lambda_ {i} ^ {y _ {i t}}}{\Gamma \left(1 + y _ {i t}\right)}
$$

where $\lambda _ { i } = e x p ( \alpha + x _ { i i } \beta ) , y _ { i } - 0 , 1 , . . . , i = 1 , . . . N , t = 1 , . . . T ,$

$\mathbf { \boldsymbol { x } } _ { i t }$ is a vector of covariates,

$i = 1 , \ldots N$ indexes the N observations and $t = 1 , \ldots$ T indexes the T time periods.

Here the conditional mean is $E [ y _ { i t } | \mathbf { x } _ { i t } , \varepsilon _ { i t } ] = \lambda _ { i }$ and its equidispersion is $V a r [ y _ { i t } | \mathbf { x } _ { i t } ] = \lambda _ { i } .$

Since observed data will almost always display pronounced overdispersion, allowing for overdispersion dictates the use of the negative binomia model (Cameron et al. 1986; Greene 2008). Thus, the negative binomial model is employed as a functional form that relaxes the equidispersion restriction of the Poisson model. A useful way to motivate the model is through the introduction of latent heterogeneity in the conditional mean of the Poisson model. Thus, we write

$$
E \left[ y _ {i t} \mid \boldsymbol {x} _ {i t}, \varepsilon_ {i t} \right] = e x p \left(\alpha + \boldsymbol {x} _ {i i} ^ {\prime} \beta\right) = h _ {i} \lambda_ {i}
$$

where $h _ { i } = e x p ( \varepsilon _ { i t } )$ is assumed to have a one parameter gamma distribution, G(θ,θ) with mean 1 and variance $1 / \theta = \kappa$

After integrating $h _ { i }$ out of the joint distribution, we obtain the marginal negative binomial (NB) distribution,

$$
\operatorname{Prob} \left[ Y = y _ {i t} \mid x _ {i t} \right] = \frac {\Gamma \left(\theta + y _ {i t}\right) r _ {i} ^ {\theta} \left(1 - r _ {i}\right) ^ {y _ {i t}}}{\Gamma \left(1 + y _ {i t}\right) \Gamma (\theta)}
$$

with $y _ { i t } = 0 , 1 , . . . , \theta > 0 , r _ { i } = \theta / ( \theta + \lambda _ { i } ) .$

The latent heterogeneity induces overdispersion while preserving the conditional mean:

$$
\begin{array}{c} E [ y _ {i t} | \boldsymbol {x} _ {i t}, \boldsymbol {\varepsilon} _ {i t} ] = \lambda_ {i} \\ V a r [ y _ {i t} | \boldsymbol {x} _ {i t} ] = \lambda_ {i} [ 1 + (1 / \theta) \lambda_ {i} ] = \lambda_ {i} [ 1 + \kappa \lambda_ {i} ] \end{array}
$$

where $\kappa = V a r [ h _ { i } ]$

Maximum likelihood estimation of the parameters of the NB model $( \alpha , \beta , \theta )$ is straightforward.

We use fixed effect estimation for the model specified. Fixed effects regression is the appropriate model to use when we aim to control for omitted variables that differ between cases but are constant over time (Baltagi 2008).
