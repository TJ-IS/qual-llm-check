# IS Avoidance in Health-Care Groups: A Multilevel Investigation

Gerald C. Kane

Carroll School of Management, Boston College, Chestnut Hill, Massachusetts 02467, gerald.kane@bc.edu

Giuseppe (Joe) Labianca

Gatton School of Business and Economics, University of Kentucky, Lexington, Kentucky 40506, joelabianca@gmail.com

he information systems (IS) literature has focused considerable research on IS resistance, particularly in Tthe health-care industry. Most of this attention has focused on the impact of IS resistance on systems initial implementation, but little research has investigated whether and how post-adoption resistance affect performance. We focus on a particular type of post-adoption resistance, which we call IS avoidance, to identify situations in which individuals avoid working with adopted IS despite the need and opportunity to do so. We examine the effects of IS avoidance on patient care delivered by health-care groups across three levels of analysis: the individual level, the shared group level, and the configural group level. We find that IS avoidance is significantly and negatively related to patient care only at the configural group level, which suggests that patient care is not degraded by the number of doctors and/or nurses in a group avoiding a system, but rather by their locations in the group’s workflow network configuration. We use qualitative data collected over 16 months at the research site to help explain these results. Implications for theory and practice are discussed.

Key words: IS use; IS avoidance; IS resistance; multilevel analysis; multimodal networks; multimethod study; social networks; centrality; configural use; healthcare; performance

## Introduction

The information systems (IS) literature has widely considered users’ resistance to IS and has identified resistance to be particularly problematic in health-care settings (Hendy et al. 2005, Timmons 2003). Information systems are supposed to positively influence health-care efficiency and quality, but these goals are likely to be thwarted if health-care workers avoid using the systems (Chau and Hu 2001). One hospital estimated that IS resistance alone hurt efficiency by 20%–40% (Poon et al. 2004). Compared with managers in other industries, managers in health-care settings are typically less able to mandate system use because health-care professionals often have greater status and freedom to resist (Doolin 2004, Kohli and Kettinger 2004). Furthermore, health-care groups face tasks that are often unstructured and uncertain and that unfold rapidly (Faraj and Xiao 2006, Klein et al. 2006), so users have considerable opportunity to avoid using systems. Because contemporary healthcare is often provided in interdependent groupbased settings (Ash et al. 2003, Kane and Alavi 2008), IS resistance may show disproportionately negative effects; a single medical professional resisting IS may affect other group members’ ability to provide appropriate care.

Most research on IS resistance in general and in healthcare particularly has investigated the impact of resistance on initial IS implementation (Gill 1996; Hirschheim and Newman 1988; Joshi 1991; Kim and Kankanhalli 2009; Lapointe and Rivard 2005, 2007; Marakas and Hornik 1996; Markus 1983; Martinko et al. 1996). IS resistance, however, does not necessarily end at implementation; it includes a broad spectrum of behaviors, ranging from active forms such as physical destruction or vandalism to more passive forms such as lack of cooperation or apathy (Carnall 1986; Coetsee 1993, 1999; Lapointe and Rivard 2005; Marakas and Hornik 1996). Although the more active forms of resistance are less likely to continue after a system is successfully implemented, the more passive forms such as being uncooperative and/or apathetic are liable to persist. Workers may accept that the system has been implemented and that they should use it, but they may still expend little time and effort to employ it effectively. Despite the importance of IS to health-care delivery, little research has investigated the performance implications of post-adoption resistance, which has prompted calls for further research into these and other post-adoption behaviors (e.g., Jasperson et al. 2005, Lapointe and Rivard 2007).

We adopt the term IS avoidance—an individual’s preference to avoid working with an information system despite the need and opportunity to do so—to describe a specific type of post-adoption resistance, highlighting three differences between IS avoidance and other types of IS resistance. First, avoidance implies that the IS resistance occurs after implementation; a system must be in place to avoid it. Second, the term describes relatively mild responses compared with extreme behaviors of sabotage or disruption through vandalism or destruction. Third, avoidance implies that the individual has the opportunity and even the need, but consciously circumvents using the system.

In this paper, we investigate whether and how IS avoidance influences the performance of health-care groups in terms of efficiency and quality of care. We do so across three levels of analysis: the individual user level, the shared group level, and the configural group level. At the individual level, we examine whether the patient’s doctor avoids using IS and, if so, the impact of the avoidance on patient care. At the shared group level, we examine whether patient care is affected by the total number of providers in a health-care team—individuals such as nurses, receptionists, and clerks—who avoid using IS. At the configural group level, we examine whether patient care is related to the avoidant provider’s position, central or peripheral, in the health-care team’s social network. Previous research has typically viewed IS resistance as a multilevel phenomenon (Lapointe and Rivard 2005). The multilevel nature of IS resistance is particularly salient for questions of performance, because if the relationship between IS avoidance and performance appears at one level of analysis (e.g., shared group) but not at another (e.g., individual), markedly different strategies might be needed for addressing the problem.

We use a two-phase, multimethod approach. In our first phase, we combine survey data with secondary organizational data to quantitatively test the relationship between IS avoidance and three metrics for evaluating patient care—efficiency of care, patient satisfaction, and quality of care. Our results indicate that IS avoidance is consistently and negatively associated with each of these measures of performance—but only at the configural group level. Contrary to our initial hypotheses, our models show that IS avoidance is not significant at the individual and shared group levels. In our second phase, we use qualitative data from a 16-month organizational study to better understand our somewhat unexpected findings. Our analysis in this phase suggests that IS avoidance can disproportionately affect performance but that groups can rely on complex network structures to compensate for the IS avoidance at the individual and shared group levels. These structures cannot, however, mitigate the effect of IS avoidance at the configural group level. Implications for theory and practice are discussed.

## IS Avoidance: Beyond Simple Lack of Use

The actual use of IS is critical in assessing its performance impact (Devaraj and Kohli 2003, Kim et al. 2005). If people do not use the IS despite its performance-enhancing capabilities, IS will be unlikely to positively impact performance. However, IS avoidance may also influence performance beyond simple lack of use. Individuals might have legitimate reasons for choosing not to use IS, such as lack of time, need, or training. IS avoidance, however, occurs when the individual consciously chooses to avoid IS despite having time, need, and ability. Little research has investigated whether conscious decisions to avoid IS—rather than simple lack of use—may affect performance very differently.

IS avoidance implies that the individual views the system negatively. Compared with other motivating factors, negative attitudes have been shown to disproportionately and powerfully affect behaviors, a characteristic known as negative asymmetry (Labianca and Brass 2006, Taylor 1991). Empirical research has shown that people tend to focus more heavily on negative stimuli as a threat than on positive stimuli as a benefit (Easterbrook 1959, Eysenck 1976, Taylor 1991). Discrepancy theorists suggest that negative stimuli, compared with positive stimuli, influence judgments and behaviors more intensely because the negative clashes more dramatically with individuals’ mostly positive, Pollyanna worldviews (Fiske 1980, Jones and McGillis 1976, Piaget 1932, Sherif and Sherif 1967). People generally expect their environment to be positive. When negative events threaten, individuals generate negative attitudes that dominate their attention and drive their judgments and behaviors. If they cannot cope with and minimize the discrepancy between the environment and their expectations, their negative attitudes can enduringly, inordinately, and powerfully influence their future behaviors (Taylor 1991). Negative attitudes, then, can often more fundamentally and powerfully predict behaviors and outcomes.

The IS literature provides evidence suggesting that the disproportionately powerful effects of behaviors motivated by negative attitudes might extend to user-system relationships. Just as psychologists find that negative attitudes often result from an inability to minimize a negative event, IS avoidance can be traced to an inability to cope with a significant negative IT-related event (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005). When individuals perceive IT as a threat, their efforts are “mostly oriented toward either diminishing emotional distress associated with the technology or reducing the perceived negative consequences associated with it” (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005, p. 518). When they cannot minimize the perceived threat, they may avoid interaction by developing system avoidance. Negative attitudes have also been shown to be disproportionately more influential than positive ones in electronically mediated relationships. In a study examining positive and negative trust in telemedicine settings, negative IS perceptions were found to be more influential than positive ones (Paul and McDaniel 2004). If some form of negative asymmetry operates within user-system relationships, IS avoidance could considerably and differently affect performance, beyond the effects of overall use levels.

## A Multilevel View of IS Avoidance

Following previous research (Lapointe and Rivard 2005), we adopt a multilevel view of IS avoidance and embrace what Burton-Jones and Gallivan (2007) call the canonical view of multilevel research, which is a “relatively functionalist, positivist, and variance-oriented perspective” (p. 659). We explore the performance impact of IS avoidance at three levels of analysis—the individual level, the shared group level, and the configural group level (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007).

A multilevel view of IS avoidance is desirable because the behaviors of single users may not sufficiently explain the performance impact of IS avoidance (Dickson et al. 1974, Markus 1983). The multilevel view would also suggest steps for managing group IS avoidance. IS avoidance by individual doctors or by support staff may affect patient care very differently, which might inform managers’ interventions to mitigate potential effects of IS avoidance. The former might entail training programs for doctors, and the latter might require broader initiatives that address all group members. In contrast, the number of IS avoidance relationships may have relatively little impact on patient care, compared with the effect of IS avoidance relationships that are situated in critical positions within the workflows. Here, rather than increased training to eliminate avoidance relationships, interventions might involve identifying key IS avoidance relationships in a group and/or reconfiguring workflows to compensate for IS avoidance relationships.

First, we explore the impact of IS avoidance at the individual level. In the health-care context, we interpret the individual level to represent the designated physician who typically has ultimate responsibility for providing care and for its outcomes, even though other team members might provide supportive care or consultation. The literature has focused considerable attention on whether doctors will avoid IS and thereby limit its potential impact on efficiency and quality of care (James 1997, Kuperman and Gibson 2003, Lapointe and Rivard 2006, Philip and David 2004). Doctors may also have greater opportunity to avoid using IS by delegating its use to others (Jensen and Aanestad 2007, Kohli and Kettinger 2004). If a doctor avoids using IS during a particular patient visit, medical care may suffer (Berner et al. 2005). Care may take longer to deliver if the doctor relies on alternative, workaround mechanisms for getting information or providing orders. Patients may be disgruntled if the doctor is unaware of information available in the system, such as the reason for the patient visit, the patient’s medical history, or relevant insurance or medication information. Quality of care may also suffer because the doctor may fail to order recommended screenings if he or she does not have access to certain patient information or fails to prescribe follow-up care according to previous tests recorded in the system. The health-care literature documents negative effects from unawareness of vital medical information (Smith et al. 2005). Furthermore, if the doctor avoids using the system during a visit, IS use by other group members is irrelevant to patient outcomes if the doctor ultimately responsible for patient care fails to access or use it.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1.</sup> IS avoidance at the individual physician level will be negatively related to patient satisfaction and the efficiency and quality of patient care.

Second, we explore IS avoidance at the shared group level—IS avoidance among health-care group members who provide support to individual doctors. Receptionists usually manage schedules and insurance/payment information. Nurses typically check and record vital information (e.g., blood pressure, pulse rates), validate medical records, and verify that patient visits are accurately captured and detailed in the system. Data clerks often enter diagnoses, deliver diagnosis information, and schedule followup appointments. Other doctors may enter information that will be important later when another physician in the group or a specialist sees the patient. These various responsibilities must be coordinated if the group is to provide efficient and effective patient care.

Unlike the individual level, where care is attributed to a specific doctor, however, support roles are often interchangeable and may involve many team members. A single nurse may be assigned to support multiple doctors, or nurses may cover for each other. Therefore, support staff avoidance may also affect efficiency and quality of patient care; the more group members avoid the available IS, the more negatively the avoidance is likely to affect care. For example, patients may wait longer if the receptionist fails to enter information documenting their arrival or if the nurse fails to check the system to learn that they are waiting. Patients may become dissatisfied if support providers ask repeatedly for the same information, such as reasons for visits, or fail to meet needs the patient has stated explicitly. Additionally, the staff may fail to tend to broader needs such as recommending routine tests, providing follow-up test results, or noting changes in vital statistics. Negative effects may also be cumulative; if a receptionist, a data clerk, and a nurse all avoid using IS, delays may increase as patients move through the system. If multiple nurses avoid using IS, their mutual support may be hindered. Therefore, the more IS avoidance relationships there are in a group, the more likely these behaviors will detrimentally affect patient care.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2.</sup> IS avoidance at the shared group level will be negatively related to patient satisfaction and the efficiency and quality of patient care.

Third, we explore the impact of IS avoidance at the configural group level, which recognizes that a group’s patient care may be more than the sum of its constituent parts. It is critically important to determine how use characteristics are distributed across the group to understand how IS use operates at the group level (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007, Kozlowski and Klein 2000). In the case of IS avoidance, the configural group level suggests that understanding who in a group avoids using IS may be as or more important for understanding the group’s ability to provide healthcare. For example, does the avoidance by a particularly prominent group member have more effect than the number of IS avoidance relationships in a group?

Some previous research has argued that social network measures can be used to measure user relationships at the configural group level (Kane 2007). Among the most heavily researched measures in social network analysis is the concept of centrality, the importance of a node’s position in a network as a result of connections to other nodes (Scott 2000). Being a central individual in an organizational network has been associated with information benefits (Burt 1992), control (Burt 2007, Fleming and Waguespack 2007, Ryall and Sorenson 2007, Tsai 2001), influence (Ibarra 1993, 1995; Klein et al. 2004; Sparrowe and Liden 2005), and power (Ahuja et al. 2003, Brass and Burkhardt 1993, Ibarra 1993, Mehra et al. 2001). If central users avoid IS systems, they are more likely than avoidant peripheral members to detrimentally affect patient care. We use the term centrality of IS avoidance to refer to the extent to which the group configuration has IS avoidance concentrated in the more socially central members of the group.

Several reasons can explain why IS avoidance relationships might be more detrimental for group performance if they are concentrated among the group’s more central members. First, group members may contribute unequally to group performance. Those central in a social network have more important positions within the group’s knowledge and workflows (Borgatti and Cross 2003; Brass 1981, 1984); their IS avoidance may be particularly detrimental because they may create information bottlenecks. Second, central individuals may provide valuable support for others in the network. People may request direct assistance, asking others to perform systems tasks on their behalf (Kraemer et al. 1993) or indirect assistance, turning to others for advice and support in using IS (Sykes et al. 2009). Central individuals are more likely to receive requests for help because they are easier to approach (Kane and Alavi 2008); but central individuals who are avoidant may be unable to help. This situation is particularly problematic in health-care settings because health-care groups must often cooperatively improvise to deal with unexpected tasks (Klein et al. 2006). For example, a patient might arrive for a routine visit but suddenly and unexpectedly show signs of a serious, life-threatening condition requiring concentrated, coordinated responses.

Third, the centrality of IS avoidance captures the influence of informal group leadership on operational activities. Regardless of who holds official positions of power (e.g., doctors, administrators), individuals who are central in the social network often have significant informal authority that makes them more influential than formal authorities (Ahuja et al. 2003, Brass 1984, Klein et al. 2004, Sparrowe and Liden 2005). IS researchers have long recognized that leaders’ information technology competency often determines IS effectiveness (Bassellier and Benbasat 2004, Bassellier et al. 2003). The centrality of IS avoidance simply extends this organizational leadership argument from formal to informal leaders. If the informal leaders avoid IS, they are more likely to influence the group and hinder IS integration, intentionally or unintentionally. Because of the potential for information bottlenecks, the inability to tap into normal helping channels, and the lack of leadership backing, we expect:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3.</sup> IS avoidance at the configural group level will be negatively related to patient satisfaction and the efficiency and quality of patient care.

## Research Setting

We studied health-care groups in a regional division of a national health maintenance organization (HMO), which we refer to by the pseudonym Alpha Medical. This HMO delivers healthcare using a collection of doctors, nurses, and support staff to address the needs of a fixed panel of patients. Although patients make appointments with particular doctors, the entire group works together to provide care. Alpha Medical has developed a robust IS infrastructure to support its health-care groups, but its ability to provide effective care relies heavily on group interactions with each other and with IS. These groups share many features that make the setting attractive for studying the effects of user-system relationships in a network: the groups share a common task, have similar composition, operate independently, and rely heavily on IS to accomplish their work.

This setting allows us to control for many network (group) factors and thus isolate user-system effects on group-level outcomes. First, these groups share a common task—delivering healthcare. Each group provides primary care and is responsible for producing baseline assessments of patients’ needs and responding with appropriate treatments or specialist referrals. Each group has a similar workload; one doctor and two support staff are assigned for each riskadjusted panel of approximately 2,000–2,500 patients treated by that group.<sup>1</sup> Second, each group has a similar composition, comprising identical staff positions: approximately 4–6 doctors and 8–12 clinical and administrative support staff with similar training, educational backgrounds, experience, and organizational responsibilities. Each group also shares an identical set of six primary IS systems—scheduling, laboratory, radiology, population registry, medical abstract, and conferencing—representing the core portfolio of IS for conducting tasks (see Appendix A for a description of these systems). Third, although these groups work interdependently to provide care, the groups themselves are independent from one another. Geographic proximity, scheduling procedures, and colocation of paper-based records mean that doctors within a group often treat one another’s patients, but rarely treat patients from other groups. Fourth, these groups rely heavily on IS to perform their tasks. As groups coordinate the significant resources of the organization to address the healthcare needs of a large panel of patients, the IS plays a critical role in delivering that care. During our initial background research, an Alpha Medical director noted, “There is no way these health-care groups can produce the kinds of results they are accountable for without effective use of the IS we provide.”

## Research Method: Phase One

We used a two-phase, multimethod approach, beginning with interviews with key informants and other medical care providers, as well as reviewing archival documents; this led to the creation and distribution of the first-phase survey. The second phase included extensive additional qualitative data collection and a reanalysis of the first-phase qualitative data to better understand our survey results. We surveyed 614 members of 40 health-care groups in the regional division of Alpha Medical in early 2005. The survey was both pre- and pilot tested with a small group of respondents before administration, ensuring that the survey and our administration procedures captured the network features we were studying. The survey was a standard sociometric instrument that gave respondents a roster of group members and the six systems that the organization had identified as being most critical for outcomes across all groups. The roster method is standard best practice for collecting social network data (Scott 2000). Respondents evaluated the frequency and depth of their interactions with each individual and each IS in the network. The survey instrument and the anchors are shown in Table 1. We enjoyed strong organizational support for our survey and achieved generally good response rates (n = 557, 91%). Furthermore, no group had lower than a 79% response rate, an important threshold for whole network analysis because it ensures that we capture most of the groups’ networks (Sparrowe et al. 2001).

We used the total number of systems that a doctor indicated preference for avoiding as the measure for IS avoidance at the individual level. Then we summed the total number of IS that all group members indicated preference for avoiding across the group and used that as the measure for IS avoidance at the shared group level.

We constructed the measure for IS avoidance at the configural group level by multiplying the total number of avoidance relationships reported by each individual group member by the normalized eigenvector centrality score of that individual in the group’s social network.<sup>2</sup> Eigenvector centrality measures a node’s relative importance in a network based on the principle that connections to well-connected nodes contribute more to the node’s importance in the network than an equal number of connections to poorly connected nodes.<sup>3</sup> Each individual’s normalized eigenvector centrality score in the group’s social network was calculated using UCINet 6.197, representing the overall centrality of each person in the multimodal network, weighted by group size. Thus, groups in which central members in the social network reported negative avoidance relationships had higher values; groups in which more peripheral members in the social network reported system avoidance relationships had lower values. These values were calculated independently for each avoidance tie, so a group member reporting two avoidance relationships with different systems was calculated twice. These values were summed across the group and then divided by the number of avoidance relationships reported by the group. Thus, this measure captures only the configuration of the IS avoidance relationships in the group (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007, Kozlowski and Klein 2000).

## Dependent Variables

Regional manager respondents believed strongly that successful task completion heavily depended on information technology and that IS avoidance adversely affected success. If the group used IS, they were more likely to provide faster and better care. Employees who avoided using IS tended to rely on alternative ways of performing essential tasks—ways seen as slower and less reliable. No single measure adequately operationalized patient care in this setting, so we chose three measures to most robustly examine the different ways negative relationships might influence patient care: efficiency of care, patient satisfaction, and quality of care.<sup>4</sup> Organizational leaders identified measures as critical, and each group was regularly provided with performance updates on these metrics. A detailed description of how each system that the group used influenced the metrics used in this study can be found in Appendix A. These dependent variables are taken from Alpha Medical’s organizational records, which are defined, collected, and audited according to industry standards.<sup>5</sup> Nonprofit and governmental regulatory bodies commonly use such data to evaluate the quality of care delivered by a health-care organization. The organization constantly monitors all groups and provides feedback on their performance using these measures. Data were available for efficiency of care and patient satisfaction at the doctor level.<sup>6</sup>

Efficiency of care is important because a key aspect of the cost of delivering care is related to how quickly groups provide care. If they can move patients through the process more rapidly, they can attend more patients, so groups were constantly evaluated on timely treatment. Patient satisfaction is a key measure from a business process standpoint for keeping patients as customers. Furthermore, patients schedule visits, so they are well suited to determine whether the group meets their needs. Finally, quality of care is arguably the most important evaluation. Patients with chronic diseases such as diabetes are often difficult to treat because they need consistent and preventative treatment over time. Nevertheless, chronic diseases are becoming the biggest sources of increasing costs in healthcare (Thorpe et al. 2004), and Alpha Medical has recently made chronic care a top priority.

Efficiency of care was operationalized in terms of the average minutes a patient waited for an appointment. Patient satisfaction measures were collected from a random sample of patients who had visited a particular doctor, and who were asked to rate their satisfaction with the care they received during a recent visit. Efficiency of care and patient satisfaction data are from the quarter concurrent with data collection. Quality of care was available at the patient level and operationalized in terms of diabetes control.<sup>7</sup> Diabetes control is a dichotomous variable available at the patient level that measures whether a diabetic patient’s long-term blood-sugar level is within recommended guidelines. Quality of care data are from the one-year period concluding with the quarter in which data were collected. Patient-level data are particularly valuable for the quality of care metrics, because individual patient characteristics play a significant role in determining care results (e.g., how faithfully the patient follows the doctor’s recommendations). If patients fail to manage their chronic diseases adequately, escalating conditions can become extremely expensive to treat and can significantly compromise the patient’s quality of life.

## Control Variables

In addition to assessing key independent variables via a survey, we obtained other metrics from existing organizational records for use as control variables, which can be found in Table 2. First, we argued that IS avoidance will influence performance separately from simple lack of use, so we controlled for individual doctors’ overall system use. Our survey asked the doctors to evaluate the frequency and depth of their work with the available IS. These measures have been used previously to evaluate IS use in similar settings (Kane and Alavi 2008). We averaged the scores across the six systems for each doctor to capture the frequency and depth of IS interaction. Second, we included several demographic control variables that have often been used in previous studies of IS use: age, gender, and tenure (Venkatesh et al. 2003). In addition, because race has been shown to be a particularly salient demographic characteristic in health-care settings (Fiscella et al. 2002, Ryn 2002), we included race measures.

Second, control data were included for grouplevel characteristics. These data account for various other possible explanations for outcomes that may be attributable to group composition. The group level included the same demographic control variables used at the individual level. Average age and tenure were calculated as the simple average of these values across the group. Gender and racial composition captured the percentage of male or white group members. We also controlled for average levels of IS use reported by all the individuals in the group. All were asked to indicate the frequency and depth of their interactions with each of the IS, and these responses were averaged across the group. We also devised a measure to control for configural IS use, but it introduced multicollinearity problems into our models (variance inflation factor = 905). Nevertheless, the results of our hypothesis testing are robust with respect to the presence or absence of this variable, so we removed it from our analyses.

Although individual network centralities provide insight on how the centrality of a particular system avoidance relationship might affect the group’s functioning, it is necessary to control for the group’s overall network centralization to isolate the effect on overall group functioning (Kang 2007). Network centralization is the dispersion in the centralities of the various nodes in a network (Freeman 1979). When the measure is high, it means that a few highly central nodes dominate the network, and the remaining nodes are peripheral. In contrast, when network centralization is low, the nodes are of roughly equal centrality and thus are able to interact more directly. Because we view a system avoidance relationship as a breakdown in the network of people and IS where an individual is avoiding a system and thus damaging the information flow within the network, we must control for the group’s network centralization, which is related to its ability to work around the breakdown (e.g., Bienenstock and Bonacich 2003), to compare across groups.

Third, we controlled for patient-specific data in relation to quality of care outcomes, accounting for the health outcomes of particular patients. In this instance, it is also important to control for patientlevel factors that represent elements of diabetes control, which are outside of the scope of the primary care group. Eye examinations, cholesterol screenings, and nephropathy screenings are all part of effective diabetes care, but are conducted by specialists outside the primary care setting. These variables may also serve as a proxy for the extent to which patients manage their own diabetes control. Whether patients seek these outside treatments likely indicates how actively they participate in their care.

## Quantitative Data Analysis

Data were entered into Excel and audited by an independent research assistant to ensure accuracy. These data were then imported into the statistical package R for analysis. Multiple regression was chosen as the data analysis method. A key consideration in the data is that the doctors and patients are clustered within groups, violating the independence of errors assumptions of ordinary least squares regression. The Huber-White robust variance/covariance matrix was used to correct for this multilevel clustering of data in the model. The Huber-White approach is based on the assumption that the error terms are correlated within clusters but uncorrelated across clusters.<sup>8</sup> The dependent variable for quality of care outcomes was operationalized at the patient rather than the physician. Alpha Medical captured patient-level data for quality of care outcomes only; therefore, data were unavailable for the other measures. Unlike efficiency of care, which is relatively independent of patientspecific factors (and otherwise controlled for by Alpha Medical’s intentional composition of the physician’s risk-adjusted panel of patients and carefully regulated workload), quality of care outcomes are critically dependent on patient-level differences, such as the patient’s willingness to follow the doctor’s recommendations for effective care, partially captured in the patient-level metrics used here. The patient-level quality of care variable (diabetes control) was dichotomous, requiring logistic regression. The Huber-White variance-covariance matrix has also been specified for logistic regression models. See Appendices B and C for descriptive statistics and the correlation matrix. Before proceeding with data analysis and interpretation, the data were checked to ensure that they conformed to the assumptions underlying the modeling approach.<sup>9</sup>

Also, note the results of some of the control variables across the three models. Actual levels of IS use are important for explaining group performance. IS use is significantly related to efficiency of care at both the individual and the shared group levels of analysis (negative coefficients indicate improved performance in this model) and significantly related to patient satisfaction at the shared group level. Thus, actual IS use by group members is positively related to performance, independent of IS avoidance. It may also be important that individual-level use is not significantly related to patient satisfaction. Doctors may fear that using the systems will interfere with the doctor-patient relationship, but our data provide no evidence to support that conclusion. Unsurprisingly, the type of primary care (e.g., adult medicine and family medicine) affects efficiency of care and patient satisfaction, as these groups provide primary care to different types of patients. Finally, it is interesting that group racial composition is significant in two of our models—efficiency of care and quality of care. Groups with more minority health-care workers were less efficient and delivered lower quality-care. We suspect that this result has more to do with the racial composition of the patient populations. Anecdotally, the racial composition of the group correlated highly with the racial composition of the patient population in the geographic area served by the group, and race has been shown to be a significant variable in health-care outcomes (Fiscella et al. 2002, Ryn 2002). The fact that the racial composition of the health-care group is not significantly related to patient satisfaction may support this interpretation.

## Research Method: Phase Two

Our results in Phase 1 were somewhat unexpected in that our hypotheses were consistently supported at the configural group level but not at the individual or shared group levels. To better understand these results, we analyzed qualitative data that we collected regarding IS avoidance in these health-care groups to provide further insight.

Four criteria are needed to establish trustworthiness in qualitative research: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability (Lincoln and Guba 1985). The researchers employed several methods to reinforce the trustworthiness of the data collection and analysis: (1) The data were collected in the natural setting and in real time and were validated by an independent researcher not in the field; (2) the researchers had a long-term involvement in Alpha Medical and intensive, firsthand observation of health-care delivery at multiple levels of the organization; (3) multiple methods and sources of data were used, including interviews, observations, meeting notes and transcripts, and system demonstrations; (4) researchers iterated between data collection and analysis to ensure a focus on the phenomena of interest; and (5) member checks on the initial data were conducted by inviting feedback and insight from participants at each stage of analysis (Lincoln and Guba 1985). Each researcher independently identified themes that emerged from the interviews and observations. Then triangulation (Jick 1979) was used to identify themes that were common to several data sources. This process of comparing data with other evidence on the same point has also been called establishing contextual validity (Diesing 1971).

The lead researcher spent 16 months conducting qualitative research within Alpha Medical to better understand the nature and impact of IS avoidance relationships in these health-care groups. Numerous interviews were conducted with employees, including unstructured interviews with regional administrators (9 interviews, 60 minutes each), semistructured interviews with group leaders (16 interviews, 60 minutes each), and focus group interviews with healthcare groups (11 interviews, 50 minutes each, with an average of 6 participants). Second, the researcher also attended a series of system demonstrations for each of the systems used in the groups (6 demonstrations, 45 minutes each). These demonstrations were held in conjunction with an internal audit of how health-care groups were actually using the available systems. Observation and interview data from meetings and system demonstrations were augmented by PowerPoint<sup>®</sup> slides, meeting minutes, and other documentation provided to meeting participants. Third, the researcher observed four groups over a typical workday (Eight hours per observation, two observations per group). Fourth, the researcher attended 16 organizational meetings related to IT usage and deployment in health-care groups over 18 months and reviewed minutes of those meetings for the prior two years.

We adopted the technique of constant comparison, iterating between data collection and analysis (Glaser and Strauss 1967). We conducted several phases of data collection and analysis, allowing the findings from each phase to guide data collection in the next. Brief project reports documented the findings of each iteration (Miles et al. 1994) and identified individuals to be interviewed, meetings to be attended, and activities to be observed. Once our data collection was complete, we turned to more systematic methods of data analysis. We transcribed the observation notes, interview transcripts, and research memos, and these data were imported into Nvivo 7 for analysis. We then conducted three rounds of coding—open, axial, and selective (Strauss and Corbin 1990). Our open coding processes focused on identifying, naming, categorizing, and describing phenomena found in the text. Codes in this stage were derived purely from the data. We coded every example of IS use and IS avoidance and sought to identify its effect on the performance of the health-care group, without concern for theoretical bearing. We employed relatively broad codes to ensure that they sufficiently captured the various types of information use relationships found in our data. This list of open codes was then arranged into general categories using axial codes. The axial codes were primarily derived from the multilevel literature describing general structures at the individual, shared group, and configural group levels of analysis, as well as possible antecedents to or outcomes of those structures. Next, we conducted selective coding, in which we decided that the overarching theme (macrocategories) of multilevel impact of IS avoidance was the central explanatory mechanism for our results.

In total, 847 distinct thematic elements were identified and grouped into 35 categories, which were grouped into 6 macrocategories—network antecedents, dyadic network structures, nodal network structures, whole network structures, positive outcomes, and negative outcomes. Although other themes were identified (e.g., differences between employee age and IS use), this paper focuses on the antecedents and consequences of IS avoidance and how the teams tried to mitigate the negative consequences of IS avoidance on patient care. A research assistant generally knowledgeable in the subject matter but not directly connected to the research project validated our coding processes. She coded the interview transcripts, observations, and meeting notes. Her independent coding resulted in a 91% interrater reliability. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion to elect which of the two categories fit better with the data. When both codes fit the data equally well, we retained both codes.

For the purposes of this study, we reanalyzed the qualitative data for answers to two questions: (1) What antecedents and consequences are found in IS avoidance relationships? (2) Why do we fail to see the relationship between IS avoidance and negative ties that we expected at the individual and shared group levels of analysis? As a result of this analysis, the negative outcomes of IS avoidance were primarily situated at the configural group level, whereas the group could often compensate for IS avoidance at the individual level through various mechanisms. This qualitative analysis provided further insight into our survey results in two important ways. First, it gave greater insight into why IS avoidance might influence performance, even when our analysis controlled for overall levels of IS use in the group. Second, it identified an explanatory mechanism for why this performance effect was not significantly related to performance at the individual or shared group levels, as hypothesized. Specifically, we found that groups developed complex use structures that helped compensate for IS avoidance at these levels and mitigated its effect on performance.

## Antecedents and Consequences of IS Avoidance

Similar to the effect of negative attitudes on other types of behaviors, we found the negative emotions associated with system avoidance had a greater socioemotional impact on users than did positive usersystem interactions. As participants were asked to describe their negative avoidance relationships with particular systems, they spent considerably more time and energy detailing specific problems with each system than with benefits provided by the systems they used regularly. In general, they were far more eager and willing to discuss their problems than to talk about the benefits associated with particular systems. They often spoke quite passionately about IS problems and their reasons for avoiding using the IS. One doctor strongly vilified a particular system, saying,

Visually, it is “hostile” and unpleasant to use. The system flickers for a while before it settles. It gives you a headache if used for too long. I just don’t like to use it. The system also gives you all sorts of extraneous stuff you don’t need. You have to scroll through everything to see what needs to be done. It also spits you out to a weird screen once you’re done, and you have to click back to get to where you were. The only good thing about that system is that we heard it is going to be replaced.

Of course, doctors were not the only ones who preferred to avoid using certain IS; nurses and other group members were avoidant as well. One nurse reported, “I don’t have the time to do everything they expect me to and use that stupid [system], too.” Although nurses and other group members were often unable to avoid the IS entirely (unlike some of the doctors), many were still able to avoid the systems by using them only at the most minimal level of functionality. In describing her avoidance, one nurse reported,

I don’t use the scheduling program for much other than to tell us who is here. I don’t really even know how to work the system; the others (systems) are much more important for what we really do. Jane loves the scheduling system, though; she sorts schedules by physician to see how each one is managing their schedule. I didn’t even know the system could do that.

Consistent with previous literature, although IS avoidance could represent a complete failure to use a system, it most often was manifest as a preference to avoid using the system any more than absolutely necessary (Berner et al. 2005, Timmons 2003).

Participants indicated reasons for their avoidance that were strikingly similar to reasons reported in studies of interpersonal negative relationships. Discrepancy theory (e.g., Jones and McGillis 1976) is particularly relevant in explaining system avoidance relationships; individuals’ intensely negative feelings powerfully expressed their disappointment as reality contrasted with their expectations about how the system should function, ultimately leading to a systemavoidance relationship. When participants described their problems with the lab system, for example, they typically compared it with another system they had used for a similar task. In contrast, newer employees who had never worked with the previous system reported few problems. Thus, the problems with the lab system were primarily in relation to how some thought it should function. Inability to minimize the impact of negative experiences also seemed salient for describing the avoidance preference (Taylor 1991). Some users traced their avoidance to past negative experiences that were so salient that they were unable to minimize the impression. One doctor indicated his disdain for the population registry system because a temporary malfunction in the system once indicated erroneously that patients had been sent important follow-up letters. The doctor felt that the incident caused him to render substandard care and that his reputation had been tarnished, and he avoided the system as a result.

## Compensating for IS Avoidance Through Complex Use Structures

The qualitative data also helped provide greater insight into why we did not find the impact on patient care that we expected at either the individual or shared group level of analysis. Users were often able to compensate for IS avoidance relationships by developing different complex use structures. At the individual level, doctors frequently compensated for their avoidance of particular systems by having someone else interact with the system on their behalf. These brokering relationships could compensate even for complete avoidance. One respondent noted,

I have seen the usage logs for the lab system. There are definitely a number of physicians who have never logged on to the system [emphasis in original], who just do not want to use it. This is a mission critical system. And you wonder how they are checking their labs. Well, they are having their nurse do it, or having their nurse manually print up the labs and then checking them off, “Do this, do this, do this.” And then someone else has to go back into the system and do the work.

During many observation periods, we saw these brokering relationships being employed on many different systems. One doctor who preferred to avoid using the scheduling system told his nurse about necessary follow-up appointments, and the nurse scheduled them. In another case, a doctor preferred to avoid using the lab system because “I only have a few minutes allotted for each patient visit; using the system for even two or three minutes cuts too much into that visit.” He relied on his nurse and/or a dedicated data clerk to use the system on his behalf. In some cases, the organization officially cultivated these brokering relationships. One regional administrator reported that doctors avoided the population registry system because it was “clunky” and “difficult to use.” The solution was to appoint a single doctor to work with the population registry on behalf of other doctors in the group. Although not exclusively associated with IS avoidance, these brokering relationships allowed doctors to avoid using the system while mitigating the impact of avoidance on patient care.

At the shared group level, another complex use structure appeared more salient for explaining our results. At this level, people preferred to work with others who shared similar usage patterns; those who preferred to avoid certain systems often worked with others who felt the same; those who used certain systems preferred to work with others who did also. Workers seeking advice or assistance from others in the group wanted coworkers who performed in similar and familiar ways (i.e., with or without the system), which resulted in clustering of like users. These clusters were significant because avoidant workers could develop common workarounds within their cluster to compensate for their shared avoidance preferences. For example, some clusters used paper announcements and memos rather than the conferencing system. We observed one cluster using Post-It<sup>®</sup> notes rather than the scheduling system to monitor how long patients had been waiting. Another cluster simply posted written schedules at the beginning of the day. Others used paper flags in the medical record rather than automatic reminders provided by the population registry system. Such shared workarounds meant that people within the cluster, could avoid using the conferencing system, the scheduling system, or the population registry system without significantly affecting others in the cluster, because the others did not expect them to enter data into the system for later use. These avoidance rela tionships had less effect on those outside the cluster because they largely preferred to avoid working with those who avoided the systems. Although these clusters occasionally caused group tension (we observed nurses exhibiting frustration with having to show another nurse in the avoidance cluster how to perform a routine task in a critical system) and overall levels of IS use were still positively related to most measures of performance, user clusters allowed the group to avoid significant negative impact of IS avoidance on patient care at the shared group level.

Brokering and clustering help explain why IS avoidance fails to influence patient care at the individual or the shared group levels, as we hypothesized, and they also provide additional insight as to why IS avoidance would be consistently and negatively related to patient care at the configural group level. Network structures that were developed to compensate for IS avoidance would be considerably less effective when the avoidant individuals were central in the social network. In the case of brokering, others are more likely to turn for help to individuals who are central to the group’s social network (Kane and Alavi 2008). If these central individuals are avoidant, others are severely limited in getting help. Avoidant clusters that hold central positions could be particularly detrimental; they might not only disrupt the ability of others outside the avoidance cluster to work together to use the systems effectively, but they might also influence group attitudes toward the system. One lead doctor reported,

The older, more experienced nurses kind of dominate the culture of the group. If they are not into the technology, it distills to the rest of the staff. So we have examples of struggle. We have some people who are just scared of a computer, and they have been willing to overcome that fear. Then we have other nurses where we have moved people around where we have seen them flourish under the leadership of people who are very computer comfortable and savvy.

When users who are central in the group are avoidant, their attitudes are more likely to affect others. Thus, IS avoidance relationships centrally situated in the team might inordinately impact performance and also interfere with the mechanisms the team has devised to compensate for avoidance.

## Discussion

This paper is a report of a two-phase, multimethod study we conducted of the relationship between IS avoidance and performance in health-care groups at three levels of analysis—the individual level, the shared group level, and the configural group level. Our first phase of research finds that IS avoidance is negatively related to performance when we control for overall IS use levels, but only at the configural group level. Our second phase provides a deeper understanding of IS avoidance, as it influences performance beyond simple levels of use and demonstrates that health-care groups could mitigate the effects of IS avoidance at the individual and shared group levels by developing complex use structures that allow them to compensate for the IS avoidance relationship in ways that were ineffective at the configural group level.

## Theoretical Implications

This research makes several key contributions specifically to the literature involving health-care IT and also to the general IS literature. First, the study provides further insight regarding the relationship between IS avoidance and performance in the health-care industry. Doctors’ preferences to avoid IS are widely considered a key factor limiting the widespread “informating” of the industry. Our results suggest that although certain types of IS avoidance are indeed detrimental to the performance of health-care teams, effective support can compensate for doctors’ IS avoidance. In particular, we find that assigning others to use IS on an avoidant doctor’s behalf successfully eliminates the potential detriments. Recognizing that IS avoidance is not necessarily universally detrimental, researchers should focus on identifying and mitigating instances of IS avoidance that most greatly affect group performance.

Second, the research responds to calls for more research into post-adoption behaviors (e.g., Jasperson et al. 2005, Lapointe and Rivard 2007). Most previous research in healthcare and in IS has focused only on resistance effects in the system-implementation phase. This study demonstrates that after a system is implemented, resistance may persist in the form of avoidance, which can influence how well IS affects performance. The study also shows a fundamentally different relationship for IS avoidance and performance than simple lack of use. Although some users might have legitimate reasons for not using a particular system (e.g., they do not need it often in their organizational role), IS avoidance focuses on workers who need the system but consciously choose to avoid it, and our results suggest that this phenomenon is more important. In our study, IS avoidance shows a consistent negative effect on patient care even when we control for overall levels of group use.

Third, our study extends the tradition of multilevel research in the health-care industry by using measures for and applications of the configural group level, often overlooked in multilevel research (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007). Adopting the social network measure of centrality as a measure for the configuration of IS avoidance relationships in the group, we demonstrate (i) that the centrality of IS avoidance is consistently and negatively related to patient care and (ii) that IS avoidance affects patient care only at the configural level, statistically apart from overall levels of use. Considering our consistent results across quality, patient satisfaction, and efficiency of patient care outcome measures, if we had failed to conduct a multilevel analysis that included configural measures, we would have concluded inaccurately that IS avoidance does not affect patient care.

Fourth, to better understand our nonsignificant results at the individual and shared group levels, we discovered that individuals compensate for IS avoidance relationships through complex network structures such as brokering and clustering. We provide further empirical evidence that users’ interactions may be an important aspect of how many organizations use IS (Lamb and Kling 2003). This feature may be relatively unique for health-care teams and similar settings with considerable individual autonomy and may apply less to more rigid environments such as manufacturing teams. IS research has only begun to explore how social network structures emerge as multiple users interact with multiple IS and the effects these structures have on performance outcomes.

## Managerial Implications

Our study’s results are particularly important in light of the recent public debate regarding U.S. healthcare reform. The United States spends more money on healthcare than any other industrialized country, but it lags considerably in health-care outcomes such as quality, efficiency, and patient satisfaction (Anderson and Poullier 1999). For example, in 2004, U.S. hospitals spent \$58 billion on 6 million hospital stays by patients suffering from diabetes (Russo and Jiang 2006). These visits are typically longer and costlier than hospitalizations for other reasons, and these patients are less likely to be insured. Therefore, even small changes in an organization’s ability to control diabetes can substantially affect costs, employee labor, and patients’ quality of life. Our results show that the proper health-care team configuration with regard to IS could help teams monitor the health of diabetics.

Increasing the effective use of IS in healthcare is widely recognized as a key component of any healthcare reform initiative and a major potential source of improving quality and affordability. IS avoidance by health-care professionals is often recognized as an important impediment to realizing the potential benefits. Our results help better understand IS avoidance and develop more targeted interventions. We find that IS avoidance by people central to the healthcare group drives the detrimental effects on quality and efficiency rather than the average level of avoidance by doctors or the average level of avoidance within a group of health-care providers.

These results suggest that managers should understand the network structure of their medical teams and counteract IS avoidant behavior of the most central health-care members by changing their behaviors or changing team structures. The manager may deal with such individuals directly by eliminating ISavoidance relationships, a solution that may be most direct but may also significantly affect others in the group. If such direct interventions go wrong (e.g., the individual quits or resists), the group may be severely impacted by the loss of a central group member who is so critically related to their informal functioning (Krackhardt 1992). The manager may also deal with the problem indirectly to decentralize the individual’s network position, by changing the group’s generative rules (Kogut 2000). For instance, at Alpha Medical, certain managers rotated assignments so that doctors worked with different nurses weekly, whereas other managers fixed assignments so that doctors worked with the same nurses all the time. These decisions changed network structures and, in turn, the position of IS avoidance relationships.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study has some limitations that the reader should carefully consider. Our biggest limitation is the use of cross-sectional data, which limits our ability to make causal arguments. Although we generally approach the analysis with the argument that individuals who avoid IS will cause adverse effects on patient care, some uncontrolled aspect of lower performance (e.g., norms of complacency) could also lead to system avoidance. Another limitation is that we use selfreported data on system use because we focus on individuals’ avoidance intentions (i.e., whether active or passive). Unfortunately, our setting prohibits us from collecting useful system use log data. Our qualitative observations reveal that users often log in and remain logged in even though they are not using the system, or they stay online under the original user’s identification. Finally, we focus on IS avoidance exclusively in the health-care setting. If researchers seek to generalize these findings to other industries, they should do so carefully. We believe that these findings might generalize quite well to other professional settings with considerable individual autonomy and fluid task requirements (e.g., law, finance, entrepreneurial ventures) but may fail to generalize well to settings with considerably different tasks and environmental characteristics (e.g., manufacturing or retail).

Conclusion. Despite these limitations, this study offers important insights into the relationship between system avoidance and patient care. Our results suggest that IS avoidance may be a distinct construct from nonuse. As researchers and managers seek to understand the relationship between IS use (or nonuse) and performance, it may be important to consider the role of system avoidance. The study also suggests that one individual’s system avoidance can affect others in a group, perhaps even some who are unaware that the particular person is avoiding the system. We also provide further evidence that usersystem relationships are not independent dyads; they should be examined in the context of their wider network of relationships.
