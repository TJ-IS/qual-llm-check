# Why Funders Invest in Crowdfunding Projects: Role of Trust from the Dual-Process Perspective

Ting-Peng Liang<sup>a</sup>, Shelly Ping-Ju Wu<sup>b,\*</sup>, Chih-chi Huang<sup>a</sup>

a Department of Information Management, National Sun Yat-sen University, Taiwan b Research School of Management, Australian National University, Australia

## Highlights:

 An integrated trust-based model from the dual-process perspective is developed.

 We aim to explore the boundary conditions in which the effect of trust varies.

 Fundraiser's ability/expertise is the major factor for funder’s trust building.

 Project type and funding level moderate the effects of trust and its antecedents.

## Abstract

While previous studies have identified several factors that influence funders’ investment behavior in crowdfunding, few have explored the underlying mechanisms, particularly the potential boundary conditions in which the effect of funders’ trust varies. This paper examines two contextual variables: project type and funding level, which moderate the relationships between key antecedents and investment decisions. The research model based on the trust theory and dual-process perspective was developed and evaluated with a web-based experiment using actual crowdfunding projects chosen from a real crowdfunding site. Our findings provide new insight into the role of trust and its antecedents in different contexts.

Keywords: Crowdfunding, Trust, Investment intention, Dual-process theories, Elaboration likelihood model (ELM)

## 1. Introduction

Crowdfunding is an open call for funding via donation or in exchange for a reward in order to support specific projects and initiatives typically through the Internet [56, 60]. It provides a new channel through which entrepreneurs can raise financial resources to undertake innovative projects and manage their ventures [7]. Although a large number of successful fundraising projects have been reported, the success rate is only around one-third. Many promising proposals were not funded for different reasons. Hence, exploring the mechanism question.

Different types of crowdfunding platforms, with the most popular being reward-based, such as Eppela, Ulule, and Kickstarter [52], have attracted much attention from media and policymakers because of their contribution to support innovative projects and economic growth [19]. Kickstarter.com, for example, reported that by 2017 almost \$3 billion US dollars were pledged for various creative projects by 12.7 million people with 4 million of them repeated bakers. According to [66], crowdfunding market grew by 167 percent in 2014 to reach \$16.2 billion, up from \$6.1 billion in 2013, and they anticipated crowdfunding sponsorship to grow to more than \$34 billion in 2015. This strong growth is due in part to the rise of Asia as a major crowdfunding region. In a 2017 report [110], China has 427 crowdfunding platforms to raise RMB 22 billion.

The rapid growth of crowdfunding market has drawn much attention on various research issues (e.g., [51, 7, 60, 1, 78, 74]). Some of them investigate why fundraisers use crowdfunding to obtain resources. For example, Schwienbacher and Larralde [87] suggested that entrepreneurs with sufficient knowledge about the use of Web 2.0 technologies were more willing to adopt crowdfunding for their business ventures. Others explore why funders sponsor projects. For instance, Gerber et al. [35] identified funders’ motivations to participate in crowdfunding projects, which include consuming products and experience, as well as social interaction.

Albeit the growth of funding scale, the failure rate of crowdfunded projects is very high. A report from Statistica indicates that, as of April 2017, the success rate of fully funded projects on Kickstarter.com was 35.79 percent. In other words, two-thirds of the listed projects failed, implying the need for more research on the mechanism and factors that affect project success. In a study by Gerber and Hui [34], they found one of the major deterrents to funders participation, which is “lack of trust”.

Trust has been identified in previous research to be a critical determinant to online users intended behaviors (e.g., [32, 111, 121, 122]). This is particularly true for the investment decision. In a recent study, for instance, Bottazzi, et al. [111] reported that trust plays a significant role in venture capital firms’ investment decisions. Their empirical data indicate that early-stage investment requires higher trust. As the funding raised from the crowd is typically through a web-based platform, which is, in essence, an early stage venture investment in the online environment. Hence, it would be reasonable to examine whether trust plays a similar role in crowdfunding. In crowdfunding literature, trust is also recognized as a critical determinant of investment intention recently [107, 109]. However, these studies treated trust as antecedents and failed to explore factors that build trustworthiness. Furthermore, inconsistent finding exists to argue that trust has no significant effect on investment decision (e.g., [118]). Given the inconsistent finding and need for deeper knowledge about factors that affect the role of trust, it is important to explore more insight into what affects the trustworthiness and the success of a crowdfunding project.

In addition to treating trust as a primary antecedent of investment intention, previous studies have identified other influencing factors, such as project type and funding level (e.g., [109]). Kickstarter statistics also show a substantial difference in success rate among different types of projects. For instance, the success rates of theater and comics were 60.23% and 52.7% respectively, while technology and journalism were 19.80% and 21.50%, respectively (accessed April 14, 2017). Given such a big difference, it is natural that we need to investigate whether the role of trust varies when project type or other contextual factors change. So far, no research has investigated these issues.

The third issue that needs investigation is that previous research has identified several types of trust such as cognitive-based versus affective-based trust. It would be interesting also to know what role these different kinds of trust play.

Based on the research gap identified above, this study intends to answer the following research questions with solid theoretical support:

#

RQ1: Whether trust plays a significant role in the success of a crowdfunding project?

RQ2: What kind of trust-related factors affect the trustworthiness of a crowdfunding project?

RQ3: Whether the role of trust differs for different types of crowdfunding projects?

The proposed research model is based on the trust theory [112] and the dual-process theories [120]. Our model includes two types of trust-building antecedents, namely performance/cognition-based and affect/personality-based factors to elaborate the role of trust in determining funders’ intention to invest in crowdfunding projects. We also include project type and funding level as two contextual moderators. This model was evaluated with a webbased experiment on real fundraising cases. The reported investment intention is further verified by the actual outcome on a real fundraising platform.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Our research model and hypotheses are described in the next section. This is followed by our research method in Section 3 and data analysis in Section 4. Section 5 discusses our findings and Section 6 concludes this study with suggested topics for further research.

## 2. Research model and hypotheses development

This study integrates the trust theory [112] with the dual-process theories [120] to serve as the theoretical basis for building the research model. According to Castelfranchi and Falcone [112], trust is a mental state which represents a complex attitude of a relying agent x who feels trust toward another agent y which is trusted, about the behavior or action relevant to the result/goal. Two types of beliefs are identified as basic components of trust: “Competence” belief and “disposition” belief [112]. Competence belief refers to a positive evaluation of agent y that y is useful and can produce the expected result. Disposition belief refers to the belief of agent x that agent y will do something agent x needs. This categorization is actually in line with McKnight et al. [71], who argue that the initial trust between two parties is not built based on their prior experience with each other or their firsthand knowledge about each other; rather, it’s based on their disposition to trust or on relevant cognitive clues such as performance or quality.

Many factors that affect online trust building have been reported. For example, Kim,

#

Ferrin, and Rao [54] proposed a framework that divides the determinants of online trust in ecommerce into four categories: (1) cognition-based: such as privacy protection, security protection, and information quality; (2) affect-based: such as reputation, third-party certification, recommendation, and word-of-mouth; (3) experience-based: Internet experiences and familiarity, and (4) personality-oriented: an individual’s disposition to trust and his/her purchasing styles. Other studies have identified factors of trust building, such as expertise ability, reputation, satisfaction, and similarity to be important determinants of cognition-based and affect-based trust (e.g., [49]). In these previous studies, there is a theoretical gap to explain how these factors affect trust. In this study, we adopt the dual process perspective to develop our research framework for filling the gap.

Dual-process theories include a group of decision theories related to recent discoveries in cognitive and neurosciences [120]. Two paths in our brain are identified as systems 1 and 2. System 1 relies on the limbic system of the brain and leads to intuitive and affective decisions while system 2 relies on the frontal cortex of the brain that leads to cognitive and reasoned decisions [124]. Typical theories derived from this perspective include the elaboration likelihood model (ELM), systematic-heuristic model [125] and the cognitive-affective model (e.g., [126, 127]). Depending on their research foci, these theories have been applied to both communication and decision-making research. In this research, we applied two dual-process theories related to decision making: the cognitive-affective model and ELM.

The cognitive-affective model argues that both rational and thinking factors such as costbenefits (the cognitive dimension) and intuitive and judgmental factors such as emotion (affective dimension) play roles in human decision making (e.g., [127]). Hence, we need to examine both factors in decision research. ELM is another dual-process theory that was initially developed for communication and later applied to decision making. The theory proposes two routes for explaining human behavior change: a “central route” and a “peripheral route”. People who are motivated or willing to process information may take the central route, spending more time and providing a rational response. On the contrary, people who are less motivated or not able to process information may take a peripheral route and use information shortcuts to make a decision.

In our research model, key factors are grouped into cognitive and affective dimensions, while project type and size are included based on ELM as they would affect the motivation for processing information. They are argued to have an effect on trust which will affect funding decisions. Fig. 1 shows our research model and hypotheses. Their development will be explained below.

## 2.1 Trust and investment intention

Trust is a major factor that influences human’s behavioral intention. Every commercial activity can be related to trust [4] and it is the fundamental principle of every business relationship [41]. Previous research has supported a positive relationship between trust and economic development [57, 96, 104]. Therefore, trust has been found to play an extremely important role in predicting investment behaviors [37, 111]. In prior research, trust has been defined as a subjective belief, a subjective probability, the willingness of an individual to be vulnerably relying on parties other than oneself, or a person's expectation [5, 11, 13, 22, 67].

In our study, trust is defined as a funder’s subjective belief that the project fundraiser will fulfill his/her promised plans and obligations as described in his/her project proposal. Logically, this should include trust in the crowdfunding website and the project proposal as a whole. Previous literature has shown that trust is an important factor that maintains the relationship between the vendor and its consumers [55, 58, 94]. Trust not only can reduce consumers cognitive risks of online transactions and their uncertainties of complex processes [61, 68], it can also increase the consumers’ willingness to participate in online activities, and once they build their trust toward the vendor, they will be more likely to maintain a long-term relationship with the vendor [76].

As crowdfunding has only emerged in recent years, it can be seen as a new online transaction type for online consumers. Ryu and Kim [86] suggest that the trustworthiness of the fundraiser is an important indicator for investors’ evaluation of such project as it reflects whether the project fundraiser is worth of being dependent upon. Previous literature has reported inconsistent findings about the role of trust (e.g., [107, 109, 118]). Hence, we posit the following hypothesis for a further confirmation:

H1: Crowdfunding funders’ trust in the project fundraiser will have a positive impact on his/her investment intention.

## 2.2 Performance/cognition-based versus affect/personality-based determinants

#

In conventional business markets, trust was built through the interaction process between consumers, the salesperson, and the enterprise. This process also applies to online digital transactions. Factors that affect online trust building have been extensively studied. For example, Kim, Ferrin, and Rao [54] proposed a framework which divides the determinants of online trust in e-commerce contexts into four categories: (1) cognition-based: such as privacy protection, security protection, and information quality; (2) affect-based: such as reputation, third-party certification, recommendation, and word-of-mouth; (3) experience-based: Internet experiences and familiarity, and (4) personality-oriented: an individual’s disposition to trust and his/her purchasing styles. Johnson and Grayson [49] studied the relationship between consumers and service providers and identified product performance, expertise ability, reputation, satisfaction, and similarity to be important determinants of cognition-based and affect-based trust. Teo and Liu [97] found that reputation, structural guarantee, and an individual’s disposition to trust affected online trust in e-commerce contexts. Walczuch and Lundgren [99] identified that reputation, similarity, prior experience, communication, information content, privacy technology, satisfaction, and individual’s disposition to trust are key factors of online trust.

Based on the findings from previous studies and in line with the trust theory, we adopt the cognitive-affective theory to include two categories of determinants that influence online trust: performance/cognition-based (Competence belief) and affect/personality-based (Disposition belief) trust. The experience-based trust was not included as a major determinant because crowdfunding is totally online and most participants have adequate Internet experience. For observation purposes, we treat crowdfunding experience and website familiarity as two control variables in our model.

Performance/cognition-based determinants refer to the confidence of a person to rely on another person’s competence or reliability to fulfill promises [103] while affect/personalitybased determinants deal with personal experiences and traits. Five factors that are closely relevant to the crowdfunding context were identified from previous studies: project information quality, fundraiser’s ability, fundraiser’s reputation, value similarity, and funder’s disposition to trust. The relationships between these five determinates and trust are elaborated below.

## 2.2.1 Project information quality

Information Quality (IQ) refers to a “consumer's general perception of the accuracy and completeness of Website information as it relates to products and transactions” ([54], p.549). When a consumer buys a product online, his/her purchasing intention depends mostly on the product information displayed on the vendor’s website; therefore, the quality of the information provided to the consumer is very important for the consumer to make the final decision (whether to buy or not). When information of better quality is provided, consumers can feel more secure as to the ability of service providers. Hence, maintaining the accuracy and completeness of the provided information allows consumers to be more confident in its reliability [72]. A recent study by Mollick [74] also indicated that in the crowdfunding context, project information quality can reflect the preparation degree, which will be considered by potential funders in their investment decision. Therefore, information quality of the proposed project that reflects the accuracy and completeness of this proposal will influence the funders’ confidence (trust) in this project and hence their decision as to whether to invest or not [12,74]. Taken together, we propose that:

H2: Project information quality will have a positive impact on trust.

## 2.2.2 Fundraiser’s ability

Ability is a “group of skills, competencies, and characteristics that enable a party to have influence within some specific domain” ([67], p.717). According to the trust theory, if the trustee is competent in certain technical area, he/she may be more trustworthy on tasks related to that area, therefore the expected goal will be more likely to achieve. Smith and Barclay [94] define ability as the perceived skills, techniques, and relevant knowledge for achieving specific tasks of the trustee. Previous studies also showed that people tend to have stronger inclination to trust someone if they perceive this person having expertise and ability [9]. For example, the ability of a salesperson can influence the consumers’ choices of the products or services, as well as their trust in the salesperson [20, 24].

When a consumer perceives the trustee to be skillful with the required expertise and ability to achieve the assigned tasks, he/she will have more trust in this trustee [47, 94]. Mollick [74] pointed out that crowdfunding and venture capital have similarities in that the entrepreneur’s prior experience and ability will influence the funders’ trust in the entrepreneur because they believe that the entrepreneur can achieve the project goal if they have sufficient expertise and skills. Therefore, we propose the following:

H3: The ability of fundraisers will have a positive impact on funders’ trust.

## 2.2.3 Fundraiser’s reputation

Reputation is the opinion or impression that people have about someone or something, including subjunctive observation or objective evaluation in general [26, 36]. An individual’s reputation can be used to predict his/her intended actions [39, 10]. Because reputation is built through social interactions occurred in the past, an individual’s reputation may reflect his/her own past performance [102], and hence can be interpreted as a potential indicator for the evaluation of someone’s competence. Baum and Silverman [6] indicated that third-party endorsements can be a signal of a startup’s reputation, which will influence the trust that potential investors have in the startup’s team and hence their investment decision. Doney and Cannon [24] argued that reputation is a second-hand information of one’s past behaviors, and consumers can use such information to evaluate if the vendor is trustworthy. In addition, the interpersonal doubts can be reduced if the trading partner has a positive reputation [49]. Baum and Silverman [6] also indicated that the accumulated reputation of a project team can influence the investor’s trust in this team. Therefore, we propose the following:

H4: The reputation of fundraisers will have a positive impact on funders’ trust.

## 2.2.4 Value similarity

Value similarity is a shared belief that two parties have similar attitudes, goals, behaviors, and lifestyles [76]. Zucker [106] argues that people build mutual trust based on the intimacy produced by their similarity. Sociologists also point out that in a social relationship, people tend to be attracted to those who are similar to themselves, and, therefore, are more willing to interact with them [49]. Previous empirical studies have shown that shared similarity can positively affect trust. When two parties have similar goals and values toward the same objects, the emotional reaction can be produced to increase mutual trust. Doney and Cannon [24] found that when individuals felt highly similar between their values and those of the vendor, they created emotional openness because they believed that they could understand the vendor better and thus they were more willing to care about a salesperson who shared the same values as they did. Clark, Drew, and Pinch [18] argued that the similarity between consumers and service providers positively influences mutual trust and thus leads to a positive impact on the sales outcome. Harms [38] found that capital providers are more willing to invest in projects proposed by the fundraisers with whom they have better feelings. Lambert and Schwienbacher [60] also argued that capital providers’ investment decisions rely on their own evaluations as to whether the proposed projects have similar beliefs or shared values with their own ideas. Therefore, value similarity is a critical determinant of the investor’s trust in the fundraiser. We propose the following:

H5: Value similarity will have a positive impact on funders’ trust.

## 2.2.5 Disposition to trust

Dispositional trust is defined as the extent to which a person intends to be willing to trust or depend on others [69]. It plays a particularly important role in the interaction between unfamiliar actors [8]. An individual’s disposition to trust may affect his/her initial trust in other objects or people. Higher disposition of trust reflects higher trusting beliefs [55]. As the disposition of trust was developed in childhood, which may vary depending on the environment that each one grew up. The degree of dispositional trust does not vary with the interactive experience or rational observation between two parties; rather, this tendency is the result of an individual’s life experience [29, 69, 85]. When one faces unfamiliar situations or people, the disposition to trust will become the most influential factor in trusting beliefs [85]. McKnight et al. [68] argued that in the web-based environment where consumers feel hesitant to transact with vendors because of the uncertainty or perceived risks, one’s tendency to assume better outcomes will lead to a secure feeling of the transaction environment. Once he/she perceives the Internet environment as secure, the follow-up behaviors such as purchasing, collaborating, or sharing will be generated. In the crowdfunding context, a funder with a higher disposition to trust will be more likely to assume that people are reliable without much further information. Therefore, we posit the following:

H6: Disposition to trust will have a positive impact on funders’ trust.

## 2.3 Moderating role of project type and funding level

Persuasion in shaping human perceptions and behavior has been examined by dualprocess theories in the social psychology literature. One theoretical perspective that can help

#

understand the contextual factors which may influence the process of persuasion through trust to users’ decision making is the elaboration-likelihood model (ELM). The ELM identifies two routes: central and peripheral based on the type of information processed by a given user (e.g., task-relevant arguments or simple cues), through which the information may influence that user’s attitude [113]. In the ELM, elaboration is defined as the extent to which a person carefully thinks about an argument [113]. Hence, elaboration is affected by the motivation of the user and cues that may affect the motivation.

One potential motivator is the importance of the decision. For crowdfunding projects, whether the project type meets the user’s interest and the size of the project are potential factors that may affect the motivation for careful thinking. In a recent study, Lambert and Schwienbacher [60] argue that project types of crowdfunding may create inspiring effects on funder’s investment intention and, in turn, affect the actual project success. For example, Tsang et al. [98] indicated that entertainment is an important attribute affecting consumer’s attitudes toward a product or service. Iyer and Kashyap [46] pointed out that some sponsors invest for obtaining financial profits while others may be interested in projects which have social values and/or create symbolic meanings for the society.

As investing crowdfunding projects on online platforms can be considered as one kind of online purchasing behaviors, crowdfunding project types are equivalent to product types, which may influence a consumer’s evaluation toward such a product [116, 117]. For example, a consumer can evaluate the quality through the introduction of its attributes of a search product (information provided by sellers regarding such a product reflects objective facts, such as a notebook computer), while for experience product, on the contrary, a consumer finds difficult to assess its quality by the introduction of its objective attributes. Hence, consumers’ attitude may differ when considering different types of products. Applying ELM to our current research, the entertaining project type is more experience-based in nature, hence the information regarding the quality provided in the project description may not play a significant part in influencing a crowdfunder’s attitude or decision in funding the project; instead, other information such as the fundraiser's reputation may have a higher impact on whether the crowdfunder trusts this proposed project or not. In other words, the user does not have to process the message based on the issue-relevant arguments presented because the depth of processing required is relatively low [119, 128].

In addition, the ELM suggests that if a product increases in personal relevance or consequences, it becomes more important to forming a veridical opinion. Thus, people are more motivated to devote the cognitive effort required to evaluate the product when involvement is high rather than low [114]. On the other hand, if a project type is more related to a crowdfunder’s personal preference or experience, or may increase more personal consequences (e.g., financial loss), the crowdfunder may have higher involvement in such a project, hence the cognitive efforts devoted to evaluating such a project is higher, and therefore,

#

the central route to persuasion should have higher effect and play more effective role in attitude, which in turn increases the impact of trust on the intention to investment. Thus, we propose that:

H7: Project type and funding level will moderate the effects of cognition/personality-based determinants on trust.

H7a: Project type will moderate the effects of cognition/personality-based determinants on trust.

H7b: Funding level of a project will moderate the effects of cognition/personality-based determinants on trust.

H8: Project type and funding level will moderate the effect of trust on investment intention.

H8a: Project type will moderate the effect of trust on investment decision.

H8b: Funding level of a project will moderate the effect of trust on investment decision.

## 3. Research method

The proposed research model was evaluated with a web-based experiment (or called Internet-based experiment [108]) using real fundraising cases. The experimental approach has several advantages compared to the survey method. Most notably, subjects were assigned to project scenarios in a controlled manner so that confounding effects of other factors (such as project type or size) can be reduced. The web-based experiment has advantages in reaching more diverse samples and rare or specific subpopulations [108]. Details of the design are presented below.

## 3.1 Experimental task and data collection

In order to control the effect of project type and funding level, a 3 × 3 factorial design was employed. The treatments were project type (economic, entertainment, and social), and the target funding level (below NT\$100,000, 100,000\~ 300,000, and above 300,000, the exchange rate 1US\$=30NT\$). Participants were randomly assigned to one of these 9 treatment cells. For each group, four proposed projects were provided, including two successful and two failed ones. A total of 36 different project scenarios were included in the experiment. These projects were carefully selected from those listed on the FlyingV website, the largest crowdfunding website in Taiwan.

#

As crowdfunding is a business on the Internet, all experimental subjects were recruited online. We posted our invitations on major social media websites such as Facebook and BBS discussion boards. Those who agreed to participate could click on our link that redirected them to our experimental website.

The experimental procedures follow the three stages suggested by Moore and Benbasat [75], including two pilot tests conducted prior to the collection of main study data. The first pilot test was used to select appropriate projects for the experiment. We picked 108 project proposals that were already closed from FlyingV (hid the actual result) as our experimental tasks. The reason that we chose cases that were closed was because we could then compare the investment intention with the actual fundraising result. Fifty subjects were randomly assigned to each one of them to view 6 project proposals and filled out a questionnaire afterward to classify these projects into proper types.

For determining proper project types, we adapted validated scales from previous studies. First, three types were identified from prior literature review: entertainment, social, and economic [42, 78, 98]. For assessing entertainment type, questions were adapted from Mitchell & Olson [73] and Shimp [92], which asked the respondents whether they perceived the project as interesting, fun, and entertaining. For the social type of project, questions were adapted from the social value of Holbrook’s [43] typology, which asked the respondents whether they perceived that the project could contribute to the society, provide an opportunity for the participation of social activities, and for establishing the connections to the society. Finally, for the economic type of project, questions were adapted from Ordanini et al. [78], which asked the respondents whether they perceived that the project provided them with economic benefits, valuable products or services, and beneficial promotion information. This process resulted in a total of 36 projects being selected as experimental tasks for later studies. These cases were used to design an experimental website that mimicked the FlyingV.

The second pilot study was conducted to pre-test construct measurements (to be described in the next subsection). Subjects were asked to read each project via the designed websites for at least 10 minutes and then they were instructed to complete a questionnaire to measuring our variables of interest such as the fundraiser’s reputation and ability, project information quality, value similarities, and their intention to invest in the selected project, and so on. One hundred valid responses were collected.

In the formal experiment, a website was built for recruited subjects to view and respond. Those who knew about the cases before were removed to ensure that the participants had no prior knowledge of the case. The operating procedures are as follows. First, recruited subjects were provided a brief description of the research purpose and estimated time. Once they agreed to participate, the system directed them to a new window for proposal review. After 10 minutes of reviewing the project proposal, they could either choose to start the survey or continue their review. During the time they answered the survey questions, they still had the access to the project web page for reviewing the content. An incentive of 100 NT dollars was offered to each subject for participation.

A total of 750 responses were received. After eliminating incomplete and inappropriate responses (e.g., duplicates and subjects under the age of 18), a total of 625 usable responses were included in the sample for construct validation and hypothesis testing. Table 1 shows the profile of participants. Fig.2 is a sample project web page.

## 3.2 Measurement

Because most constructs in this study (e.g., trust, investment intention, and project information quality) have been well established in the existing literature, we adapted previously validated measures as appropriate. We followed the validation procedures described in the literature [75] for all of the adapted items and used the commonly accepted advice on wording questions when developing and finalizing the questionnaire [90]. To ensure content validity, we asked experts to review the questionnaire. The questionnaire was piloted using 108 project proposals and pre-tested among 50 students in a big university before being accepted as the final version (refer to Section 3.1 for detail). No items were dropped from the pilot. Appendix 1 lists the measurement items and their sources.

We adapted measures from Dabholkar and Bagozzi [21] for the dependent variable, funder’s investment intention, which was operationalized as the degree to which the funder will or intend to invest. The measurements for funder’s trust and individual’s trust disposition were adapted from Gefen [30]. Measurement of the information quality was adapted from [23, 54]. Project fundraiser’s ability was adapted from Johnson and Grayson’s [49] measures and reputation was adapted from Kim et al.’s [54]. Measurements for value similarity was adapted from Johnson and Grayon [49], measurements for institution-based trust were adapted from Ribbink et al.’s [82], while measurements of system-based trust were operationalized as users trust in purchasing (in our case, investing) or searching for goods/service information online.

## 3.3 Control variables

To rule out the possible effects of individual characteristics that might affect funder’s intention to invest, we treat them as control variables in our model, including funder’s age, gender, income, education, and prior experiences of crowdfunding and website familiarity. To measure the website familiarity, we asked the participants questions such as whether they are familiar with the crowdfunding platform or with the process of searching for items. In addition, institution-based trust, which deals with one’s belief that the necessary structural conditions are present in the Internet setting to ensure a successful outcome [68], is treated as a control variable in this study. Despite its possible influence on funder’s trust, it is not the main factor that we intend to study when we use the elaboration likelihood model as our main theoretical lens. This also mitigates the concern that an experimental website may not generate real institution-based trust.

## 4. Data analyses and results

Partial least squares (PLS) analysis was applied to validate both the measurement model and the structural model. As a component-based approach for estimation, PLS is suitable because it can test complex relationships by avoiding inadmissible solutions and factor indeterminacy [28, 33]. Moreover, PLS can be used not only for theory confirmation, but it can also suggest where relationships might or might not exist [33]. We used SmartPLS 3.0 [83] with the parameter setting of bootstrapping to be 1000 resamples.

After purification [65], the psychometric properties of the instrument were assessed. As shown in Appendix 1, the research measures are all operationalized as reflective by following the four decision rules suggested by Jarvis et al. [48]. To ensure the appropriateness of the research instrument, content validity, reliability, and construct validity were assessed and reported as follows.

## 4.1. Content validity

To ensure content validity, we first conducted a thorough review of the relevant literature [95]. The instrument items were then reviewed by a panel of experts (one professor and two graduate students in information systems), followed by the pilot tests described in Section 3.1. These pilot studies provided a necessary check for content validity.

## 4.2. Reliability

The assessment of the measurement model includes the estimation of internal consistency for reliability, and tests of convergent and discriminant validity for construct validity [15]. Table 2 below shows the descriptive statistics for the constructs, the internal consistency of the scales, and the correlations between variables.

First, internal consistency was calculated using Cronbach's alpha and Fornell's composite reliability [27]. Cronbach's alpha (CA) values of all variables were higher than 0.8, greater than the minimum suggested threshold of 0.70 [77]. In contrast to Cronbach's alpha, which implicitly assumes that each item carries the same weight, composite reliability relies on the actual loadings to construct the factor score and is thus a better measure of internal consistency [27]. Composite reliability should be greater than the benchmark of 0.7 to be considered adequate [27].

As shown in Table 2, all composite reliabilities (CRs) of constructs had a value higher than 0.7, indicating the good internal consistency of our measures [77]. Additionally, all average variance extracted (AVE) values of constructs were higher than 0.50, which suggests that more than 50% of the variance of the measurement items can be accounted for by the constructs [27].

## 4.3. Construct validity

Construct validity was examined by assessing the convergent validity and discriminant validity [17]. Convergent validity is considered acceptable when all item loadings are greater than 0.50 [101] and the items for each construct load onto only one factor with an eigenvalue greater than 1.0. As shown in Table 3, the items for each construct did load onto only one factor with an eigenvalue greater than 1.0. The average variance extracted (AVE) can also be used to evaluate discriminant validity. Discriminant validity can be checked by examining whether the correlations between the variables are lower than the square root of the average variance extracted. The AVE from the construct should be higher than the variance shared between the construct and other variables in the model [16]. Table 2 indicates that all square roots of AVE values are greater than the corresponding off-diagonal elements, which indicates a good discriminant validity.

Because the data were collected from a single source, we tested for the common method bias. For this purpose, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted by performing an exploratory factor analysis with all variables included [80]. The factor analysis produced neither a single factor nor one general factor that accounted for the majority of the variance. This suggests that the study results were not affected by the common method bias.

## 4.4. Structural model assessment

After validating the measurement model, the structural model was assessed to estimate path coefficients and $\mathtt { R } ^ { 2 } .$ . Figure 3 and Table 4 show the results of the assessment and hypothesis testing. Solid lines in Figure 3 indicate significant relationships. The relationship between intention and actual outcome is not tested in the PLS model and will be examined later.

The results in Table 4 show that our model explains 33% of the variance of the dependent variable, indicating an acceptable explaining capability. As expected, funder’s trust on fundraisers is positively related to the funder’s intention to invest (H1 is supported), while project information quality, fundraiser’s ability, fundraiser’s reputation and value similarity are positively related to the funder’s trust (H2, H3, H4, and H5 are supported). However, contrary to our prediction, a funder’s disposition to trust has no impact on the funder’s trust toward the fundraiser (H6 not supported).

This observation is probably due to the nature of crowdfunding projects in our experiment. On the crowdfunding website, funders’ trust is mostly developed through the acquired information and the online environment. That is to say, for a funder to make his/her decision regarding whether to invest in a project mostly depends on his/her perceptions of the credibility demonstrated in the proposal, rather than on his/her disposition to trust. Thus, in the crowdfunding context, an individual’s disposition to trust did not have a significant impact on the funder’s trust in the fundraiser. Other possible explanations may be related to the influences of project type and target amount set for raising. To assess potential moderating effects of project type and target funding level (H7 & H8), we conducted a multi-group analysis (MGA)

as described in the following section.

## 4.5 PLS multi-group analysis

Because the experimental setting included project types and funding levels as two contingency factors, a multi-group analysis was conducted to check their moderating effects. The reason that we examine these two factors is because previous literature indicates that they have effects on consumer intention. For instance, Tsang et al. [98] argued that entertainment is an essential attribute affecting consumers’ attitudes toward intention. Iyer and Kashyap [46] pointed out that some sponsors invest for obtaining financial profits while others may be interested in projects which have social values and/or create symbolic meanings for the society. Our experiment used an “all-or-nothing” model, that is, a project failed if the target amount was not fully reached by the deadline. For potential investors, the target level may affect the perceived probability whether the project will succeed, which could be one factor in evaluating their investment decisions [1]. If the target amount is set too high to achieve, the potential sponsors may hesitate to invest [74].

To test the effects of project type and target funding level, we split the sample into three subgroups by project type (entertainment, social, and economic) and another three subgroups by the funding level (high, medium, and low). The approach of Keil et al. [53] was adopted to statistically compare the corresponding path coefficients. The result, as shown in Table 5, indicates that (1) two factors are found to have significant differences in different types of projects and (2) slightly significant difference was found between low and high funding levels in terms of the effect of reputation on trust.

## 4.5.1 Effects of project type

Regarding project type, value similarity has a significantly higher impact (β= 0.250) on funders’ trust in entertaining projects than that in social projects (β=0. 006). This is consistent with Tsang et al. [98], who found that the content and form (entertainment) are important in terms of their perceived value and are crucial to the effectiveness of Web advertising. For social projects, funders’ trust is more motivated by the content itself and may be less affected by factors such as similarity. This may be because entertainment projects are more experienceoriented in nature (i.e., hard to assess quality before consumption), potential funders are less likely to perceive the value from project description and more likely to rely on their judgment (i.e., the peripheral route in ELM), in which value similarity plays a more influential role. The interaction effects are plotted as shown in Fig.4 below.

The effect of trust on investment intention also differs between entertainment and economic types of projects: the effect in the entertainment group (β=0. 637) is significantly higher than that in the economic group (β=0. 460). In other words, funders’ trust is more important to attract funders’ investment for entertainment projects than for economic projects. This may also be explained from the ELM perspective [114] in that entertainment projects are different from economic projects in that the former is more experience-based while the latter is more rational. Hence, investment decisions on these two types of projects may go through two different routes (peripheral versus central, respectively). This is also consistent with different effects of value similarity on trust. Fig. 5 depicts the interaction effect.

## 4.5.2 Effects of funding level

Reputation effects differ significantly among different funding levels. The impact of reputation is higher for low funding level project (β=0. 250) as compared to high funding level (β=0. 119). This is probably because less financial risks are associated with low funding project and thus low personal involvement in decision making, which led to more peripheral route [114]. Therefore, fundraisers’ reputation is more effective in building trust from potential funders when the fundraising level is relatively low.

Overall, we have found a few significant differences in trust building and investment intention when project type and funding level varied. Although many differences are not statistically significant, some of them are close to significant on the one-tail basis. Our findings show the contingency effect of fundraising success.

## 4.6 Intention and actual outcomes

As the projects used in our experiment were actual crowdfunding cases, we can check whether the investment intention is positively associated with the actual project success. That is, whether higher investment intention in our experiment reasonably indicates actual project success. Although actual investors were not surveyed in our study, we expect to see high correlation as an indicator of generalizability.

We conducted an ANOVA test to compare the mean scores of investment intention of the successful and the failed projects. The results show that the successful project group had a significantly higher mean of investment intention (F=5.226, p =0.02), which confirms that investment intention data we obtained are a good predictor of the actual fundraising result. Although the actual funders of those success cases differ from our experimental subjects (who had no knowledge of the actual result), the statistical significance also shows the external validity of this study.

## 5. Discussion and implications

This paper presents an integrative trust-based model with dual-process perspectives and empirical results from a web-based experiment to examine the role of trust and explore factors that affect funders’ intention to sponsor projects in different contexts. The result is verified with the actual funding outcome of those cases. In this section, we discuss several issues derived from our empirical findings and provide theoretical and managerial implications.

First, trust has been found to interpret the investment intention of funders, which is highly correlated with the actual project success. This is similar to online purchasing when customers decide whether it’s worth buying a certain product or service. Therefore, funders can be viewed as online consumers [38] and their investment intention can be viewed as purchase intention. The result that trust is a significant determinant to crowdfunding investment intention is consistent with findings from previous trust-based research on online shopping (e.g., [30, 32]). A possible reason is that trust can reduce consumers’ cognitive risks of online transactions and their uncertainties of complex processes [61, 68].

Second, performance-based trust factors were found to have significant effects on potential funders’ trust. Project information quality, fundraisers’ reputation and fundraisers ability/expertise had significant impacts. Fundraisers’ ability/expertise had the largest effect size, while project information quality was the second highest. According to the trust theory [112], competence belief is a basic element for positive evaluation to build trustworthiness. ELM [113] also suggests that cognitive cues through central route are an effective approach to attitude persuasion. In other words, fundraisers need to provide better information about the project and trust-building information about themselves to let potential investors believe they are capable of carrying out the project.

Platform managers should ensure that all information regarding the project types and quality, fundraisers’ expertise, ability, and reputation are sufficiently available and transparent for funders to evaluate. This allows funders to better know the potential of proposed projects. Providing information about performance assessment may be helpful as well, such as where the funding is allocated, who will manage it, how much the project is going to cost and how long it will take to finish. This kind of information enables a better understanding of crowdfunding practices and, at the same time, reduces uncertainties and the risks associated with start-up projects.

Third, by applying the dual-route mechanism from ELM, we are able to better interpret why effect sizes of certain factors differ among different project types. In particular, value similarity was significant in entertainment-oriented projects but not significant in social and economic-oriented projects. For social projects, funders’ trust are more motivated by the content itself, while entertainment projects are more experience-oriented in nature (i.e., hard to assess quality before consumption), the information regarding the quality provided in the project description may not play a significant part in influencing a crowdfunder’s intention to invest in the project; instead, the crowdfunder’s trust is more likely to be affected by funders perception regarding whether the fundraiser has similar values or interests.

In addition, the effect of reputation differs significantly among different funding levels. The impact of reputation is higher for low funding level project as compared to high funding level projects. Therefore, fundraisers’ reputation information instead of project quality is more effective in building potential funders’ trust when the project’s target funding level is low. The effect sizes of trust in different project types also varied. This implies that project creators may want to pay special attention to those factors unique to their project types. Although many other differences did not reach the statistically significant level, their differences may have practical implications that are worth attention.

#

Finally, what we found interesting is, contrary to Teo and Liu’s [97] study, that an individual’s disposition to trust affected funder’s trust in the crowdfunding context. This may be because of the global (attitudinal/affective) nature of the trust disposition, which does not “accurately determine an individual’s trust in another under particular circumstances” ([50], p. 1307). In general, we found that potential funders’ trust in the project creator tends to be influenced more by the performance/cognition-based determinants such as project information quality, fundraiser’s ability, and reputation. We also found that the effect of value similarity on trust and the effect of trust on investment intention vary in different project types. In particular, entertainment projects tend to be different from economic and social projects in affecting more by value similarity and trust. This may be due to the affective nature of entertainment projects that lead to more peripheral route thinking in the decision process.

Our findings contribute to the literature in several ways. First, we have shown that trust is indeed a major factor in explaining funders’ intention to invest in crowdfunding projects. This is consistent with prior literature that trust plays a key role in the online context (e.g., [49]). Furthermore, we have applied the cognitive-affective model to study different types of trust antecedents – performance/cognition-based and affect/personality-based trust, and found that factors associated with performance/cognition-based trust carry more weights as compared to affect/personality-based trust. That is to say, when deciding whether or not to invest in a project, potential funders tend to consider more about the fundraiser’s ability, the proposed projects’ information quality, as well as the fundraiser’s reputation. This is very useful for fundraisers for preparing their proposals. Finally, this research advances our understanding of the underlying trust-building process which leads to the potential funder’s final investment decision.

Our results also shed much light on prior studies such as Zhao, et al. [118] in that we explore the direct effect of antecedents and the moderating roles of project types and funding levels. Effects of certain factors are better captured in our study. For instance, the effect of value similarity was reported having no effect in [118] but is found significant and to have moderating effect in our study. The interaction effects reported in our study allow us to better understand the role of antecedents and trust on investment intention.

Practical implications of our findings are given below. First, it is critical for platform operators and fundraisers to build initial trust with potential funders. Providing a trustworthy transaction environment and creating a professional image are essential requirements for project success. Crowdfunding platforms may need to enhance their trust-building mechanisms between the fundraiser and the investors. For example, in addition to posting project narratives, it may be helpful to provide functions that highlight the project creator’s relevant ability and reputation, and use well-defined indicators to facilitate sponsors’ evaluation of project quality and other concerned factors. For fundraisers, they need to pay special attention to show their ability and project quality to build potential funders’ trust. This is especially important for affect-oriented entertainment projects than for economic-oriented projects.

This study is not without limitations. First, we selected real projects from FlyingV as our experiment tasks, which might be influenced by other contextual factors specific to FlyingV itself but not included in our study. For example, we did not investigate the social capital that is embedded in the crowdfunding platform [105] which may possibly influence funder’s intention to invest. Therefore, future research may collect information regarding the social networks or other relevant information.

Second, our research model was developed based on initial trust due to the experimental website, which might leave out other determinants to online funders’ trust. The experimental website may restrict subjects’ initial trust assessment as compared with their trust on real fundraising sites.

Third, because of the nature of online studies, over half of our subjects were students. Although this is in line with the actual population of Internet users, subject profiles may deviate from the actual funders in crowdsourcing. It would be helpful if the sample frame could fit the actual crowdsourcing funders in the future.

Future work could also explore fundraiser’s purpose for fundraising. Our study focused on reward-based crowdfunding. Although the primary goal of crowdfunding is to raise money; for many fundraisers, they may use fundraising as a pre-marketing channel as it can help firms to gain a better knowledge of their consumers. In this sense, crowdfunding can be used as a tool to explore the market potential of a product or service.

In addition, there is a lack of understanding of the impact of different types of platform. Studies are needed to differentiate the activities of different crowdfunding platforms, and to establish particular factors shaping success in these different types of platform. Moreover, to our best knowledge, there has not been any study exploring the potentials for public institutions in the use of crowdfunding. It would be interesting to study the crowdfunding activities involving public sectors.

Finally, a study to provide feasible solutions for difficulties in managing platforms would be very useful. These difficulties may be associated with the protection of ideas and investment, identification of new areas where crowdfunding could easily grow, institutional support for innovative start-ups using crowdfunding, and lack of experience in dealing with platform internationalization.

## 6. Conclusion

This study has applied two dual-process theories to investigate factors that affect funders intention to invest in crowdfunding projects. An integrated trust-based model was proposed from an elaboration likelihood perspective and tested with an online experiment using 36 real crowdfunded projects. By substantiating different dimensions of trust-related determinants, we have demonstrated significant effects of performance/cognition-based and affect/personalitybased factors on funders’ trust and the effect of trust on funders’ intention to invest in innovative projects through crowdfunding platforms. Our results indicate that all three categories of trust factors had significant effects on potential funders’ trust. Fundraisers’ ability/expertise had the largest effect size, while project information quality was the second highest. In addition, we are able to identify two contextual conditions: project type and funding level, in which the effects of trust and its antecedents vary. In line with ELM, we find that project types have moderated the effect of trust-related antecedents, while funding levels have moderated the effect of trust on investment intention. These findings make significant theoretical contributions to the crowdfunding literature and provide practical guidelines for platform providers and fundraisers.
