RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Exploring value networks: theorising the creation and capture of value with open source software

Lorraine Morgan<sup>1</sup>, Joseph Feller<sup>2</sup> and Patrick Finnegan<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Business Informations Systems Group, Lero, National University of Ireland, Galway, Ireland; <sup>2</sup> Business Information Systems, University College Cork, Cork, Ireland; <sup>3</sup>Information Systems, University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia

Correspondence: Lorraine Morgan, Business Informations Systems Group, Lero, National University of Ireland, Galway, Moycullen Road, Galway, IRL, Ireland. Tel: þ 353879720910; E-mail: lorraine.morgan@nuigalway.ie

## Abstract

The emergence of open source software (OSS) as a form of peer production and innovation challenges theories of organisation and strategy due to its nonreliance on traditional governance mechanisms to organise production. OSS requires firms to rethink the processes that facilitate value creation and capture. The objective of this paper is to theorise how firms create and capture value from OSS. We derive a model from extant research and refine it through the study of three inter-organisational networks. The findings reveal how a firm’s ability to access a value network of complementors is crucial for effective value creation and capture. Two types of networks are evident: a high-density network of familiar partners and a low-density network of multiple, often unfamiliar, partners. Leveraging these networks depends on the level of commitment, volume of knowledge exchange and the alignment of objectives among participant firms. Effective governance is revealed as critical for creating and capturing value within both types of network; and depends on both formal and informal mechanisms.

## Introduction

Open source software (OSS) is an exemplar of peer production, a phenomenon that challenges traditional notions of governance (Benkler, 2002, 2006). Peer-production is ‘a model of social production, emerging alongside contract- and market-based, managerial-firm based and state-based production’ (Benkler & Nissenbaum, 2006, p. 400). It is, primarily, an ‘Internet-based effort whereby volunteers contribute project components, and there exists some process to combine them to produce a unified intellectual work’ (Krowne, 2005, p. 5). Unlike hierarchical and market modes of production, commons-based peer production (CBPP) ‘relies on decentralised information gathering and exchange to reduce the uncertainty of participants’ (Benkler, 2002, p. 375), and is characterised by (i) decentralisation of action and decision-making and (ii) utilisation of social cues instead of prices or managerial authority as a means of motivating and organising efforts (Benkler & Nissenbaum, 2006).

Emerging from a community-based software development context (West & O’Mahony, 2008), OSS has become important in the business models for firms in a variety of industries (Fitzgerald, 2006; Rajala & Westerlund, 2008), particularly in the context of open innovation (West, 2007). However, the engagement of firms with OSS communities raises concerns about the quality and utility of OSS code, organisational control and governance. In particular, the shift from proprietary code to OSS requires a reconsideration of the processes that facilitate value creation and value capture. While the importance of value networks (VNs) as a context for value creation/capture has been identified (West & Gallagher, 2006; West, 2007), neither the attributes/ dynamics of such networks, nor the governance mechanisms that enable them, have been adequately theorised.

The objective of this paper is therefore to theorise how firms create and capture value from OSS. We begin by analysing extant research on value creation and capture to delineate constructs and the relationships between these constructs, in the form of theoretical propositions. This is followed by a discussion of the research design. We then use three case studies to refine our preliminary model. Finally, we conclude by discussing the implications of our work.

## Theoretical grounding and building a conceptual model

In this section, we analyse extant research and develop a preliminary theoretical model to guide our work. Specifically, we analyse existing theories on value creation/ capture in general and extant literature on value creation/ capture with OSS.

## Value creation

Although the process of value creation can be conceptualised from a contingency perspective in terms of use value (customer’s perception of the usefulness of the product and the amount they are willing to pay) and exchange value (monetary amount paid by the customer) (cf. Bowman & Ambrosini, 2000; Lepak et al, 2007), other work conceives value creation as a single universal dimension. Specifically, within the business model context, value creation is defined as the process of identifying a relevant customer segment, the value proposition for those customers, and the ways in which the business model will provide that value (Chesbrough & Rosenbloom, 2002; West, 2007). For OSS business models in IT vendor firms, West (2007) found that business buyers (customer segment) enjoyed lower costs and avoided vendor lock-in (value proposition). Because customers expected a richer ‘whole product’ solution than that provided by the OSS project community (integration, customisation, support, etc.), such vendors combined priced and unpriced complementary assets to create value. Complementary assets, also called complementarities (Amit & Zott, 2001), are those assets (such as resources, capabilities, know-how, goods or services) that surround the successful commercialisation of an innovation (Teece, 1986; Dodgson et al, 2008). However, such complementary assets are often to be found in the context of a VN. Thus, a firm’s relationship with providers of complementary assets in a network determines its value creation potential, meaning that creating and sustaining relationships with other network members is vital (Shafer et al, 2005; West, 2007). In order to understand the impact of the characteristics of OSS and the importance of complementarities resident within VNs on value creation with OSS, we follow Amit & Zott’s (2001) exploration of theoretical foundations for value creation in the context of e-business. Specifically we evaluate the applicability of traditional theoretical approaches to value creation with OSS, revealing their limitations in this context (see Table 1).

Transaction cost economics (TCE) (cf. Coase, 1937; Williamson, 1981) provides a single company-oriented analysis of cost minimisation where transaction efficiency is identified as a major source of value, that is, enhanced efficiency reduces costs (Amit & Zott, 2001). One of the limitations of this theory, however, is its stringent focus on transactions and the view of the boundaries between market and hierarchy (Rajala & Westerlund, 2008). As already indicated, OSS projects do not rely either on markets or on managerial hierarchies to organise production. Furthermore, in the pursuit of transactional value, partners involved in OSS projects often choose cooperative and collaborative modes with higher transaction costs as long as eventual joint gains prevail over transaction costs (Zajac & Olsen, 1993).

Porter’s (1985) value chain framework examines the configuration of the firm’s primary and support activities that enable it to add value to a product and to compete within its industry. However, this framework focuses on value creation as a linked chain of activities; a perspective that leads to the development of strategies that concentrate on controlling this chain (Peppard & Rylander, 2006). Porter (1985) further argues that a firm’s value chain links to the value chain of both suppliers and of buyers of products and services, resulting in a large stream of activities called the value system. However, there is a major distinction between value creation in the value system and an OSS context. For example, companies in the classical value system occupy a particular position within the system and add value to inputs before passing them on to the next actor in the chain. Relationships between these companies (eg suppliers, substitutes, etc.) can thus be described as simple exchange relations, mainly dealt with by means of arms-length transactions (Vanhaverbeke et al, 2007). This is in stark contrast to the embedded relationships that exist in OSS contexts (Cooper, 2005).

In contrast to the Porterian model and TCE-based theory, the knowledge-based view (KBV) treats knowledge as a key resource underlying value creation (Grant, 1997). A firm’s success rests on its ability to offer new and superior customer value, which in turn relies on its ability to leverage employee knowledge as the basis for innovations that create value for customers (Kang et al, 2007). However, the existing literature on KBV has some significant shortcomings in the context of value creation with OSS. For example, many of the perspectives on KBV are rather static in that they see the control and protection of knowledge as the basis for sustainable competitive advantage as it is difficult to imitate (Eishenhardt & Santos, 2002; McEvily & Chakravarthy, 2002). In other words, the dominant view is how best a firm can accumulate, apply, integrate and protect knowledge inside a firm. From an OSS perspective, a firm’s knowledge extends beyond its boundaries as the OSS production process enables knowledge flows with other firms. Thus, as a firm increases its internal knowledge base by bringing in external knowledge, it can use this knowledge to innovate (Vanhaverbeke et al, 2007).

It is evident from Table 1 that existing value creation frameworks have some shortcomings in examining value creation with OSS. For example, models like TCEs and the value chain framework do not account for the nature of alliances, competitors, complementors and other members in what some refer to as value networks (Peppard & Rylander, 2006; West, 2007) or ecosystems (Iansiti & Levien, 2004; Adner, 2006; Peltoniemi, 2006). VNs are entities consisting of several connected individuals or organisational actors that transform and transfer various resources (Helander & Rissanen, 2006). These networks are viewed as vehicles for producing, synthesising and distributing ideas and increasingly the success of a firm is linked to the depth of their ties to network partners.

However, the importance of external sources of innovation and knowledge is not particularly new. All organisations, to some extent, interact with their external environment to source ideas and knowledge and collaborate on innovations (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; von Hippel, 2005). For example, there has been extensive research and studies on strategic alliances (see, e.g., Bronder & Pritzl, 1992; Lei & Slocum, 1992; Shan et al, 1994 and Gerlach, 1992); interorganisational networks (see Arora & Gambardella, 1990; Nelson, 1991; Mowery et al, 1996; Powell et al, 1996); strategic partnerships (Henderson, 1990); and joint ventures (see Oliver, 1990; Campbell et al, 1991). However, a key difference with the concept of an open innovation VN is that equal importance is placed on the role of external knowledge as well as internal knowledge as a source of innovation. In prior theorising about innovation, external knowledge was seen as playing a useful, but supplemental, role as the firm was the locus of innovation with activities within the firm the central object of the study (Chesbrough et al, 2006). In addition, research on traditional inter-organisational relations takes an armlength transaction-oriented focus. This is in contrast to the emphasis placed on interaction and the importance of joint value maximisation and shared governance in open innovation VNs (Vanhaverbeke & Cloodt, 2006).

Research on VNs takes a more holistic view of value creation, comprising four dimensions – value creation, transactions, governance and finally networking (Vanhaverbeke & Cloodt, 2006). However, they have to be considered jointly to understand the process of value creation and cannot be sufficiently addressed by theoretical frameworks that only address one of these dimensions, such as those discussed above, which tend to neglect the importance of combining and governing the resources/ capabilities of various partners outside the firm in VNs. A network offers the firm the potential to share risk, generate economies of scale (Katz & Shapiro, 1985; Shapiro & Varian, 1999), share knowledge and facilitate learning (Van Alstyne, 1997; Dyer & Singh, 1998; Dyer & Nobeoka, 2000). When these networks function properly, they allow firms to create value that no single firm could have created alone (Adner, 2006). In a VN environment, organisations focus not on the firm or industry, but on the value-creating system itself, which includes suppliers, partners, allies and customers and other network players working together. The firm focuses on creating value, where value is determined by the resources and capabilities assembled, combined and governed by different partners and how well they perform joint tasks (Vanhaverbeke & Cloodt, 2006). The importance of competitors in a firm’s VN has also been highlighted by West (2007), as these competitors often collaborate to further develop or stimulate adoption of a shared technology. In the context of OSS, Dahlander (2005) proposes that relationships between the firm and the OSS community (users and developers) in a network are equally important. To begin theorising the creation of value creation, we identify the following construct and proposition:

Value network: Entities of several connected individuals/ organisational actors that transform and transfer various complimentary resources and capabilities.

## Proposition 1: Participation in a VN is critical to the ability of a firm to create value with OSS.

Learning how to create value when companies are highly reliant on each other in a VN, however, is an underexplored area in the literature. For the majority of companies, decisions are usually made within the boundaries of the firm and the external environment viewed as an arena where firms compete with one another (Vanhaverbeke, 2006). In a VN, value is co-created or co-produced. Thus, companies with complementary capabilities have to be fully committed to cooperate. Knowledge exchange in a network leads to value creation as it facilitates joint learning, fosters problem-solving, and the integration of complementary resources necessary for the joint creation of products, technologies and services (Parise & Henderson, 2001). It has also been argued that innovation is positively influenced by a firm’s access to complementary skills and a broad knowledge-base that facilitates different types of knowledge exchange in a network (Simard & West, 2006).

The value a firm creates from being part of a network depends on how well partners’ objectives are aligned to each other and on partners’ commitment to invest in complementary assets (Teece, 1986; Moore, 1991). For example, Vanhaverbeke (2006, p. 218) suggests that, ‘creating value cannot be done unilaterally based on the efforts of a single, focal firm, nor can it be done without keeping in mind the different and divergent interests of all collaborating partners’. We, therefore, identify the following constructs and present three propositions:

Commitment: The degree to which network participants express a willingness and desire to establish a cooperative effort and sustain a valued relationship.

Knowledge exchange: The degree to which network participants exchange ideas and knowledge in a reciprocal manner.

Alignment of objectives: The degree to which the network identifies and integrates divergent objectives and goals in order to achieve a shared understanding of performance requirements.

Proposition 2: Commitment in a VN facilitates value creation with OSS.

Proposition 3: Knowledge exchange in a VN facilitates value creation with OSS.

Proposition 4: The alignment of objectives in a VN facilitates value creation with OSS.

Successfully ensuring alignment of objectives and partners’ commitment, however, relies on two important issues. First, the firm has to structure and manage the VN so that the potential of the network to create joint value is maximised. Second, it has to make agreements with network participants to share this jointly created value (Vanhaverbeke & Cloodt, 2006). Thus, resources and capabilities of network actors have to be effectively combined and governed at the network level. Most impor tantly, each firm need to choose an appropriate governance mode for their relations with other network partners. Governance is thus necessary to ensure that network participants engage in collective and mutually supportive action, conflict is addressed, and network resources are acquired and used efficiently and effectively (Provan & Milward, 2001). Trust, leadership and a unifying vision play an important role in bringing disparate partners together in a network and the absence of internal competition among participants in the network is crucial (Gomes-Casseres, 2003). Thus, a firm has to actively nurture its VN to manage potential tensions or conflict between participants. Additionally, the firm has to make a number of arrangements with other participants to remain within the network, for example, offer incentives such as access to information and knowledge, compensation etc. (Vanhaverbeke & Cloodt, 2006). Lundell et al (2011) suggest that while firms may have different business motivations for contributing to a VN, they each have some incentive, which is strong enough for them to contribute to its further evolution. We can therefore delineate the following construct and present our fifth proposition:

Effective governance: The degree to which the resources and capabilities of different stakeholders in the network are combined and managed.

Proposition 5: Effective governance of a VN facilitates value creation with OSS.

## Value capture

While similar to value creation, value capture has been conceptualised from a contingency perspective that posits that the capture or realisation of exchange value is a function of a bargaining process between buyers and sellers (cf. Bowman & Ambrosini, 2000; Bowman & Swart, 2007). Value capture is also viewed as an important element of a business model (Chesbrough & Rosenbloom, 2002; Shafer et al, 2005; West, 2007), which explains how a firm captures value from its value creation in order to sustain the business model (Dahlander, 2005; West, 2007). Some of the key steps in formulating a value capture strategy are defining a revenue model, ensuring the cost structure is consistent with the customer’s perception of value (Amit & Zott, 2001), and establishing durable external relationships between the firm and customers and third parties (Chesbrough & Rosenbloom, 2002; West, 2007).

OSS has limited appropriability, and thus reduced potential for value capture, compared with proprietary software because the source code is available for reuse and modification by competitors, customers and complementors. Therefore, the revenue model focuses on the sale of complementary goods and services to complete the whole product solution (Iansiti & Richards, 2006; West, 2007). In terms of establishing durable external relationships, firms make source code open in the hope of attracting external contributions from third parties and competitors. Other value capture strategies include nonmonetary gains such as access to tacit knowledge and an excellent reputation useful in marketing. As with value creation, complementary assets play an important role in capturing value from an innovation and so the innovator must entice third-party suppliers of these complementarities to complete the innovation. Thus, a firm’s participation in a VN of potential complementors determines the value captured and establishing durable relationships is imperative (West, 2007). As with value creation, various theoretical approaches have explained how value is captured (see Table 2).

Traditional neoclassical economic theory focuses on value captured in the form of monopoly rents (Moran & Ghoshal, 1996; Pitelis, 2002). It has been argued that this theory views the firm as essentially a perfectly efficient ‘black box’ concerned with maximising profits and has nothing to say about the internal organisation of the firm or innovation for that matter (Teece, 1986; Hart, 1995). Thus, OSS innovation is not easily explained in neoclassical economic terms. The production of goods in a neoclassical firm includes a formal division of labour that uses proprietary knowledge, is guarded by restrictive intellectual property rights (IPR) and managed ‘within’ a hierarchy that guides and governs the process (Baumol, 2002). In contrast, OSS production and distribution is practically based on the absence of a hierarchy and is fundamentally about cooperation and collaboration.

Industrial organisational theory of the 1950s and 1960s is also useful in determining the likely profitability of an industry and in turn the value appropriated by firms (Porter, 1981). However, this view has been criticised for it preoccupation with value captured in the form of monopoly rents as the basis for explaining and predicting firm performance (Moran & Ghoshal, 1996; Pitelis, 2002). Yet again, OSS suggests activities that are the opposite extreme of this theory. OSS is not about erecting barriers to entry and excluding potential rivals. Rather, OSS promotes anti-rivalry and inclusiveness. These two dimensions result in positive network externalities where cooperation between contributors becomes the rule, not the exception (Cooper, 2005).

Similar to industrial organisational theory, the resourcebased view (RBV) of the firm is concerned with questions of value appropriation and sustainability of competitive advantage (eg Barney, 1997). This view conceptualises the enterprise as a bundle of resources and capabilities. In order to create and sustain competitive advantage and capture above-normal rates of returns, these resources must be scarce, valuable and reasonably durable (Barney, 1997). Firms also establish alliances so as to acquire complementary assets (Teece, 1986). However, proponents of the RBV emphasise that a sustainable competitive advantage is based on those resources and capabilities that are owned and controlled ‘within’ the boundaries of a single firm (Dyer & Singh, 1998). From an OSS perspective, however, resources should not be closed off within one single firm. Rather, durable, valuable and scarce resources of different firms should be combined in order to capture value (Vanhaverbeke et al, 2007). It is apparent from Table 2 that the dominant theoretical frameworks for value capture are primarily based upon ownership and control as the key levers in achieving strategic success and thus aim to protect, rather than share, valuable resources and capabilities that are housed within the firm. According to the majority of these theories, firms engage in armslength and self-enforcing interactions in markets and exchanges are governed within a hierarchy. Such theories focus largely within the firm and take less notice of the potential value of external resources that are not owned by the firm (Chesbrough & Appleyard, 2007).

Porter’s value-chain analysis may be somewhat valuable in examining open innovation VNs; however, value is determined by the performance of individual partners, not by the cohesion and structure of the network as a whole (Vanhaverbeke & Cloodt, 2006). Similarly, the stream of research on dynamic capabilities recognises and examines the role of external and economic complements to the firm’s own assets (Teece, 1986; Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000). However, the focus is on controlling key complements that afford a comparative advantage, which facilitate entry into new industries (Chesbrough & Appleyard, 2007). Finally, while the RBV stresses issues such as independence and the role of competition between firms based on the unique resources and capabilities it possesses; in contrast, OSS emphasises the interdependence of complementary resources of firms in a VN in order to introduce a new innovation to the market (Vanhaverbeke et al, 2007). As with value creation, the VN created around a business shapes the role that suppliers, customers and third parties play in influencing the value captured from commercialisation of an innovation (Chesbrough & Rosenbloom, 2002). In an OSS VN, firm often gain a large pool of users and third-party complementors to increase the value of their product/service. Users often reveal their internal complements for use by others because they may not be able to capture value from minor improvements, or because they gain other benefits from the disclosure, for example, recognition (West, 2007). We can, therefore, identify the importance of a VN for value capture and present our sixth proposition:

## Proposition 6: Participation in a VN is critical to the ability of a firm to capture value with OSS.

In a VN, value capture has to be considered jointly with the value creation strategy because in both cases the commitment of the participants, the exchange of knowledge among them and the alignment of objectives determine the strength of the network and the amount of value captured (Vanhaverbeke & Cloodt, 2006). Each participant should capture some value from its contribution to the network in order to remain committed. Peppard & Rylander (2006) also argue that the flow of knowledge and other resources in the network is vital for its sustainability. Firms can capture value by developing superior knowledge-sharing routines with partners in the network. This, however, is dependent on incentives that encourage partners to be transparent, to transfer knowledge and prevent free riding on the knowledge acquired from the partner (Dyer & Singh, 1998). Thus, we can identify that commitment, knowledge exchange and the alignment of objectives in a network is important for value capture with OSS and present the following propositions:

Proposition 7: Commitment in a VN facilitates value capture with OSS.

Proposition 8: Knowledge exchange in a VN facilitates value capture with OSS.

Proposition 9: The alignment of objectives in a VN facilitates value capture with OSS.

It has been further suggested that each participant reaps some benefits to ensure that one stays committed. Fair distribution of value in a network is also important because while some participants are automatically better off in the network, others might be worse off and have to receive some benefits or incentives in order to stay committed to the VN. Thus, the value captured in a network depends on how well participant resources are combined and managed within the network. Again, firms will need to choose an appropriate governance mode for its relations with each network partner. In order to capture value, a firm will have to orchestrate the network partners, lead and nurture them while minimising any potential tensions and instilling a unifying vision (Vanhaverbeke & Cloodt, 2006). We can, therefore, identify the importance of governance and present our final proposition:

Proposition 10: Effective governance of a VN facilitates value capture with OSS.

We now conclude our process of building a preliminary model from extant research by presenting the constructs and the relationships between them in Figure 1, which serves as the conceptual model for the study. Nevertheless, while we are able to delineate the factors, understanding how they enable value creation and capture with OSS cannot be determined from extant research. The various theoretical frameworks have limitations when applied in the context of OSS and do not provide a complete picture in explaining value creation and capture with OSS. After all, the dominant argument that has emerged in this section is the need for a VN in order to successfully create and capture value with OSS, something not easily explained in traditional theories of the firm.

## Research method

Our research method comprised case studies of three focal firms and the partner firms within their VNs. Our method is consistent with the approach of Benbasat et al (1987) and Yin (1989) as we employ a priori theory, and with Eisenhardt (1989) and Madill et al (2000) in that we seek to reveal pre-existing, relatively stable and objectively extant phenomena and the relationships among them.

The choice of case sites was based on the company’s history of engagement with OSS. Specifically, we choose focal firms engaged in activities such as embedding OSS in products/services, or in suites of applications, sourcing the software from communities, and active involvement in OSS communities. Firms that simply used OSS products for cost saving purposes were excluded from the study. The data were gathered using semi-structured interviewing during 2010 and 2011, based on an interview guide (cf. Patton, 1990). Semi-structured interviewing was employed to allow greater flexibility (cf., Burgess, 1982). Interviews lasted 1–2 h, with follow-up interviews used to clarify and refine issues that emerged during transcription. Interviews were complemented by a review of public documents including firms’ websites, white papers, brochures etc. See Table 3 for summary of firms/ interviewees.

NRC currently employs 1200 staff and has activities in Finland, U.S.A., Hungary, Germany, China and Japan. In terms of OSS adoption, there have been major efforts in the area – one concerning telecommunications infrastructure and products where Linux has been used as one of the primary platforms for a wide product range. OSS is part of the company’s core strategy and employees are actively encouraged to participate in communities etc. Network participants include the Eclipse Community of which Talend, Opex, Bredex, and APC are members. Additionally, the company network with other companies such as AB Networks, universities, research institutes, suppliers and customers.

Interviews were transcribed, generating 500 pages of data. Data analysis was undertaken using coding techniques proposed by Strauss & Corbin (1990). Consistent with the work of Lings & Lundell (2005), while we use the coding techniques proposed by Strauss & Corbin (1990), we do not undertake grounded theory. The approach encourages the researcher to be flexible qand creative (Sarker et al (2000) while imposing systematic coding procedures (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). Open coding is ‘the process of breaking down, examining, comparing, conceptualising, and categorising data’ to determine the main ideas (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). These ideas were then grouped by meaningful headings (informed by constructs that had emerged from the analysis of the theoretical frameworks) to reveal categories and subcategories. Axial coding involved making connections between the categories and sub-categories. As a list of codes began to emerge, the analysis moved to a higher or more abstract level, looking for a relationship between the codes. Once a relationship had been determined, the focus returned to the data to question the validity of these relationships (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). The final step, ‘selective coding’, is the process of determining a core category or categories; the one(s) connected to most of the other categories. In this study, three core categories emerged: value creation, capture and network. Issues of validity and reliability were addressed using tactics consistent with Yin (1989) (see Table 4).

## Analysis

## How VNs enable value creation

Our findings reveal that a VN of complementors enables value creation for customers in a number of ways. This value creation includes improved product quality, faster releases and lower costs. Complementors in the network build or innovate on top of a firm’s customer offering by providing various enhancements and complements. These various complements are vital for offering the ‘whole product’ approach demanded by customers. As complementors in the network make code available for use, firms need not ‘reinvent the wheel’ and can focus on higher-level requirements and value-add features for customers. As a result of the shared collaborative effort of partners in network companies, customers are also receiving better products in a faster timeframe. As firms share development efforts in the network, one can observe any failures or errors that have resulted along the way. Thus, they find it easier to produce a high-quality product first time round, rather than produce something that contains bugs that usually become transparent once deployed to customers in the field. OSS networks are an effective knowledge base in that they provide expertise and advice on how to advance software, provide knowledge on different domains and assist in the creation of solutions or prototypes that can be leveraged in different products for customers.

All of the study participants explained that they deliver products to customers at a lower cost because of the co-development activities that take place in the network. The development of standards in a network is also very beneficial from a customer perspective. As the Chief Technology Officer at AB Networks explained, ‘if customers value standardisation, they take this as a reason for lower costs and prices’. Moreover, due to financial constraints, customers are continuously looking for cost savings. Where the firms studied used to deliver their software and make customers pay for different licenses, they now deliver OSS versions of the product that customers receive at a lower price. The OSS Strategist at Osmosoft explained ‘you are able to bring a product to market at a lower cost than you would have done perhaps if you had to utilise a greater amount of proprietary software. There is a definite cost benefit’ (OSS Strategist, Osmosoft).

Interestingly, two types of networks are evident from the analysis. One is a high-density network of familiar partners while the other resembles a low-density network of multiple, often unfamiliar, partners. Many of the study participants including AB Networks, Talend and Philips Healthcare have steady, cooperative relationships with a limited number of familiar partners including subcontractors, suppliers etc., who offer them ideas and knowledge relevant to their customer offering. In addition, these firms have established long-term relationships with partners such as suppliers, sub-contractors etc. to assist with activities such as product support, integration and delivery to customers. Thus, this type of networking manifests itself in the form of bilateral, recurring exchange relationships and agreements between firms and subcontractors, or in strategic alliances or joint ventures with acquainted partners over a long-term period. In many cases (eg AB Networks, APC), a significant portion of firms’ revenue is devoted to certain suppliers or subcontractors with which they have repeated exchanges. In other cases (eg Philips Healthcare, NRC), the development of high-density networks occur as a result of certain employees representing the company in collaborative international research-funded OSS projects involving research institutes, companies, universities etc. These cooperative linkages often become more personal over time, with employees exchanging information and ideas with individuals in other organisations in an informal capacity. In order to gain knowledge and ideas externally, firms also participate in OSS conferences and collaborate with many suppliers, universities and research institutes. Additionally firms engaging in high-density networks often invite known participants for co-innovation of OSS technologies. If the technology reaches a certain level of maturity, an open ecosystem where any organisation can become a member is preferred. For example, the managers at APC described how they initiate some projects on open source platforms such as SourceForge.Net or Google Code to seek the utmost level of feedback and expertise from complementors and spur adoption of their technologies. This open ecosystem often resembles a low-density network comprising a multitude of complementors that offer feedback, knowledge etc. for further development of the technology. Lowdensity networks manifest as multilateral linkages, often over a short period, with a broad range of different stakeholders, typically without prior relationships.

In low-density networks, firms gain access to a broader range of ideas and knowledge of new complementors, resulting in enhancements that increase the value of their existing customer offering. Two of case study organisations, that is Osmosoft and NRC, choose relationships and linkages with multiple, often unfamiliar, partners and OSS communities to avail of benefits such as access to solutions and ideas they never considered themselves, specialised knowledge or capabilities and the potential to co-create products with external partners. The Director of OSS Operations at NRC described how they access external knowledge and ideas through networking with members on a global basis. For example, the company is a member of the Eclipse OSS Network, a network that comprises over 120 members. The network focuses on building an open development platform comprised of extensible frameworks, tools and runtimes for building and managing software across its lifecycle. Similarly, the managers at APC explained that they have partnered with the IP intermediary, Innocentive, to innovate around OSS and have a license agreement with Intellectual Ventures, a company focusing on IP and OSS innovation. Based on the above analysis, we refine Proposition 1 as follows:

Proposition 1a: A firm’s participation in a high-density VN of complementors enables value creation with OSS by allowing firms to improve their customer offering through developing or improving products together in a cooperative and collaborative manner.

Proposition 1b: A firm’s participation in a low-density network of complementors enables value creation with OSS by allowing firms to further develop technologies that increase the value of their existing customer offering.

## Network attributes that facilitate value creation

The study found that commitment, knowledge creation and alignment of objectives facilitate value creation with OSS. In a VN, value is co-created or co-produced and so firms with complementary capabilities need to be fully committed to cooperating in the network. Strong and ongoing commitment in a high-density VN facilitates value creation with OSS as firms spend time sharing expertise and competencies with existing members who contribute various efforts that improve the firm’s customer offering. Study participants (eg Talend, APC, NRC, Philips Healthcare and Bredex) share development efforts, create standards and as a result deliver attractive value propositions to the customer in the form of good quality, low-cost products in a faster timeframe. If commitment is not visible in a network, then various participants may not contribute either the complements or knowledge required.

The level of commitment in a high-density network is strong due to the repeated exchanges and interactions that take place among partners. Thus, these types of networks survive because of the long-term, credible commitment displayed. For high-density networks involving AB Networks, Opex, APC, NRC, Philips Healthcare and Philips Innovation Site, this long-term commitment has enabled a huge amount of trust to be built among members in the network. Forming joint ventures and strategic alliances and consortia with familiar partners (eg Philips Healthcare’s alliances with hospitals, software houses and research institutes) has strengthened relationships and portrayed an even stronger commitment to the network.

The analysis also revealed that a greater level of commitment is required in low-density networks to facilitate value creation with OSS. Firms spend time sharing expertise and competences and contribute various efforts that motivates new stakeholders to reveal complements for use in the firm’s customer offering. In forming networks with new participants and attempting to strengthen any weak ties, companies, as newcomers, need to signal a high level of commitment through hard work and by engaging very actively with other network participants. Only then will they be accepted to the network and recognised as true contributors. For example, the Director of OSS Operations at NRC explained that, ‘if you come to a network as an outsider, it’s like going to a foreign country without you knowing the language or knowing the people – you don’t know how to get around. So you need good communication, collaborative skills and commitment. Once you get into that country, you start learning the language and so forth and then you know you can start doing so much more’. If a high level of commitment is absent, then partners or new complementors can be reluctant to invest resources or time in the network and value creation will suffer as a result. Nonetheless, commitment needs to be ongoing in both types of networks, otherwise the innovation and collaboration that is taking place will cease at some point and the network abandoned. The Program Director at APC pointed out that ‘if you start a project and then run away after a short period of time, the others will not stay’. Management support and reward incentives promote networking and collaboration. Holding various face-to-face events for network participants and the provision of financial support by firms displays commitment and trust and consequently encourages other members to reveal their internal complements for use in a firm’s product offering. For example, Philips Healthcare and APC host various face-to-face events for network participants and, in many cases, cover their travel expenses. We, therefore, refine Proposition 2 regarding how commitment in a network facilitates value creation and capture with OSS, as follows:

Proposition 2a: Commitment in a high-density VN facilitates value creation with OSS if firms spend time sharing expertise and competencies with existing members and contribute various efforts that improve the customer offering.

Proposition 2b: Commitment in a low-density VN facilitates value creation with OSS if firms spend time sharing expertise and knowledge and contribute various efforts that motivate new stakeholders to reveal complements for use in the customer offering.

The study revealed that knowledge exchange in a network facilities value creation as it enables the integration of resources and skills of complementors that results in the joint creation of products. Access to complementary skills and a broader knowledge-base in the network facilitates different types of specific and diverse knowledge exchange and positively influences mutual learning and firm innovation. The study shows that substantial knowledge exchange in a high-density VN facilitates value creation with OSS by enabling firms to access knowledge specific to their customer offering. The Business Architect at Philips Healthcare explained that the firm has been investigating the use of OSS in the area of communication protocols and image processing algorithms, and that the knowledge exchanged with network members has been invaluable. He revealed; ‘all partners need a good level of knowledge exchange, otherwise you cannot come up with a common product’. The knowledge that is exchanged in high-density networks is more precise and specific to company needs and goals. As the Director of Software Services at Philips Healthcare described ‘the “specific-ness” makes it more valuable because in the end you cannot spend your entire day exchanging knowledge’.

In low-density networks, the study found that a high volume of knowledge exchange facilitates value creation with OSS as it enables firms to access new and diverse knowledge of new complementors that further improves their customer offering. Several of the study participants (eg Osmosoft, NRC, APC and Bredex) explained that the more knowledge that is exchanged, the more this facilitates learning as companies are learning something new and integrating these ideas or innovations in their customer offering. Thus, repeated interactions in high-density networks of familiar partners may prevent firms from accessing diverse knowledge and ideas that can be found in low-density networks of multiple partners.

Face-to-face interaction was believed to be important for superior knowledge exchange, especially with new network participants. All of the study participants frequently attend various OSS events that promote more knowledge exchange and often presents them with the potential to meet new contributors. Additionally, all study participants hold public events and face-to-face meetings on a regular basis where members from networks are invited to participate. These events provide firms with the opportunity to discuss ideas for new products, standards etc., and capture knowledge and ideas for more innovation. We now refine Proposition 3 regarding how knowledge exchange facilitates value creation in a network:

Proposition 3a: Knowledge exchange in a high-density VN facilitates value creation with OSS by enabling firms to access knowledge specific to their customer offering.

Proposition 3b: Knowledge exchange in a low-density VN facilitates value creation with OSS by enabling firms to access new and diverse knowledge that further improves their customer offering.

Our analysis also revealed that the successful alignment of objectives in a high-density VN facilitates value creation with OSS by instilling a common vision and goal among members for product development and improvement. Study participants at Philips Healthcare explained that in sharing development efforts and creating standards with other members, a common goal and vision is crucial. They believe that if this is absent from the network, then improvements for products or standards that are appealing to customers will not materialise and complementors may leave the network. While it is recognised that the objectives will be different among the various partners, they need to share a common vision and goal for product development or improvement. All of the study participants reported that this was essential in facilitating value creation, with the Partnership Project Manager in Philips Healthcare further explaining; ‘we want to improve our equipment and they (hospitals) may experiment with them. They can do their treatments better. That’s the idea. We learn how to improve the system in such a way that it is of real value to the markety and these are different objectives but we both come to the same thing’.

In high-density networks the absence of conflict motivates firms to work on new products, features and standards that are appealing to customers. Many of the networks that firms such as AB Networks, the Anna Freud Centre, Talend, Bredex and Opex participate in have achieved long-term sustainability. Study participants in these companies have worked with the same partners for many years developing complements or enhancements for products and services. Members receive various rewards in the form of an enhanced reputation or increased visibility as a result of working together with others and sharing development efforts that reduce time to market and improve products for customer.

In addition, it was found that the successful alignment of objectives in a low-density VN facilitates value creation as new complementors are incentivised to work on OSS features and complements that further improve the firm’s customer offering. For example, study participants at Osmosoft and NRC explained that they need to effectively communicate with and convince new complementors that there is a win:win situation for all and incentives such as recognition, reputation, access to available code and expertise of others, to be gained from joining the network, aligning objectives and working towards a common goal. We, therefore, refine Proposition 4 regarding how the alignment of objectives in a network facilitates value creation with OSS, as follows:

Proposition 4a: The alignment of objectives in a highdensity VN facilitates value creation with OSS by instilling a common vision and goal among members for product development and improvement.

Proposition 4b: The alignment of objectives in a lowdensity VN facilitates value creation with OSS by incentivising new members to work on innovative features and complements that improve the customer offering.

## How effective governance facilitates value creation and with OSS

Our analysis revealed that the level of commitment, volume of knowledge exchange and alignment of objectives depends on the effective governance of resources and capabilities in the network. The effective governance of a high-density VN facilitates value creation with OSS by promoting an informal process that encourages collaboration, commitment, transparency and fair exchange in coproduction activities, which in turn improves the customer offering. High-density networks arise as a result of recurring subcontracting arrangements or joint ventures and alliances with familiar partners over a long-term period. In this type of network, the governance process is more informal and less stringent in that there are little restrictions imposed as to how to collaborate and interact. Nevertheless, study participants in firms such as AB Networks, Opex and Talend, that operate in such networks, revealed that everyone is aware of the rules and as a result, firms are not as apprehensive about making code available and are aware of the ideas and knowledge they can share with network members. As the Head of Technology at Osmosoft, BT further explained, ‘governance is essential for companies. From a corporate point of view you absolutely have to make sure you are managing and monitoring what people are doing. Now that should not mean stifling the interactions that go on or stopping the flow of information but you’ve got to know the balance between maintaining a reasonable information flow across that corporate boundary which is an important boundary to be honest, because there is a change in liability at that point’.

In contrast, it was revealed that the effective governance of a low-density network facilitates value creation by providing a formal process that encourages new complementors to contribute assets in the network. When firms look to renew or broaden their existing network by searching and connecting with relatively unknown stakeholders, the governance process becomes more formal and more ‘arms-length’. Additionally, those firms that engage in networks of weak ties with multiple partners are less likely to invest resources up front as these linkages may be short term in nature; there may be perceptions of certain risks involved such as opportunistic behaviour or actions by unfamiliar members; and even unsuccessful delivery of ideal results than initially anticipated. Interestingly, one study participant pointed out that, ‘you need controls, you need ground rules. We are all people and people in mobs tend to behave like mobs. What you need are real serious constraints against misbehaviour. Additionally, rules must be out in the open and then work forward from that’ (CTO, Opex).

We, therefore, refine Proposition 5 regarding how effective governance in a network facilitates greater value creation with OSS:

Proposition 5a: The effective governance of a high-density VN facilitates value creation with OSS by providing an informal process that encourages collaboration, commitment and transparency in co-production activities.

Proposition 5b: The effective governance of a low-density VN facilitates value creation with OSS by providing a formal process that encourages new complementors to contribute assets.

## How a VN enables value capture

Our study revealed that a VN enables value capture for firms in different ways. Co-development activities in the network result in lower development and maintenance costs for firms. For example, the Partnership Project Manager at Philips Healthcare explained that ‘if there are people in the world helping you to maintain the software, your own maintenance costs can drop substantially’ while the Head of OSS Operations at NRC also revealed that the joint development of software in the Eclipse Community enables them to share development and maintenance costs. Both Osmosoft and NRC have also recruited people who are well established in the community. As one manager explained ‘you can use OSS networks as a vehicle for recruitment. If you are working with a particular suite of OSS technologies and you are looking to recruit people who are experts at working with those particular technologies, then one opportunity would be to look to the network to do the recruitment (Head of OSS Innovation, Osmosoft).

As stakeholders in the network develop, maintain and market products together, this frees up time for firms to focus on other core activities such as providing consultancy and support to customers. While customers receive products at a low cost or sometimes for free, they are willing to pay for complementary services such as support, consultancy and training. The CEO at Bredex described how the firm decided to provide their OSS tool free of charge and concentrate on what they considered customers to really want, that is, support, training, consultancy etc. He further explained that, ‘customers who refuse to spend a couple of thousand dollars on a software license are willing instead to pay the same amount or even more for consultancy or training’ (CEO, Bredex). VNs also enable firms to improve their software development processes inside the organisation. The open, collaborative mode of development that takes place in external VNs has been applied inside two of the firms, which encourages more ideas and knowledge to be shared among business units or teams internally. Study participants at Philips Healthcare and NRC explained that as a result of engaging in OSS VNs and gaining access to the knowledge and experiences of all members, they were inspired and educated on how best to apply OSS principles and practices inside their organisations and develop internal networks that utilise openness, empowerment and collaboration between developers.

The study also found that a high-density network enables value capture with OSS as firms benefit from the expertise of existing complementors and lower their development and maintenance costs as a result. Firms have access to expertise, technologies, standards, the ability to work and exchange useful information and work with others towards a common goal. On the other hand, low-density VNs enable value capture as firms benefit from the expertise of new complementors in the network that not only lowers costs but also enhances their visibility and reputation in the wider marketplace as a result. For example, study participants from APC, NRC, Talend, Bredex and Opex reported that the Eclipse network increases their visibility inside and outside the network, and thus they have more effective access to potential customers. The Head of OSS Innovation at Osmosoft also revealed that there is much to be gained from open collaboration that is centred around enterprise software solutions. Consequently, this group aspires to working in as open a manner as possible and make extensive use of social media such as blogs and Twitter. Besides leading a successful OSS project – the web-based client-side wiki platform Tiddlywiki – the group participate in a number of other communities, primarily those engaged in the development of web-based collaboration and open web technologies. Additionally, the company have recruited engineers from these communities to work on new OSS technologies. Based on this analysis, we refine Proposition 6, regarding the role of VNs in value capture, as follows:

Proposition 6a: A firm’s participation in a high-density VN enables value capture with OSS primarily by allowing firms to benefit from the ideas, knowledge and expertise of existing complementors and lower their development and maintenance costs as a result.

Proposition 6b: A firm’s participation in a low-density VN enables value capture with OSS primarily by allowing firms to benefit from the expertise of new complementors, and increase their visibility and market share as a result.

## Network attributes that facilitate value capture with OSS

Our analysis also revealed that commitment, knowledge creation and alignment of objectives facilitate value capture with OSS. The level of commitment displayed by various members in a network is extremely influential in facilitating value capture with OSS. Firms (eg Bredex and Talend) attract complementary goods or services to their network and capture value through the sale of complementary assets to complete the whole product solution to customers. To accomplish this, these firms need to entice complementors to the network and establish durable relationships with them. Attracting contributors and ensuring resilient and trustworthy relationships with them depends on the level of commitment displayed in the network. As with value creation, the study showed that strong and ongoing commitment in a high-density VN facilitates value capture with OSS as firms benefit from lower development and maintenance costs as a result of the continuous sharing of expertise and knowledge with existing complementors. Moreover, the contribution of complements from existing members enables firms to specialise in and gain profits from complementary assets. Additionally, the study showed the firms contribute code and other efforts and are seen by other members as credible contributors. Firms such as APC, NRC, Osmosoft and Philips Healthcare also hold and attend OSS events where they capture ideas for more innovation in the company, as well as ideas and knowledge on how to better improve their software development processes inside the firm.

On the other hand, the analysis revealed that lowdensity networks of multiple complementors require a greater level of commitment than for high-density networks, in order to facilitate value capture with OSS.

Frequent interactions, collaboration and contribution of code and other efforts are a vital requirement for firms in order to benefit from the expertise that resides with new complementors in these networks. As low-density networks can increase the visibility of firms in the wider marketplace and present them with the opportunity for more profits, firms need to display a high level of commitment so that they are seen as credible and trustworthy contributors. Nonetheless, the study revealed that accomplishing commitment in a network is often difficult, particularly in a low-density network, because the mindset of open collaboration is difficult to achieve among employees in organisations, particular if they are not well acquainted with partners. Thus, there needs to be motivation among network participants to stay committed to the network and organisations need to introduce incentives that provide the impetus for employees to spend time sharing expertise and knowledge with multiple partners. For example, the Head of Software Technology at NRC and Product Manager at APC explained that they have teams of people in the company whose reward incentives are tied to working and collaborating with open source communities. We, therefore, refine Proposition 7 regarding how commitment in a network facilitates value capture with OSS, as follows:

Proposition 7a: Commitment in a high-density VN facilitates value capture with OSS as firms can lower their development and maintenance costs through the continuous exchange of knowledge and expertise.

Proposition 7b: Commitment in a low-density VN facilitates value capture with OSS as firms can benefit from the knowledge and expertise of new complementors and increase their visibility in the market place.

Knowledge exchange in a VN also facilitates value capture with OSS in different ways. All of the firms studied capture value from the sale of high-quality products or complementary services and thus, need to gain knowledge suitable for support, training or other complementary services and goods that provide the whole product solution to customers. As with value creation, the study found that substantial knowledge exchange in a high-density VN facilitates value capture with OSS as it enables firms to integrate the specific knowledge they need in their customer offering and gain profits. However, a high volume of knowledge exchange in a lowdensity VN facilitates value capture with OSS as it enables firms to (i) benefit from the expertise and knowledge of new complementors and (ii) recruit experienced staff to work in their respective organisations. In terms of benefitting from the expertise of new complementors in low-density networks. the Director of OSS Services at NRO explained ‘somebody else from a totally different contexty from a different industry comes in and asks a question or proposes a solution that had never crossed our mindyso now when you get these people involved in the same project with a different background, then you can really innovate and do something great. That’s OSS at its best’.

Additionally, several of the study participants explained that the exchange of diverse knowledge with new communities and partners results in new ideas for innovation in the company and the possibility of recruitment. For example, the OSS Strategist at Osmosoft added that there are broader opportunities in a network for knowledge exchange that is not as focused or specific. In his words, ‘you might get some really out there ideas’ or the possibility to recruit new expertise. The study revealed that many of the companies could only apportion so much of their time engaging in diverse knowledge exchange. As one further elaborated ‘you can’t afford to spend all day every day engaging in very discursive exchange of knowledge with a wide groupy there needs to be some sort of focus to these exchanges’ (Head of OSS Innovation, Osmosoft).

The study revealed that all of the firms studied have excellent knowledge-exchange routines in place, for example public discussions, mailing lists, wikis etc., that facilitate value capture. They believe that face-to-face interaction is important for superior knowledge exchange, especially with new network participants, in addition to frequently hosting and attending various OSS conferences, fairs and technical events. All of these knowledge-sharing mechanisms enable firms to exchange knowledge not only with existing members, but also potential new complementors. Several of the study participants (including Osmosoft, NRC, Opex and APC) explained how they present at various events outside the network, which presents them with the opportunity to meet new contributors or recruits that are interested in joining their network. We, therefore, refine Proposition 8 regarding how knowledge exchange in a network facilitates value capture with OSS, as follows:

Proposition 8a: Knowledge exchange in a high-density VN facilitates value capture with OSS by enabling firms to access knowledge specific to their customer offering.

Proposition 8b: Knowledge exchange in a low-density VN facilitates value capture with OSS by enabling firms to integrate the knowledge of new complementors into new innovations and maximise profits.

The successful alignment of objectives in a network was also viewed as being crucial in facilitating value capture. The successful alignment of objectives in a high-density network facilitates value capture with OSS as it enables firms to lower their development and maintenance costs and gain profits in the marketplace as a result of sharing a common vision and goal for product development with network members. Many of the firms have experienced long-term network sustainability as a result of good communications and commitment between all members and the absence of conflict. This has resulted in firms continuously working on new product development and improvements. Furthermore, the analysis revealed that many of the companies such as Talend and Bredex are recognised as top leaders in their field primarily because of the visibility afforded to them from network sustainability.

The alignment of objectives in a low-density VN facilitates value capture with OSS as firms can enhance their reputation and visibility among new customers and gain profits as a result of convincing new members that there is a win:win situation for all in the network. The analysis revealed that aligning objectives in a network of multiple partners is often problematic. When collaborating with multiple stakeholders in a relatively unfamiliar OSS network, there is a need for negotiations and consensus and decisions need to be made. There was agreement among all study participants that when dealing with multiple partners, the end result may be very different from the one originally anticipated. As the Manager of OSS Initiatives at APC explained, ‘since there are more stakeholders, this (ie alignment of objectives) might be more difficult but this is the way to go, I believe. You have to step away from the idea that in the end it will all turn out as you wanted it to. That’s part of the risk’.

However, given time, a high level of commitment and knowledge exchange, it becomes easier to reach consensus and more effectively influence others about the company’s ideas and opinions and convince stakeholders that there is a win:win situation for all. In addition, it was found that there has to be a lack of conflict in the network, otherwise new complementors will not participate or contribute any effort. We, therefore, refine Proposition 9, regarding how the alignment of objectives in a network facilitates value creation and capture with OSS, as follows:

Proposition 9a: The alignment of objectives in a highdensity VN facilitates value capture with OSS by enabling firms to capture market share as a result of network sustainability.

Proposition 9b: The alignment of objectives in a lowdensity VN facilitates value capture with OSS by enabling firms to enhance their visibility among customers of new members that have joined their network.

## How effective governance facilitates value capture with OSS

As with value creation, governance of a network also facilitates value capture in different ways. The effective governance of a high-density VN facilitates value capture with OSS by enabling firms to make more efficient use of the expertise and knowledge to be found among existing complementors. Study participants at Philips Healthcare and Osmosoft explained that they improve their software development processes inside the organisation, lower their development and maintenance costs and gain profits as a result of co-development activities in the network. Nonetheless, if governance in the network is ineffective, complementors may withdraw and company profits, reputation, product quality and innovation suffer as a result. The presence of informal safeguards and few restrictions in high-density networks promote an environment of collaboration, transparency and fair exchange between members and enable firms to benefit from complements and improvements to be found in the network.

The analysis also revealed that the effective governance of a low-density VN facilitates value capture with OSS by enabling firms to access the expertise of new complementors that provides firms with opportunities for new innovations and the ability to gain profits from a wider marketplace. While the benefits of networking are evident, OSS networks come with a number of potential risks (eg legal risks, support risks etc.), especially for those firms engaging in new linkages and networks with multiple stakeholders. Thus, there are a number of formal safeguards in place and participation in low-density networks is subject to reviews and advice from legal departments, steering committees, technical advisory boards, internal reviews and formal agreements. Study participants at NRC, APC and Bredex explained that the presence of formal safeguards in networks promotes more visibility and encourages new complementors to join the network and contribute expertise that firms can benefit from. In describing the success of the Eclipse Open Source Network, the Director of OSS Services at NRC described how the governance model is one of the most unique characteristics of this network and has very formal safeguards in place, including annual dues from all members and governance by a Board of Directors. This study participant further elaborated how, ‘the governance of that network and the working model make it very easy for different people to come and joinyeverybody knows the rules. It’s an open place. Everybody can plug in their little technology and everybody can enhance their technology. And nobody can take that home and turn that into a competitive advantage in the wrong way’.

Additionally, all of the study participants believed that everyone should be aware of the rules of participation in networks. Members make available code and other efforts in the network and need reassurance that no opportunistic behaviour will occur. Additionally, there was general consensus among all study participants that new complementors are incited to join the networks once rules and procedures are out in the open. We, therefore, refine Proposition 10, regarding how effective governance a network facilitates value capture with OSS, as follows:

Proposition 10a: The effective governance of a highdensity VN facilitates value capture with OSS by enabling firms to make more efficient use of the expertise of existing complementors and lower costs as a result of co-development activities.

Proposition 10b: The effective governance of a low-density VN facilitates value capture with OSS by enabling firms to access the knowledge and expertise of new complementors that enhances innovation in the firm and provides them with greater market share.

Thus, the cooperative and collaborative development effort that takes place in this type of network is in line with proposals of Chesbrough et al (2006) and Vanhaverbeke et al (2007) and also extends Schumpeter’s (1934) theory of innovation in that it involves new methods of exchange and collaborative development that improves a customer offering, rather than just new production processes.

Firms also choose to operate in low-density VNs, always on the lookout for new linkages and relationships with potential complementors that enhance their innovation potential. Similar to the knowledge-based (KBV) perspective (cf. Grant, 1997; Kang et al, 2007), knowledge is an extremely important resource that organisations want to take advantage of in these types of networks. However, unlike the KBV view, firms share, rather than protect, diverse knowledge in these types of networks to generate new innovations and facilitate continuous learning and collaboration.

In this study, access to new and diverse knowledge enables value creation and capture with OSS as it provides firms with the ability to coordinate and combine their traditional resources and capabilities for innovation in new and distinctive ways and therefore, improve the value of their existing customer offerings. Again, this supports findings from Simard & West (2006) who propose that central to an open innovation strategy is firm membership of diverse ecosystems that hold distributed knowledge, which is key to their innovation capacity.

## Conclusion

The theorising process employed in this study proved to be highly effective, utilising empirical evidence in a value creation/capture context to extend theory based on research in a variety of disciplines such as strategic management, IS and economics. Having done this, we now call for research to test our model and contend that the study’s theory may be advanced in future research using several research approaches. For example, the constructs and propositions presented in this study provide positivist researchers with a useful starting point to begin testing the theory of value creation and capture with OSS from a variance perspective (cf. Wheeler, 2002). In supporting a positive research agenda, quantifiable measures and hypotheses are required. Empirical indicators are the link to turning broad propositions into testable hypotheses. Thus, we have extracted some sample indicators (see Table 6) derived from the axial coding of our data that may prove useful to other researchers.

A positivist research approach could begin by substituting specific empirical indicators, such as those outlined in Table 6, into propositions to form testable hypotheses. These hypotheses could focus on one or two constructs at a time, rather than the entire theory all at once. For example, the main premise of Proposition 5b is that the effective governance of a low-density VN facilitates value creation with OSS by providing a formal process that encourages new complementors to contribute assets.
