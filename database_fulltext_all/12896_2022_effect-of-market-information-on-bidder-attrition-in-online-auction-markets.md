# EFFECT OF MARKET INFORMATION ON BIDDER ATTRITION IN ONLINE AUCTION MARKETS<sup>1</sup>

Hossein Ghasemkhani Management Information Systems, Krannert School of Management Purdue University, West Lafayette, IN, U.S.A. {hossein@purdue.edu}

Paulo Goes Management Science, Freeman School of Business Tulane University, New Orleans, LA, U.S.A. {pgoes@tulane.edu}

Arvind K Tripathi Information Systems and Operations Management, University of Auckland School of Business Auckland, NEW ZEALAND {a.tripathi@auckland.ac.nz}

Information generated in online markets can affect both buyers’ and sellers’ expectations and therefore their choices. In this research, we investigate the effect of market information, generated in online auction markets, on buyers’ expectations and choices. To clear large inventories, sellers often conduct many auctions selling identical items over time, which creates an online auction market where competition dynamics spill over from one to auction to another. In these markets, bidders can participate in many auctions over a period of time, observe market information (supply, demand, and competition), and gain experience to increase their payoffs. We observe that despite having an opportunity to compete and win in future auctions, many bidders stop participating in these auction markets. We argue that observed market information affects their choices. We explore how bidders form expectations about market supply, demand, and competition based on information from two sources—market design parameters and behavior of market participants. By employing a hierarchical Bayesian latent attrition model, we empirically detect and investigate the effect of bidders’ expected market supply, demand, and competition on their attrition in these markets. Our study shows that the effect of market information on attrition is nuanced by bidders’ value heterogeneity. Through the lenses of behavioral economics theories, we show that the attrition behavior of high-value bidders is completely opposite that of low-value bidders. We discuss the practical implications of our findings.

Keywords: Online auction markets, expectation of supply and demand, bidder attrition, behavioral economics

## Introduction

Over the last two decades, online auctions have become an important complementary channel to the fixed/posted price channel for online retailers. Retailers sell large inventories of identical items through a mix of sequential (Goes et al., 2010), overlapping (Bapna et al., 2009) or simultaneous (Anwar et al., 2006; Peters & Severinov, 1997) online auctions, via their own or third-party auction platforms. These identical-item auctions, conducted over time to clear a sizable inventory, create unique markets, where bidders have the opportunity to participate in many auctions to obtain crucial market information and change their strategies to maximize their payoffs. We call these markets online auction markets, where bidders compete while anticipating demand, supply, and competition in future auctions.

These online auction markets are different from isolated online auctions in many ways. In isolated online auctions, consumers mostly deal with product and seller uncertainty (Dimoka et al., 2012) and seek information to mitigate these uncertainties (Tripathi et al., in press). Online auction markets add another dimension to this uncertainty, which we call market uncertainty. In isolated online auctions, learning and competition are mostly limited to the focal auction, whereas in online auction markets, it spills over to competing parallel or future auctions (Zeithammer, 2006, 2007) where bidders are forward looking and compete with future bidders (Zeithammer, 2006). Therefore, competition dynamics in auction markets are more complex compared to isolated auctions and are influenced by market parameters and bidders’ strategies. Market uncertainty associated with the competition increases winners’ curse or the possibility of losing an auction and, therefore, bidders seek market information to mitigate this market uncertainty.

In contrast to retail markets, online auction markets provide unprecedented information richness and, to some extent, shift the balance of power in terms of market information between buyers and sellers. Traditionally, information asymmetry regarding supply and demand tips in favor of sellers. For example, even in retail markets on the internet, only sellers are privy to the market supply and demand information. In contrast, online auction markets reveal information openly about the demand, supply, and competition dynamics of the market via the number of bidders, level of competition, and number of items being sold, winning price, winning bidders, and their bidding strategies. This market data allows both buyers (Goes et al., 2012) and sellers (Zeithammer, 2007) to modify their own strategies. Although online auction markets comprise sequential, overlapping, and simultaneous auctions, the endings of successive auctions and bidder participation in these markets are mostly sequential, one after another (Anwar et al., 2006; Peters & Severinov, 1997). Hence, in this paper, we have used the terms online auction market, sequential auctions and auction sequence interchangeably.

A key to success in retailing is the seller’s ability to retain its customers (Wang & Splegel, 1994). Similar to other forms of retail, auction markets also have to attract new customers and retain the existing ones to maintain higher numbers of bidders in these auctions because a higher number of bidders increases bidding competition (Bajari & Hortacsu, 2003) and clearing prices (Pinker at al., 2003). In fact, a large number of auctions on eBay fail because of their inability to attract a sufficient number of bidders. Retaining unsuccessful bidders for future auctions helps increase the total number of bidders (and bids) and is no less critical than attracting new bidders. Thus, our research is focused on understanding what drives auction participants to exit before the auction sequence is over, which is vital for the success of online auction markets.

As an auction sequence rolls from the first auction in the sequence to the last, the percentage of new entrants in the auction sequence usually decreases (Goes et al., 2010). Therefore, as the auction sequence progresses, retaining active (Yao & Mela, 2008) and repeat bidders (e.g., Goes et al., 2010) who continue participating in the auction market after being unsuccessful becomes critical for the success of these auction markets. However, despite having the opportunity to participate in future auctions, more than 50% of unsuccessful bidders decide to exit the auction sequence (Goes et al., 2010). As auction platforms compete to attract new bidders, retaining current bidders maintains healthy competition in auctions, a key to success for online auction markets.

In online auctions, bidders are heterogeneous in their value, demand (Goes et al., 2010), and bidding behavior (Goes et al., 2012). For example, owing to their type, bidders compete as a reseller (Goes et al., 2010) or an institutional bidder (Bapna et al., 2009), exhibit impatience (Zeithammer, 2006), and employ different bidding strategies such as reactive bidding (Zeithammer & Adams, 2010) when they respond to bidding frenzy (Häubl & Popkowski Leszczyc, 2019) in competitive market dynamics (Yao & Mela, 2008). While the literature has explored heterogeneity in bidding behavior, the effect of bidders’ value heterogeneity has not been sufficiently explored. We argue that understanding the role of value heterogeneity in online auction markets is important because bidders with different values influence market competition in myriad ways. For example, bargain-hunting low-value bidders who bid early, hoping to get a deal, may not win all the time, but their participation provides critical and much-needed bidding activity in the early stages of auctions (Bapna et al., 2008). In turn, this early activity attracts more bidders, snowballing the bidding competition between different types of bidders and eventually propelling the winning bids (Gillian et al., 2006). Low starting bids lower the barrier to entry and attract more bidders, including high-value bidders who may just want to enjoy auction participation (Ariely & Simonson, 2003). Overall, we suggest that while the number of bidders is important for higher winning bids, understanding the bidding dynamics infused by different bidder types- low or high-value bidders, is also important for designing online markets. We contend that bidders’ value heterogeneity may affect their perception of market supply, demand, and competition and therefore, their odds of attrition in online auction markets.

There is a rich literature in marketing and economics on customer attrition. In settings commonly referred to as contractual settings, customers enter into contracts with firms, and firms provide services or products until the contract is terminated. In these settings, firms know whether customers are “active” or not. Inactive customers stop receiving services or products as their contract, membership, or subscription ends. Examples of digital contractual settings are video and music subscription services such as Netflix and Spotify. In contrast, in noncontractual settings, such as those we study, customers have no formal contracts with the firm. When customers become disenchanted, they simply switch to another firm without the focal firm’s knowledge (Fader et al., 2010). Schmittlein et al. (1987) were among the first to study the problem of identifying and counting the number of active customers when churn is not directly observable. This situation where the attrition is known to the customer but not the firm is referred to as silent or latent attrition (Schweidel & Knox, 2013). Online platforms such as Facebook and Twitter and online retailers offer services in a noncontractual way.

When customer churn is directly observable in contractual settings, researchers and firms can use survival analysis to study the churn (e.g., Fader & Hardie, 2007; Schweidel et al., 2008). Alternatively, one can focus on the attrition event and use standard models such as logistic regression or machinelearning models such as ensemble models to predict attrition or investigate the factors affecting attrition (e.g., Coussement & Van den Poel, 2008; Gustafsson et al., 2005; Lemmens & Croux, 2006; Neslin et al., 2006; Verbeke et al., 2012). Examples of such factors are customer characteristics and activities, design factors, and environmental parameters. On the other hand, when churn is not observable, it is not possible to directly predict or explain it as an outcome. Therefore, we can only stochastically infer attrition based on the available data on the frequency and recency of customer transactions on the platform. In other words, although one cannot determine with certainty whether a customer is active or not, it is possible to use the available data to infer how likely it is for customers to be active at any point in time. Models using this approach to study attrition are referred to as latent attrition models (Braun et al., 2015).

Going forward, we will be using terms such as risk, uncertainty, and probability. It is important to note the distinction between risk and uncertainty based on the Keynesian concept of uncertainty. According to Knight (1921) and Keynes (1937), risk refers to situations where the outcomes are not known but the probabilities of the outcomes are known. However, in fundamental uncertainty, neither the outcomes nor their probabilities are known (i.e., known unknown vs. unknown unknown.) The distinction is important not just from a theoretical standpoint, but also from a practical standpoint. For example, it is well-established that people generally prefer known probabilities (risk) over unknown probabilities (uncertainty) (e.g., Tversky & Kahneman, 1992). Based on this definition, in real-world situations like the auction markets we study, it is reasonable to assume that bidders face some degree of uncertainty. Therefore, going forward, we will use the term uncertainty to refer to the lack of information about outcome probabilities on the part of bidders. Also in the paper, we use the term “risk” to refer to the likelihood of attrition—for example, when we talk about attrition risk. This application is based on the dictionary definition of risk as the possibility of an unwelcome circumstance (Oxford English Dictionary, 2019), and not on the economic definition of risk à la Knight (1921).

In this study, we define participation in the auction sequence as submitting a bid, and similar to other studies of noncontractual settings (e.g., Ascarza et al., 2018), if we observe bidder participation in an auction, we conclude that the bidder is still active at the time of the auction. This research explores why bidders, who have opportunities to participate in future auctions, leave online auction markets. To the best of our knowledge, prior research has not examined the effect of market information on bidder attrition in online auction markets. We aim to fill this gap and investigate the effect of market information and auction design parameters on bidders’ decision to exit using a latent attrition model. Further, we also show that bidders’ value heterogeneity affects their odds of attrition. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The following section summarizes the relevant literature. Then, we present a conceptual model and develop testable hypotheses. The next section describes our data. After that, we discuss our research method and present the estimation results and discussions. The final section concludes with directions for future research.

## Literature Review

Extant literature has examined the role of bidder and seller strategies in designing online auctions. However, we have yet to understand the effect of bidders’ expected market supply, demand, and competition on their attrition. This research aims to fill that gap. We identify five research streams that are relevant to our work.

The first stream comes primarily from the economics literature on sequential auctions for identical items. The focus of this literature is on auction mechanism design and equilibrium prices in sequential auctions. McAfee and Vincent (1993) discussed bidders’ risk aversion as a reason for declining prices and Engelbrecht-Wiggans and Kahn (1999) presented a distributional argument to explain why the demand for early items exceeded that of later ones. This literature has presented conflicting results related to price trends across a sequence of auctions: increasing (Milgrom & Weber, 1982; Raviv, 2006), decreasing (James & Brett, 1984; Kannan, 2010), and constant (Weber, 1983) price trends. Along with price trends, a few of the studies have also examined the market dynamics created by auction markets. Scholars have found that sequential auctions are different from multiple independent auctions (Engelbrecht-Wiggans & Weber, 2006), because the sequential nature of these auctions facilitates information transfer (Corazzini et al., 2019) and information spillover from one auction to another. Information transfer in these sequential auctions affects bidder participation and strategies (Bapna et al., 2009; Engelbrecht-Wiggans & Kahn, 1999; Jeitschko, 1998). We add to this research stream by investigating the effect of bidders’ expected market supply, demand, and competition information on their decision to leave or continue participating in online auction markets.

The second research stream focuses on the design and operational challenges of sequential auctions from the auctioneers’ standpoint (Lu et al., 2019). For example, to clear a given inventory in a finite horizon, this literature strives to determine the optimal number of auctions, their duration, the interval between subsequent auctions, degree of overlap, and the number of items in any single auction (lot size), to optimize the seller’s objective function. For example, higher lot sizes and fewer auctions in a short time horizon can expedite inventory clearance by offsetting the holding and obsolescence costs but increase market supply and threaten to lower the market clearing price. On the other hand, smaller lot sizes, in a longer time horizon, fail to capitalize on the available surge in market demand and risk loss of revenues, along with higher holding and obsolescence costs. Studies focusing on this problem typically determine optimal supply based on market demand. Tripathi et al. (2009) determined an optimal lot size for an online auction market where non-overlapping auctions are conducted sequentially and demand is uniform. Because the choice of auction mechanism has a significant effect on the optimal lot size and seller revenues (Bapna et al., 2009), recent studies have developed optimal lot-sizing policies for varying auction mechanisms and stochastic demand (Chen et al., 2011). A few others optimize starting bids and even suggest scrapping some inventory with high holding costs, to maximize the auctioneer’s revenues (Ghate, 2015). However, these studies focus on market-level outcomes such as revenues and often make restrictive assumptions about bidding behavior. In contrast, our research aims to examine the effect of market information on bidder behavior by examining an individual bidder’s decision to exit the auction market.

The third stream is empirical in nature. Using observational data from sequential auction markets, the focus has been on detecting diversity in bidding and participation patterns both within an auction and over the sequence of auctions. In sequential auctions, bidders learn from others’ bids and change their bidding behavior in later auctions (Jeitschko, 1999). Bapna et al. (2009) found that auction design parameters and the structure of the auction market induce heterogeneity in bidding strategies, which, in turn, affect sellers’ revenues. It has been shown that bidders factor-in upcoming auctions when bidding in current auctions (Zeithammer, 2006). Literature (Goes et al., 2010) has shown how bidders learn in these markets and change their willingness to pay (WTP). Further, scholars argue that bidders are heterogeneous in their demand, and cost structures and that this heterogeneity affects their learning (Goes et al., 2010) and bidding strategies (Bapna et al., 2004; Goes et. al, 2012). Missing in all these studies on bidding behavior in sequential auctions is our understanding that bidders are heterogeneous in their value and its effect on bidder attrition, which plays a critical role in the successful outcome of these auctions. With the learning that takes place, and the information that is widely available, why do bidders decide to leave a sequence of auctions that could still offer them the opportunity to win in future auctions? Our study aims to investigate this question. We extend this literature and argue that bidders’ heterogeneity in value and cost structure significantly affects the impact of market supply, demand, and competition on their attrition from these markets.

The fourth stream relates to consumer decision theory. Studies investigating consumer attrition in online retail markets have found that consumers’ expectations and evaluation of products and services play a large role in purchase decisions. Therefore, forming and updating expectations is a central concept in consumer decision theory (Oliver & Winer, 1987). Consumers use a variety of market cues, information from various sources, and their own prior experience to form and update their expectations about product prices, quality, and other relevant product information. Consumers’ expectations affect their choices and decisions. While most of the literature on consumer expectations focuses on pricing, promotions, and product quality (e.g., Blattberg et al., 1978; Krishna, 1992; Gopal et al., 2006), we argue that in online auction markets, consumers can form expectations about supply, demand, and competition as well, and evaluate the trade-offs between expected participation cost, likelihood of winning, and expected payoff. Consumers are able to form these expectations because of the availability of market information in online auction markets. Further, these expectations may change as consumers acquire more information and experience to process the information. Our work contributes to this literature by investigating the role of expectations about supply, demand, and competition on bidder attrition.

The fifth stream related to our work is the literature on customer retention and attrition, particularly latent or silent attrition. Recall that, in latent or silent attrition, attrition is only known to the customer but not the firm (Schweidel & Knox, 2013). Earlier studies in this domain focused on estimating the number of active customers and did not try to investigate what drives attrition. In these studies, it is assumed that customers randomly transact with the firm until they become inactive at some point in time that is not observable by the firm. Examples of such models are the Pareto/negative binomial distribution model (Schmittlein et al., 1987), beta geometric/negative binomial distribution model (Fader et al.,

2005), and the gamma/Gompertz distribution model (Bemmaor & Glady, 2012). In these models, it is assumed that customer attrition follows a stochastic process, and the resulting models can be used to infer the probability that a customer has left or is still active or “alive.” More recent papers have come up with ways to include covariates to investigate the effect of variables of interest on attrition. Studies such as Abe (2009) and Singh et al. (2009) included only time-invariant variables, while later studies utilized more flexible latent attrition models that allowed the inclusion of time-varying covariates like characteristics of customer transactions (Ascarza et al., 2018; e.g., Braun et al., 2015; Schweidel & Knox, 2013). Our study builds on this literature and utilizes a hierarchical Bayesian latent attrition model to study bidder attrition in online auction markets.

## Conceptual Framework

In this section, we discuss our conceptual framework and develop research hypotheses. We focus on a sequential auction market where an inventory of identical consumer products is sold through consecutive auctions using the open ascending uniform price mechanism. According to consumer decision theory (e.g., Oliver & Winer, 1987), consumers use market information to form expectations about the goods and services they intend to purchase. In a seminal study, Nelson (1970) showed that market information (price and quality of the product) affects consumer behavior and, eventually, market structure. In online auction markets, the quality of the product does not change<sup>2</sup> and price is determined by market dynamics; therefore, we posit that it is market dynamics (Ariely & Simonson, 2003) that affects consumer behavior and eventually market structure. Since these markets are more transparent compared to traditional retail markets, participants not only can observe market transactions (supply and demand), but also market dynamics along with the actions/decisions of buyers and sellers.

Marketing literature has documented that consumers seek external information from the market and environment (Murray, 1991) to reduce purchase uncertainty (e.g., price and quality uncertainty). However, in online auctions, purchase uncertainty comes from other sources, such as losing the auction or paying too much because of unexpected competition (Cheema, et al., 2005), and consumers therefore seek external information to mitigate these purchase uncertainties. In these markets, consumers seek information to deal with uncertainty and, as a result, they form expectations about supply and demand (Thaler, 1985). Since consumers are heterogeneous, their search and formed expectations would also be different (Goering, 1985) and possibly driven by their individual characteristics, cost structure, and prior experience.

Online markets have morphed into social markets, where bidders are influenced by other participants. Recent advancements in technologies have altered information flow in online auction markets, setting them apart from traditional retail markets. We observe that bidders learn by observing other participants and the environment (auction market) and, as a result, change their bids and participation strategies (Goes et. al, 2012). Drawing from social learning theory (Bandura, 1971), we posit that this learning from external sources also influences their market expectations and affects their decision to stay in or leave these markets. To the best of our knowledge, prior literature has not investigated how learning from others and markets can influence consumers’ decision-making—in particular, the decision to leave the auction sequence.

Supply and demand information has always played a key role in sellers’ decision-making, e.g., in market design (Engelbrecht-Wiggans & Kahn, 1999), but not so much in consumer decision-making, partially because most markets do not provide this information to consumers. In fixed-price retail channels like Amazon.com, transactions between sellers and buyers are not public. In contrast, participants in online auction markets can observe market transactions. Since bidders consider auctions a competition (Malmendier & Lee, 2011) and other bidders their competitors (Ariely & Simonson, 2003), estimated market supply and demand information allows them to estimate their expected costs (including participation costs) and payoffs in these markets.

Challenging the utilitarian approach, prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979) argues that consumers compare options in relative terms and therefore proposes a value function, which is centered at a reference point to explain consumer decision-making under uncertainty, such as in online auction markets. The reference point refers to the status quo and explains that consumers exhibit seemingly irrational behavior because they overweigh their current options over others (“certainty effect”). While the reference point in prospect theory refers to payoffs, other scholars have proposed expected probabilities of the outcomes as reference points (Viscusi et al., 1987) to explain limitations on rational behavior when consumers make decisions under uncertainty.

In online auction markets, the reference point of the value function can be thought of as the expected price based on bidders’ experience and expected supply and demand. We argue that consumers’ value heterogeneity affects their perceptions of the gains and losses, as does their desire and effort to mitigate these losses and maximize their payoffs (Gorbenko, 2019). Therefore, it is important to understand the role of bidders’ value heterogeneity in their decision to leave the online auction market or continue participating. A large body of literature has shown that consumers’ heterogeneity in their value and cost structure affects their behavior in online markets (e.g., Sundararajan, 2004; Ghose et al., 2006). Following that literature, we assume that low-value consumers are price sensitive (Gopal et al., 2006; Lee, et al., 2008) and generally have lower search and participation costs compared to highvalue bidders. As one would expect, high-value bidders are quality-sensitive and often less patient (Isaac et al., 2007) compared to their counterparts. We posit that this value heterogeneity affects bidders’ reference points and value functions. For example, something that high-value bidders consider to be a bargain could be an expensive purchase for low-value bidders. Further, value function and reference points change as consumers gain more information and experience (Klein & Oglethorpe, 1987). This research aims to examine whether the effect of bidders’ expected supply and demand information in online auction markets on their decision to exit is moderated by their value heterogeneity.

Consumers must balance the trade-off between the cost of participation and the expected payoff upon winning when participating in current or future auctions. Since auction mechanism and design parameters affect bidders’ costs and payoffs (Bapna et al., 2009), they also affect bidders’ decisions to participate in the auction market. Further, consumers’ understanding of the equilibrium prices in auctions and their values also play a significant role in consumer attrition. Prior work has shown that bidders’ experience and their cost structure affect their bidding strategies (Goes et al., 2012). We add to that literature and argue that consumers’ participation experience, values, and individual preferences, along with their expectations of supply, demand, and competition, contribute to the formation of a broader expectation of the winning price and the probability of winning. These expectations consequently influence their decision to continue in the market or drop out. We summarize our model of consumer behavior in online auction markets in Figure 1. Although we cannot observe consumers’ formed expectations of the winning price, realized surplus, and probability of winning, we are able to observe their participation and continuation patterns. In what follows, we use this conceptual model to formulate hypotheses that link antecedents such as supply information, demand information, sequence experience, and bidders’ individual information to consumers’ decisions to continue or drop out.

While a significant number of studies in consumer decision theory pivot on utility theories driven by rational behavior, literature in behavioral economics has argued that consumers are not always rational. Indeed, in online auction markets, the final price is determined by a confluence of market forces and users’ rational or idiosyncratic behavior because psychological, emotional, cognitive, and social factors also play a significant role in consumer decision-making (Ariely & Simonson, 2003). In fact, in these markets, utility and behavioral economic theories often provide competing arguments to explain bidders’ choices.

## Expectation of Market Supply

One way for sellers to offload a sizable inventory of identical items and minimize holding costs is to conduct a sequence of auctions (Chen et al., 2011; Kannan & Kopalle, 2001). The primary reason for conducting auctions in a sequence is to manage the supply of items while maintaining a sustainable demand. Therefore, sellers determine the number of items to be sold in each auction, auction lot size, to maximize expected payoffs over an auction sequence (Tripathi et. al., 2009; Pinker et al., 2010, Ghate, 2015). Since demand depends endogenously on inventory, sellers do not reveal total inventory and lot sizes for future auctions. Therefore, supply uncertainties, which are inherent to sequential auctions (Neugebauer & Pezanis-Christou, 2006), affect demand and market equilibrium. Overall, there are various factors, such as product obsolescence, market competition, and the stochastic nature of demand, which affect sellers’ supply decisions (lot size). A higher/lower lot size increases/decreases the rate of supply and affects market dynamics, and thereby the market equilibrium (Ghate, 2015; Chen et al., 2011). In addition to supply uncertainty, a supply shock, i.e., a significantly higher/lower supply in a short span (e.g., a higher lot size in an auction), may disrupt the demand cycles and eventually the expected revenues for the auctioneer.

For the bidders, sequential auctions are akin to multiperiod games, where they participate in many auctions and continuously adjust their strategies based on market signals (Goes, et al., 2012) and competing bidders (Oren & Rothkopf, 1975), making it difficult for sellers to estimate market demand (Parizi & Ghate, 2016). Therefore, we suggest sellers that need to carefully orchestrate and tweak their supply strategies in real time as the auction sequence progresses (Odegaard & Puterman, 2006). Since sellers usually do not reveal the size of their overall inventory for strategic reasons (Yin et al., 2009), bidders try to estimate available inventory by observing sellers’ actions in auction markets and change their bidding behavior accordingly (Goes et al., 2012). While higher lot sizes in sequential auctions expedite inventory clearance, they also put downward pressure on clearing prices (Pinker et al., 2003). Since the lot size in an auction is observable to bidders, we posit that changes in lot size could affect bidders’ odds of attrition through two mechanisms.

First, bargain-hunting low-value bidders may perceive a larger lot size in an auction as a signal of higher inventory. So, a higher lot size is likely to raise these bidders’ expectations for lower prices in future auctions. Lower expected prices translate into a higher probability of winning and a higher expected payoff for low-value bidders (Bapna et al., 2003a). Indeed, consumer surplus is nondecreasing with increases in the lot size (Vakrat & Seidmann, 1999a). Therefore, we posit that, on average, higher lot sizes reduce the odds of attrition for low-value bidders.

The second effect is related to the perceived desirability of auctioned items. In the multiunit uniform price auctions that we study, a higher lot size leads to revenue compression for auctioneers (Feng & Chatterjee, 2010). For example, in an auction where the lot size is 3 and the top four bids at the close of the auction are \$150, \$130, \$110, and \$109, winning bidders will pay \$110 each (the lowest winning bid), following the uniform pricing rule. In this case, the seller’s revenue is \$330. On the other hand, if the seller had instead conducted three sequential auctions, each with a lot size of 1, the seller could have earned as much as \$390 (\$150+\$130+\$110). Thus, higher lot sizes may result in loss of revenue for the seller.<sup>3</sup> Therefore, higher lot sizes signal the seller’s desire to clear the inventory in a shorter time period even if there is a potential loss in revenue. Auctioneers may expedite inventory clearance for various reasons, such as falling demand or product obsolescence (Tripathi et. al., 2009; Chen et al., 2011; Ghate, 2015). Expedited inventory clearance is more prevalent when products have relatively short life cycles, e.g, electronic goods.

Therefore, high-value bidders are likely to perceive the seller’s desire to clear inventory as a signal of the impending arrival of newer models of the auctioned product and be dissuaded from participating in the auction sequence because their WTP for the product may be negatively affected. For high-value bidders, bargains negatively correlate with quality (Ariely & Simonson, 2003); thus, we posit that an increase in expected supply leads to increased odds of attrition for high-value bidders.

Overall, we contend that while a higher lot size signals expedited inventory clearance, it could lead to different decisions for high- and low-value bidders because of differences in their value functions and reference points. Generally, high-value bidders are more likely than costsensitive bidders to be quality sensitive (Ariely & Simonson, 2003); thus, high-value bidders are more likely to avoid buying older/obsolete technology than low-value bidders, who may be trying to mitigate the risk of missing a good bargain. Formally, we hypothesize:

H1a: When low-value bidders expect a higher available supply of items, they are less likely to leave the auction market.

H1b: When high-value bidders expect a higher available supply of items, they are more likely to leave the auction market.

Auctioneers clearing a large inventory of items schedule a sequence of auctions. When the next auction in a sequence begins immediately after the first one ends, it helps auctioneers retain bidders for future auctions without affecting demand and demand cycles (Overby & Kannan, 2015). We know that a large percentage of bidders from previous auctions (i.e., repeat bidders) continue participating in successive auctions (Goes et al., 2010). However, it is not clear why unsuccessful bidders drop out of the auction market, despite being familiar with the market platform and knowing that winning bids in auctions are likely to be lower than the retail price (Lee et al., 2008). In fact, the literature indicates that consumers are willing to wait and may postpone their purchases in anticipation of future deals and promotions (Mela et al., 1998); hence, one can expect that bidders may linger, anticipating future auctions in an auction sequence. Therefore, if bidders can participate in future auctions to win an item they desire, it is not obvious that odds of attrition of bidders should increase when the time between successive auctions increases.

Since auctioneers do not reveal the size of the inventory or make announcements about upcoming auctions for strategic reasons, bidders form expectations about future supply from various cues they observe—for example, time between successive auctions. We posit that a longer time period between successive auctions increases supply uncertainty for bidders expecting future auctions selling identical items. As supply uncertainty increases, repeat bidders from previous auctions are more likely to drift away and start searching for the item elsewhere. Supply uncertainty affects bidders’ expectation of market supply and bidders may perceive the latest auction as the end of the auction sequence. This perception about the end of supply becomes stronger as the time gap between successive auctions increases. We posit that bidders’ odds of attrition in the presence of supply uncertainty are influenced by the following two mechanisms.

First, for customers in any market, there is a search cost associated with finding stores/websites that suit their needs The search cost not only entails finding an online or offline store, but also the time spent familiarizing oneself with storefronts or platforms and the policies and rules of auction houses. Search cost is important because it affects payoffs; thus, consumers tend to stick with the stores/markets that suit their needs and visit them frequently, even if they represent a suboptimal choice. In fact, bidders are so anchored to auction markets that they sometimes overbid in auctions (Malmendier & Lee, 2011). Note that bidders overbid irrespective of their experience, which confirms the significant role of anchoring and search costs in online auction markets. Owing to possible differences in income and the opportunity cost of time, search costs are likely to be lower for low-value bidders than for high-value bidders; therefore, in the presence of supply uncertainty, lowvalue bidders are more likely to leave the auction market, compared to high-value bidders.

Second, a longer time gap between successive auctions (supply uncertainty) could also increase competition and winning bids.

First, a time gap between successive auctions accumulates demand with the arrival of new bidders. Based on their experience and market information, repeat bidders may employ different bidding strategies, compared to new/inexperienced bidders. A mix of bidding strategies leads to higher competition and higher winning bids. Second, a longer time gap is likely to build anxiety and a fear of missing out among unsuccessful bidders from previous auctions (Przybylski, et al., 2013), partly due to their similar experience in previous auctions. Both of these factors lead to higher expected bidding competition and winning bids. When expected winning bids are high, low-value bargain hunters are more likely to leave the market compared to their counterparts. Therefore, we posit that, in the presence of supply uncertainty, bidders are more likely to leave the auction market to find the product or an alternative outside the auction sequence. However, we expect the impact to be more pronounced for low-value bidders. Formally, we hypothesize that:

H2a: As the time between successive auctions increases, lowvalue bidders are more likely to leave the auction market.

H2b: As the time between successive auctions increases, highvalue bidders are less likely to leave the auction market.

## Expectation of Market Demand

Now, we focus on bidders’ expectation of market demand, and first consider the potential effect of the number of bidders in each auction on the odds of attrition. In this regard, prior research has shown that a higher number of bidders leads to a higher number of bids and eventually higher winning bids (Nekipelov, 2007). Therefore, the number of participating bidders (Yao & Mela, 2008) has a positive impact on bidding competition in auctions (Bajari & Hortacsu, 2003). This increased market competition could influence participating bidders as they contemplate continuing their participation in an auction market. A higher number of bidders in an auction could signal higher market competition, lower probability of winning, and therefore lower expected payoff. Since online auction markets provide opportunities for repeat participation, bidders need to estimate the value of future participation. This value measures the expected payoff from future participation and depends on various factors, including the expected number of competing bidders in future auctions. In sequential auctions, where repeat bidders often come back in subsequent auctions (Goes et al., 2010), the number of participating bidders in a current auction provides a cue for the number of bidders and competition in future auctions. Therefore, upon observing more bidders in a current auction, bidders anticipate higher market demand and, as a result, fiercer competition over time. Thus, they may decide to stop participating.

On the other hand, while a higher number of bidders appears to increase competition, high-value bidders might perceive this as a signal of popularity, desirability, or even quality of the auctioned item (Ariely & Simonson, 2003). Most ecommerce platforms allow participants to sort listings based on the number of bids/views/ratings, knowing that buyers tend to flock to popular listings (Ockenfels & Roth, 2001). Indeed, prior research has found that bidders in online auctions exhibit herd behavior and may rush to auctions with a higher number of bids and bidders (Simonson, et al., 2008). In the absence of a fixed price, bidders look for cues in the auction environment to refine their valuation for an item (Häubl & Popkowski-Leszczyc, 2003), which can be influenced by the presence and behavior of other auction participants (Ariely & Simonson, 2003). Herd behavior in auctions affects bidders’ valuation (Häubl & Popkowski-Leszczyc, 2019) and therefore their expected payoff. In other words, when bidders perceive increased competition from a higher number of bidders, their valuation of winning the auction may increase because of the opponent effect (Heyman et al., 2004). These effects are likely to be stronger for high-value bidders, as they consider others’ interest in the item as a cue for higher quality or valuation of the item and continue participating to win the auction, reducing their odds of attrition.

In summary, while a higher number of bidders signals higher expected demand in the future, it is construed differently, owing to heterogeneity in bidder values. We posit that highvalue bidders consider this a signal of quality, but low-value bidders see this as a signal of higher prices. Formally, we hypothesize that:

H3a: When low-value bidders expect a higher number of auction participants, they are more likely to leave the auction market.

H3b: When high-value bidders expect a higher number of auction participants, they are less likely to leave the auction market.

Since many online auctions are conducted in an open ascending price format, longer auction duration normally results in more competition, because longer duration (1) accumulates demand (arriving bidders), and (2) gives the participants more time to counterbid if they are outbid. Therefore, a longer auction duration could influence bidders’ perceptions of demand, competition, and winning probability in an auction market. Indeed, prior studies have found that a longer auction duration has a positive impact on prices (Dholakia et al., 2002; Bapna et al., 2003a; Cox, 2005). A longer duration might lead to bidding wars (Roth & Ockenfels, 2002) because of reactive bidding from inexperienced bidders (Zeithammer & Adams, 2010) or incremental bidding from experienced bidders who consider it an equilibrium strategy (Peters & Severinov, 2006), resulting in higher prices and lower payoffs. When the auction duration is longer, bidders need to counterbid to fight last-minute sniping. This back-and-forth counterbidding also increases bidders participation costs. Overall, a longer auction duration could adversely affect the bidder’s decision to continue participating in an auction sequence, in particular for low-value bidders looking for bargains.

While many studies point to higher participation costs with an increase in auction duration, evidence from the behavioral economics literature suggests that high-value bidders may prefer auctions with longer durations (Ariely & Simonson, 2003). We posit that these bidders are less likely to leave the sequence with an increase in auction duration for two reasons. First, in longer auctions, as bidders spend more time, they learn about market dynamics, level of competition, and evolving bidding strategies, such as jump bidding (Isaac et al., 2007) and last-minute sniping (Ockenfels & Roth, 2006). Scholars have argued that bidders treat auctions as games and other bidders as their opponents and derive value from competing against their opponents (Hershlag & Zwick, 2000). We argue that high-value bidders are in a stronger position than low-value bidders to compete and beat their opponents, for example, by employing jump-bidding strategies. Second, while participating, bidders may become the leading bidder for some portion of the auction. This status of leading or winning bidder, however short, creates a sense of ownership for the bidders, termed the endowment effect (Ariely & Simonson, 2003). In longer auctions, bidders are more likely to experience the endowment effect and thus attempt to maintain their winning status for a longer period of time. Therefore, the endowment effect is likely to be more significant in longer auctions than in shorter auctions. The power and significance of the endowment effect arising from loss aversion is welldocumented (Kahneman et al., 1991). In practice, we observe that auction platforms appear to leverage the endowment effect to influence bidding behavior. For example, bidders with leading bids are reminded/notified using keywords such as “leading bid,” “winning,” etc. to reinforce the impact of the endowment effect. Further, upon being outbid, bidders are reminded of it to reinforce their sense of loss, as loss aversion is a stronger driver than gains (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). Carmon and Ariely (2000) argue that the endowment effect changes bidders’ subjective valuation of the item, and often leads to overbidding in online auctions. It is conceivable that high-value bidders are more capable of overbidding and therefore more likely to engage in overbidding, compared to low-value bidders. Formally, we hypothesize that:

H4a: As the auction duration increases, the likelihood of attrition for low-value bidders increases.

H4b: As the auction duration increases, the likelihood of attrition for high-value bidders decreases.

## Bidders’ Experience in the Market

In auction markets, bidders gain experience through participation, which enables them to better estimate market demand and supply and other relevant market information. In this process, bidders trade their time for market information and expect to use this information to find better bargains and win future auctions. Note that auction platforms do not provide this market information; bidders collect this information as they participate in this market. Therefore, we argue that there is a participation cost for finding bargains in these auctions, and bidders’ participation costs increase with the length of time they stay active in the auction market (measured as the length of time since placing their first bid in their first auction). This increase in participation costs could lead to a decrease in bidders’ expected payoff from future participation. Further, the marginal value of additional market information diminishes over time because, beyond a certain level, additional information may impair individuals’ ability to process and interpret information and eventually their decision-making (Chewning & Harrell, 1990). Hann and Terwiesch (2003) define the disutility of participating in such repeated online transactions as frictional cost, which can have a significant effect on bidding behavior. As bidders remain in the auction market longer, this frictional cost increases and, thus, bidders need to determine whether to continue or drop out of the auction sequence (Engelbrecht-Wiggans & Menezes, 2001). A longer time period spent in an auction market also imposes a waiting cost, which increases bidders’ participation costs (Vakrat & Seidmann, 1999b) and forces bidders to spend more time monitoring auctions (Davis & Maggard, 1990; Weinberg, 2000). Since bidders are heterogeneous in terms of participation costs, which increase with participation duration, bidders with higher participation costs are at a higher risk of attrition. We conjecture that the increase in costs related to participation duration is higher for high-value bidders than for low-value bidders. Since high-value bidders are likely to be less patient (Isaac et al., 2007) and have higher opportunity costs in terms of time, they likely face a greater attrition risk.

Although additional participation costs could increase the odds of attrition, especially for high-value bidders, there are two other important aspects that need to be considered regarding the time spent participating or observing auctions. It can be considered (1) a positive investment to attain the goal, i.e., the reward perspective, or (2) a wasteful expense, i.e., the cost perspective or sunk-cost attribution (Meyer, 1994). We discuss how each of these two perspectives could affect bidders’ decisions.

Consumers often have difficulty assessing their own WTP for goods (Ariely & Simonson, 2003), especially in online auctions where the final price is not fixed. In order to refine their WTP, individuals observe other bidders and take cues from them, either in online market environments or via various community forums. Bidders participate in many auctions because information acquired during an auction is valuable (Häubl & Popkowski-Leszczyc, 2003). Studies have shown that bidders ability to refine their WTP increases with their participation experience (Goes et al., 2010). As bidders gain experience, refine their WTP, and learn more about the auction sequence supply and demand, their chance of winning also increases. Bidders not only refine their WTP, but also their bidding strategies (Goes et al., 2012). As bidders spend more time in an auction sequence, they compare different bidding strategies in terms of payoffs and costs, observe winning strategies, and employ these strategies to score wins in later auctions. As a result, time spent in the auction sequence can be viewed as a positive investment, which puts bidders in a position to increase their expected payoff from participating.

Regarding the cost perspective, behavioral economists have found evidence that many people do not ignore irrecoverable sunk costs. In other words, as bidders spend more time in the market, they might feel more committed to continuing participating until they win. This potential escalation of commitment stems from bidders’ psychological inability to ignore irrecoverable sunk costs (Phillips et al., 1991). The initial investment followed by the negative feedback from not winning could push bidders to justify their previous decisions and lead them to invest more rather than to rationally withdraw (Rubin & Brockner, 1975; Staw & Ross, 1987; Teger, 1980). The experience and knowledge gained through time and effort as well as the commitment, driven by the inability to ignore sunk costs, can spur bidders with longer participation durations to continue participating. Moreover, the behavioral economics literature argues that bidders often participate in online auctions because of their addiction to excitement (Herschlag & Zwick, 2000), which is likely to increase with a longer participation duration. These effects are in contrast to the effect of participation costs, and we hypothesize that their overall effect is more potent for low-value bidders, given their lower participation costs. Formally, we hypothesize:

H5a: With increased participation duration in an auction market, low-value bidders are less likely to leave.

H5b: With increased participation duration in an auction market, high-value bidders are more likely to leave.

## Data and Measures

Data was collected from a well-established, reputable online auction platform owned by a large retailer. This auction house conducts many auctions selling identical items. We call a series of auctions selling identical items a sequence of auctions, or an auction market. The number of auctions in an auction sequence depends on the total initial inventory. The auctioneer determines the number of units (lot size) to offer in each auction of a sequence. Since a single seller runs all the auctions on this platform, we do not have to worry about seller heterogeneity and seller reputation, thus removing the effect of trust in the seller (Ba & Pavlou, 2002) on bidding behavior. Similar to most other reputable platforms, bidders on this site are required to register, which allows us to track every bidder and their bidding patterns across multiple auctions. Users also must pay an annual membership fee of less than \$50, which many consider to be a small price for the discounts and the generous return and refund policy offered by the platform. Users can observe other bidders behavior, their bids, winning bidders, and all auction design parameters for ongoing auctions. Bidders who have participated in previous auctions are considered experienced bidders because they have more market information than new bidders. The auctioneer does not reveal information about future auctions and the number of items left.

This auction house uses the open ascending uniform price auction mechanism. In uniform price auctions, multiple units of the same item might be offered in a single auction and all the winners pay the same price. This price is equal to the marginal bid, defined as the lowest winning bid. For example, at the end of an auction selling three identical items, if the top three bids are \$200, \$180, and \$170 from three different bidders, each of the three winning bidders pays only \$170. In this open auction format, bids are observable and bidders can enter the auction at any stage.

For the purpose of this study, we selected an auction sequence conducted to sell an electronic consumer product—namely, a digital camera.<sup>4</sup> This auction sequence was one of the largest sequences in our dataset, providing rich data for our analysis. The sequence ran for 130 days from 7/30/2003 to 12/06/2003. A total of 318 auctions were conducted in this sequence which attracted more than 1,000 unique bidders who participated in over 3,300 auctions across all the auctions. The starting bid for all auctions was \$1 and there was no reserve price. We define participation in an auction as submitting at least one bid in the auction. Since bidders’ final bid in an auction represents their WTP in that auction (Goes et al., 2010), we only consider a bidder’s final bid in any given auction. ## Model

When deciding on what empirical model to choose, it is important to recognize that we are pursuing two important goals. First, we want to build a model to stochastically evaluate the likelihood of attrition of a bidder at any point in time. Second, conditional on the aforementioned model, we want to measure the impact of various factors, such as market supply, demand, and competition information, on the likelihood of attrition. This will enable us to investigate our research hypotheses and provide insights for designing and managing online auction markets. It is important to note that our estimation of the bidder’s state, active or inactive, is probabilistic, meaning that we do not know with certainty if they have left the market or not. Our only assumption is that a bidder is still participating while the bidder is submitting bids. We also assume that all bidders start in the active state. The stochastic nature of inference of hidden states in HMM is perfect for capturing this uncertainty.

While traditional latent attrition models such as the Pareto/negative binomial (NBD) model (Schmittlein et al., 1987) and the beta-geometric/negative binomial model (BG/NBD) can be used to evaluate the likelihood of attrition at any point in time as a function of bidding history, they do not allow us to include time-varying covariates. In particular, we want to investigate the relationship between attrition and market design parameters used by the bidders to capture market information, such as auction duration and lot size, etc. Note that these market design parameters are time-varying covariates. Therefore, we use a flexible nonhomogeneous hidden Markov model, which models attrition through two latent states as shown in Figure 3. We refer to these states as “active” and “inactive.” All bidders who participate in an auction market start in the “active” state, and a few might transition into the “inactive” state at some point in time.

The states in a hidden Markov model are not observable, but there are observable outcomes, which are probabilistic functions of these latent states (Rabiner, 1989). The “inactive” state is an absorbing state, meaning that once a bidder enters this state, they will stay there until the end of the auction sequence. This way of defining states captures the way attrition works, and the hidden Markov model mirrors the way that states are known to the bidders but not to the auctioneer or the researcher. One of the benefits of using the hidden Markov approach is that it gives us a stochastic segmentation of bidders at any point in time to measure the probability of attrition at the bidder level (Singh et al., 2011). Hidden Markov models have been extensively used in the literature to the study customer-firm relationship and churn in both contractual and noncontractual settings (e.g.,

Ascarza et al., 2018; Netzer et al., 2008; Schweidel et al., 2014; Schweidel & Knox, 2013).

A hidden Markov model (HMM) is a stochastic model in which the distribution of some observable outcomes depends on the state of an unobservable underlying Markov process (Zucchini et al., 2016). In order to characterize an HMM, we need to know or estimate three groups of parameters: (1) initial probabilities of belonging to each state for every bidder, (2) the probabilities of transitioning from each state to other states for every bidder at any point in time, and (3) the conditional probability of the observed outcome, conditional on each given state (Rabiner, 1989). The probability distributions over the initial states in our case are straightforward and deterministic, as all bidders are active when they start participating. Therefore, the initial probability of being active is valid for all the bidders; likewise, the initial probability of being inactive is zero. The initial state probabilities for bidder i, $\pi _ { i }$ , are defined as below.

$$
\pi_ {i} = P (s t a t e _ {i 1} = s) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & s = a c t i v e, \\ 0 & s = i n a c t i v e. \end{array} \right.\tag{1}
$$

Next, we need to model the dynamic evolution of states for bidders over time. The Markov property means that the probability of being in a given state in a given period for bidder i depends on the bidder’s past states only through to the bidder’s state in the previous period. Equation (2) shows the transition probabilities for active bidder $i ,$ at the end of time period t. We are interested in $p _ { i t } ^ { a t t r i t i o n , }$ which captures the attrition probability for bidder i at time t.

$$
\begin{array}{l} P \big (s t a t e _ {i t} | s t a t e _ {i, t - 1} = ^ {\prime} a c t i v e ^ {\prime} \big) = \\ \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} p _ {i, t - 1} ^ {a t t r i t i o n} & s t a t e _ {i t} = i n a c t i v e, \\ 1 - p _ {i, t - 1} ^ {a t t r i t i o n} & s t a t e _ {i t} = a c t i v e. \end{array} \right. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Our HMM is nonhomogeneous in the sense that the transition probability from the active state to the inactive state is not constant, and it is a function of bidder-level and environmental factors. We use the logistic regression model in Equation (3) to capture the relationship between variables of interest and the probability of attrition. Our main variables of interest are represented in the $x _ { i t }$ vector, and the estimated coefficients for these variables will be used to investigate the research hypotheses. $w _ { i t }$ is a vector of controls (MU, WinningDummy, FinalBid, and ReferencePrice) that could also be associated with the risk of attrition, and ?? is the vector of coefficients for these controls.

$$
p _ {i t} ^ {a t t r i t i o n} = \frac {e x p (x _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \beta_ {i} + w _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \gamma)}{1 + e x p (x _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \beta_ {i} + w _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \gamma)}.\tag{3}
$$

To account for unobserved bidder heterogeneity, we allow the vector of coefficients, $\beta _ { i } ,$ to be different for different bidders. Each of these coefficients is assumed to be normally distributed such that the mean of the distribution is a function of each bidder’s willingness to pay, as shown in Equation (4).

$$
\begin{array}{l} \beta_ {i} ^ {j} = \alpha_ {0} ^ {j} + \alpha_ {1} ^ {j} M W T P _ {i} + \xi_ {i} ^ {j}, \xi_ {i} ^ {j} \sim N \big (0, \sigma_ {j} ^ {2} \big), \\ \forall j \in \\ \left\{ \begin{array}{l} I n t e r c e p t, N o r m a l i z e d L o t S i z e, T B A, \\ \left\lfloor N B, A u c t i o D u r a t i o n, B i d d e r D u r a t i o n \right\}. \end{array} \right. \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

Therefore, for each variable in $x _ { i t } ,$ we estimate three secondlevel parameters, i.e., $\alpha _ { 0 } ^ { j } , \alpha _ { 1 } ^ { j }$ and $\sigma _ { j } ^ { 2 }$ . By studying the estimates for $\alpha _ { 0 } ^ { j } \mathbf { \lambda }$ and $\alpha _ { 1 } ^ { j } \mathrm { . }$ we can compare how the likelihood of attrition varies for high vs. low-value bidders for each variable of interest. In order to have enough bidders at each level of value, we discretized the MWTP variable. The discretization was done such that we had at least 25 bidders at each level; we used the weighted average of MWTP as the value for each level and performed the discretization to minimize the squared sum of deviations from the actual value. This gave us 27 different levels for MWTP, so the discretized MWTP values closely mimic the actual continuous values of the measure. We later discuss how we utilize a hierarchical Bayesian approach to estimate each parameter using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC).

The complete transition probability matrix, P, is provided below. The first row of the matrix provides the probabilities of transitioning from the active state, while the second row shows the probabilities of transitioning from the inactive state. Since the inactive state is an absorbing state, once a bidder enters this state, they will not move back to the active state, and hence the probability of moving from the inactive state to the active state is zero.

$$
P = \left[ \begin{array}{c c} 1 - p _ {i, t - 1} ^ {a t t r i t i o n} & p _ {i, t - 1} ^ {a t t r i t i o n} \\ 0 & 1 \end{array} \right].\tag{5}
$$

Finally, statistical inference on the latent states was done through the data on the observed outcomes which are conditionally dependent on the latent states. The observed outcome in our setting is the bidder’s decision to participate in an auction by submitting a bid. Bidders who leave the auction market certainly do not submit bids anymore, so their probability of participating is zero. On the other hand, there may be bidders who are still following the auction market but only decide to participate in some auctions. Since our focus is on investigating attrition from the overall sequence of auctions, and to simplify the model, we use a simple binomial distribution to model the probability of participating in a single given auction conditional on being active. As we see in Equation (6), we only have a single parameter to estimate in this equation. If one is interested in exploring why active bidders decide to participate in a given auction, covariates in this model could be included to explore the relationships.

$$
p (Y _ {i t} = 1 | s t a t e _ {i t} = a c t i v e) = \theta .\tag{6}
$$

Now that we have characterized initial state probabilities, transition probabilities, and the conditional probabilities of outcomes, we can construct the joint probability distribution of observed activities of bidder i in an online auction market from the bidder’s first auction participation to the last one in the sequence.

$$
\begin{array}{l} {P (s t a t e _ {i 1}, \ldots , s t a t e _ {i T}, Y _ {i 1}, \ldots , Y _ {i T}) =} \\ {\pi_ {i} P (Y _ {i 1} | s t a t e _ {i 1}) \prod_ {t = 2} ^ {T} P (s t a t e _ {i t} | s t a t e _ {i, t - 1}) P (Y _ {i t} | s t a t e _ {i t}).} \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

## Identification

Intuitively speaking, the way that latent states are identified in our latent attrition model is through the observable outcome. For example, a bidder is considered active when participating (placing bids) and inactive when the bidder stops participating. However, for bidders who have stopped participating, there is a chance that they have transitioned to the inactive state. The longer it has been since the last participation by the bidder, the more likely it is that the bidder has left the auction market. It is important to note that the estimation of a bidder’s state, active or inactive, is probabilistic and captures the uncertainty about the bidder’s actual state (Schweidel & Knox, 2013).

We develop a nonhomogeneous HMM model with two states, which is identified the same way that general HMM models are identified. For example, we can infer from Petrie (1969) that hidden Markov models are generally identified up to label switching, which is not an issue for us given one of the states in our model is an absorbing state. Allman et al. (2009) used Kruskal’s theorem (Kruskal, 1977) to prove that generic choices of parameters in HMM are identifiable. Since our focus in this study is on bidder attrition from the auction sequence, and not on capturing what impacts participation in a given auction, we do not include any covariates in Equation (6) and model the average probability of participation for active bidders using a single parameter, θ.

## Empirical Analysis and Results

In this section, we explain how we estimate the latent attrition model using a Bayesian approach and discuss the findings. The hidden Markov model is a likelihood-based model that is amenable to the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) estimation; thus, we estimate the model using an MCMC estimator with multiple Gibbs and Metropolis steps. Bayesian estimation has the benefit of providing draws of the parameters instead of point estimates. This is especially valuable when we are interested in capturing heterogeneity, as is the case in our model. For prior distributions of the parameters, we used noninformative distributions. The estimation steps are listed below:

1. We draw the latent states for each bidder in each period using a data augmentation approach (Scott, 2002). Each time period in our analysis is an auction in the sequence, which provides an opportunity for the bidders to participate. In each iteration, for each bidder, we start from the last auction of the sequence where the bidder was inactive in the previous iteration. If the bidder was active in all the auctions, we start from the last auction. We use an independence sampler (Gilks et al., 1998) to draw the updated state, which can switch to active or remain inactive. If it switches to active, we stop and move to the next bidder. Otherwise, we keep going backward (we are taking advantage of the fact that the inactive state is an absorbing state.) For the prior distribution of the states, we use a prior distribution that assigns the increasing probabilities of becoming inactive from the last observed participation to the last auction in the sequence. Intuitively, bidders who do not participate in auctions for a more extended period of time are more likely to leave. We experimented with multiple prior distributions here to ensure that our results are not driven by any specific prior distribution.

2. We draw the within-class parameters(the vector ${ \boldsymbol { \beta } } _ { i } )$ For each class of bidders (bidders with the same MWTP), we use a random-walk Metropolis-Hastings step (Rossi et al., 2005). The prior for each class of bidders is calculated using the between-class parameters in a hierarchical fashion.

3. We draw the between-class regression parameters $\alpha _ { 0 } ^ { j }$ and $\boldsymbol { \alpha } _ { 1 } ^ { j }$ for each $j .$ We do so conditional on the current values of within-class parameters and the covariance matrix of $\beta _ { i }$ . We use normally distributed priors with mean 0 and a large variance for each j to make it noninformative.

4. Conditional on the current values of within-class and between-class parameters, we draw the covariance matrix for $\beta _ { i } .$ . We use the inverse-Wishart distribution for the covariance matrix with a noninformative semiconjugate inverse-Wishart prior distribution (Hoff, 2009).

5. Conditional on the augmented states, we draw the probability of submitting a bid, conditional on being active. We use a single-parameter binomial distribution to model this. Uniform distribution (between 0 and 1) is a conjugate prior for the binomial distribution.

We programmed the estimation procedure in R and ran the estimator 600,000 times. We discarded the first 200,000 draws as the burn-in sample. We further “thinned” the sample by only keeping every 40th draw to mitigate autocorrelation, which is usually an issue with Metropolis-Hastings. We inspected trace plots to verify convergence of the Markov chains. We also estimated two shorter chains with different random starting points to further ensure that

The variable TBA measures the time gap between bidder i’s current auction and the next auction in the sequence. The intercept estimate for this variable is positive and significant which shows that the odds of attrition increase for an average bidder with an increase in supply uncertainty. However, looking at the coefficient of MWTP, it is easy to verify that bidders’ type, i.e., high vs. low value, influences the effect of supply uncertainty (TBA) on attrition. A closer look reveals that the odds of attrition for low-value bidders are higher compared to high-value bidders because the coefficient of MWTP is negative and significant. In general, low-value bidders need to search more to fulfill their demand because of their more limited and lower valuation. Therefore, it is intuitive that these bidders are more likely to drift away searching for a bargain elsewhere with an increase in supply uncertainty in the auction market. In contrast, highvalue bidders may be less likely to switch or change their status quo and start a new search because of their potentially higher search costs. Note that with increase in supply uncertainty, high-value bidders are more likely to leave, but their odds of leaving are lower than that of low-value bidders. Given the definition of this variable, it takes negative values for overlapping auctions, which happens when the next auction begins before the first auction ends. It can thus be inferred that reducing the gap between the auctions in the sequence and having overlapping auctions might help the auctioneer reduce the odds of attrition.

In auction markets of commodities, a higher number of bidders usually leads to higher competition as well as higher winning bids and lower payoffs for the winners (Nekipelov, 2007).

Our results suggest that high-value bidders demonstrate strong herd behavior (Dholakia & Soltysinski, 2001) and likely perceive more bidders as a signal of quality (Ariely & Simonson, 2003) and desirability of the product.

In H4a-b, we posited that longer auction duration would increase (decrease) the odds of attrition for low-value (highvalue) bidders. Looking at Table 4, we see that the estimated intercept for AuctionDuration is positive and significant, whereas the coefficient for MWTP is negative and significant. This means that H4a is supported for average bidders (???????? = 0), and suggests that for them, the increased cost of longer auctions dominates; therefore, they are more likely to leave when the auction duration increases. This effect is stronger for lower value bidders, as the coefficient for MWTP is negative and significant. Prior research (e.g., Lucking-Reiley et al., 2007) shows that longer auction durations result in an increase in auction prices and, intuitively, higher prices could result in a higher attrition of bidders. Looking carefully at the estimates for the intercept and MWTP, we see that for very high-value bidders, it may be possible for the overall effect of AuctionDuration on attrition to even be negative. In summary, we find evidence that the endowment effect is more dominant for high-value bidders and find some evidence in favor of H4b. More research is needed to learn about the effect on very high-value bidders.

Finally, in H5a-b, we hypothesized that longer durations of activity in a sequence, measured by BidderDuration, increase (decreases) the odds of attrition for high-value (lowvalue) bidders. A positive effect could be attributed to the incremental cost of participation, while a negative effect would support the dominance of either experience or sunkcost effects. Based on the results from Table 4, the estimated intercept is positive and significant. This means that with an increase in BidderDuration, the odds of attrition go up for average bidders (???????? = 0). Therefore, while we do not find support for H5a, H5b is supported. Ceteris paribus, being active for a longer period of time negatively impacts bidders’ perceptions of the expected payoff for future participation, resulting in larger BidderDuration values being associated with a higher risk of attrition. There are two types of delay for customers—procedural delays and correctional delays. BidderDuration could be interpreted as a form of correctional delay for bidders trying to achieve their goal of winning. Correctional delays threaten the very purpose of the service encounter (Hui et al., 1998). Also, time is a resource and customers have to make decisions regarding the use of their shopping time (Leclerc et al., 1995). Therefore, waiting time might be perceived by customers as a waste of time (Larson, 1987).

Previous studies have established that consumers’ decisionmaking in online auctions is a complex process and is influenced not only by economic factors but also by psychological, emotional, cognitive, and social factors (Ariely & Simonson, 2003). We argue that this confluence of economic and psychological factors becomes more important to understand in online auction markets, where bidders participate repeatedly and compete with other participants. Using the lenses of utilitarian and behavioral economic theories, this research investigates whether bidders’ value heterogeneity affects their decision to leave the auction market. We find that economic factors tend to play a dominant role for low-value bidders, whereas psychological factors are relatively more important in the case of high-value bidders. We believe that this result may be due to the following reasons: Low-value bidders are constrained by their willingness to pay; thus, they are more likely to be driven by economic factors. In contrast, highvalue bidders, driven by the endowment effect (Ariely & Simonson, 2003), may overbid because they have less constraint on their willingness to pay, enjoy these sequential auctions as repeated games, or derive value from beating their opponents via the opponent effect (Heyman et al., 2004). While these two types of bidders seem to pursue different strategies, we conjecture that online auction markets facilitate generous knowledge transfer between these two groups. Indeed, previous studies have shown that in sequential auctions, bidders learn and adjust their bids (Jeitschko, 1998; Goes et al., 2010) and bidding strategies (Goes et al., 2012) to maximize their payoffs; however, bidders’ learning and knowledge transfer in online auction markets regarding different types of bidders has not been previously investigated. Future research should explore the nuances in the knowledge transfer in these online auction markets. Finally, our study shows that while all bidders have access to the same information pertaining to supply, demand, and competition in these markets, because of heterogeneity, bidders take different cues from this information resulting in higher or lower odds of attrition.

## Discussion

The structure of online auction markets allows participants to observe various aspects of market dynamics and transactions, which separates these markets from other online markets (e.g., retail). Freely available information in these markets can be used to form expectations about supply, demand, and competition. We developed a hierarchical Bayesian latent attrition model to estimate the effect of market information on attrition in these auction markets. To the best of our knowledge, the literature has not explored whether buyers’ expectations about demand, supply, and competition in auction markets can influence their odds of attrition. This may be due to the fact that typical online markets (e.g., online retailing) do not allow buyers to observe let alone collect the market data needed to estimate the demand and supply that can affect market equilibrium. Our results also highlight the effect of bidders’ value heterogeneity on their decision to exit the market. The literature only points to links between higher lot sizes and expected winning bids in the same auction (Pinker et al., 2003; Chen et al., 2011). Our research builds on that literature and investigates the effect of expected supply, demand, and competition on bidder attrition.

This study makes many theoretical contributions. First, our research highlights an overlooked area in online auction markets and investigates the relationships between bidders’ expectations of market supply, demand, and competition and their attrition. Consumer acquisition and retention have been well-researched in the marketing literature, which finds that acquisition is costlier than retention. We investigate why repeat bidders, which make up to 50% of the buyer pool may leave, despite opportunities to win in future auctions. Second, our research provides a deeper understanding of bidder behavior in this market, which could help market makers design better market mechanisms and retain repeat bidders. We attempt to quantify the effect of market parameters on bidder attrition. For example, we find that the average odds of attrition for a low-value bidder whose value is one standard deviation below the mean (average bidder), goes down by about 34% as a result of a one standard deviation increase in observed supply, ceteris paribus.

Third, researchers have proposed stochastic dynamic optimization models to design revenue-maximizing auction markets where bidders are strategic and forward-looking (e.g., Chen et al., 2011; Parizi & Ghate, 2016). Our research adds to that stream of auction literature, as with real-time information on bidders’ arrival and attrition, designing an auction market can be a dynamic decision-making process where the auctioneer makes decisions over time based on the information available and accounting for future attrition.

Our study also has many practical implications. First, understanding what drives bidders’ exit from the market is crucial for efficient market design. Yao and Mela (2008) show in the context of auction platforms that market designers incorporate both buyer and seller behavior to estimate market fees and threshold payoffs for all stakeholders. We add to that work and argue that incorporating bidders’ exit decisions should be a critical component for market design.

We suggest that practitioners can utilize our findings in conjunction with the existing approaches to build dynamic optimization models, which help maximize their expected payoff in sequential auctions over time. Auctioneers information set includes the information they have from the beginning of the sequence, i.e., total inventory size, and the updated information they gain throughout the sequence regarding the market dynamics. So, at any point in time, a rational auctioneer can consider the information set and make decisions by solving or approximating a dynamic programming problem (Chen et al., 2011). In doing so, the auctioneer takes into account both the immediate payoff from the current auction and the discounted payoffs from future auctions. The immediate payoff is the expected revenue from the current auction, conditional on the decisions made by the auctioneer and the environmental factors. The auctioneer can calculate this through a price model, which estimates the final price of the auction, conditional on the information set. The payoff from future auctions can also be calculated using the price model above, combined with a model for bidder arrival and attrition.

Second, the dynamic programming model discussed above can also be used to estimate customers’ projected values to make more informed decisions. After all, in the auction environment, the customer’s lifetime value shouldn’t be based only on their purchases, because even those customers who do not win provide value to the auctioneer through their participation. Note that platform owners can also use randomized experiments in online auction markets to collect detailed behavioral data by manipulating users’ behavior in these markets.

Third, our results provide guidelines to auction managers to optimize auction parameters to impact the odds of attrition. For example, our study highlights how bidders’ value heterogeneity affects the impact of auction design parameters on the odds of attrition. The optimization of design parameters can increase allocative efficiencies in these markets.

Fourth, auctioneers can use our model to dynamically estimate the risk of attrition for participating bidders. These estimates can be used to employ promotions/discounts and other customer retention strategies to target at-risk bidders.

Finally, our research also has implications for policy makers. We highlight the importance of access to information in online markets. We show how market structure can affect information flows and eventually market dynamics and alter outcomes. We highlight that online auction markets allow bidders to observe supply, demand, and competition, which is not possible in other online markets (e.g., Amazon.com). We would like to point out that a few auction platforms have recently changed their design in a way that makes it difficult for bidders to obtain market information. These changes validate our claim that auction market design changes information asymmetry and can provide more information to buyers, allowing them to be more strategic and thus to extract higher payoffs. By limiting the market information available to buyers, sellers may be able to extract higher rents in these markets.

## Conclusion & Future Research

In contrast to fixed price channels, prices in online auctions are dynamically determined by competing bidders (Pinker et al., 2003) and therefore a higher number of bidders is always desirable for auctioneers. The ability to retain old bidders is critical for maintaining demand and eventually success and higher revenues in online auction markets. Therefore, it is critical for auctioneers to understand why bidders decide to leave the auction market. In these markets, price fluctuates from one auction to another; therefore, savvy consumers wait for periods of low demand to reap bargains. Strategic consumers trade their time for market information and use this information to find good bargains in future auctions. However, despite having the opportunity to participate and win in future auctions, many bidders leave the auction market. The attrition of bidders in these markets affects market dynamics and demand and, eventually, seller revenues.

To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first study to empirically detect and investigate attrition in these markets. The findings of our study offer insights into the B2C subsector of online auction markets, where sequential auctions are used to clear large inventories. We tested our research hypotheses using real-world data from a large online auction market and found that bidders’ perceptions of market supply and demand significantly affects their decision to leave auction markets. We also highlight that while the decision-making of low-value bidders is driven mostly by utilitarian economic theories, the decision-making of high-value bidders seems to be rooted in behavioral economic theories.

While our study makes many theoretical and practical contributions, there are many limitations to this study. First, one could argue that bidders leave because they find an option outside the auction market. In our study, no close alternatives were available on the same auction platform during the same time period. While an outside option could be available on a competing platform, it is not clear whether the impact of such an option is significant. For example, it has been argued that bidders have limited attention and are so anchored to the auction they participate in that they normally do not even look at alternative options available on the same auction platform (Malmendier & Lee, 2011), let alone options outside the platform. And, irrespective of their experience, bidders consistently overbid in online auctions (Malmendier & Lee, 2011), which is further evidence of their anchoring in the current auction and lack of interest in exploring outside options. Therefore, we believe that alternative options do not represent a significant factor in this study. However, future studies should explore the role of competing platforms in auction market dynamics, including bidder attrition.

Second, similar to other academic studies, our research also relies on limited publicly available online auction data to estimate our model. In contrast, auctioneers and auction platform owners may have access to more detailed market and user behavioral data that can be used to estimate much more sophisticated models and structural parameters. These models, powered by detailed data, can better estimate bidders expected supply, demand, and competition, as well as their decision to leave the market. Finally, we do not include any covariates in our conditional choice model (Equation 6), which captures the probability of participation in a given auction for an active bidder. This is due to the limitations of our dataset, as well as the fact that the focus of our study is on the attrition from an overall auction sequence, as opposed to any specific auction; therefore, the lack of covariates capturing auction dynamics is less likely to affect our results. Generally, covariates included in the conditional choice model capture the short-term effect on within-auction participation, while the covariates included in the state transition model capture the lasting impact of covariates on attrition (Netzer et al., 2017). Including covariates in the conditional choice model could help us learn about bidders’ short-term and dynamic decision-making regarding participation; thus, this could be an interesting opportunity for future research.

Our study invokes a number of opportunities for future research. First, one could explore the trade-offs between higher short-term payoffs (through increased prices) and lower long-term payoffs (because of increased attrition) by modeling bidder arrival and attrition in online markets. Second, it would be interesting to conduct field experiments with the aim of disentangling the exact underlying processes affecting attrition by isolating specific aspects and controlling for all possible confounding factors. Third, future research could build and explore a structural model that directly models bidders’ decision-making by constructing their utility function and estimating structural parameters. The structural model could be set up as a single-agent model that focuses on bidders only or perhaps, more interestingly, models the dynamic decisions of the bidder and the seller as they interact with each other. This sort of dynamic equilibrium/game model would be interesting because it would enable researchers to study how sellers adjust their decisions based on lot size, auction duration, and other parameters in response to bidders’ choices. Finally, it would be interesting to explore the effect of additional supply information on bidder attrition. As of now, auctioneers do not give any signals about future available supply to influence bidders’ perceptions of supply. We do see this kind of signaling in some other markets, such as the air travel market, where sellers sometimes disclose the remaining seats left to attract and retain customers. However, those markets are not directly comparable because they operate on a fixed-price model, in contrast to the auctions in our context. Future research could explore how auctioneers’ decisions to fully or partially reveal available future supply might affect the odds of attrition.

## About the Authors

Hossein Ghasemkhani is an Assistant professor of management at Krannert School of Management, Purdue University. Before joining Purdue, he was a postdoctoral associate at the MIT Sloan Initiative on Digital Economy. His research focuses on new technologies and the tangible and intangible ways these technologies impact individuals and organizations. He earned his Ph.D. in business administration from the Foster School of Business at the University of Washington.

Paulo Goes is the dean of the Freeman School of Business at Tulane University. Before joining Tulane, he was the dean of Eller College of Management at the University of Arizona. He received his Ph.D. in business administration from the University of Rochester with a specialization in computer information systems and spent the first part of his academic career at the University of Connecticut. Dr. Goes served as the editor-in-chief of Management Information Systems Quarterly from 2013-2015. His research interests are in the areas of design and evaluation of IT-enabled business models, data analytics, innovation exploration, ecommerce and online auctions, database technology and systems, and technology infrastructure. His research has appeared in several top academic journals including MIS Quarterly, Information

Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Management Science, Operations Research, IEEE Transactions on Communications, IEEE Transactions on Computers, and many others. In 2014 he was recognized with the INFORMS Information Systems Society Distinguished Fellow Award and in 2016 he was named an Association for Information Systems Fellow.

Arvind Tripathi is an associate professor of information systems at the University of Auckland Business School. Before joining the University of Auckland, he was at the Foster School of Business, University of Washington, Seattle. He received his Ph.D. in business administration with a specialization in the management of information systems. He serves on the editorial boards of POMS, Decision Sciences, and Decision Support Systems. His research interests are in the areas of digital markets, online communities, open innovation, and social media. His research is interdisciplinary and has been published in several top-ranking academic journals in the information systems, marketing, management, and computer science areas, such as Management Information Systems Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Retailing, Research Policy, IEEE Transactions on Knowledge and Data Engineering, European Journal of Operations Research, and many others.
