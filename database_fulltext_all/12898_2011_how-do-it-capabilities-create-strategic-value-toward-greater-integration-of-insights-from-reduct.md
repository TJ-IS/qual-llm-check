# How do IT capabilities create strategic value? Toward greater integration of insights from reductionistic and holistic approaches

Lior Fink

Ben-Gurion University of the Negev, Beer-Sheva, Israel

## Abstract

After more than two decades of intensive research, researchers are still struggling to explain the strategic value of information technology (IT) capabilities. The current study suggests that sufficient advancement has not been made in this area of research because of the gap between the richness of theoretical formulations and the minimalism of the reductionistic approach taken to test them. While theoretical formulations describe complex relationships between IT capabilities and competitive advantage, reductionistic explanations rely on models that represent the integration of simple relationships. This area of research can, therefore, benefit from the wider adoption of a holistic approach that attributes competitive advantage to profiles of IT capabilities. This study draws on the resource-based view and configurational theory to develop two reductionistic (direct and mediation) and two holistic (gestalt and profiledeviation) models of IT strategic value. The four models are tested with data collected from IT managers. The empirical analysis illustrates the insights that can be gained by drawing on the relative strengths of reductionistic and holistic explanations of IT strategic value. Keywords: IT strategic value; IT capabilities; competitive advantage; resource-based view; configurational theory

## Introduction

The strategic value of information technology (IT), in particular IT-based competitive advantage (ITCA), has been one of the most intensively studied issues at the confluence of IT and organizational research. This issue received particular attention following the publication of Carr’s (2003) provocative article, which has instigated intensive ongoing discussions among academics and practitioners about whether or not IT creates differential value. While most of the discussions were conceptual and descriptive, numerous attempts have been made to test theoretical predictions with quantitative data (e.g., Dehning & Stratopoulos, 2003; Santhanam & Hartono, 2003; Tippins & Sohi, 2003; Bhatt & Grover, 2005; Aral & Weill, 2007). However, empirical studies of IT strategic value have generally taken a reductionistic approach, which models complex effects through a set of simple relationships among variables. Two fundamental models of IT capabilities dominate the empirical literature on IT strategic value. The first model defines a set of IT capabilities, which are considered to be directly associated with competitive performance. For example, Dehning & Stratopoulos (2003) hypothesize that managerial IT skills, technical IT skills, and IT infrastructure are positively associated with the duration of sustained competitive advantage. Taking a similar approach, Bhatt & Grover (2005) and Zhang et al. (2008) hypothesize about the direct associations between different IT capabilities (e.g., IT infrastructure, IT business experience, and relationship infrastructure) and performance. The second model defines IT capability as an overarching construct, hypothesized to mediate the relationship between IT resources and competitive performance. For instance, Bharadwaj (2000) and Tippins & Sohi (2003) hypothesize about how IT capability, comprised of physical IT resources, human IT resources, and IT-enabled processes, is associated with performance. Common to both fundamental models is the assumption that relationships among constructs can be deconstructed into a set of simple unidirectional relationships. The main implication of such a reductionistic approach is that each relationship in the model can be examined in isolation, that is, each relationship can be described by a separate hypothesis and be tested empirically. Accordingly, each IT capability has an additive effect on competitive performance, independent of other IT capabilities. The upside of the reductionistic approach is its ability to depict sequential chains of causal effects. Its downside is the diminished ability to analyze complex forms of interaction (Meyer et al., 1993). A main predicament of IT strategic value research is that complex forms of interaction have frequently been proposed in conceptual explorations (e.g., Mooney et al., 1996; Ross et al., 1996; Piccoli & Ives, 2005; Overby et al., 2006; Kohli & Grover, 2008), yet a reductionistic approach has typically been taken in empirical investigations.

The main thesis proposed in this paper is that the recurrent adoption of a reductionistic approach to explore IT strategic value has disproportionately increased the dominance of reductionistic explanations that take the form of ‘arrows connecting boxes’ (Soh & Markus, 1995, p. 30). This paper argues that significant advances can be made in this area of research by relying more heavily on a holistic approach that models complex effects as relationships among profiles, which are not susceptible to being reduced to simple relationships among variables (Venkatraman & Prescott, 1990). In the organizational context, the holistic approach draws on the roots of contingency theory and configurational theory, and highlights the importance of fit, alignment, or congruence. This approach asserts that ‘the parts of a social entity take their meaning from the whole and cannot be understood in isolation’ (Meyer et al., 1993, p. 1178), and it thus provides a distinct set of assumptions about the interrelationships among organizational attributes. The term ‘holism’ has different meanings in different disciplines and contexts. This paper considers holism as the general principle underlying configurational theory in the organizational literature (Meyer et al., 1993). The term ‘holistic approach’ is preferred here to ‘configurational approach’ to emphasize the conflict with the reductionistic approach (Venkatraman & Prescott, 1990).

This paper shows how to revitalize research on IT strategic value through the introduction of theoretical concepts that have proved to be valuable in organizational research. Especially after the notion of IT as a utility was articulated (Carr, 2003), it is essential for the field to develop a body of knowledge that is rooted in more diverse explanations of how IT capabilities create strategic value. Further, the paper demonstrates the value that may be gained by integrating the insights emerging from reductionistic and holistic explanations of IT strategic value. Whereas previous studies have employed multiple approaches to shed light on the contradictions among alternative conceptualizations, this paper shows how the insights provided by multiple approaches can be integrated. In so doing, the paper suggests that future efforts to understand how IT creates strategic value should attempt to achieve a more balanced combination of reductionistic and holistic approaches.

The paper proceeds as follows. The next section provides the theoretical basis for the development of four reductionistic and holistic models of IT strategic value. The third section describes the research methodology used to collect data and the data analysis techniques used to test the four models. The final section demonstrates the value of combining reductionistic and holistic explanations of IT strategic value in both practical and theoretical terms.

## Theoretical approaches to IT as competitive advantage

## IT capabilities and ITCA

Research efforts often adopt an outcome-oriented perspective of IT and describe it as a set of capabilities. Researchers are fairly consistent in defining IT capabilities. Sambamurthy & Zmud (2000) define IT capabilities as ‘combinations of IT-based assets and routines that support business conduct in value-adding ways’ (p. 108). This definition is consistent with other definitions of IT capabilities (Bharadwaj, 2000; King, 2003; Chang & King, 2005; Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005). However, researchers are less consistent in developing classifications and typologies of IT capabilities. They describe a range of capabilities that span the continuum from technical capabilities, such as IT infrastructure quality (Bhatt & Grover, 2005), to IT-enabled organizational capabilities, such as value innovation and operational excellence (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 2000).

Despite this variance, IT capability typologies typically include two types of capabilities – IT human capabilities and IT infrastructure capabilities (e.g., Bharadwaj, 2000; Dehning & Stratopoulos, 2003; King, 2003; Melville et al., 2004; Bhatt & Grover, 2005; Zhu & Kraemer, 2005; Fink & Neumann, 2007). Researchers hold that IT professionals require a combination of technical, behavioral, and business capabilities so as to serve their organizations effectively (Lee et al., 1995; Bassellier & Benbasat, 2004; Fink & Neumann. 2007). In this context. 'technical capability’ relates to the technical knowledge and skills of IT personnel based on their specific expertise in technical areas; ‘behavioral capability’ encompasses the interpersonal and management skills of IT personnel to interact with and manage others; and ‘business capability’ covers the knowledge of IT personnel about the overall business environment and specific organizational context. Whereas IT human capabilities are defined in terms of knowledge and skills possessed by IT personnel, IT infrastructure capabilities are defined in terms of shared services provided by the IT unit. Broadbent et al. (1996) define IT infrastructure as the base foundation of budgeted-for IT capability, shared throughout the firm in the form of reliable services and usually managed by the IT group. In line with this definition, Weill et al. (2002) identify 10 clusters of shared IT services, reflecting either a physical capability (channel management, security and risk management, communication, data management, application infrastructure, and IT facilities management) or a managerial capability (IT management, IT architecture and standards, IT education, and IT research and development). The present study draws on these conceptualizations to perceive IT capabilities as comprising five capabilities – three IT human capabilities (technical, behavioral, and business) and two IT infrastructure capabilities (physical and managerial).

The perception of IT capabilities as a potential source of competitive advantage is not a novel one. The most commonly used theoretical foundation for this perception is the resource-based view (RBV) of the firm (Wernerfelt, 1984; Dierickx & Cool, 1989; Barney, 1991). The RBV argues that heterogeneity and immobility of firm resources can provide the basis for superior competitive performance. Firm resources that are strategically valuable and heterogeneously distributed enable firms to outperform their competitors. However, such advantages cannot be sustained if competitors can acquire strategically equivalent resources to implement the same valuable strategy. Therefore, for a firm to sustain its competitive advantage, its valuable and rare resources should not be open to imitation or substitution. Research frequently draws on the RBV to explain why IT can be a source of competitive advantage (e.g., Mata et al., 1995; Bharadwaj, 2000; Caldeira & Ward, 2003; Peppard & Ward, 2004; Wade & Hulland, 2004; Bhatt & Grover, 2005; Oh & Pinsonneault, 2007). However, there is a paucity of research that draws on the RBV to explain how IT can be a source of competitive advantage. Put differently, research uses the RBV mostly to describe direct relationships between IT capabilities and competitive performance. Once IT capabilities are recognized as strategic firm resources – as a consequence of being strategically valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable, and unsubstitutable – they are typically hypothesized to influence competitive performance directly. However, the extant literature on IT strategic value implies that more intricate structural mechanisms, in the form of mediated causality (Broadbent & Weill, 1997; Ravichandran &

Lertwongsatien, 2005; Fink & Neumann, 2007) or complex interactions (Piccoli & Ives, 2005; Overby et al., 2006; Kohli & Grover, 2008), underlie the relationship between IT capabilities and competitive advantage.

ITCA is defined in this study as the ability of IT to create differential firm performance. To examine different explanations of the relationship between IT capabilities and ITCA, the next two subsections develop four models of IT strategic value. First, two reductionistic models are developed: a direct model and a mediation model. Despite theoretical and structural differences between the two models, both take a reductionistic approach to the relationship between IT capabilities and ITCA in the sense that they decompose this relationship into simple relationships among variables. Second, two holistic models are developed: a gestalt model and a profiledeviation model. These models are considered holistic because they formulate a single relationship between profiles of IT capabilities and ITCA.

## A reductionistic approach

The Oxford English Dictionary (2009) defines reductionism as ‘the practice of describing or explaining a complex (esp. mental, social, or biological) phenomenon in terms of relatively simple or fundamental concepts’. A reductionistic approach to organizational research is thus defined as ‘an approach whereby researchers seek to understand the behavior of a social entity by separately analyzing its constituent parts’ (Meyer et al., 1993, p. 1177). The basic premise of a reductionistic approach to IT strategic value is that the linkage between IT capabilities and ITCA can be modeled with a set of simple relationships in which each IT capability has an independent, additive effect on another IT capability or on ITCA. Accordingly, enhancing a single IT capability should have a deterministic effect on ITCA, independent of the levels of other IT capabilities. The dominant mode of inquiry in these models is a reductionistic analysis, in which each of the effects is analyzed and tested as a separate hypothesis. The prevalence of this approach has produced a large stream of research targeted at identifying IT capabilities that causally affect ITCA and the underlying structure of causality. A review of the relevant literature suggests that two reductionistic structures, direct and mediation, have been dominant in explaining how IT capabilities create strategic value. The direct and mediation models of the relationship between IT capabilities and ITCA are depicted in Figure 1 and developed below.

Dehning & Stratopoulos (2003) apply the RBV to hypothesize that managerial IT skills, technical IT skills, and IT infrastructure have a positive effect on the duration of competitive advantage. Grover (2005) examine the direct effect of IT business expertise, relationship infrastructure, and IT infrastructure quality on competitive advantage. They apply the RBV to hypothesize that only IT management capabilities (IT business expertise and relationship infrastructure) positively affect competitive advantage. A similar approach is adopted by others, who attribute heterogeneity and immobility to IT capabilities to causally link them to superior competitive performance (e.g., Bharadwaj, 2000; Caldeira & Ward, 2003; Santhanam & Hartono, 2003). The RBV provides a sound theoretical basis to associate IT human and infrastructure capabilities with ITCA. IT human capabilities are not susceptible to rapid imitation for a number of reasons: they are tacit, result from socially complex processes, and evolve over long periods of time through the accumulation of experience. This argument is often used to theoretically account for the strategic value of behavioral and business capabilities (Mata et al., 1995; Bharadwaj, 2000; Dehning & Stratopoulos, 2003; Bhatt & Grover, 2005). However, the ability of this argument to underpin the strategic value of technical capability is debatable. Despite the general agreement among researchers that technical knowledge and skills may be a source of competitive advantage, some argue that the advantage is not sustainable because of high resource mobility (Mata et al., 1995), while others argue that sustainability may stem from resource complexity, context dependence, and reliance on time consuming educational activities (Dehning & Stratopoulos, 2003; Wade & Hulland, 2004). IT infrastructure capabilities are likewise not subject to rapid and low-cost imitation. Whereas technical components (hardware, networks, databases, and applications) are commoditized to a large extent, they are integrated and tailored to a firm’s strategic context in a time consuming, pathdependent, socially complex, and causally ambiguous process (Weill & Broadbent, 1998; Bharadwaj, 2000; Zhu & Kraemer, 2002). Each of these characteristics enhances the strategic value of physical and managerial capabilities. On the basis of the above discussion, it is reasonable to formulate the following direct hypothesis, which is reduced into five simple hypotheses (Figure 1a):

Hypothesis 1: IT capabilities positively affect ITCA.

Hypotheses 1a–1e: Technical, behavioral, business, physical, and managerial capabilities positively affect ITCA.

Mediation model of IT strategic value Guided by a process approach, some researchers work under the assumptions that cause-and-effect relationships exist among IT capabilities and that particular IT capabilities mediate the influence of other IT capabilities on organizational performance. A dominant perception in this regard is that of IT infrastructure capabilities as a product of IT human capabilities and as a source of strategic value (Broadbent & Weill, 1997; Kayworth et al., 2001; Fink & Neumann, 2007). The structural implication of this perception is that IT infrastructure capabilities mediate the relationship between IT human capabilities and ITCA. At the input end of the mediator, the development of IT infrastructure capabilities requires IT personnel to possess technical, behavioral, and business capabilities. Technical capability is crucial because it allows IT personnel to identify, integrate, and utilize technical components in the process of developing and maintaining IT services. Technical knowledge and skills are essential for effectively integrating new systems with old ones, for delivering data across locations and applications, for optimizing IT investments, and for recognizing opportunities to apply new technologies (Duncan, 1995; Ross et al., 1996). Effective management of IT services also depends on IT personnel having a behavioral capability because of the dramatically increasing horizontal nature of business processes and the boundary-spanning role of IT (Lee et al., 1995; Ross et al., 1996; Bassellier & Benbasat, 2004). A business capability is necessary for IT personnel to be able to deploy IT services in a manner that meets strategic business objectives (Duncan, 1995). At the output end of the mediator, IT infrastructure capabilities influence competitive performance (Keen, 1991; Davenport & Linder, 1994; Broadbent et al., 1999; Bharadwaj, 2000; Byrd & Turner, 2001b). In the face of increasing competitive pressures and time compression, IT infrastructure capabilities can dramatically facilitate or inhibit major strategic moves (Duncan, 1995). Thus, the success of strategic innovations and new business initiatives often depends on a firm having the necessary physical and managerial IT infrastructure capabilities in place (Weill et al., 2002). The above reasoning leads to the following mediation hypothesis, which is reduced into eight simple hypotheses (Figure 1b):

Hypothesis 2: IT infrastructure capabilities mediate the effect of IT human capabilities on ITCA.

Hypotheses 2a–2c: Technical, behavioral, and business capabilities positively affect physical capability.

Hypotheses 2d–2f: Technical, behavioral, and business capabilities positively affect managerial capability.

Hypotheses 2g–2h: Physical and managerial capabilities positively affect ITCA.

## A holistic approach

A closer look at IT capability definitions highlights the importance of internal consistency among IT capability elements. For instance, King (2003) defines IT capabilities as ‘bundles of internally consistent elements that are focused toward the fulfillment of an IT or business objective’ (p. 22). A question that is rarely addressed in research concerns the relationship between the two parts of this definition: how is the internal consistency among IT capability elements related to the fulfillment of IT and business objectives? This research question can be best addressed theoretically and empirically through a holistic approach.

The Oxford English Dictionary (1989) defines holism as a term coined ‘to designate the tendency in nature to produce wholes (i.e. bodies or organisms) from the ordered grouping of unit structures’. This term has become associated with the idea that the whole is greater than the sum of its parts, suggesting that a reductionistic approach only partly captures the interactions among the parts of a whole, and therefore the parts cannot be fully understood in isolation. The basic premise of a holistic approach to IT strategic value is that the linkage between IT capabilities and ITCA should be modeled with a composite relationship in which the effect of each IT capability on ITCA is contingent on other IT capabilities. The main assumption of the holistic approach, which distinguishes it from the reductionistic approach, is that the relationship between IT capabilities and ITCA cannot be broken down into simple relationships that are analyzed and tested independently. In describing the usefulness of such an approach, Venkatraman (1989) argues that ‘if the system were decomposed into a set of bivariate contingencies, it is plausible that internal inconsistencies (mutually conflicting directions) among multiple pairwise contingencies would exist’ (p. 432).

From a theoretical standpoint, the holistic approach to IT strategic value draws on configurational theory to establish the importance of congruence among IT capabilities. The configurational theory of organizations has emerged as a holistic alternative to established reductionistic approaches by shifting attention from the role of individual organizational attributes to the role of profiles (configurations) of multiple organizational attributes. The theory suggests that organizations are best understood as coherent patterns of distinct attributes that commonly occur together (Miller, 1986; Meyer et al., 1993; Mintzberg & Lampel, 1999; Whittington et $a l . ,$ 1999). This assumption about the tight coupling among organizational attributes contradicts previously dominant perceptions of organizations as loosely coupled aggregates of attributes. To describe the unique assumptions underlying configurational theory, Meyer et al. (1993) contrast it to contingency theory. Contingency research represents a reductionistic analysis of unidirectional linear relationships between external contingencies and internal attributes. To statistically isolate the effects of each variable, it typically downplays complex forms of interaction. In contrast, configurational research represents a holistic analysis of bidirectional nonlinear relationships among organizational attributes. Instead of isolated effects, it seeks to identify complex interactions (Meyer et $a l . ,$ 1993). Configurational theory also makes predictions about the relationship between organizational attributes and performance. It is concerned with how profiles of multiple attributes, rather than individual attributes, are related to organizational performance (Delery & Doty, 1996). Its key prediction is that a small number of congruent profiles should describe a large proportion of high-performing organizations (Miller, 1986). Underlying this prediction is an assumption of equifinality, which refers to the situation in which two or more profiles can be equally effective in achieving high performance (Delery & Doty, 1996; Fiss, 2007).

Research has rarely adopted a holistic approach to explore IT strategic value. However, the literature does offer two perspectives, gestalt and profile-deviation (Venkatraman, 1989), that may advance the understanding of how the congruence among IT capabilities is related to ITCA. Two models of IT strategic value are developed below based on these perspectives.

Gestalt model of IT strategic value Miller (1996) argues that ‘the heart of distinctive competence and competitive advantage may lie not in the possession of specific organizational resources or skills y rather, competitive advantage may reside in the orchestrating theme and integrative mechanisms that ensure complementarity among a firm’s various aspects’ (p. 509). The literature offers various conceptualizations of integrative mechanisms of complementarity among IT capabilities. Henderson & Venkatraman (1993) define four fundamental domains of strategic choice – business strategy, IT strategy, organizational infrastructure and processes, and IT infrastructure and processes – and argue that both the alignment among the domains and the alignment among their underlying dimensions determine the strategic value of IT. The IT infrastructure and processes domain has three underlying dimensions: IT architecture, IT skills, and IT processes. Henderson & Venkatraman’s (1993) logic suggests that the alignment between IT human capabilities (IT skills) and IT infrastructure capabilities (combination of IT architecture and IT processes) has positive strategic effects. Tippins & Sohi (2003) describe IT competency as consisting of three co-specialized resources: IT objects, IT knowledge, and IT operations. They rely on the definition of Clemons & Row (1991) of co-specialization, according to which one resource has little or no value without another, to conclude that achieving IT competency depends on the combination of IT objects, knowledge, and operations. Finally, Aral & Weill (2007) draw on earlier conceptualizations of complementarity to describe IT capabilities as a mutually reinforcing system of competencies (skills) and practices (routines), where routines help individuals and groups develop skills in specific ways of working, and skills are needed for the effective execution of routines toward specified goals (Nelson & Winter, 1982).

These integrative mechanisms are the cornerstones of a gestalt model of IT strategic value, which views ITCA as a product of the complementarity among IT capabilities. The importance of resource complementarity to competitive advantage is recognized by the RBV, although this concept is not well developed in the theory (Wade & Hulland, 2004). Complementarity in deployment or application of firm resources means that the strategic value of each resource’s relative magnitude may increase with an increase in the relative magnitude of other resources (Amit & Schoemaker, 1993), implying a relationship of bilateral dependence between complemen tary resources (Teece, 1986). Resource complementarity in IT capabilities therefore means that the strategic value of an IT capability increases with the deployment of other IT capabilities. Consequently, the combined deployment of complementary IT capabilities should result in higher ITCA than their individual deployment.

Resource complementarity may exist between specific knowledge-based (human) and process-based (infrastructure) IT capabilities, as a result of the interdependencies between knowledge and process in the provision of IT services (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Tippins & Sohi, 2003; Aral & Weill, 2007). Complementarity is expected to exist between technical capability (human) and physical capability (infrastructure) because these capabilities are each necessary but not sufficient conditions for ITCA: only their consistent combination can lead to a competitive impact. Consider, for example, a firm with a high technical capability that is not comple mented by a high physical capability. Absent the necessary physical infrastructure, the firm’s superior technical knowledge and skills cannot be translated into an organizational impact. Similarly, complementarity is expected to exist between behavioral and business capabilities (human) and managerial capability (infrastructure) because of the mutually reinforcing nature of the relationship between business skills and managerial practices (Aral & Weill, 2007).

Adopting a holistic approach, the gestalt model seeks to identify congruent profiles of IT capabilities. On the basis of the assumption that IT strategic value depends on the complementarity between human and infrastructure capabilities, IT capability profiles are considered congruent when they reflect this complementarity. Profiles are congruent when complementing IT capabilities are deployed or not deployed together, and they are incongruent when a particular IT capability is deployed without its complementing IT capabilities. For instance, firms in which only technical capability is high, only managerial capability is high, or only both are high deploy IT capabilities in a manner that does not capitalize on their complementarity. Congruent profiles of IT capabilities may be characterized by different degrees of complementarity between human and infrastructure capabilities – complementarity may be non-existent, it may exist among a subset of IT capabilities, or it may exist among all IT capabilities. Accordingly, three congruent profiles of IT capabilities can be defined. The first profile, designated ‘no complementarity’, characterizes organizations in which IT capabilities are all low. The second profile, designated ‘partial complementarity’, characterizes organizations in which part of the IT capabilities are high and they complement one another. For example, a firm in which only technical and physical capabilities are high has a profile of partial complementarity. The third profile, designated ‘full complementarity’, characterizes organizations in which IT capabilities are all high, and thus the complementarity among IT capabilities is brought to its full degree. These three profiles are considered congruent because they all reflect the complementarity among IT capabilities, that is, complementing IT capabilities are deployed or not deployed together.

The gestalt perception of strategic value as residing in integrative mechanisms of IT capability complementarity is rooted in previous research on the alignment (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993), co-specialization (Tippins & Sohi, 2003), and mutual reinforcement (Aral & Weill, 2007) between human and infrastructure capabilities. While the reductionistic models perceive ITCA as dependent on individual IT capabilities, the gestalt model perceives ITCA as dependent on the degree of complementarity in IT capability profiles. Because the three congruent profiles defined above represent increasing degrees of complementarity, the following gestalt hypothesis can be formulated:

Hypothesis 3: Full complementarity IT capability profiles have higher ITCA than partial complementarity IT capability profiles, which have higher ITCA than no complementarity IT capability profiles.

Profile-deviation model of IT strategic value The gestalt model aims at identifying congruent profiles of IT capabilities and their associated levels of ITCA. However, if particular profiles are considered to be congruent, then any deviation from these internally consistent, ideal profiles should result in a loss of performance. While Venkatraman (1989) makes a clear distinction between gestalt and profile-deviation perspectives, others consider them as part of a multivariate, systems perspective (Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985), in which the distance of a profile from the ideal-type profile, reflected in deviation measures, is negatively associated with organizational performance. This paper develops two separate models of IT strategic value based on gestalt and profile-deviation perspectives. The gestalt model focuses on a small number of congruent profiles, and the profile-deviation model focuses on the negative effects of deviations from these congruent profiles. The two models are founded on the same rationale of congruence. However, the gestalt model is based on a dichotomous view of congruence as either existent (congruent profiles) or non-existent (incongruent profiles), whereas the profile-deviation model is based on a continuous view of congruence, according to which incongruence represents a continuum. To demonstrate this difference, let us assume that a congruent profile is one in which technical and physical capabilities are both high, whereas the other three IT capabilities are low (i.e., a partial complementarity profile). Unless IT capabilities are changed along a path from one congruent profile to another, any change in IT capabilities would move the profile further away from the equilibrium created by the complementarity among IT capabilities. For instance, enhancing business capability in such a profile may have a negative effect on ITCA, unless this change is accompanied by enhancements in behavioral and managerial capabilities, which together would lead to a full complementarity profile. This rationale underlies the following profile-deviation hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4: The deviation of an IT capability profile from its respective congruent IT capability profile is negatively associated with ITCA.

## Methodology

A common methodology for testing reductionistic models of IT strategic value has been to collect data through a survey of IT managers, and to analyze the data with structural equation modeling (SEM) techniques (e.g., Byrd & Turner, 2001a; Tippins & Sohi, 2003; Bhatt & Grover, 2005; Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005). Survey-based data collection has also been commonly used to test holistic models (e.g., Miller & Friesen, 1982; Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985; Bensaou & Venkatraman, 1995; Delery & Doty, 1996; Bergeron et al., 2001; Lee et al., 2004; Ferratt et al., 2005). Because this methodology has been common in both reductionistic and holistic research, it was used in the current study to test the direct, mediation, gestalt, and profile-deviation models of IT strategic value.

## Instrument construction

A survey instrument was constructed to collect data from IT managers in Israel. A population of IT managers was preferred based on the assumption that they were likely to be the most informed about IT capabilities in their organizations (Sethi & King, 1994; Bassellier & Benbasat, 2004; Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005). Measures of technical, behavioral, and business capabilities (IT human capabilities) were based on the measures of Byrd & Turner (2001a), who operationalized the knowledge and skill domains outlined by Lee et al. (1995). Measures of physical and managerial capabilities (IT infrastructure capabilities) were developed on the basis of the framework of Weill et al. (2002). In line with the approach associating IT infrastructure capabilities with the extent of shared IT services (Broadbent et al., 1996; Broadbent et al., 1999), 10 items were constructed to assess the range of shared IT services in each of the 10 infrastructure clusters. Those items were a-priori classified as physical or managerial capability based on Weill et al. (2002). Measures of ITCA were based on measures developed by Byrd & Turner (2001b), who defined ITCA as consisting of four strategic mechanisms through which IT has the ability to enhance competitive advantage. Specifically, Byrd & Turner (2001b) argued that innovativeness, market position, mass customization, and difficulty to duplicate are the strategic mechanisms underlying ITCA. Questionnaire items – listed in Appendix A – used a seven-point scale anchored at the ends with ‘strongly agree’ and ‘strongly disagree’.

The initial instrument was pre-tested in nine semistructured interviews with IT managers and academics with the aim to assess the understandability, relevance, and completeness of the items. Next, the instrument was pilot tested in three Web-based surveys of three different convenience samples of IT managers. The decision to use the Web to administer the survey was based on considerations of accessibility, efficiency, and validity. In recent years, Web-based administration of surveys has gained popularity in academic research following the development of procedures to overcome methodological difficulties. Research comparing Web-based methods with other administration modalities confirmed the validity of the former methods (Stanton & Rogelberg, 2001). In total, 37 questionnaires were returned in the three pilot tests. The reliability of the scales was demonstrated with Cronbach’s a coefficients that ranged from 0.77 to 0.91.

## Data collection

To reach the target population, the most comprehensive database of IT professionals nationwide (managed by the primary IT community provider in Israel) was queried for IT professionals in management positions. The survey was designed to minimize data integrity concerns, such as non-sampled and multiple responding, by using personalized links sent via e-mail. It was also designed to reduce authenticity and confidentiality concerns by providing a means of communication with the research team. A total of 361 questionnaires were returned in one round. Estimating the response rate in Web-based surveys is not a straightforward matter because of the difficulty to ascertain the number of respondents actually contacted (Stanton & Rogelberg, 2001). Therefore, the number of recipients who clicked on the questionnaire page link (1311 recipients) was used as a proxy and yielded a response rate of 27.5%. Non-response bias was first assessed by comparing early and late responses (Armstrong & Overton, 1977). t-tests comparing early responses (lower quartile of response time) with late responses (upper quartile of response time) found no statistically significant mean differences. The possibility of non-response bias was also rejected by comparing the functional role distribution of respondents and non respondents $( \chi _ { 3 } ^ { 2 } = 3 . 8 0 , ~ p = 0 . 2 8 )$ . Of the 361 returned questionnaires, 68 were dropped for the following reasons: the respondents held non-management positions, evaluated small-sized organizations, were unfami liar with their organizations, or returned partially filled questionnaires. The final dataset of 293 respondents included chief information officers (15.9%), senior IT managers (26.2%), junior IT managers (24.1%), and IT professionals in other managerial positions (e.g., chief executive officers and chief technology officers) (33.8%). The main industries in the final dataset were telecommunications (16.7%), technological development (13.5%), financial services (7.1%), manufacturing (6.0%), health services (5.0%), defense (5.0%), and distribution and retail (4.7%). The possibility that the position of the respondent caused significant biases in the data was rejected by comparing the different levels of IT management for each construct with a multivariate general linear mode (GLM) procedure. The analysis found no statistically significant differences among IT management levels.

Finally, to ensure that organizational characteristics caused no significant bias in the data, additional analyses were performed on the characteristics of organizational size and reporting level of the top IT executive. Organizational size was measured by the number of employees, and the dataset included the following categories: 50 or fewer employees (10.4%), 51–250 employees (20.8%), 251–500 employees (13.2%), 501–1000 employees (13.5%), and over 1000 employees (42.0%). The dataset included top IT executives who reported to the chief executive officer (55.4%), and top IT executives who reported to others (44.6%). A multivariate GLM procedure showed that organizational size significantly affected physical capability (F ¼ 1.73, po0.05), as a consequence of significant differences in a single item (PC6). This procedure showed no other significant effects of organizational size or reporting level on any of the constructs, thus rejecting the possibility that these organizational characteristics significantly biased the data.

## Empirical analysis

## Assessment of the reductionistic models

The reductionistic models were tested using covariancebased SEM techniques with the EQS 6.1 software and the maximum likelihood estimation method. In accordance with the recommended two-step approach (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988; Hair et al., 2006), the measurement model was separately estimated and respecified prior to testing the direct and mediation structural models. The a priori measurement model was revised by iteratively dropping items that shared a high degree of residual variance with other items, based on reported standardized residuals. Standardized item loadings for the a priori and revised measurement models, as well as item descriptive statistics, are shown in Appendix A. The matrix of loadings and cross loadings for the revised measurement model is presented in Appendix B.

The six constructs in the revised measurement model were tested for construct reliability, unidimensionality, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. All composite construct reliabilities were above 0.80 and considerably above the recommended 0.70 threshold. Construct unidimensionality was established by separate confirmatory factor analyses that produced goodness-of-fit index (GFI) and comparative fit index (CFI) values above the recommended 0.90 threshold. Convergent validity was demonstrated by showing that all standardized item loadings were statistically significant (at the $p { < } 0 . 0 0 1$ level) and above the recommended 0.50 threshold (Hair et $a l . _ { i }$ , 2006). In fact, all but two standardized item loadings were above 0.70. Convergent validity was also demonstrated by showing that all average variance extracted (AVE) values were above the 0.50 threshold, indicating that the variance captured by the construct was larger than the variance due to measurement error (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Discriminant validity was assessed by comparing two nested models for each pair of constructs – an unconstrained model that freed the correlation between the two constructs and a constrained model that set the correlation between them to 1.0. The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value for the unconstrained model was significantly lower (at the ${ p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 }$ level) than the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value for the constrained model in all possible pairwise comparisons, providing evidence of discriminant validity (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988).

## Assessment of the holistic models

The first step in assessing the holistic models involved using a principal component analysis to transform the 22 IT capability items in the revised measurement model into five IT capability factors (technical, behavioral, business, physical, and managerial capabilities). Such a transformation is an important preliminary step in using cluster analysis to test holistic models, because the principal component analysis extracts orthogonal factors that overcome the problem of multicollinearity. Galbraith & Schendel (1983) argue that by using principal component analysis to transform the original data, clustering routines are performed in a metric space with orthogonal dimensions, which makes the use of distance analogies in cluster analysis conceptually sounder.

Common factor analysis might be more appropriate than principal component analysis for assessing the holistic models, given the reflective nature of constructs. However, comprehensive studies showed that the two factor extraction methods generally yielded very similar results (Hair et al., 2006), and that ‘the correlations between the different types of scores were typically close to unity’ (Velicer & Jackson, 1990, p. 5). To confirm that the factor scores in this study were not biased by the choice of extraction method, the analysis was repeated with the popular common factor analysis extraction method – principal axis factoring. The correlations between the factor scores produced by the two extraction methods were indeed very close to unity. and ranged from 0.955 to 0.993. Given these correlations, the principal component analysis method was preferred in order to follow as closely as possible the analytical approach used to test holistic models with cluster analysis (e.g., Galbraith & Schendel, 1983).

Assessment of the gestalt model Cluster analysis is the most popular statistical technique for identifying organizational profiles, because it allows the inclusion of multiple attributes as sources of profile definition and, therefore, can provide very rich descriptions of profiles without over-specifying the model (Ketchen & Shook, 1996). In this study, cluster analysis followed the recommended two-stage procedure in which a hierarchical clustering algorithm is used to determine the number of clusters and their centroids, which subsequently serve as the starting points for non-hierarchical clustering algorithms (Ketchen & Shook, 1996; Hair et al., 2006). The consensus among methodologists is that ‘the best solutions may be those obtained by using hierarchical and non-hierarchical methods in tandem’ (Ketchen & Shook, 1996, p. 446). A hierarchical cluster analysis with average linkage agglomerative method and squared Euclidean distance measure was performed using SPSS 15.0. The cluster variate (i.e., set of variables used to calculate the similarity between objects) was comprised of the five IT capability factor scores. The agglomeration coefficient – the most widely used method to determine the appropriate number of clusters (Ketchen & Shook, 1996) – was used as a ‘stopping rule’. Increases in the agglomeration coefficient were relatively small (below 0.63) until the stage at which the agglomerative process moved from four to three clusters (an increase of 1.31). This stopping rule suggested a four-cluster solution. Subsequently, a non-hierarchical (K-means) cluster analysis was performed to classify the responses into four clusters. The non-hierarchical procedure used the cluster centroids from the hierarchical four-cluster solution as the cluster seed points. The procedure used an iterative method with running means. Figure 3 shows the four cluster centroids that emerged from the non-hierarchical procedure and the average factor score for ITCA in each cluster.

As can be seen in Figure 3, the four clusters represented four distinct profiles of IT capabilities. Cluster 2 included organizations high on all five IT capabilities, defined previously as a full complementarity profile, and Cluster 3 included organizations low on all IT capabilities, defined previously as a no complementarity profile. Cluster 1 included organizations with high technical and physical capabilities and low behavioral, business, and managerial capabilities. Cluster 4, with a profile opposite to Cluster 1, included organizations with low technical and physical capabilities and high behavioral, business, and managerial capabilities. Clusters 1 and 4 were thus partial complementarity profiles. However, while the complementarity in Cluster 1 was between the technically oriented capabilities, the complementarity in Cluster 4 was between the business-oriented capabilities. The cluster analysis results supported Hypothesis 3, by showing that the full complementarity profile (Cluster 2) had higher ITCA than the partial complementarity profiles (Clusters 1 and 4), which had higher ITCA than the no complementarity profile (Cluster 3). A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test showed significant differences among clusters in ITCA $( \mathrm { F } = 2 1 . 6 2 , ~ p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Scheffe post hoc comparisons showed significant differences in ITCA (at the po0.01 level) between Cluster 2 (highest), Clusters 1 and 4 (intermediate), and Cluster 3 (lowest). The finding that Clusters 1 and 4 had similar levels of ITCA, despite their opposite profiles, confirmed the existence of equifinality in IT capability profiles.

Assessment of the profile-deviation model In a profiledeviation analysis, deviation is measured as a distance from a profile described as a point in a multidimensional space (Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985). The ideal profiles that serve as the starting point for the profile-deviation analysis can be derived either theoretically (e.g., Govindarajan, 1988; Delery & Doty, 1996) or empirically (e.g., Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985; Lee et al., 2004). In this study, following the method of Lee et al. (2004), the ideal profiles were the four congruent profiles emerging from the cluster analysis. The analysis involved two steps. First, a profiledeviation measure was calculated for each observation as the Euclidean distance between the IT capability profile (five IT capability values for the organization) and its respective congruent IT capability profile (five IT cap ability values for the cluster centroid). Next, following the standard procedure for assessing the performance impact of profile-deviation (Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985; Govindarajan, 1988; Venkatraman & Prescott, 1990), a Pearson correlation test was used to examine the relationship between profile-deviation and ITCA. The test showed a significant negative correlation between profiledeviation and ITCA (r ¼ -0.238, po0.001), thus providing support for Hypothesis 4.

## Discussion and conclusions

The analysis of the four models, summarized in Table 1, demonstrates the wealth of insights to be gained from using both reductionistic and holistic approaches in explaining the strategic value of IT capabilities. The direct model shows that a single IT human capability (business capability) and a single IT infrastructure capability (managerial capability) directly affect ITCA. This finding confirms previous conceptualizations that drew on the RBV to argue that managerial IT skills are the only IT resources capable of sustaining ITCA (Mata et al., 1995). This finding may suggest that other, more technical or physical IT capabilities have no strategic value. However. the mediation model shows that other IT capabilities do indeed have strategic value, which cannot be attributed to a direct resource-based mechanism, but rather to a process mechanism that assumes the existence of cause-and-effect relationships among IT capabilities and considers knowledge-based (human) capabilities as antecedents of service-based (infrastructure) capabilities. The mediation model shows that technical and behavioral capabilities affect ITCA through their effects on physical and managerial capabilities. When either the direct model or the mediation model is tested alone, the resulting conclusion may be that some IT capabilities are not strategically valuable. When the two reductionistic models are tested together, the resulting conclusion is that different structural mechanisms are responsible for the strategic value of different IT capabilities.

Similarly, the empirical analysis confirms that the profile-deviation model extends the gestalt model. The gestalt model shows that IT capability profiles cluster around congruent profiles with different degrees of complementarity among IT capabilities – no complementarity, complementarity among either technically oriented or business-oriented capabilities (partial complementarity), or complementarity among both technically oriented and business-oriented capabilities (full complementarity). These congruent profiles, in which complementing IT capabilities are deployed or not deployed together, empirically emerge as ideal profiles. However, the gestalt model, with its dichotomous view of congruence, does not address the consequences of the degree of incongruence. This is the context in which the profile-deviation model has explanatory value, because it shows that the deviation of a profile from its respective congruent profile (i.e., its degree of incongruence) is negatively associated with ITCA. The profile-deviation model extends the gestalt model because the gestalt model defines the locus of congruence in a five-dimensional space, and the profiledeviation model defines the consequences of the fivedimensional distance from congruence.

The integration of reductionistic and holistic findings is most interesting, especially because pervious research has largely treated them as representing two alternative explanations (Venkatraman & Prescott, 1990). Essentially, the value of such integration is attributed to the focus of reductionistic models on the manner in which individual IT capabilities are associated to one another and to ITCA, and the focus of holistic models on the manner in which the profile of IT capabilities is associated with ITCA. Using either reductionistic or holistic models may lead to a partial understanding of how IT capabilities create strategic value. Perhaps an example will best illustrate this point. Let us assume that an organization seeks to enhance ITCA by enhancing its IT capabilities. On which IT capability should the organization focus its efforts? The literature, which is largely reductionistic in nature, suggests that managerial capability should be the focus of attention in such a situation because of its significant effect on ITCA (e.g., Mata et al., 1995). However, such an approach pays no attention to the level of other IT capabilities. Is such an approach appropriate for an organization with a managerial capability that is high relative to other IT capabilities? The findings of this study suggest that the answer is negative. The value of the holistic approach lies in its ability to provide a holistic assessment of whether the profile of IT capabilities is congruent or incongruent. When the profile is congruent, any change in a single IT capability is strategically counter-productive, and multiple IT capabilities should be changed to maintain the complementarity within the profile. When the profile is incongruent, changes in IT capabilities can be strategically productive, as long as they move the profile closer to congruence. When there is a need to change IT capabilities, the reductionistic approach becomes more valuable than the holistic approach, given its ability to provide a reductionistic account of the unidirectional relationships among IT capabilities. The reductionistic approach provides an explicit prescription for changing IT capabilities. For instance, it shows that behavioral capability positively affects physical and managerial capabilities, and therefore one way to influence these IT capabilities is to change behavioral capability.

The study is not free of limitations. Primarily, limitations have been associated with the data analysis techniques used in this study to test the various models. In particular, cluster analysis techniques have been criticized for their extensive reliance on researcher judgment. This study does, however, follow the guidelines of Ketchen & Shook (1996) to minimize this problem and ensure the validity of insights provided by the clustering technique. Ketchen & Shook (1996) conclude their analysis by suggesting that ‘to maximize the development of knowledge, cluster analysis should be used in combination with other methods to test sophisticated theoretical models’ (p. 455). This study proposes such an approach by drawing on the combined strengths of holistic and reductionistic methods. It is likely that future research on the relationship between organizational attributes and performance will adopt such a combined approach to enhance the breadth and depth of causal explanations. Likewise, future research on the strategic value of IT may adopt such a combined approach to provide a more solid theoretical and empirical basis for the ongoing discussion on whether or not IT constitutes a source of competitive advantage.

Studies frequently test alternative models of fit in an organizational context (e.g., Drazin & Van de Ven, 1985; Delery & Doty, 1996; Lee et al., 2004). The alternative models are treated in many of these studies as providing alternative explanations of the same organizational phenomenon. In a similar vein, this study could focus on the contradictions between reductionistic and holistic findings. For example, the direct model shows that only business and managerial capabilities affect ITCA, whereas the gestalt model shows no difference in ITCA between a profile with high business and managerial capabilities and a profile with low business and managerial capabilities. Furthermore, the mediation model shows no effect of business capability on managerial capability, whereas the gestalt model clearly shows an association between these two capabilities. Despite the importance of such findings, this study aims at bridging the gap between reductionistic and holistic approaches.

An important merit of the holistic approach to IT strategic value lies in its ability to reconcile the seemingly contradictory findings that have emerged in previous studies. On the one hand, research has acknowledged the strategic value of technically oriented IT capabilities, such as IT infrastructure flexibility (Byrd & Turner, 2001a). On the other hand, research has also highlighted the strategic importance of business-oriented IT capabilities, such as managerial IT skills (Mata et al., 1995). Less effort has been put into understanding the strategic value of the interdependencies between technically oriented and business-oriented IT capabilities. This is where the holistic approach should come into play. The reductionistic approach seeks to attribute a causal role to each organizational attribute included in the research model. Conversely, the holistic approach seeks to attribute a causal role to the profile of attributes. This orientation increases the contribution of the holistic approach in situations in which the attribute profile is as consequential as the attributes themselves. The findings of this study show that this is the case for IT capabilities. The finding that completely opposite IT capability profiles are associated with the same level of ITCA supports such a conclusion. It matters as much that the profiles are congruent as it does that specific IT capabilities are high or low.

However, there are drawbacks to the holistic approach. The holistic focus on capability profiles draws attention away from the unidirectional relationships between capabilities. For instance, the gestalt model shows that complementarity exists among behavioral, business, and managerial capabilities. The results do not show, however, how these three capabilities are causally interrelated. This is where the reductionistic approach adds value. Reductionistic models are valuable because of their ability to examine whether behavioral and business capabilities (IT human capabilities) positively affect managerial capability (IT infrastructure capability). An important conclusion of this study is, therefore, that research should combine reductionistic and holistic approaches. Because of the substantial resources necessary for researchers to adopt both approaches, it is completely understandable that such a combined reductionisticholistic approach cannot become the common modus operandi. Combining these approaches may divert attention away from understanding the focal constructs under investigation. For this reason, a combined approach should be used to explore organizational phenomena that have already undergone considerable scrutiny. Such is the case for IT strategic value, which has been the subject of intensive research for the past two decades. This study shows that research in this area can be advanced by combining reductionistic and holistic explanations of IT strategic value.

Organizational profiles represent a holistic and integrated way to meaningfully capture the complexity of organizational reality (Bensaou & Venkatraman, 1995; Ketchen & Shook, 1996). Because of the complex, boundary-spanning, and integrated nature of contemporary IT, managers are no strangers to making holistic IT decisions. However, it seems that a holistic view guides managers’ decisions about tangible IT resources to a greater extent than it guides their decisions about intangible IT resources. It is apparently more natural for managers to include considerations of fit in decisions about IT components, where fit has a more tangible sense, than in decisions about IT capabilities. Therefore, a key practical implication of this study is that managers should seek to make holistic decisions about IT capabilities. They should understand their organization’s IT capability architecture the same way they understand their organization’s IT component architecture. They should think about how a particular IT capability would be integrated into the existing IT capability architecture the same way they think about how a particular technology or system would be integrated into the existing IT component architecture.
