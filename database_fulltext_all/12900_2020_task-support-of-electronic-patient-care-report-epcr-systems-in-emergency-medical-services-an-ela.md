# Task support of electronic patient care report (ePCR) systems in emergency medical services: An elaboration likelihood model lens

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Electronic patient care report Healthcare information technology Emergency medical services Elaboration likelihood model Absorptive capacity Information security

## A B S T R A C T

This paper aims to investigate the antecedents of task support in the context of electronic patient care report (ePCR) in the emergency medical service (EMS). Although electronic health record has generated signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interest, the area of EMS has been largely overlooked in the literature. This study examines characteristics related to the ePCR system—information quality, security protection, and normative cues—and their impact on EMS task support, through the lens of the elaboration likelihood model. We further propose a moderating role of absorptive capacity and top management belief as elaboration likelihoods. Several theoretical contributions and managerial implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

When a 911 medical emergency call is placed, one of the nation’s more than 20,000 emergency medical service (EMS) agencies responds to the call, performs <sup>fi</sup>rst-aid operations at the critical initiating stage (the so-called “golden time of patient treatment”), and subsequently hands o<sup>f</sup> the patient to a proper medical facility [1]. For many years, EMS operated on policies and procedures derived from old military rules and relied on <sup>fi</sup>eld personnel’s experience and best guesses, with handwritten documentation of patient care. Recently, concerns for patient outcome and reimbursement rate at national, local, and agency levels have called for an information- and data-driven EMS [2]. In addition to the formation of the National EMS Information System in 2000, main focus has been on the digitization of the patient care report (PCR), which not only records <sup>fi</sup>eld data, but is also critical at the time of transfer of care to the emergency department.

The PCR includes an objective description of the patient's physical conditions (e.g., mechanism of injury, chief complaint, treatment, etc.) in su<sup>fi</sup>cient detail to demonstrate the patient s functional status at the time of transport. When an electronic patient care report (ePCR) system is used, this job is not only recorded digitally but also performed through a series of drop-down lists and checkboxes on handheld devices by paramedics. Although some point out that these <sup>fi</sup>xed-data format of ePCR may limit the narrative aspect of traditional PCR [3], ePCR systems have the potential to improve EMS record availability and legibility for emergency department clinicians, as well as to improve quality assurance, outcomes research, and billing for EMS agencies [4].

Moreover, by integrating with other health system components such as electronic health records (EHR), ePCR of EMS is expected to improve healthcare for the entire community. As a result, the need for deploying ePCR systems by EMS agencies is widely recognized [5].

Despite the potential of ePCR, its use has been slow, especially compared to the level of EHR use [6]. A review of the literature also con<sup>fi</sup>rms that although the stream of literature in EHR and other information technologies (ITs) used in healthcare has been expansive and growing, there is a dearth of studies that examines ePCR in the area of EMS in general. Such a big gap between the two could be attributed to the fact that while hospital and ambulatory services’ EHR implementation and use is mandated by the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act of the American Recovery and Reconstruction Act (ARRA) with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant incentive funding, the decision to use ePCR in EMS agencies is largely made by senior managers such as the chief of an individual EMS agency on a voluntary basis. In addition, several key factors of an ePCR system, such as information quality and security concerns, still remain to be addressed and resolved [4,5,7]. Perhaps due to these concerns, it was reported that, among those agencies using ePCR, the majority do not exchange data with any other providers, and the use is limited to report generation only [8].

Against this backdrop, this study aims to examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of ePCR characteristics and EMS agencies’ information processing in using ePCR on EMS task support. A review of EHR literature reveals that researchers have explored important variables of EHR use such as individual attributes (e.g., [9–11]), organizational factors (e.g. [12,13],), and environmental factors (e.g. [14,15],). Interestingly, some researchers notice the importance of information processing in EHR use; for instance, it is found that whether the system can result in expected outcomes depends on decision makers’ conditional cognition toward the target [16]. Unlike EHR as mandate use in hospitals, ePCR use in EMS organizations is largely driven by the senior management’s cognitive process about ePCR toward EMS task support and surrounding factors around the individual EMS organization. Because an EMS organization’s ePCR use for task support is most likely to follow and mimic the information processing by the senior management, we employ an elaboration likelihood model (ELM) as the main theoretical lens to capture the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a key individual’s cognition on ePCR’s task support. A senior manager’s cognitive process for this matter may re-<sup>fl</sup>ect an individual information processing but, more importantly, represent an organization’s interests and take organizational factors into account. Thus, the senior manager representing an EMS organization would evaluate a system’s e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness in supporting key tasks through organizational deliberations based on its own merits (i.e., central route) or with cue-based impressions $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ peripheral route). In this sense, it would be natural to expect that the senior manager’s information processing as to how ePCR should be utilized for EMS tasks may hinge upon key organizational characteristics of individual organizations such as absorptive capacity and top management belief for better task sup port, which may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the strength or direction of the relationship between ELM variables and ePCR task support.

This current study addresses the following research questions: (1) What central and peripheral routes in ePCR in<sup>fl</sup>uence task support in an EMS organization? (2) Do organizational characters such as absorptive capacity and top management belief determine the routes as elaboration likelihood in the case of ePCR? To answer these questions, we conduct a study using survey data collected from EMS agencies in the U.S., and, through the noble framework of ELM, we identify central and peripheral factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence task support in the ePCR context. The results of the study o<sup>f</sup>er important contributions to the current litera ture on EMS and health information technology in general.

This paper is organized as follows. A literature review on the guiding theories is presented in the next section, followed by the research model and hypotheses. Next, we describe the data collection and research methodology used in this study, and the results and analysis are discussed. The paper concludes with the theoretical and practical implications of the study, as well as limitations and future directions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Elaboration likelihood model

ELM describes how information is processed to result in recipients changes in decision-making, attitude, or behaviors $[ 1 7 , 1 8 ]$ , Previous literature on ELM has illustrated that such changes are made based on two di<sup>f</sup>erent information processing routes, which require either the subject’s cognitive endeavors or external cues [19]. That is, the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context the information recipients encounter would push them to pursue a certain information processing path to interpret the given information. For example, one can analyze the given information in detail with cognitive e<sup>f</sup>orts to make judgments. Under other circumstances, one may simply trust or accept the information if its source has enough credibility [20]. The key contextual factor that determines the routes of information processing is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as elaboration likelihood and has been examined as a critical factor in the success of persuasion [21].

In the ELM literature, central route and peripheral route indicate those two di<sup>f</sup>erent types of information processing [22]. The central route is related to objective analysis and assessment on the accuracy, completeness, and relevancy of the information or issues, as well as the validity of reasoning in the argument to form the judgment [18]. It requires internal processing of information with much cognitive endeavor. Similarly, the central route is involved when an organization is learning to adopt a new system or routine through such processes as intuiting, interpreting, and institutionalizing [23–25]. According to the literature, the judgments formed through the central route tend to be persistent, consistent, and predictive, because the decisions are made through the objective assessment of information and logical reasoning [17].

Unlike the central route, the peripheral route involves checking the cues that surround the information. According to the literature, it entails less cognitive endeavors, because the cues are related to the tone of the argument, the source of the information, general impression, or other surface-level characteristics rather than the actual strength of the contents [20]. For instance, in the organizational context, reputations of the providers, well-known endorsements, or third-party certi<sup>fi</sup>cates can be in<sup>fl</sup>uential as critical external cues in an organization’s decisionmaking [26]. Individuals or organizations tend to make judgments by using those cues when the detailed evaluation is costly and time-con suming, necessary resources are not ready, or they cannot manage the information processing directly [22]. Previous studies demonstrate that nonexperts, when reading health information online, are more likely to pay attention to the credibility of the source, but less a<sup>f</sup>ected by the objective information quality provided [27]. In another example, it is shown that mimetic and normative in<sup>fl</sup>uences strengthen the assimilation of enterprise resource planning systems by an organization [28]. Interestingly, studies illustrate that judgments formed by peripheral cues tend to be less consistent and easier to sway [22].

In ELM, recipients are under various underlying conditions in terms of capability, belief, availability, and preoccupation when elaborating information. These variations play an important role in determining the kind of information processing route an individual or organization takes. Previous studies on ELM demonstrate that information recipients rely more on the central route of information processing when elaboration likelihood is perceived to be high, and the peripheral route is often taken when elaboration likelihood seems to be low [20] For example, knowing the importance of accuracy and consistency of in formation regarding patient care details when handing o<sup>f</sup> the patients to the emergency service at the hospital, the EMS senior manager would likely take a central route and review the details of the documentation function of ePCR. On the other hand, if an EMS senior manager is not required to use ePCR, he or she may approach this decision through a peripheral route and consult the ePCR use by the peers or leading agencies. As such, we view that there are di<sup>f</sup>erent ways of processing ePCR-related information $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ either central route or peripheral route) depending on di<sup>f</sup>erential attitudes of EMS organizations and its impacts on the EMS task support, and the current study aims to explain that with ELM. While ELM emanates from a dual process theory describing an individual’s change of attitudes, this study employs it to understand an EMS organization’s persuasion of ePCR use and its task support using the senior manager as a proxy of an organization. Senior managers of EMS organizations are chosen as "key informants" [29] because they are known to possess special quali<sup>fi</sup>cations such as status, experience. or specialized knowledge that enable them to provide in formation on organizational properties rather than just personal attitudes and perception. In addition, an individual agency evaluates ePCR’s e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness in supporting key tasks and the possibility of adoption through an organizational learning process [25]. As argued by Vera and Crossan [107], strategic leadership, shaped by the senior management s beliefs and deliberations, has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on an organization's change in thoughts and actions. As such. we argue that organizational deliberations of using ePCR for EMS task support can be examined through the senior manager's cognitive process. which hinge upon key organizational characteristics of the individual agency. Thus, we theorize two organizational factors absorptive capacity and top management belief – as elaboration likelihoods when information is processed by a senior manager representing the best interest of his/her EMS organization. This modeling approach is consistent with the ecological literature [30] that suggests to mitigate a possible ecological fallacy by conceptualizing cross-level studies with variables not measurable on the individual level. Advancing this literature, we in corporated two organizational factors in the ELM as the senior manager’s cognitive process is used as a proxy of an organization’s information processing with ePCR use. Both organizational factors are readily available for senior managers, and thus may change the routes of persuasion and the strength of the relationship between ELM variables and ePCR task support. These two factors are further discussed below.

## 2.2. Absorptive capacity and information systems

Cohen and Levinthal, in their seminal work, describe absorptive capacity as the ability of an organization to “recognize the value of new, external information, assimilate it, and apply it” to improve performance [31]. They argue that an organization’s members’ cognitive and learning capabilities, which are built on memory development from related knowledge and learning skills, directly contribute to the ab sorptive capacity of the organization. Absorptive capacity is often associated with a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s investment in research and development, which focuses on generating and exploiting new knowledge for innovation, as well as other factors such as complimentary external knowledge sources (e.g., a strategic partnership), past experience, and in-house knowledge base and processes [31–33]. As such, an organization’s absorptive ca pacity is not only the ability to acquire and assimilate external knowledge, but also the capability to process and integrate such knowledge internally [34]. There are four dimensions of absorptive capacity—acquisition, assimilation, transformation, and exploitation—that give organizations the basis for developing di<sup>f</sup>erent types of competitive advantage [33].

This concept has been utilized in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts to examine such key business activities as technology sourcing [34], product development [35], and supply chain partnership [36]. In particular, a number of studies has examined the potential relevance of absorptive capacity to a variety of information systems research issues at the organizational or interorganizational level. To start with, business-IT knowledge, a subset of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s absorptive capacity, impacts performance and IT effectiveness [37,38]. Studies also <sup>fi</sup>nd that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s absorptive capacity positively a<sup>f</sup>ects the assimilation of complex IT through lowering knowledge barriers and better knowledge acquisition [28,39]. Moreover, research has found that absorptive capacity, when conceptualized as a capability, compliments the use of IT in generating business value. Pavlou and El Sawy [40] <sup>fi</sup>nd that in the development of new products, the ability to e<sup>f</sup>ectively use IT functionalities enhances the absorptive capacity, which in turn impacts a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s competitive advantage. Deng et al. [32] demonstrate that in the context of engineering work, organizational member’s absorptive capacity enhances the impact of IT on task productivity and the e<sup>f</sup>ective use of IT for problem solving and decision support.

In this study, we adopt the original de<sup>fi</sup>nition of absorptive capacity as “the ability to value, assimilate, and apply new knowledge” [31,33] and focus on an EMS organization’s technological capability when implementing and assimilating ePCR in performing EMS tasks. As such, absorptive capacity is an important factor for di<sup>f</sup>erentiating an EMS organization’s ability to use ePCR for task support and likely an ela boration likelihood in an EMS organization’s information processing related to ePCR.

## 2.3. Top management beliefs

Top Management belief, generally conceptualized as the commit ment, involvement, or championship by the top management in the information systems (IS) literature, is considered a precious organiza tional driver [41] and has been found to be an important variable in a variety of contexts such as IS development and implementation (e.g.

[42,43],), adoption of innovations (e.g. [44],), IS usage (e.g., [105]), IS outsourcing success (e.g. [45],), strategic alliances in the IT industry (e.g. [46],), and quality-oriented organizational systems (e.g. [47],). According to the attention-based view of the <sup>fi</sup>rm [48], what decision makers do depends on the issues and answers they focus their attention on, and top management belief in IS <sup>fl</sup>ows from a number of sources and structures, both internal and external to the <sup>fi</sup>rm. For instance, connectedness of EMS chiefs to external information sources and knowledge communities with respect to ePCR enables them to become more aware of new "organizing visions" being developed and promoted in the community (cf. [49],). Through these external connections, top management can also gain much better intelligence about the state of the ePCR marketplace and the pace of technological changes.

Top management belief is likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence an organization's elaboration likelihood during the information processing of the use of ePCR in an EMS agency. Because an early outright decision on either the use or rejection of ePCR is premature and undesirable, the ultimate decision on ePCR by an EMS organization can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a variety of factors, including the type of operations performed, availability of functions, patient information protection, information from other organizations, and so on. When the balance among various factors is dynamically adjusted in light of changing conditions, the senior manager’s inclinations regarding the preferred focus and attention that a<sup>f</sup>ect the use of ePCR are a most precious organizational in<sup>fl</sup>uence (cf., [50]). As such, top management belief can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact an EMS organization's use of ePCR.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

This study examines the characteristics of ePCR as antecedents to EMS task support. From the utilitarian perspective, information systems are designed with the intention of helping users perform their tasks [51]. This is true in the healthcare industry, and even more so in its subset of <sup>fi</sup>eld service-oriented EMS, where information systems are implemented mainly to support organizations’ tasks. Information sys tems have had an impact on an organization's ability to perform tasks as required by business processes, and in that sense the task support is concerned with how information systems help or hinder innovation, productivity, and problem solving related to business tasks [32,52]. Previous studies have attempted to measure successful task support of information systems by examining decision e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness [53], decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence [54], e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of task completion [55], and timely task completion [56], etc. Following Deng et al. [32], we conceptualize ePCR task support as an aggregated use of the system in supporting decision-making and problem solving when providing care in the <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Following the lead of the literature, we identify several major constructs that may a<sup>f</sup>ect ePCR task support. First, we choose information quality of ePCR as the central route, because the main role of ePCR is to store, process, and provide the accurate information for enhancing job e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. Information quality of ePCR thus may drive the paramedics’ cognitive processing to objectively analyze and assess the accuracy, completeness, and relevancy of the information in <sup>fi</sup>eld operation and during handing o<sup>f</sup> patients to emergency rooms. We argue that when the paramedic information provided through ePCR is vital and perceived to be working with other systems such as EHR and the practices in the ER, the paramedics in an EMS organization perceive good task support. Second, security protection and normative cues – positive views of ePCR provided by external organizations or institutions – are adopted as peripheral route variables. In other words, the perception that ePCR has appropriate security measures and cue-based information from other EMSs or reliable sources that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the use of ePCR for task support. Lastly, we adopt absorptive capacity and top management belief as the elaboration likelihood of ePCR in the EMS community. We posit that di<sup>f</sup>erential levels of these factors across EMS organizations would in<sup>fl</sup>uence the processing of central cue and peripheral cues on task support di<sup>f</sup>erently. ## 3.1. Information quality and ePCR task support

Information quality refers to the accuracy, completeness, and timeliness of the given information. Information quality is perceived high when the contained messages are justi<sup>fi</sup>ed and compelling [57]. It has been argued that information quality is one of the most important factors for improving patient safety and outcomes [58], and it is critical that ePCR provides EMS personnel with timely and accurate information on patients’ age, gender, chief complaint, and medical history [7]. Furthermore, the quality information of ePCR enhances the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of overall work <sup>fl</sup>ow of an EMS organization by improving legibility and billing, preventing lost charts, and reducing data errors [4]. DesRoches et al. [59] illustrated that high quality content in healthcare informa tion technology is the main driver that leads to improved clinical decision and avoidance of medication errors in ambulatory care. In addition, the potential of ePCR systems to support quality assurance processes can be the primary motivation for ePCR use, and these processes are strongly related to information quality. ePCR would not provide satisfactory task support to an EMS organization, and its <sup>fi</sup>eld operators in particular, if the information processed by ePCR is not relevant, su<sup>fi</sup>cient, accurate, or compatible with the hando<sup>f</sup> process [7]. Therefore, when the information quality is high, the ePCR can provide better and more e<sup>f</sup>ective task support to an EMS organization. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1. Information quality is positively related to ePCR task support.

## 3.2. Security protection and ePCR task support

In this study, security protection refers to the extent to which an organization evaluates that the ePCR systems of the organization are properly protected by relevant safeguard technologies and security measures. In promoting ePCR products, safeguard technologies play an important role [4]. For example, ePCR vendors emphasize the security of their ePCR product by illustrating the cloud server where their ePCR systems are working on, or highlighting the strength of encryption algorithms and access control used in the package [60]. In addition, from the organizational user perspective, having relevant security measures, including security policies, can be considered as critical components of security protection of ePCR [61].

In addition, previous studies have indicated that keeping patient data secure and private is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant concern for healthcare providers [62]. In 2016, more than 25 million patient records were reportedly compromised in the U.S. [63]. The industry is also witnessing increasing breaches due to hacking and malware as well as more sinister criminal elements when breaches happen [64,65]. Although the Security Rule from the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, or HIPAA, requires that healthcare providers set up physical, ad ministrative, and technical safeguards to protect electronic health in formation, security risks still threaten the industry and patients [66].

The role of information security in healthcare information tech nology has been examined repeatedly due to the increasing threats and the sensitive nature of medical information [67,68]. Prior studies pay attention to the negative impact of security concerns on information systems use. For example, Tang et al. [69] point out that the security concern is one of main barriers to information systems use in health care. A security breach incident can result in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>nancial and legal burdens to healthcare organizations because of the potential liability from data loss as well as impact on productivity [70]. Considering the potential impact of security breaches, EMS organizations are likely to pay attention to security protections when implementing ePCR. Xu et al. [71] illustrated how perceived e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of information assurance alleviate security concern. Based on the arguments above, an EMS provider is likely to be more comfortable in using ePCR to support its tasks when the system has good security protection. Thus, we posit:

H2. Security protection is positively related to ePCR task support.

## 3.3. Normative cues and ePCR task support

The ELM literature has consistently identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the source of given information as the important referenced cues. The more credible and in<sup>fl</sup>uential the source of persuasion is, the stronger impact is made on a recipient’s decision-making [57]. Information from a credible and in-<sup>fl</sup>uential source can sway decision makers to simply trust and follow the information by diverting attention from the cognitive process [72]. Conversely, extant studies in the ELM literature have noted that decision makers respond more negatively to low-reputation sources than to ones with good reputations [73]. In practice, reports by or practices of government organizations or reputable institutions have more credibility and often lead to e<sup>f</sup>ective persuasions [16].

In this study, normative cues refer to the indicators or information, which an EMS agency receives, that ePCR is used and/or recommended by credible and in<sup>fl</sup>uential sources such as governmental organizations, well-known institutions, or leading EMS organizations. Such normative cues have been an important source of IS use and assimilation: in the case of enterprise resource planning, for instance, top management tends to be guided by “peer pressure” or “best practices” demonstrated by leading companies and industry consultancy [28,74]. Moreover, normative cues might be even more important in the case of ePCR. Few EMS organizations have the time or expertise to carefully evaluate every aspect of ePCR features to suit their own functionality needs [75]. Therefore, the observation or communications that in<sup>fl</sup>uential agencies or institutions recommend the use of ePCR, can guide EMS organizations to think that their own tasks will be supported well [76]. Furthermore, the perceived prevalence of use in the EMS industry and the perceived success of leading organizations help to manifest the use of ePCR [77], and EMS organizations will more likely be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with ePCR task support if they observe successful use of it among their peers and partners [75]. Based on these arguments, when the normative cues about ePCRs use are received by an EMS organization, there is a high likelihood that it will achieve satisfaction from its use of ePCR in supporting the tasks. Therefore, we posit:

H3. Normative is positively related to ePCR task support.

## 3.4. Absorptive capacity as elaboration likelihood

In the theory of ELM, the main factor that drives individuals to select between the central route and the peripheral route for information processing is the elaboration likelihood. In prior literature, selfe<sup>fi</sup>cacy, which is often de<sup>fi</sup>ned as one's belief in one's own ability to perform or succeed in speci<sup>fi</sup>c situations or to accomplish a task [78], has been adopted as a key elaboration likelihood for individual deci sion-making [26]. In a similar fashion, we posit that absorptive capacity, which is an organization’s capability to assimilate and apply new knowledge to achieve desirable performance [33,79], moderates the e<sup>f</sup>ect of information quality (the central cue) as well as that of security protection and normative cues (the peripheral cues) on ePCR task support, and is thus an elaboration likelihood for this study. In the context of this study, an EMS organization’s absorptive capacity depends on its members’ cognitive capability to absorb and use new knowledge and information [79], and is thus directly related to the EMS personnel’s perception that they have the knowledge and skills to use ePCR e<sup>f</sup>ectively. As EMS personnel’s self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy toward ePCR exhibits the con<sup>fi</sup>dence in one s own ability to exert control over individual performance and behavior to attain expected outcome [78], an EMS organization with high absorptive capacity will have collectively positive expectations toward task support of ePCR [31]. As such, absorptive capacity impacts how an organization processes information, a<sup>f</sup>ecting the level of ePCR task support when taking di<sup>f</sup>erent routes.

Absorptive capacity, primarily a function of an organization’s prior related knowledge, allows it to use new knowledge to meet its goals [33]. As such, an EMS organization with higher absorptive capacity is more likely to be able to process and utilize the ePCR’s quality information to achieve better task support. Further, similar to self-e<sup>fi</sup>- cacy helping individuals focus on relevant message processing [22], absorptive capacity enhances EMS organizations’ ability to process and assess information concerning the quality of ePCR. On the other hand, when an organization’s competency and skills are limited due to low absorptive capacity, its personnel would hesitate to evaluate and fully commit to ePCR on its own merit, because they have insu<sup>fi</sup>cient knowledge or capability to do so [22]. Thus, we posit that absorptive capacity positively impacts the relationship between information quality and ePCR task support.

H4a. Absorptive Capacity positively moderates the relationship between information quality and ePCR task support.

Concerns for security and privacy are prevalent to EMS personnel in healthcare industry [66]. However, even though it is an important issue, security itself is not the <sup>fi</sup>nal goal of the system. Rather, as the literature points out, the concern for security is often an impediment to regular tasks for employees and likely to bring stress to EMS personnel [80]. Because of its high technical barrier, not every EMS member can assess the genuine status of information assurance of ePCR [7]; issues such as whether the network protocols that ePCR adopts are secure, whether the software package has vulnerability, or if the devices contain malware cannot be easily judged by organizations with little prior knowledge or low technology capabilities [81,82]. Instead, they are likely to lean on credible organizations or external experts for such assurances [83], choosing to depend on a peripheral route, rather than a central route, in forming the attitude toward the ePCR. The perception that ePCR has high level of security protection can be a cue to encourage EMS organizations with low absorptive capacity to engage in the use of the system. We therefore posit:

H4b. Absorptive Capacity negatively moderates the relationship between security protection and ePCR task support.

With inadequate ability to assess the information quality of ePCR themselves, organizations with limited prior knowledge and low level of technology capabilities are likely to depend on information from reputable sources to achieve task support goals with ePCR. Further, individuals in organizations with low technology capabilities are likely to be distracted by issues that do not require cognitive skills [84], and without the competency to make judgments themselves, they are more likely to rely on the reputation of the source than on the quality of the message [73]. While EMS organizations with high absorptive capacity can expect to achieve ePCR task support goals by taking advantage of the quality of information processing that ePCR provides, those not capable of assessing the system on its own merit may look to follow the footsteps of industry leaders, credible institutions, or other authorities. Therefore, such normative cues are likely to change the attitudes toward ePCR task support satisfaction when an EMS organization has limited absorptive capacity. In other words, normative cues have a greater impact on ePCR task support experienced by EMS agencies with low absorptive capacity than by those with high absorptive capacity. Therefore, we posit:

H4c. Absorptive Capacity negatively moderates the relationship between normative cues and ePCR task support.

## 3.5. Top management belief as elaboration likelihood

In the ELM literature, involvement, which is often de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a continuum of personal relevance or a willingness to expend one’s e<sup>f</sup>ort toward some particular consumer activity, has been used as a key ela boration likelihood for individual decision-making [17,26]. Similarly, we argue that top management belief, which is an organization’s commitment, involvement, or championship to attain desirable goals [41], works as an elaboration likelihood, and thus moderates the e<sup>f</sup>ect of information quality (the central cue), security protection, and normative cues (the peripheral cues) on ePCR task support. Top management belief depends on senior managers’ positive engagement, vision, and attention [79], and is thus directly related to resource allocation and support. Therefore, an EMS organization with higher top management belief in the use of ePCR is likely to be able to better process and evaluate information about ePCR task support.

Similar to high-involvement helping individuals focus on relevan message processing [22], top management belief helps EMS organizations to process and assess information about the quality of ePCR. On the other hand, a lack of top management belief would likely lead to insu<sup>fi</sup>cient resource or support for the use of ePCR, and, as a result, the EMS organization would hesitate to evaluate and fully commit itself to ePCR on its own merit. Thus, we posit that top management belief positively impacts the relationship between information quality and ePCR task support.

H5a. Top management belief positively moderates the relationship between information quality and ePCR task support.

Security protection is one of main concerns coming from using the digitalized reporting systems in the area of EMS [5]. Although not every EMS organization can evaluate the quality of security by itself [7], when the top management of the organization has a strong belief in the ePCR, security concerns might not impact the use of ePCR for task support. However, as we discussed above, when the top management belief is weak, an EMS agency is more likely to seek out credible sources or external experts to alleviate security concerns [83], even though security itself is not the ultimate goal of the system. In other words, the organization’s perception that ePCR is well protected by appropriate security measures can be an important cue to drive EMS organizations with low top management belief to engage in the use of the system. We, therefore, posit:

H5b. Top management belief negatively moderates the relationship between security protection and ePCR task support.

With inadequate support from top management regarding ePCR use, it is likely that organizations depend on cues from reputable or relevant sources to form the perception for ePCR task support. In other words, while EMS organizations with high top management belief can expect to achieve task support goals based on the merits of ePCR use, those without top management belief may look for external cues from industry leaders, credible institutions, or other authorities. That is, normative cues have a more important impact on ePCR task support experienced by EMS agencies with low top management belief. Therefore, we posit:

H5c. Top management belief negatively moderates the relationship between normative cues and ePCR task support.

## 4. Methods

## 4.1. Data collection

To test the research model of ePCR task support in EMS agencies, the current study utilizes a "key informants" methodology for data collection, a method that relies on a selected set of members to provide information about a special setting [85]. This methodology has been widely adopted by empirical information systems studies (e.g. $[ 8 6 , 8 7 ] , )$ . In this method, informants are not chosen at random; rather, they are selected because they possess special quali<sup>fi</sup>cations such as status, experience, or specialized knowledge. For our survey-based study, targeted respondents assume the role of a key informant and provide information on a particular unit of analysis (an organizational implementation of ePCR in this study) by reporting on divisional or organizational properties rather than personal attitudes and perception.

We administered an online survey through the National EMS Management Association (NEMSMA). NEMSMA is a professional association of EMS systems with nearly 1500 members representing CEOs, chiefs, and managers from every size and type of service, regardless of the EMS system model, organizational structure, or agency a<sup>fi</sup>liation. Along with it, our sampling frame consists of a list of EMS organizations in the U.S., and the target respondent at each EMS organization was its chief or equivalent administrator. The members of NEMSMA were asked to identify the ePCR systems that were already implemented at their facility at the time of the survey. Of the members targeted, 171 usable responses were obtained with a response rate of 11.4 %. Table 1 summarizes the demographics of the 171 responses, showing that 39.2 % were EMS o<sup>fi</sup>cers (including chief, assistant chief, deputy chief, and battalion chief), 42.1 % were administrators (including director, manager, and coordinator), and 14 % were <sup>fi</sup>eld caregivers (including captain and paramedic).

We <sup>fi</sup>rst checked for respondent bias to ensure that the respondents did not systematically di<sup>f</sup>er from the nonrespondents. Based on the guidelines suggested by Babbie [88], the respondents and nonrespondents were compared with respect to two important organizational features: the total amount of budget and the number of FTEs. For this test, we randomly selected 50 responses from the nonrespondents in the membership and the respondent EMS organizations. The results of the t-tests of the group means revealed no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences at the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance level of 0.05 (cf. [89],). Although some preliminary steps were taken to ensure appropriate selection of key informants, a formal check was administered as part of the questionnaire [29]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, two items regarding key informant quality were used to assess the informant’s knowledge about the ePCR and his or her involvement with ePCR implementation, on a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point scale. The mean score for informant quality for each item was 4.2 and 4.3, respectively, indicating that respondents were appropriate. As a result, all responses were retained. Furthermore, 91 % of the respondents had been using ePCR for more than one year, and the respondents’ EMS experience was 24.2 years on average, well representing the EMS community.

## 4.2. Operationalization of constructs

Multiple items are used to measure all latent constructs in this study. (Refer to Appendix A for the speci<sup>fi</sup>c items used.) We adapt ex tant measures from prior literature to <sup>fi</sup>t to the ePCR context using the <sup>fi</sup>ve-point Likert scale.

To capture an EMS organization’s intended use of ePCR in their corresponding work environments, we conceptualize the dependent variable, task support, as an aggregated use of ePCR in decision support, namely decision rationalization and problem solving. Four items are adapted from Deng et al. [32], to capture the extent to which ePCR is used to improve the decision-making process or enhance the problem-solving capabilities. Absorptive capacity is measured with four items adapted from Liang et al. [28]. They assess the extent of users’ knowledge bases and reasoning mechanisms as well as the organization’s readiness for assimilating ePCR systems, with particular attention paid to the context of postimplementation, where technical support and training are essential. Information quality is measured using three items adapted from Wixom and Watson [90] to capture the quality of in formation provided through ePCR. Top management belief assesses the extent to which top management considers that using ePCR can po tentially bene<sup>fi</sup>t the organization. Three items are adapted from Liang et al. [28]. Security protection in this study captures the perception of ePCR systems in protecting the security of patient information associated with ePCR use. Three items are adapted from Kim and Ahn [91] to assess the extent to which an organization s ePCR system provide secure methods for dealing with the patient information. Three items are adapted from Liang et al. [28] to measure normative cues, which capture the perceived extent to which ePCR is generally promoted by those EMS agencies that have adopted ePCR and/or the government and industry institutions. We emphasize the importance of industry network primarily because the top management of the EMS organization and government agencies have played a critical role in cultivating the norm of ePCR use and assimilation [92].

Two variables etotal budget and annual call volume eare used in our model to control the dependent variable, as both contextual variables could also in<sup>fl</sup>uence the task support of ePCR. Respondents were asked to report the organization’s total budget in millions of dollars. Annual call volume is captured through the number of calls the respondent’s organization received per thousand population per month, as shown in Appendix A.

## 5. Results

This study deploys partial least squares (PLS) to analyze the data, because this method has main strengths in following areas. First, PLS is well known for its capability in analyzing small size samples [93]. As organization-level data are utilized, only 171 samples are available to empirically test the hypotheses. Secondly, PLS is capable of predicting dependent variables, while other covariance-based methods pursue the goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t [94]. As noted, the absorptive capacity and top management belief involve exploratory relationships, and we are establishing the new theoretical ground. Therefore, this study pays more attention in predicting the variance of the dependent variable instead of testing the <sup>fi</sup>tness between observed correlations and model parameters. Therefore, PLS is deemed a proper method for this study.

## 5.2. Assessment of common method variance

Prior to discussing the results of the structural model, it is important to note that the study design dealing with self-reported data collected from the same source at one time raises a legitimate concern of common method bias, because correlations among them may be systematically contaminated by any defect in that source (e.g., [98]). However, reliance on key informants might be the only realistic, feasible way to get the ePCR information in this study, and the practical utility of samesource self-report measures made it virtually inevitable in the present study context [99]. In addition, a statistical viewpoint indicates that the common source data problem might not be as damaging if the data are used to test interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects [100]. Thus, although we cannot totally rule out concerns of common method bias, the results should be con sidered in light of these mitigating in<sup>fl</sup>uences as well as the practical di<sup>fi</sup>culties involved in obtaining this type of data from multiple methods.

To ensure that common method variance is not overly in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the results, we assess its potential impact with two additional statistical analyses: (a) Harman’s one-factor test [101] and (b) Lindell and Whitney’s [102] marker variable test. In Harman’s one-factor test, the emergence of a single factor that accounts for a large proportion of the variance in factor analysis suggests a common methods bias [98]. No such single factor emerges, and the <sup>fi</sup>rst factor accounts for only 30.74 % of the total 74.38 % variance. Second, the Lindell and Whitney [102] marker variable test uses a theoretically unrelated marker variable to adjust the correlations among the model’s principal constructs. We use a single item variable, ownership status, for which there exists little theoretical basis for a relationship with our research variables. The average correlation of the study’s principal constructs with it $( r \ =$ 0.076) is low and insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. We found that both the nature and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the results remained unchanged even with the introduction of the marker variable, providing no evidence of common methods bias. Taken together, we conclude that common method bias is not a serious threat in this study.

## 6. Discussion and implications

In this study, we set out to examine how an EMS organization processes the relevant information when using ePCR to support emergency medical tasks. We <sup>fi</sup>rst propose ePCR’s information quality, security protection, and normative cues as both central and peripheral routes to ePCR task support. Through the lens of ELM, we identify absorptive capacity and top management belief as the elaboration likelihoods. We posit that absorptive capacity and top management belief positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the central route and negatively impact the peripheral route. Using the data collected from members of National EMS Management Association, we examine the dual role of absorptive capacity and top management belief: absorptive capacity, on one hand, moderates the relationship between information quality and task support in a positive way and, on the other hand, in<sup>fl</sup>uences the relationships between task support and security protection and between task support and normative cues in a negative manner. However, while top management belief shows a positive moderating role in path with the central route of information quality, it, unexpectedly and positively moderates the peripheral route of security protection as well, and it moderating role with the normative cues turned out to be insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. These surprising results will be further discussed below. Apart from few nonsupported hypotheses, our overall results indicate that absorptive capacity and top management belief are two elaboration likelihoods that direct the EMS organizations to go through either the central or the peripheral route. An EMS organization takes the central route of in formation processing as it perceives ePCR’s ability for task support with its quality information provided and top management’s support for the ePCR use. Conversely, security protection and normative cues represent the peripheral routes for an EMS agency when using ePCR for task support.

## 6.1. Implications to researchers

Our study contributes to the literature of healthcare IT in several signi<sup>fi</sup>cant ways. First, not only is this the <sup>fi</sup>rst comprehensive study on EMS’s use of ePCR, an important but largely ignored area of healthcare information technology, but it also looks deep into the characteristics and antecedents of task support provided by such information systems to <sup>fi</sup>eld support-oriented healthcare providers. Furthermore, we extend and apply the theory of ELM in the context of healthcare information technology and, in so doing, identify information quality as the central route and security protection and normative cues as peripheral routes when EMS organizations process information about ePCR’s task sup port. Although versions of normative cues such as source credibility [17], reputation [26], sorting cue [72] have been used as peripheral route factors in prior ELM literature, none of the studies tested how security protection and normative cues in the organizations work together as peripheral cues. This is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant insight into the understanding of information processing and organizational task supports in the healthcare information technology, given the importance of information security in the healthcare industry.

Second, we adopt absorptive capacity and top management belief, two important constructs in recent literature on organizational learning and technology innovation, as the elaboration likelihood factors that moderate the relationships between antecedents and task support in the case of EMS’s use of ePCR. It is a unique approach, because previous studies mainly showed how individual factors, such as self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy [26], knowledge [17] and attitude [21], determined the routes, while factors in an organizational setting have not been considered. In addi tion, our research reveals the pertinent roles that absorptive capacity and top management belief play in information systems use. In the case of EMS’s use of ePCR, while absorptive capacity is a positive factor of task support satisfaction when the central route is taken, it actually impedes the successful use and assimilation of such a new technology when the organization processes information through the periphera route. In addition, the results of the moderating role of top management belief illustrate that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of information quality on task support of ePCR can also be boosted when the top management shows strong support for the technology. Those nonsupported moderating results of top management in the relationship between security protection and task support are also worth noting. We note that although security protection has been examined as a peripheral route factor [26], it may also call for signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cognitive e<sup>f</sup>orts in information processing, especially to those who have strong interests in security issues. This is perhaps why that path shows a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating impact in an opposite (i.e., positive) direction. To avoid such idiosyncratic impacts of security protection, future ELM studies may consider replacing this construct with a pure peripheral cue such as security seals [83]. In addition, top management belief does not display a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating role in the relationship between normative cues and task support, indicating that outside experts or industry norms do not improve on the EMS organizations’ perception of using ePCR for task support even when top management belief in ePCR is low. Taking together, our study reveals the overall positive role of top management support in the ePCR use.

## 6.2. Implications to practitioners

This study also presents important practical implications. EMS organizations, like many other <sup>fi</sup>eld service-oriented agencies, tend to focus on the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of operations. Not all are familiar, let alone pro<sup>fi</sup>cient, in advanced information technology, and there has been a lot of uncertainty in their migration to ePCR. In an EMS organization that possesses signi<sup>fi</sup>cant technology experience and capability with a proven track of successful assimilation of new knowledge (i.e., high level of absorptive capacity), the merits of ePCR, such as its information quality, should be emphasized for its members to e<sup>f</sup>ectively use ePCR to support EMS tasks. That means the organization can handle more sophisticated systems able to provide quality information, instead of simple and easy systems. On the other hand, for those EMS agencies that are not technology-savvy, it is likely that their members would be increasingly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by “peer pressure” and “best practices” (i.e., normative cues). It also implies that ePCR systems with security seals or certi<sup>fi</sup>cate can be comfortably perceived to improve the task support.

In addition, the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of top management belief shows that top management support has strengths in facilitating the process that requires more cognitive endeavors. It is important for the top management to show their strong belief in and support for the system to enhance the association of the central routes with the satisfaction that their personnel can achieve when using ePCR for task support. The organizations with strong top management support can focus on improving the ePCR system to serve paramedics with interfaces or system structures that can provide more detailed and contextualized information. This implies that, in such cases, in-house developed ePCR may be a better option than o<sup>f</sup>-the-shelf software, which is usually generic to the context.

## 6.3. Limitations and future directions

Although this study was designed and conducted based on strong theoretical background and with proper methods and a validated set of instruments, it is not without limitations. We address these limitations of the study with possible future research directions. First, we only collected data from a single person per organization as a key informant when conducting an organizational-level study. While this is a wellknown and widely accepted practice in the literature, collecting data from multiple informants of the same organization can provide more reliable data by checking the interrater reliability between informants. Such a multiple-informant approach would improve the reliability of results, allow the examination of the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the consensus of ePCR decision makers, and further extend the understanding on the phenomena. Secondly, this study tests the cross-sectional relationships among information quality, security protection, normative cues, and task support across multiorganizations. Therefore, it does not show how changes of information quality or security protection improve task support within the same organization. Future study can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from exploring the longitudinal relationships. Lastly, one of the moderating relationships were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at 90 % con<sup>fi</sup>dence level. Nowadays, studies in the information systems literature tend to use 95 % or 99 % con<sup>fi</sup>dence level for hypothesis testing. However, considering the fact that this study explores the new moderating relationships with sample size of 179, we believe that 90 % con<sup>fi</sup>dence level still provides meaningful implications.
