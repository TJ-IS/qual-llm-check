# Information Feedback, Targeting, and Coordination: An Experimental Study

Matthew J. Hashim,<sup>a</sup> Karthik N. Kannan,<sup>b</sup> Sandra Maximiano<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> University of Arizona, Tucson, Arizona 85721; <sup>b</sup> Purdue University, West Lafayette, Indiana 47907 Contact: mhashim@email.arizona.edu (MJH); kkarthik@purdue.edu (KNK); maximiano@purdue.edu (SM)

Abstract. There are many contexts in which an “everybody else is doing it” attitude is relevant. We evaluate the impact of this attitude in a multithreshold public goods game. We use a lab experiment to study the role of providing information about contribution behavior to targeted subsets of individuals, and its efect on coordination. Treatments include one in which no information is provided and three other treatments, i.e., where information is provided to a random sample of subjects; to those whose contributions are below the average of their group, and to those whose contributions are above the average of their group. We find that the random provision of information is no diferent than not providing information. More important, average contributions improve with targeted treatments. Coordination waste is also lower with targeted treatments. The insights from this research are more broadly relevant in the contexts of piracy, open innovation, and crowdfunding.

## 1. Introduction

Our research problem was originally motivated by the piracy context. Producers of digital products such as music, games, and software argue that high piracy rates (e.g., an estimated 90% of games are pirated, 2D Boy 2008; 95% of music downloaded is pirated, IFPI 2009) lead to huge losses (e.g., \$21.5B due to movie piracy). Yet they also believe that pirates have an “everybody else is doing it, so maybe it is OK” attitude (Levin 2011). These two actions seem contradictory. On one hand, information good producers invest in increasing awareness of piracy with the hope of reducing it by mobilizing public opinion for regulatory or legal actions. On the other hand, the “everybody else is doing it” notion increases perceptions that “piracy is all pervasive,” thereby reducing the barriers to it. That is, consumers engage in piracy because they believe that it is being done by everybody else. Therefore it is unclear whether sharing piracy information with consumers helps or harms producers of information goods. Efectively, our paper aims to answer the following research questions: Does knowledge about others’ extensive free-riding lead to more free-riding? More important, can we shape overall freeriding behavior by skewing the provision of such information to only a subset of market participants? Even though our original motivation was the piracy context, the questions raised here also relate abstractly to other information systems domains where free-riding can occur (including the crowdfunding context, Zhang and Liu 2012, and collaborations in open innovation projects, Zhang and Wang 2012). Thus, in abstract, we also investigate the outcome implications of skewed provision of information about others’ actions.

We address these questions from the perspective of the social welfare generated in the market, which is in turn dictated by individuals’ actions. For example, in the piracy context, arguments to limit piracy are based on social implications (e.g., the number of jobs lost, quality of the content produced, etc., Telang and Waldfogel 2014). In crowdfunding contexts, arguably, platform owners such as Kickstarter are concerned about the welfare of its backers and creators. A setup wherein individual contributions have implications for the welfare generated can be studied using a coordination game. Specifically, we use a multithreshold public good game.<sup>1</sup> One reason for choosing the public good game is that information goods, including music, games, and software, have the characteristics of a public good (Varian 1998). Even the crowdfunding context may be perceived as similar to a public good context (Solomon et al. 2015). Furthermore, the multiplicity of thresholds in our game represents diferent quality levels that can be attained depending on how the individuals as a whole contribute to the public good. Such a characterization of quality levels is also consistent with claims in the piracy context that the quality of the digital good produced (e.g., features delivered, access to support resources, etc.) depends on the total individual contributions (Cho and Ahn 2010, Danaher et al. 2014, Jain 2008). In such games, it turns out that the actions that lead to the social optima are also individually maximizing. Yet subjects are known to have problems coordinating to the optimal. Consequently, the aforementioned question becomes equivalent to: How does skewed information about free-riding in public good games afect the participants’ ability to attain the maximum social welfare?

Prior literature has shown that subjects in coordination games fail to coordinate efectively (i.e., do not attain the highest social welfare). Prior literature has also considered mechanisms, such as the provision of information on other players’ behavior, to improve coordination, but with mixed results (see Section 2 for a more detailed literature review that also considers other mechanisms to improve coordination). Studies that have analyzed information provision assume symmetric information. Even then it is unclear whether subjects would react similarly to the information provided. For instance, if individuals have social preferences (e.g., altruism, reciprocity, eficiency, etc.), as experimental evidence seems to suggest, they may react diferently to information. Therefore, provisioning information symmetrically may not improve coordination, hence, the need to study the aforementioned question. It turns out that experimental research using a multithreshold public good game is also generally scarce. Even in that setting, to our knowledge, no one has studied the impact of targeted information strategies.

For the analysis, we conduct a laboratory experiment to investigate how contributions and outcomes compare when information about others’ contributions is targeted to subgroups as opposed to randomly providing information to subjects. We use the lab setting because it is dificult to obtain naturally occurring data of individuals’ behavior under diferent information regimes. In some contexts even naturally occurring data are unreliable. For example, subjects may lie about their behavior or various consequences arising from the Hawthorne efect (Landsberger 1958). Furthermore, it is not as easy to efectively control the information flow in a real setting (or field experiment), as in an experimental lab. These problems are overcome through the use of a controlled setting and exogenous manipulation of information feedback in a laboratory environment. Randomization of our treatments allows us to tightly control possible confounding and selection efects that are typical with naturally occurring data.

We initially conduct our experiment by providing no feedback to the subjects so as to evaluate the similarity of our multithreshold game results to prior literature. We discover that, consistent with prior literature, subjects find it dificult to coordinate. We follow up on our initial experiment by considering other information treatments, some of which were motivated by the “everybody else is doing it” attitude. Specifically, our analyses focus on the following three treatments: random feedback (i.e., contribution information is provided to a random sample of subjects), targeted above feedback (i.e., information is provided only to subjects whose contributions are above the average of their group), and targeted below feedback (i.e., information is provided only to subjects whose contributions are below the average of their group). We find that targeted information feedback results in the greatest level of contributions by subjects, whereas randomly providing information to subjects is inefective. Between the two targeted treatments, sending information to those who are contributing on average more to the good provides a higher level of contributions among the subjects. We also present insights about group contribution information interacting with inequity aversion in creating interesting outcomes.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We review the literature in Section 2. Section 3 introduces our multithreshold public good game and the equilibrium analysis of the game for our experimental parameters. Section 4 presents our information regimes, behavioral hypotheses, and experiment implementation details. Results are provided in Section 5, followed by concluding remarks in Section 6.

## 2. Related Literature

Our paper relates to research that investigates the role of information about past contributions in cooperation and coordination in public goods games.<sup>2</sup> We review related literature in Section 2.1; in Section 2.2, we survey related information systems (IS) literature.

## 2.1. Information Strategies for Cooperation and Coordination

This section details public goods experiments in economics literature. Typically, the total group contribution level is revealed as feedback in such experiments. A few other variants have also been explored. In a voluntary contribution mechanism (VCM), Sell and Wilson (1991) compare the impact of providing no information, aggregate information, and individual level information about other members’ contributions. They find that contributions are significantly higher with individual level information than with the other two, which are not statistically diferent from each other. Results from Weimann (1994) are inconsistent with Sell and Wilson (1991); he finds no significant diference in the mean contributions when subjects were provided with the individual level information compared to the average contribution of their group (i.e., aggregate information). To reconcile the two results,

Croson (2001) also considers a VCM with feedback at the aggregate and individual levels. Even though they find no significant diferences in the average contributions between the two levels, the individual level feedback generated a higher variance in the contributions.

Variants of the individual and the aggregate level contributions feedback have been analyzed separately. For example, Croson and Marks (1998) find that anonymity decreases contribution amounts and increases the variance. Also, when information on individual contributions is probabilistically revealed, Anderson and Staford (2009) find that the a priori possibility of an announcement increases public contributions slightly, whereas the ex post possibility appears to promote free-riding. The individual level information feedback has also been studied in other coordination games such as the minimum-efort games.<sup>3</sup> Even there, however, the results are mixed. Berninghaus and Erhart (2001) and Brandts and Cooper (2006) show a positive efect on coordination, while Devetag (2005) and Van Huyck et al. (1990) find that ex post information does not solve miscoordination problems.

In a field study of on-air fundraising for a public radio station, Shang and Croson (2009) compared diferent variants of revealing aggregate information. Providing the 90th and 95th percentile information to potential donors increased the level of overall contributions and was also efective with first-time donors. Similarly, a natural field experiment in the context of public utilities was conducted by Ayres et al. (2013), who shows that information about energy use presented in “peer comparison reports” is efective at modifying the behavior of high energy use consumers, thus significantly reducing their consumption.

Just as in earlier research, our paper investigates the efect of information about previous contributions on coordination. Here, however, we use a multithreshold public goods context. (See Section 3 for more details on why the multithreshold public good game is appropriate.) Experimental research using a multithreshold public good game is generally scarce. To our knowledge, only sequentially attained thresholds<sup>4</sup> have been studied in prior literature in this context (Bagnoli et al. 1992, Normann and Rau 2011). We are unaware of any study on the impact of targeted information on coordination in a public good game (including a multithreshold).

## 2.2. Related Information Systems Literature

Increasingly, IS literature is using experimental economics in various contexts, such as auctions (e.g., Adomavicius et al. 2012, 2013; Bapna et al. 2010; Cason et al. 2011), online dating (e.g., Bapna et al. 2016), and privacy (e.g., Brandimarte et al. 2013, Tsai et al. 2011). As to digital piracy, we are only aware of Hashim et al. (2014), who investigate the role of the source of advice on piracy decisions. Below we provide an overview of the economics of digital piracy and the crowdfunding literatures.

Most economics of piracy research demonstrates the importance of pricing digital goods and the associated efects on piracy decisions (Chen and Png 2003, Khouja and Park 2008). Accordingly, pricing decisions are often studied in conjunction with other factors. For example, Sundararajan (2004) focus on the use of technology deterrence in preventing piracy. Product bundling has also been explored in limiting piracy when used in conjunction with deterrence strategies (Bhattacharjee et al. 2009, Gopal and Gupta 2010). Chellappa and Shivendu (2005) capture the desire for pirates to sample digital goods, and model that aspect along with pricing considerations. Liu et al. (2011) takes into account word-ofmouth efects with optimal software pricing. Impacts on the level of quality of information goods developed by vendors have also been modeled in the presence of piracy (Lahiri and Dey 2013). Patch management has also been investigated to consider the impacts of restricting or allowing pirated software users to receive security patches (August and Tunca 2008, Kannan et al. 2016, Lahiri 2012). By doing so, they provide policy guidance for vendors as well as social planners under various piracy contexts. As mentioned earlier, antipiracy and information feedback mechanisms do not appear to have been studied previously.

The problem studied here can also relate to the crowdfunding context. Earlier research has established that the crowdfunding context reflects a public good game (Solomon et al. 2015, Wash and Solomon 2014). A number of papers have considered the factors that afect contributions in the public good game. Burtch et al. (2015) use a field experiment to show how the lack of anonymity for the contributors afects contribution behavior. Herzenstein et al. (2011) and Zhang and Liu (2012) show the existence of “rational” herding. On the other hand, Burtch et al. (2013) show that crowding out efects can also occur when contributions are less important to the recipient of the funds. Lin et al. (2013) study how information about borrowers’ friends serves as a signal of credit quality that afects contributions. Our paper relates to how such contributions may be afected when a subset of the segment knows about others’ contributions.

Free-riding on the eforts of others is also a concern in the open innovation context (Johnson 2002). Fang and Neufeld (2009) show that collaborator efort is sustained if collaborators strive to improve the welfare of the community and the software code. Other motivational factors have also been considered. Roberts et al. (2006) show that intrinsic and extrinsic rewards improve the quality of contributions to software projects. Also, collaborator ties sustain open source projects when the ties are based on past performance on projects (Hahn et al. 2008, Zhang and Wang 2012). Our paper relates to these works because the use of targeted information as a mechanism to sustain collaborator efort does not appear to have been studied before in open innovation. Next, we describe the basic multithreshold public good game used for the experiment.

## 3. The Multithreshold Public Good Game: Theory and Experimental Results

The purpose of this section is three-fold. In Section 3.1 we describe the single period multithreshold public good game and the parameters used in the experiment. Because the game we propose is also a contribution to the body of knowledge, for the sake of completeness, we provide the theoretical predictions in Section 3.2. Last, in Section 3.4 we evaluate the use of an experiment described in Section 3.3 and the attainment of equilibrium when the information provision is similar to prior literature. In other words, we ensure that changing the details of the game does not change the subjects’ ability to attain the equilibrium. We believe it is important to evaluate this aspect before studying the impact of targeted information. The results show that players are unable to reach or maintain equilibrium outcomes in the multithreshold public good game. The main result from the analysis is that coordination is dificult in the absence of a mechanism that improves players’ ability to eficiently coordinate.

Before describing the game, we discuss why a multithreshold game is best suited to our context. First, as opposed to a single-threshold game, the multiplicity of thresholds facilitates a larger possibility of equilibria, from ineficient to eficient, thus allowing for a more precise comparison of the impact of diferent information strategies. Second, between the single- and multithreshold games, ordinal comparisons of policies may even be reversed.<sup>5</sup> Third, compared to the continuous public good games, multithreshold games have an advantage in that the Pareto-optimal outcomes are supportable as Nash equilibria (Palfrey and Rosenthal 1984, Bagnoli et al. 1992). Next, we describe the game.

## 3.1. The Multithreshold Public Good Game

We consider n symmetric individuals concerned with the provision of a single unit of a public good with a specific quality. The quality can be one of the elements of the ordered set $\mathcal { Q }$ and is represented by an index variable $q$ (starting at 1). Each consumer is given an initial amount $E _ { i } ,$ which can be invested in a private account or used towards the provision of the public good. Every consumer, $i ,$ simultaneously and independently chooses to contribute $x _ { i }$ from their initial endowment to the public good.

## 3.3. Experimental Implementation of the Multithreshold Public Good Game

In the experiment we implemented the multithreshold game as described in Section 3.1. We set $n = 5 ,$ $E _ { i } = 5 0 .$ , and used the threshold and quality parameters in Table 1.<sup>11</sup> Subjects play the game repeatedly for 16 rounds under a strangers matching protocol. Therefore, at the beginning of each round, subjects are randomly re-matched with a diferent group of subjects. Subjects are never informed of who is in their group or about individual contributions of their group members. There are various reasons for choosing a strangers matching. First, it enables us to most appropriately capture the equilibrium for the one-shot game (Andreoni and Croson 2008, Croson 1996). Second, it avoids reputation efects and therefore makes coordination harder for the subjects. It is well known that the partner design itself enables higher coordination in a public good context. Finally, many real settings consider consumers who do not necessarily interact with the same groups.

At the beginning of each round, each subject receives an endowment.<sup>12</sup> Next, subjects are asked about their beliefs regarding the average contribution in their group. Belief elicitation was not incentivized to avoid interference of the elicitation process with contribution behavior (Gächter and Renner 2010).

Subsequently, all subjects are reminded of their own allocation rate from the prior round. Subjects then choose their individual contribution to the public good, x . For x , subjects were allowed to enter only integers in <sup>[</sup>0, 50<sup>]</sup>.<sup>13</sup> After all of the subjects input their contributions, the quality level attained and the individual profit for the round are displayed to each subject. Recall, however, that subjects are never informed about their group members’ individual contributions to the public good. These procedures are repeated for every round.<sup>14</sup>

The experiment was conducted at the Vernon Smith Experimental Economics Laboratory (VSEEL) at Purdue University in the spring of 2010. Subjects were recruited by email using the laboratory’s online recruitment system, and subject participation was limited to a single session. The computerized experimental environment was implemented using z-tree (Fischbacher 2007). A total of 20 subjects participated in the No Info treatment, and they were randomly assigned to individual computers. Communication was not allowed during the session. Copies of the experiment instructions were provided to each subject and were read aloud by the experiment administrator. A copy of the instructions used to conduct the experiment is available in Online Appendix A. Completion of control questions was required to ensure that each subject understood the experimental procedures before starting the actual experiment. Any subject who failed to answer the control questions after three attempts was personally assisted by the experiment administrator.

Under this scenario, information feedback about group members’ contributions may be used as a coordination device. In fact, information feedback has been shown to aid coordination, even in a repeated game with random-matching. For example, in a repeated random-matching prisoner’s dilemma game, information on the current partners’ past actions can theoretically be enough to sustain any level of cooperation (Takahashi 2010). Moreover, if information feedback may be used as a coordination device, then identity of the person to whom information is provided can make a diference. Therefore, in this section we present and motivate the information regimes implemented and discuss related behavioral hypotheses. Given our previous results and our main goal of studying the implications of targeted information feedback on coordination and cooperation, to derive our behavioral hypotheses we do not assume any equilibrium play.

Based on the anecdotal evidence about the “everybody else is doing it” attitude, it appears that targeting of information should play a role. In this section we develop the related hypotheses.

## 4.1. Information Regimes (Treatments)

Our study involves four information regimes. First, we conduct a No Info regime, in which no information feedback is provided across the rounds. In each of the other three regimes, before individuals make their contribution decisions, a subset of subjects in each group is informed about the average previous round contribution of their actual group members.<sup>16</sup> The subset that receives information difers across the three regimes. In the Random Info regime, the subset of subjects to receive the information is randomly decided. In the Target Below (Target Above) regime, the subjects to receive the information are those whose previous round contributions are below (above) the average previous period contribution of their present group members.

Even though the information about others’ contributions may help subjects to coordinate, it is unclear whether the information would afect each consumer in the same way. More specifically, if individuals have social preferences, such as inequity aversion, and/or are conditional cooperators, this information is likely to make their preferences more salient and, indeed, to whom information is provided may have an efect on individuals’ choices. These preferences motivate our Target Below and Target Above treatments. As our main goal is to investigate the efect of targeting information, the best control treatment would not be the one in which we do not provide information, but instead a treatment in which subjects would still receive information but not in a targeted manner (i.e., Random Info). Moreover, we must keep the same amount of information in the non-targeted treatments to make comparisons. Both of these concerns justify our choice for conducting a treatment in which information is randomly provided to a subset of subjects.

## 4.2. Behavioral Hypotheses

As previously shown, subjects are not playing according to equilibrium predictions. The miscoordination is likely due to incorrect beliefs about their group’s contribution behavior and how crucial their own contribution is in achieving a certain quality level. Therefore, when information about the group’s contribution behavior is provided, subjects may revise their estimate about how crucial their contribution is, and change their individual contribution level. In our game, if subjects receive information and use it to correctly revise their estimates about others’ contributions, we expect them to coordinate more eficiently, i.e., with less waste, and possibly to a higher quality level.<sup>17</sup>

## Hypothesis 1. Treatments where information is provided achieve higher eficiency than No Info.

The efect of targeting strategies on coordination depends on the type of preferences we assume the subjects to have. First, consider that players care only about monetary payofs. In this case, even if information feedback aids in coordination, we do not expect diferences in coordination across the information treatments (i.e., Random Info, Target Below, Target Above). That is, targeting information to those below or above the average contribution of the group should make no diference in the individuals’ ability to coordinate to an equilibrium point compared to what may happen in Random Info. If subjects have no social preferences, they would not put any particular weight on having contributed more or less than the average. Also, we have no reason to a priori expect any diference in the number of subjects who contributed below or above the average or in the size of the deviations of individual contributions to the group average across treatments.

Individuals, however, may have social preferences, such as an aversion to inequity, a preference for conformity (i.e., “everybody else is doing it” attitude) or they may exhibit reciprocal behavior. In this case, information about the average contribution of their group members may make inequality concerns more salient. Thus, targeting information may influence subjects’ decisions diferently across treatments. In particular, consider that individuals have a preference for strict relativism (see Bolton and Ockenfels 2000) and dislike earning more or less than the average (see Section 3.2). In this instance, we expect the targeted treatments to result in a diferent level of coordination compared to Random Info. If a player observes a low average contribution level in Random Info compared to what she expects to contribute, her optimal choice, given her inequity-aversion preferences, is to decrease her own contribution. However, if a player observes a high average contribution level in Random Info compared to what she expects to contribute, the player may encounter disutility from other players doing worse, and thus may increase her contribution. Because these adjustments will be simultaneously made, we expect no differences in the quality provided in the Random Info treatment as compared to the quality in the No Info treatment. We do, however, expect a convergence to a more symmetric contribution level.<sup>18</sup>

Hypothesis 2. If individuals have strong inequity-averse preferences or have strong reciprocal concerns, we expect more symmetric contributions (but not necessarily a higher quality) in Random Info than in No Info.

For the same reasons stated above, we believe the targeted feedback treatments will also reach more eficient equilibria. However, we expect diferences in the average contributions. In Target Below, the subjects contributing below the average will first move along the solid black threshold lines shown in Figure 1 to a more symmetric rate of contribution. Because subjects above the average do not receive information feedback, their behavior is not expected to change. Consequently, in Target Below, it is possible to observe a higher quality level over time, as well as more asymmetric contributions. The opposite result is expected for the Target Above treatment.

Hypothesis 3. If individuals have strong inequity-averse preferences or have strong reciprocal concerns, we expect progressively higher contributions (quality level) in the Target Below treatment and lower contributions (quality level) in the Target Above treatment.

An implication of Hypothesis 3 is that the average contribution is higher with Target Below than Target Above.

Our behavioral hypotheses assume that subjects who do not receive information do not make any particular adjustments to their behavior. However, given that those who receive information may adjust their behavior, with consequences for the total contributions and quality provided, those who do not receive information may also update their beliefs across rounds and adjust their behavior accordingly. This process is unclear, however, as is whether it will amplify the efects of Hypothesis 3 or change the direction of our predictions. This is further investigated in our analysis.

## 4.3. Implementation

A fundamental feature of our experiment is the information provided to targeted individuals. Next we describe implementation of the multithreshold public good game described in Section 3.1, but now including the information regimes introduced in Section 4.1. To implement targeting, a subset of subjects in each group is informed about the average of previous round contributions of their actual group members before making their own contribution decisions. Given our strangers matching protocol, the average contribution of their actual group members is computed using the individual contributions that each member of the current group made in the previous round.<sup>19</sup> In addition to the explicit information provision in our information treatments, all subjects in all treatments are informed about the quality level reached once contribution choices have been made. However, subjects are never informed about the actual average contribution of their group members in the current round.<sup>20</sup>

In addition to the No Info treatment introduced in Section 3.3, we conducted sessions for the information treatments. Four sequences of our treatments were assembled, each of which involves two distinct information regimes. Table 3 shows how the rounds correspond to each information regime for the four sequences over all 46 rounds. Note that the regimes where information is provided are paired with the No Info regime in Sequences B, C, and D, whereas the No Info regime is paired with the Random Info regime in Sequence A. We withheld information in the middle (rounds 17–31) in Sequences B–D to study the long-term “sustained” efects of information. Also, the sequences were designed such that subjects would not be “polluted” by other information regimes in the middle rounds before information is reintroduced. Otherwise, we cannot safely tease apart the changes in outcomes to simply the same treatment in the third block of rounds.

Implementation of the sequences was identical to that discussed in Section 3.3. Thus, the same setup applies here. However, there is an exception, beginning with Round 2, where information about average contributions to the public good is provided to a subset of subjects depending on the treatment. The provision of information follows beliefs to prevent the information from being a focal point in influencing individual beliefs. Also, in the first round in each session subjects receive no information feedback as we have no prior actions at that point.

In the information provision rounds (i.e., after Round 1), particular subjects in the targeted treatments who contribute below or above the average contribution receive information. We use the same algorithm to determine the number of participants who will receive information in the Random Info treatment. We do this to provide the same quantity of information to the subjects in each period, thus facilitating direct comparison of results between the information treatments. More specifically, we count the number of subjects who would have been targeted, and then randomly select the same number of subjects for the randomly provided information.

## 6. Conclusion

In marketing, targeting to convince a participant to engage in a particular activity such as purchasing a product or service is universally well known. Usually, the information provided seeks to explain the value of the product. In our context, we are motivated to study the “everybody else is doing it” attitude based on some contradicting observations in the piracy context. There are two related questions: How does the information about others’ free-riding behavior lead to an individual’s free-riding? Can we influence behavior based on providing targeted information to a subset of participants in the market? We learned that the problem we began studying has broader contextual appeal. The insights we generate should be applicable to other information contexts where free-riding may occur such as crowdfunding open innovation.

Many of these contexts, e.g., piracy and crowdfunding, have been shown to exhibit characteristics of a public good game. We, therefore, study the problem as a multithreshold public good game. The game we present does not appear to have been studied in the literature and is also better suited to our context than other types of public good games studied previously. We analyze the equilibrium for this game and conduct the experiment when no information about others’ contributions are provided. By implementing that experiment, we conclude that the multithreshold game presents coordination problems just as other standard public good games.

Mechanisms, such as the provision of information about other players’ behavior, were studied in prior literature to improve the ability of players to coordinate their actions in a game. In line with that and motivated by the “everybody else is doing it” attitude, we consider targeted information strategies. Specifically, we consider the following three treatments: (1) In Random Info, some participants are randomly selected for information; (2) In Target Below, participants whose contributions are below the average of the group receive it; and (3) In Target Above, the opposite is true.

Results from the experiment are summarized below. We find that Random Info is not better than No Info from an average contribution standpoint, consistent with prior work (e.g., Sell and Wilson 1991, Croson 2001). A more surprising result is that targeted information strategies generate higher contributions, which initially appears to be in contrast with the predictions from strict inequity aversion or conformity (i.e., “everybody else is doing it” attitude) models. However, we do find that those subjects who receive information in the targeted treatments seem to act in accordance with inequity aversion. That is, subjects who receive information in Target Above decrease their contributions towards the group average, whereas those who receive information in Target Below act in the opposite manner. The Target Above treatment benefits from an indirect efect of information, where subjects who do not receive information still adjust their contributions upwards to maintain their current quality level or to reach the next level. Compared to Target Below, the indirect efect of information is the key factor leading to higher levels of contributions in Target Above. Last, Random Info performs poorly because a miscoordination problem arises when subjects use information to attempt to coordinate contributions simultaneously from above and below the group mean. The simultaneous increase and decrease of contributions leads to contribution decay in Random Info because the contributions are missing the thresholds, and excess contributions are being wasted.

Overall, the conclusions from our paper provide evidence that it is possible to improve coordination in those settings where individuals may be targeted with information, ultimately leading to a more eficient (and socially desired) result. Although we do not find a significant diference in coordination in those cases where information is provided randomly versus not being provided, we do find diferences between those treatments and the treatments that use targeted information. Thus, it may be worthwhile to use targeted information strategies instead. Additionally, our research makes contributions to practice by asking questions of the status quo. For example: Do the resources spent by industry groups on reporting the prevalence of piracy help or hurt their cause? Does the strategy of litigating individuals help in mitigating piracy? Also, what types and specificity of information should crowdfunding platforms use to improve the coordination of contributions towards projects by members of the platform?

In practice, policies related to our research have been or could be implemented in a variety of ways. For example, a software producer could use a support forum for their paying customers to provide information about piracy and thank them for doing the “right thing.” Such a mechanism relates to Target Above treatment. Similarly, the Random Info treatment relates to the vendor issuing a press release or news announcement where the producer does not know exactly the type of customer who is receiving the information. Incidents wherein artists such as Metallica berated fans from the stage about piracy (e.g., Jones 2000) can be perceived to be equivalent to Random Info. We have also seen instances where artists such as Madonna poisoned the piracy networks with anti-piracy rants (instead of the actual song, Kaufman 2003). These could be vehicles where the Targeted Below treatment can be executed.

Future research could extend our findings with those of Reuben and Riedl (2013) by considering the enforcement of contribution norms and targeted information strategies with heterogeneous populations. Researchers could also consider a field experiment to quantify the efects of targeted information provision. In practice we may be unable to reach the entire group of people who are below or above an average level. Still, if individuals in a group do not receive information, they may behave as if they do not receive information at all. Thus it might be worthwhile to examine interactions between the no information group and the targeted groups. As with any research, ours has some limitations. Although we approximate the typical population of pirates by using university students, we cannot capture the entire range of potential pirates. Also, we used an abstract context to implement the experiment to be sure that the observed efects were only from our controlled manipulations, and thus unafected by any potential framing efects.
