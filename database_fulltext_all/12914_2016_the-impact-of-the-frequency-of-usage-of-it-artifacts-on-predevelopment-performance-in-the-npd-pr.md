The impact of the frequency of usage of IT artifacts on pre-development performance in the NPD process

## Abstract

Responses from 152 managers from a wide range of industries were used to test a conceptual model examining the influence of IT and non-IT resources on IT capabilities and their subsequent effects on pre-development stage outcomes. It was found that the resources of IT infrastructure, IT embeddedness, firm outward focus, and competitive intensity have varied effects on the frequency of usage of general purpose and collaborative IT artifacts. Firms with higher levels of usage of collaborative artifacts in their NPD process have improved pre-development stage performance, including the collaboration.

Keywords

New product development, resource-based theory, IT artifacts, path analysis.

## 1. Introduction

The new product development (NPD) process and its management have become increasingly complex and time critical [1]. Nambisan [61, 56] argues that the rapid infusion of information technology (IT) artifacts will have a significant influence on the management and outcomes of NPD. Specifically, he suggests that IT-enabled innovation processes are active rather than passive and will foster improved analysis of data, enable more efficient communication and efficient problem solving, and achieve higher levels of integration than previously possible [61, 56). Further, IT-enabled innovation will make an organization more flexible and responsive [46, 61, 77].

There are two perspectives for approaching IT artifacts: attributes and functions. Whereas the attributes of IT perspective focuses on the components of the artifact, including hardware and software, the functions perspective focuses on what the artifact does, such as enabling communication, collaboration, coordination, and networking. This paper follows the functions perspective and will focus on the pre-development stage of NPD.1

The pre-development stage, also known as the “front-end of innovation”, has been acknowledged as critical in the NPD process and has been linked strongly to the overall project and market success of new products [8, 24 ]. This stage is often messy and iterative [18], with project team members requiring constant communication and feedback on ideas and concepts, and often needing to collaborate across functions in order to shape these ideas and concepts for development [24]. Diverse functional areas such as marketing and sales, manufacturing, design, and engineering work cooperatively, share information, and jointly determine the requirements for the product to be developed [6, 15]. Moreover, the pre-development stage may also involve communication and collaboration with external partners such as suppliers, distributors, and customers [15]. Thus, varied types of IT resources and capabilities that facilitate information sharing and problem-solving among core and extended team members are likely to be useful.

In this study, we focus on the functions of two categories of IT artifacts used in the pre-development stage: general purpose and collaborative. General-purpose artifacts include e-mail, desktop tools, and shared files and drives. Collaborative artifacts consist of social networking sites, blogs and micro-blogs, virtual simulation applications, wikis, requirements management software, dedicated intranet sites, conferencing, and dedicated open innovation applications. General purpose artifacts have been used for years for NPD and other activities, while collaborative artifacts are gaining in usage frequency for NPD activities [7]. However, few studies have examined the frequency of usage of collaborative IT artifacts in the NPD process (36, 37, 45) and none that we could find focuses on the pre-development stage of the NPD process. Further, it is important to shed light on those factors that influence the frequency of usage of these IT artifacts, and their impact on NPD outcomes.

The Resource-Based View (RBV) of the firm provides guidance for examining antecedents to the frequency of IT artifact use in the form of IT and complementary resources. Complementary resources are non-IT resources that, combined with IT resources, create value for the firm [11, 34, 55, 77]. Complementary resources can be organizational resources, which include culture [63, 55] and competitive scope [67]. The present study examines the influence of four resources - IT infrastructure and IT embeddedness (IT resources), and firm outward focus and competitive intensity (non-

IT resources) – on IT capabilities, manifested by the frequency of usage of general purpose and collaborative IT artifacts. Aligned with the RBV, our study posits that these resources and capabilities have a significant effect on pre-development stage outcomes, such as the number of concepts/prototypes generated, efficient team collaboration, and management evaluation.

Thus, our research makes four contributions to both theory and practice. First, by examining IT resources and IT capabilities in an NPD context, this study adds to the emerging research stream on this topic [9, 10] and addresses the calls for more research that integrates IT and innovation [56, 77]. Second, investigating the effect of IT capabilities (i.e. the frequency of usage of general purpose and collaborative IT artifacts) on various pre-development outcomes provides a deeper understanding of IT value and insights into which category of artifacts act as levers that can be managed to promote pre-development performance [9, 11, 12]. Third, from the perspective of the RBV of the firm [3, 4,13], this research provides greater understanding of the role of IT resources, complementary resources, and IT capabilities in a particular stage of the NPD process. Prior research has noted the need to examine the role of IT in different stages of the NPD process [23]. Lastly, this research highlights the need for NPD management to work closely with organizational IT specialists to ensure that appropriate IT resources and capabilities are implemented for NPD management, and that these resources and capabilities are used effectively to improve cross-functional and interorganizational information and knowledge exchange.

The present paper is organized as follows. The next section discusses the rationale for focusing on the pre-development stage of the NPD process and reviews the specific artifacts examined in this study. We also discuss the resource-based view of the firm. Next, we present our conceptual model and hypotheses. Our paper concludes with a discussion of the results, including their implications for both theory and managerial practice.

## 2. Background

## 2.1 Focus on the pre-development stage

The pre-development stage marks the point at which initial product ideas are conceived, product concept definitions formulated and evaluated, and projects tentatively planned [17-20]. In general, the pre-development stage is characterized by the generation of a large number of ideas and evaluation of those ideas before large amounts of resources are committed [22]. This stage is often called the “fuzzy frontend” because market and technological uncertainty, risk, and the need for information sharing and processing are at their highest [42]. Decisions made during the front-end of NPD affect a firm’s options regarding which products can be developed and commercialized, and often influence the success of these outcomes [8, 17].

However, managing knowledge, communication, and coordination during this stage is notoriously difficult, particularly when pre-development personnel and projects span different organizational functions and stakeholder organizations [1, 2]. Failure to communicate, collaborate, and formulate appropriate ideas during the pre-development stage can result in a significant waste of scarce resources and possible market failure [18]. Moreover, the pre-development stage is different from the later stages of the NPD process, which are relatively well-structured [42] and well implemented [21].

## 2.2 IT Artifacts

Prior research has shown that IT artifacts can be used successfully for knowledge gathering, integration, dissemination, collaboration, and enabling NPD activities [59]. Moreover, general purpose IT artifacts (e.g., e-mail, word processing and spreadsheet software, shared files and drives) have been found to be used throughout the NPD process [2, 3, 4, 22], including the pre-development stage. Recently, however, there is a growing impetus to use new platform and Web 2.0 enterprise artifacts (34) for NPD (5, 56). We collectively term these “collaborative IT artifacts” (e.g., social networking sites, blogs and micro-blogs, virtual simulation applications, wikis, requirements management software, dedicated intranet sites, product lifecycle management applications, cloud-based file sharing, video conferencing, and dedicated open innovation applications). Such collaborative IT artifacts supplement and support the more general purpose IT artifacts.

While many, if not all, of the collaborative artifacts examined in this study are not activities undertaken in this stage of the NPD process. Thus, we examine those IT artifacts relevant for this stage (See Table 1) and do not include IT artifacts such as computer-aided-design (CAD) that are typically used in the development stage of the NPD process.

The use of collaborative IT artifacts allows users both inside and outside a firm to more easily communicate and collaboratively ideate, design, and manage the predevelopment stage of the NPD process [1, 2]. In a survey of 90 organizations, Kenly and Poston [7] found that firms are using Web 2.0 enterprise technologies in all stages of the NPD process; however, usage is highest in the front-end of the process. For example, CDC Software’s use of social networking technologies among global NPD team members has promoted collaboration and enabled knowledge transfer at each stage of the NPD process6. Through sentiment analysis of comments on Twitter, a large enterprise software firm categorizes positive and negative responses to existing products to identify problem areas for improvement in the next software version. Internally, the same firm uses Yammer to update distributed team members on customer requirements and design revisions. Firms also develop open innovation systems to closely collaborate with key customers. For instance, a leading computer manufacturer established an internally developed open innovation software application to closely interface with communities of customers on design features and attributes for future product models from key businesses, like mainframe servers. Such a custom web application allows customers to view and rate product features, suggest enhancements, and collaborate with other customers to develop specifications for future models. This information is automatically sorted and categorized into product-feature roadmaps.

## 3. Theoretical Background

## 3.1 Resource-Based Theory

The resource-based view (RBV) of the firm posits that firms have different resources (i.e. assets) [80] and that firm performance depends on those resources and how they are used [14, 16]. Moreover, higher performance is promoted when resources are relatively scarce and cannot be imitated or substituted [16].

In the IT literature, IT infrastructure is considered a resource [14]. Further, the implementation of IT applications necessitates a redesign of the process for which the application is being used [63]. Thus, we argue that IT embeddedness, defined as the frequency to which IT has become an integral and assimilated part of the NPD process, is also a resource. However, IT resources alone do not create value [11, 72]. Rather, IT resources in combination with complementary non-IT resources can create competitive substitutable [14, 16]. Complementary resources can be organizational resources, which include culture [55, 63] and competitive scope [67]. This study focuses on the organizational resources of firm outward focus (i.e. culture) and competitive intensity (i.e. competitive scope). We propose that these four resources – IT infrastructure, IT embeddedness, firm outward focus, and competitive intensity - contribute to creating barriers which enable a firm to maintain and/or enhance its competitive advantage [3, 4, 16].

Capabilities are “repeatable patterns of actions in the use of assets to create, produce, and/or offer products to a market” [14, p. 109]. Put another way, capabilities are skills, processes, and implanted routines that deploy or transform a firm’s resources into desired outcomes [15]. The effective combination of these different resources generates capabilities [58]. Capabilities develop over time and become routinized within the firm. As a result, they become difficult to copy, leading to a competitive advantage. IT artifacts are generally considered resources [57]. However, in this study, we examine the “frequency of usage” of general purpose and collaborative IT artifacts and view these as capabilities. Based on prior research by Kawakami, Barczak, and Durmusoglu [66], we contend that these are IT capabilities because achieving a particular level of IT usage takes time, they represent specific technological and managerial skills, and they support the NPD process [13].

## 3.3 Antecedents

IT Infrastructure. In the NPD literature, IT infrastructure has been defined as the extent to which a firm has the computer hardware, software, and human resources necessary to support the use of various NPD-focused IT artifacts, as well as the sophistication of such infrastructures [6, 9, 25]. Implicit in this definition is the notion that the infrastructure connects the hardware and software with users to create an information system. Thus, this definition is aligned with the IT literature, which indicates that an IT infrastructure includes platform technology (hardware and operating systems), network and telecommunication technologies, data, and core software applications, as well as human infrastructure (e.g., knowledge and skills), and shared services that connect users with the technical components [57].

A firm’s IT infrastructure is a key resource [27] that facilitates cross-functional processes such as NPD, by enhancing connectivity across various functional groups and externally with partners, suppliers, and customers [26, 27]. Access to a range of systems and applications through the IT infrastructure allows NPD team members to more easily and quickly share necessary project-related information, and to coordinate activities. These benefits are likely to drive greater usage of general purpose and collaborative IT artifacts as NPD project teams discover the advantages of using these various artifacts that are part of the infrastructure to accomplish their work [9, 10]. Additionally, firms that have a strong IT infrastructure are more likely to experiment with new IT technologies [64], suggesting that such an infrastructure may lead to NPD teams evaluating and adopting a variety of IT artifacts, thus enhancing the frequency of artifact usage.

Although recent NPD research [9, 10] found that IT infrastructure had no association with IT artifact usage, the measure of usage in those studies was a dichotomous measure (whether a specific artifact was used or not) and the IT artifacts examined were aggregated (i.e., no distinction was made between general purpose IT artifacts and collaborative artifacts). Thus, given the importance of IT infrastructure to IT artifact implementation, we hypothesize:

H1: In the pre-development stage of NPD projects, firms with a more developed IT infrastructure will have a higher frequency of usage of: a) general purpose IT artifacts (e.g., MS Office, e-mail, and shared files/drives), and b) collaborative IT artifacts (e.g., social networking sites, blogs and micro-blogs, wikis, requirements management software, dedicated intranet sites, product lifecycle management tools, cloud-based file sharing, video conferencing, and dedicated open innovation tools). <sup>7</sup>

IT Embeddedness. The NPD literature defines IT embeddedness as the degree to which information systems and IT artifacts play a significant role in the development of new products, in the sharing of information among project team members, and in managing the interdependencies of the NPD project team [9, 25). In other words, IT embeddedness refers to the extent to which IT has become an assimilated part of the NPD process, enabling NPD activities to become more effective [9, 28]. Unless IT systems and artifacts are integrated into the NPD process, they are unlikely to be used and their benefits will not be realized [70]. Thus, IT embeddedness is similar to IT assimilation, which Armstrong and Sambamurthy [71, p. 305] defined as “the extent to which IT is infused into specific business activities.” As well, it is supported by Fichman and Nambisan [68] who argue that firms benefit more from IT when IT applications are fitted into their NPD process.

In order to be assimilated into the NPD process, firms need to build up the stock and use of IT artifacts over time [67]. Thus, firms with a high level of IT embeddedness have integrated IT artifacts into their NPD process [9, 25]. As a result, project team members are likely to have some familiarity with those artifacts and will use them for particular activities [9]. However, the presence of existing stocks of resources enables further resource accumulation [69]. Thus, the implantation of certain IT artifacts into the NPD process is likely to encourage greater usage of those same artifacts for different activities and more experimentation with other IT technologies. In fact, previous research has shown that IT embeddedness has a positive and direct relationship with IT artifact usage [9, 10]. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2: In the pre-development stage of NPD projects, firms with more embedded IT will exhibit higher frequency of usage of: a) general purpose IT artifacts, and b) collaborative IT artifacts.

Firm’s Outward Focus. The outward focus of a firm is an aspect of an organizational climate and culture that refers to the degree to which a firm sources and employs information from customers, develops a responsive strategy that will meet customer needs and wants, and implements that strategy [29, 30]. A number of studies have found that an outward focus on customers, and the utilization of knowledge gained from customers, allows firms to employ pertinent information more effectively in product ideation and design [31]. For example, in their study of 173 Australian firms, Reid and Brady [21] found that market orientation, including a customer orientation, had a significant influence on the pre-development stage of the NPD process.

In addition to customer information, innovative ideas may result from engagement with customers through customer partnerships or interaction using collaborative IT artifacts, including social media [6]. Customer involvement can enhance NPD efficiency and reduce the time-to-market of new products [31]. Frequent and effective communication with customers can open a project up to new information and

#

knowledge [67] and result in improvements to both the type and value of customer information a firm receives [32]. It is expected that firms with an outward focus will use a greater range of IT artifacts, especially collaborative technologies because such artifacts allow firms to connect with larger and more diverse assemblages of customers. In addition, we expect such firms will have higher usage of both general purpose IT artifacts and collaborative IT artifacts to communicate with these customers, facilitate customer insights, and enhance product ideation. Further, firms with an outward focus will likely use these artifacts to share this information among team members and project stakeholders in order to make better decisions about which concepts to progress. We therefore hypothesize:

H3: In the pre-development stage of NPD projects, firms with a stronger outward focus will exhibit higher frequency of usage of: a) general purpose IT artifacts, and b) collaborative IT artifacts.

Competitive Intensity. We define competitive intensity as a situation in which competition is fierce due to the number of competitors in the market and the lack of competitiveness, can influence how IT is used within a business process [72, 33]. Moreover, competitive scope and intensity have been identified as a complementary urce [67]. As competition intensifies, the results of a firm’s behavior are less deterministic and more random, and outcomes are influenced by the actions and strategies of a firm’s competitors [33]. More broadly, the literature on corporate entrepreneurship suggests that as competition intensifies, firms need to engage in greater levels of entrepreneurial activity, such as innovation, exploration, and strategic renewal [35]. Thus, we argue that firms facing competitive environments will have higher levels of usage of both general purpose IT artifacts and collaborative IT artifacts in order to increase the breadth of market information they acquire and utilize, and to expand the sources of that information. The increased breadth of information and information sources and greater use intensity of IT artifacts will enhance the efficiency and efficacy of communication both within the firm and with customers and other stakeholders. We thus hypothesize:

H4: In the pre-development stage of NPD projects, firms facing stronger competitive intensity will exhibit higher frequency of usage of general purpose IT artifacts, and b) collaborative IT artifacts.

## 3.4 IT Artifacts and Pre-Development Performance Outcomes

One way to improve pre-development outcomes is to utilize IT artifacts [22, 36]. With regard to the usage of general purpose IT artifacts such as e-mail, Microsoft Office, and shared files/drives, studies have found that such channels and platforms support knowledge integration [5] and dissemination, and enhance communication and collaboration [5]. E-mail, for example, is a frequently used channel that facilitates increased collaboration and communication, reduces interaction costs, and enhances information sharing and dissemination [5]. Shared drives provide access to project documentation and shared databases of work plans, schedules, product designs, engineering and marketing data, project planning documents, and project histories that support knowledge integration [5, 22]. Hence, shared drives enable access to all project information from a single entry point, and can facilitate knowledge accumulation and transfer within NPD teams, thereby improving product innovativeness [5]. However, Durmusoglu and Barczak [22] found that e-mail and shared drives had no impact on NPD effectiveness in the pre-development stage.

#

In the case of collaborative IT artifacts, research shows that Web-based video conferencing contributes to knowledge dissemination and communication and collaboration [5]. Virtual simulation applications enable computer simulation of new product concepts, and quicker and more realistic testing with customers [22]. These artifacts provide a cost-efficient method for evaluation and experimentation, and can market performance [22].

Research has also begun to investigate the increased use of social media and other Web 2.0 enterprise artifacts in the NPD process [6, 7] (e.g., wikis, blogs, social networking sites, and cloud file-sharing sites). Early evidence of the influence of these collaborative IT artifacts is mixed, however, with Marion et al. [6] demonstrating that, while concepts/prototypes generated and collaboration are positively influenced by the frequency of usage of wikis, cloud-based file sharing and dedicated open innovation tools in the development stage, these outcomes are not influenced by the frequency of use of blogs, Twitter, or social networking applications.

One of the critical outcomes of the pre-development stage of the NPD process relates to the number and quality of the ideas and concepts generated [6, 38]. Successful generation of concepts ultimately results from iterations of the idea for the new product and the proposed design concept and solution. Since IT artifacts can facilitate the collection and development of ideas from a multitude of sources within and outside the firm, greater use of both general purpose and collaborative IT artifacts may help increase the diversity of ideas generated [39], resulting in more new product concepts and prototypes.

NPD team collaboration at the front-end of innovation is defined as an affective process in which two or more individuals or units work together, have mutual understanding, share resources, and achieve collective goals [39, 40, 41]. The predevelopment stage thus requires efficient cross-functional collaboration as parallel actions are occurring that involve engineering, design, marketing, and manufacturing. In addition, such collaboration enables information acquisition and dissemination [38, 42]. Thus, we argue that the greater use of both general purpose and collaborative IT artifacts will enhance efficient team collaboration in addition to increasing the number of concepts and prototypes generated. Stated more formally:

H5: Firms who use general purpose IT artifacts more frequently in the predevelopment stage of NPD projects will: a) generate more new product concepts and prototypes, and b) have a greater level of efficient team collaboration.

H6: Firms who use collaborative IT artifacts more frequently in the predevelopment stage of NPD projects will: a) generate more new product concepts and prototypes, and b) have a greater level of efficient team collaboration.

## 3.5 Management Evaluation

As noted earlier, the pre-development stage is where initial product ideas are conceived, product concept definitions are formulated and evaluated, and projects are tentatively planned [17-20]. The development of a large number of ideas, concepts, and prototypes by the NPD team is likely to be viewed positively by management, as it allows for a wide range of opportunities for the firm. This, in turn, can generate a great deal of discussion and feedback from management and stage approval. In other research, Souder [43] found that harmony (or greater collaboration) between departments improves the success of NPD projects. Similarly, Kahn [40] indicated that collaboration, rather than simply interaction, is a primary factor for NPD success, and prior research indicates that greater collaboration between NPD team members leads to higher new product performance [78]. More recently, Nakata and Im [44] provided further evidence that cross-functional integration of NPD team members has a positive impact on new product performance. In other words, combining the skills, knowledge, and efforts of various functional team members through collaboration results in better performing NPD projects. Thus, we hypothesize:

H7: Project teams with a higher level of efficient team collaboration will generate a higher number of product concepts and prototypes in the pre-development stage.

H8: Project teams with a higher level of efficient team collaboration will have an increased likelihood of receiving management evaluation in the pre-development stage.

H9: Project teams who generate a higher number of product concepts and prototypes will have an increased likelihood of receiving management evaluation in the pre-development stage.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1 Sample and data

Data collection for this research utilized an online business management panel administered by Latitude Insights Ltd., an Australian market research field firm. Each of the panellists on the database was emailed an invitation to participate in the survey. In total, a sample of 152 managers was obtained in order to derive data for hypothesis testing. The sample is purposive and cross-functional in design, and focused on those participants who can provide appropriate insights into NPD and IT artifact phenomena of interest. Important benefits of this form of online data collection include the speed of data collection (one week), the ability it provides to carefully screen those individuals who complete the questionnaire in order to make sure they are appropriately qualified to answer the survey, and the ability to ensure that all questions in the survey are fully answered.

To ensure an appropriate sample selection, two qualifying questions were employed to screen the business panelists. First, respondents had to be heavily involved in the NPD process of their organizations; and second, they needed to have a strong understanding of how IT artifacts were used in their firms to manage the NPD process. Online data collection of this type has a number of advantages and disadvantages. While the panelists may not represent the full spectrum of Australian business, they nonetheless represent businesses that undertake NPD and use various IT artifacts to accomplish this task. Because NPD involves participants from many parts of an organization, and increasingly involves participants from multiple firms, we sought to understand multiple perspectives and increase the generalizability of our findings. This type of sample is consistent with previous research in NPD [2, 9, 10]. Table 2 shows the characteristics of our sample.

## 4.2 Measures

We adopted a project-level analysis for this research as the application of IT artifacts used for NPD predominantly occurs at the project level [46]. The design of our survey was based on recent work undertaken by Marion et al. [6]8. Measures for the study are reflective rather than formative, and were based on existing scales and adapted where necessary. Appendix 1 provides all measures used in the research, along with their sources and confirmatory factor loadings.

The design of the questionnaire and its administration online also contributed to minimizing issues of common method bias, in that the survey used constructs with known properties, thereby reducing the potential for item ambiguity [73]. The research firm’s instructions to respondents reassured them of their anonymity and that there would be full de-identification of data, thereby reducing evaluation apprehension and the potential for socially desirable responses [73]. Several of the scales and items within scales were reverse coded to ensure respondents had to think deeply about their answers. Items for each of the scales were randomized in blocks in the questionnaire, and the software used by the field house prompted respondents to fully complete questions.

To measure the impact of project-level IT artifact usage frequency on the outcomes of the NPD pre-development stage, a set of 16 IT artifacts was generated in conjunction with industry participants [6, 9, 12, 22]. Respondents were asked to indicate the extent of use of these IT artifacts within the pre-development stage of an NPD project using a 6-point Likert-type scale (0 = Not used, 5 = Used to a great extent). Previous research suggests that self-reported measures of usage correlate well with actual usage [9, 50]. Principal components analysis on the ratings for the 16 identified IT artifacts yielded two distinct categories (see Table 1). These two categories were classified as: 1) general purpose IT artifacts (i.e., Microsoft Office, e-mail, and shared files/drives), and 2) Collaborative IT artifacts (i.e., social networking sites, blogs and micro-blogs, virtual simulation software, wikis, requirements management software, dedicated Intranet sites, product lifecycle management tools, cloud-based file sharing, Twitter, video conferencing, and dedicated open innovation tools).

For our resource measures, the firm’s outward focus was based on the research of Remneland-Wikhamn and Wikhamn [29], and the past work of Barczak, Sultan, and Hultink [9] informed the measures for IT infrastructure and IT embeddedness. Competitive intensity of the market environment was based on the work of Jaworski and Kohli [51] and Langerak [52].

To measure pre-development stage outcomes, respondents were asked to indicate on a three-item scale whether the IT artifacts used influenced nine specific predevelopment stage performance outcomes (fewer/slower, average, or greater/faster than average) relative to projects that did not use these IT artifacts. The scales were developed based on previous appropriate metrics for the pre-development stage [47, 48, 49]. Principal components analysis on the outcome items yielded three pre-development stage outcome measures: efficient team collaboration, number of generated concepts and prototypes, and management evaluation and approval.

During this purification process, four items were removed for poor contribution to reliability and for their lack of use in the predevelopment stage of the NPD process. ## 6. Discussion

The present research addressed a pertinent and timely issue associated with the role of IT in improving the pre-development stage of the NPD process. Product innovation is at the heart of business growth strategies and the need to communicate and collaborate with multiple internal and external stakeholders is central to a successful NPD project. Drawing on the resource-based view of the firm, the approach developed in this study highlighted several resource-related antecedents to the frequency of IT artifact use in the pre-development stage of the NPD process, and examined how the usage frequency of IT artifacts are related to several performance measures at the pre-development stage of NPD projects.

## 6.1. IT Infrastructure

IT infrastructure was found to be related to the frequency of use of collaborative IT artifacts but not with the usage frequency of general purpose IT artifacts in the predevelopment stage of the NPD process. These findings partially support previous research that found that IT infrastructure had no association with IT usage [9, 10]. Importantly, these prior studies [9, 10] did not examine the frequency of usage of IT artifacts nor did they investigate many collaborative artifacts. Rather, they focused on more standard technologies (e.g., e-mail, scheduling and project management software, databases, and online surveys). Collectively, these results suggest that the existence and usage frequency of general purpose artifacts (i.e., e-mail, desktop tools, shared drives/files) may be so pervasive that they are no longer perceived as part of the infrastructure by product innovation staff, but rather as simply means for getting any kind of work accomplished. In fact, e-mail has been referred to as “more like a habitat than an application” [65, p30].

By contrast, collaborative IT artifacts are newer, are often promoted as cutting edge technologies for interaction with others, and while questions remain as to their value for NPD, having such artifacts as part of the firm’s infrastructure is necessary for NPD members to use them. Thus, these findings suggest that firms seeking more advanced collaboration and communications through frequent use of collaborative IT artifacts, such as cloud-based software, Twitter, and other platforms and applications, need to have a more developed and advanced IT infrastructure, and must ensure their internal NPD personnel and external collaborators understand the value of using these artifacts, have access to them, and can indeed use them proficiently.

## 6.2. IT Embeddedness

IT embeddedness is related to the usage frequency of general purpose IT artifacts, but not with the usage frequency of collaborative IT artifacts in the predevelopment stage of the NPD process. These results concur with previous research that found that IT embeddedness was positively and significantly associated with IT usage [9, 10]. However, again, these prior studies investigated the usage (not usage frequency) of more standard technologies (e.g., e-mail, scheduling and project management software, databases, and online surveys) [9, 10]. For many firms, artifacts tasks and thus are used very frequently. In fact, Ducheneaut and Pellotti [65] argue that e-mail is embedded into our work day and provides the means for most communication. Since these general purpose artifacts are pervasive in all organizations, NPD team members both inside and outside the organization appear to know how to use them effectively to ensure appropriate project management and communication throughout the pre-development stage. Accordingly, these general purpose IT artifacts and their frequent usage appear to be embedded into the NPD process and activities [68].

However, as noted earlier, collaborative IT artifacts such as social media are not yet being used to a large extent for NPD, implying that they are not yet embedded into the NPD process, including the pre-development stage. As a result, project teams maybe unsure as to how, when, and why to use them, or how to ensure external stakeholders are able to use them appropriately. Thus, our findings support the notion that unless IT artifacts are integrated into the NPD process, they will not be used frequently, and their benefits will not be realized [68, 70].

It is important to note that embeddedness may be a double-edged sword, especially when general purpose artifacts are so deeply ingrained within firms’ NPD processes. Such intense assimilation can make it difficult to redevelop an infrastructure and workflow process that enables the implementation of and routinizes potentially more powerful collaborative artifacts.

## 6.3.Firm’s Outward Focus

We found that a firm’s outward focus is positively related to the frequency of use of general purpose IT artifacts. NPD projects often require teams to collaborate with customers, suppliers, and other entities including market research agencies, packaging development firms, design and engineering firms, marketing communication agencies, and retailers. Firms may also deal directly with end users, especially in developing new industrial products. As general purpose IT artifacts are likely to be omnipresent within communicate and coordinate with these outside partners.

Interestingly, and contrary to our expectations, the results show that a firm’s outward focus has a negative relationship with the degree to which collaborative IT artifacts are used in the pre-development stage of the NPD process. This finding contrasts somewhat with the findings of Tambe, Hitt, and Brynjolfsson (62) who found that firms with an increased external focus have better NPD results.

## 6.4. Competitive Intensity

The competitive intensity present in a firm’s market environment is related to the usage frequency of general purpose IT artifacts, but has no significant relationship with the frequency of use of collaborative IT artifacts. Firms facing competitive environments are often confronted with a dilemma about whether to invest in new systems and processes, including IT, or whether to use available funds to drive sales and marketing communications activities. The present research suggests that firms in highly competitive environments are ‘sticking to their knitting,’ and focusing on using general purpose IT artifacts frequently and avoiding overinvesting in infrastructures that environments, especially for SME’s, often means that managers are resource and time constrained and fall back on IT artifacts they know well, rather than trying to pursue new applications, channels, and software.

## 6.5. IT Artifacts and Pre-Development Outcomes

It was found that the usage frequency of general purpose IT artifacts by a firm has a direct positive relationship with the number of concepts and prototypes generated by the NPD team, but is not related to the degree to which a firm engages in efficient team collaboration. The former finding echoes the results of Marion et al. [9] who found that the frequency of use of general purpose IT artifacts were associated with the number of concepts/prototypes generated in the development stage of the NPD process. Combined, these results provide evidence of the value of such IT technologies for NPD activities.

The lack of association with efficient team collaboration contradicts previous research indicating that general purpose IT artifacts facilitate increased communication and collaboration [5]. One argument for this finding may be that the prevalence of these artifacts and their usage has either lessened their impact, or created a plateau with respect to effective and efficient team collaboration. Focusing only on e-mail, Ducheneaut and Pellotti [65] contend that this artifact is “seriously overloaded” [p. 37] indicating that people are inundated with e-mails at work. Thus, the frequent use of email and the other general purpose artifacts may actually be detracting from efficient collaboration among NPD personnel. For example, developing a new product concept as part of a NPD project might be done by an individual using a word processing or presentation application, and emailed to colleagues for comment and further work. This may not be as efficient as working collaboratively via a cloud-based application where the team can work simultaneously, but nonetheless results in a usable outcome. Our research may thus be capturing a phase of transition in some firms where product developers understand how to use general purpose IT artifacts very well, where project work may still be organized around their use and produces appropriate outcomes, but where teams may be working in a linear fashion rather than in an interactive and collaborative manner.

Our study found that the usage frequency of collaborative IT artifacts is related to both the degree to which concepts and prototypes are generated by a firm, and also to the degree to which a firm engages in efficient team collaboration. Our finding regarding collaborative IT artifacts indicates that fir seeking collaboration, communication, and overall efficiency in the pre-development stage of the NPD process need to consider the frequent use of these platforms and channels. The need to develop competency in the use of collaborative IT artifacts is reinforced by the finding that efficient team collaboration also has a significant relationship with the number of generated concepts and prototypes. Our research suggests that NPD project teams need to be ambidextrous in their approach, remaining effective in the frequency of use and application of general purpose IT artifacts, but open and skilled in the application of collaborative IT artifacts and the improved interactivity, information flow, and knowledge management afforded by these new technologies.

## 6.6. Management Evaluation

The findings show that management evaluation, which includes timely feedback from managers and the rapid approval of projects, is related to the number of generated concepts and prototypes, and to efficient collaboration of team members. This concurs with Cooper [42] who argues that one goal of the pre-development stage is to have a large number of ideas/concepts. Further, these findings support prior work that found that collaboration improves NPD performance [44, 78]. Thus, it appears that in the predevelopment stage of an NPD project, when uncertainty about the market and product exists, management evaluation and approval is associated with the ability of the team to work together effectively and efficiently in developing new concepts and prototypes.

## 7. Implications and Conclusions

## 7.1 Managerial implications

The findings of this research have several important implications for IT and NPD managers. In particular, the research points to an increasing role for IT departments and specialists to be more fully integrated into a firm’s NPD program, and to provide increased support for NPD projects. First, if firms want their NPD teams to utilize collaborative IT artifacts frequently, including social networking, project wikis, weblogs and micro-blogs, they need to put into place an IT infrastructure that trains and supports NPD teams and project stakeholders in the appropriate use of such platforms and applications. A robust and effective IT infrastructure is a major resource for a firm, thus, expanding a firm’s current IT infrastructure to include new IT artifacts should facilitate frequent usage of those technologies for NPD. Based on our findings, this will in turn lead to more efficient team collaboration and help increase the number of concepts/prototypes generated in the pre-development stage.

Secondly, firms who wish to be actively engaged in open innovation need to develop an IT infrastructure in partnership with the external entities with whom they wish to collaborate. This is necessary to ensure that these external partners can gain access to and engage with the firm’s systems, thereby enabling knowledge sharing and dissemination. The increased adoption of open innovation in projects will require the firm’s IT specialists to understand how to grow the firm’s own capabilities and also to understand how to integrate systems and applications with those of key collaborators [37]. It is only in this way that frequent usage of IT artifacts among innovation partners can occur. Third, firms who want to ensure the frequent use of collaborative IT artifacts will have to embed them within their NPD process. Currently, firms seem unsure as to which platforms to use, for what purpose, and how to best use particular artifacts in the context of NPD. As firms gain more experience and knowledge about what works best, when, and how, certain applications may be able to be embedded within and routinized for particular activities and stages of the NPD process.

## 7.2. Theoretical implications

This study offers several important theoretical implications. First, this research adds to the emerging literature on the use of IT artifacts for NPD [8, 9, 10] thereby answering the call for more studies that integrate IT and innovation [8, 56]. By doing so, and by focusing on usage frequency, it helps to shed more light on the increasing role of IT in NPD.

Second, investigating the effect of IT resources (i.e., infrastructure and collaborative IT artifacts) in the pre-development stage of the NPD process offers a deeper understanding of the value of IT for NPD activities. Our results suggest that frequent use of IT artifacts has clear value in that they influence NPD phase outcomes. However, their worth cannot be leveraged unless they are part of the IT infrastructure and embedded in the NPD process and activities.

Finally, from the perspective of the RBV of the firm [3, 4, 13], this research provides support for the relationship between IT resources, complementary resources, and IT capabilities. Further, it examines these relationships within a particular stage of the NPD process, an area of research that has been lacking [23].

## 7.3 Limitations

As with all studies, this research has several limitations worth noting. First, the study examines the constructs of interest at one point in time, across industries. Thus, the results cannot be generalized to particular industries or businesses. Future research could examine the conceptual framework in specific industries to determine whether the relationships in the model hold. Second, the study uses single respondents. However, the use of screening questions to select respondents provides assurance that the informants were well qualified to report on the variables in the study. Third, the sample is restricted to Australian companies, limiting the generalizability to other countries and continents. Future research could explore the research questions examining firms in other countries with more or less developed IT infrastructures. Fourth, the research adopts a multi-artifact approach to the front-end of the NPD process. In the future, it could be useful to examine the usage frequency and importance of specific IT artifacts and their influence on any stage of the NPD process; for example how ideation software applications are used inter-organizationally to foster co-creation of ideas in the pre-development stage.

## 7.4. Conclusions

In conclusion, it is well understood that the pre-development stage of the NPD process is often complex and risky, and that firms are increasingly looking towards IT to improve the management and success of this process. Nonetheless, to date there has been little empirical evidence to inform understanding of the factors that influence usage frequency of various IT artifacts, and how the frequency of usage of these channels and platforms contribute to the performance outcomes associated with predevelopment activities. By drawing on the resource-based view of the firm, our results indicate that IT embeddedness, firm outward focus, and competitive intensity have a positive impact on the usage frequency of general purpose IT artifacts. By contrast, IT infrastructure had a positive effect while firm focus had a negative effect on the frequency of use of collaborative IT artifacts. In addition, the usage frequency of both general purpose and collaborative IT artifacts influenced the number of generated concepts and prototypes while firms who frequently incorporate collaborative IT artifacts into the pre-development stage also have more efficient team collaboration.

## Biography

## Mike Reid

Mike Reid is Associate Professor of Marketing at the School of Economics, Finance and Marketing, RMIT University. He publishes on product innovation management with an emphasis on success factors. He has published in Journal of Marketing Management, Journal of Advertising, and Journal of Strategic Marketing amongst others.

## Erik-Jan Hultink

Erik-Jan Hultink is Professor of New Product Marketing at the Faculty of Industrial Design Engineering, Delft University of Technology, The Netherlands. He publishes on product innovation management in such journals as the Journal of the Academy in Marketing Science, and the Journal of Product Innovation Management amongst others. He is the co-founder and board member of the Dutch chapter of the Product Development and Management Association (PDMA).

## Tucker Marion

Tucker Marion is The Sam and Nancy Altschuler Faculty Fellow, Bornstein Endowed Faculty Fellow, Professor of Technological Entrepreneurship at the D'Amore-McKim School of Business, Northeastern University. He publishes on Product development processes and tools that foster improved metrics such as time-to-market and commercialization success rates. He has published in the Journal of product Innovation Management, Journal of Business Venturing, Sloan Management Review amongst others.

## Gloria Barczak

Gloria Barczak is Professor of Marketing, at the D'Amore-McKim School of Business, Northeastern University. She has researched and published extensively in the area of product innovation management with particular emphasis on the NPD process and the management of NPD teams. She has published in Industrial Marketing Management, Business Horizons, and the Journal of Product Innovation Management, amongst others.
