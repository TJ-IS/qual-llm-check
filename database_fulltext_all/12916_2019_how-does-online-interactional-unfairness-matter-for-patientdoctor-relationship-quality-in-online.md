# How does online interactional unfairness matter for patient–doctor relationship quality in online health consultation? The contingencies of professional seniority and disease severity

Xiaofei Zhang<sup>a</sup>, Xitong Guo<sup>b</sup>, Kee-hung Lai<sup>c</sup> and Wu Yi <sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Business School, Nankai University, Tianjin, China; <sup>b</sup>School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, China; <sup>c</sup>Faculty of Business, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Kowloon, Hong Kong; <sup>d</sup>College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, Tianjin, China.

## ABSTRACT

In recent years, the development of online health-consultation platforms has drawn signi<sup>fi</sup>cant research attention to online patient–doctor relationship development, particularly in developing countries, where there are limited health care resources for each patient. However, online interactional unfairness in patient–doctor relationship development has been largely overlooked in the context of information and communication technology (ICT). This study proposes and tests a model that examines how online interactional unfairness (ie, interpersonal unfairness and informational unfairness) in<sup>fl</sup>uences online patient–doctor relationship quality and the contingent roles of a doctor’s professional seniority and a patient’s disease severity. Using archival data with 83,553 observations from a leading online health-consultation platform in China, this study employed rare-event logistic regression to test the model. The results show that online interpersonal unfairness and online informational unfairness have negative and positive e<sup>f</sup>ects on relationship-quality incentive, respectively, in the Chinese health care context and that a doctor’s professional seniority and a patient’s disease severity strengthen the link between unfairness perceptions and relationship-quality incentive. This study advances the knowledge of online patient–doctor relationship development in ICT-based health care in China and provides practical insights for online health care stakeholders for managing unfairness in the health care context.

## 1. Introduction

The patient–doctor relationship is an important part of health care practice and is essential for developing high-quality health services. Given its practical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance, ample research attention has been devoted to examining the antecedents and outcomes of patient–doctor relationships (Duan, Qiu, Yu, & Hu, 2014; Beckman, Markakis, Suchman, & Frankel, 1994; Pilnick & Dingwall, 2011; Vick & Scott, 1998). In recent years, the use of information and communication technologies (ICTs), such as online communities, online consultations, and mobile applications, has increased in popularity in the health care setting, particularly in China, where health care resources per patient are limited. Among these, online health consultation (OHC) is an emerging service and o<sup>f</sup>ers an alternative source of health and medical information for patients (Lu, Shaw, & Gustafson 2011). This health service enables patients to make inquiries regarding their health conditions and obtain professional health information and suggestions from online doctors.

Through this service, patients can even establish an enduring online relationship with doctors. Prior literature posits that service quality is not a su<sup>fi</sup>cient indicator of the success of interaction-based services, but the relationship quality between service stakeholders is (Crosby, Evans, & Cowles, 1990). Examining such online patient–doctor relationships in an OHC context is important because it di<sup>f</sup>ers from the traditional patient–doctor relationship. In particular, the online relationship is primarily formed through interactions between patients and doctors through ICTs and is rarely in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by o<sup>fl</sup>ine health institutions, treatments, or medicines (eg, waiting time in hospitals and medicine quality). Further, this relationship is particularly important for the health care industry in China. On one hand, the ICT-based health service is an e<sup>f</sup>ective approach to alleviate the supply-and-demand contradiction of health care resources in China (Wu & Po, 2016). On the other hand, the patient–doctor relationship is an important resource for diagnosing diseases in Chinese society, which is culturally collectivism oriented (Lowry, Zhang, Zhou, & Fu, 2010). Hence, developing high-quality patient–doctor relationships in an OHC becomes the top priority for both health care practitioners and patients in China. Yet, to the best of our knowledge, the underlying mechanism of patient–doctor relationship development in ICTbased platforms is not clear in the Chinese health care context.

The introduction of health care ICTs changes the power structure formed in the traditional patient– doctor relationship, in which doctors play a dominant role (Burkhardt & Brass, 1990). Patients now can search for disease-related information from various sources with the help of ICTs. They even have a certain degree of medical knowledge of their diseases before consulting a doctor. Hence, patients and doctors are encouraged to interact with each other through an equal status of “mutual participation”, with shared power and responsibility (Rider, Malik, & Chevassut, 2014). Although ICTs extend patients information sources, patients solely rely on professional advice from doctors when it comes to <sup>fi</sup>nal decision-making for disease treatment due to information sensitivity and the outcome severity of their health problems. Therefore, online interactions between doctors and patients are still largely unfair as the former possess professional knowledge that the latter lack. Online interactional unfairness can also be a critical challenge in online patient–doctor relationship development (Toombs, 1987).

Although the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of interactional (un)fairness has been widely acknowledged in the literature (Cropanzano, Ambrose, Greenberg, & Cropanzano, 2001; Turel & Connelly, 2013), its insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant role in individuals’ decision-making has also been demonstrated in prior studies (Leung, Smith, Wang, & Sun, 1996; Zahra & Newey, 2009; Frenkel, Li, & Restubog, 2012; Ucho, 2014). The inconsistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings on interactional unfairness deserve further research attention. To this end, this study aims to take two approaches to unravel these inconsistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, this study proposes examining online interactional unfairness from a multidimensional perspective rather than a unidimensional perspective in the OHC context. In particular, interactional unfairness is speci<sup>fi</sup>ed into subdimensions such as online informational fairness, which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as patients’ perceptions of unfairness on the unequal information provided by doctors in online interactions, and online interpersonal fairness, which refers to patients’ perceptions of unfairness on the treatment that they receive from doctors in online interactions (Greenberg, 1993; Colquitt, 2001). The multidimensional perspective provides a more <sup>fi</sup>nely tuned understanding of the e<sup>f</sup>ects of interactional unfairness on patient–doctor relationship development in the online context. Accordingly, the <sup>fi</sup>rst research objective of this study is to investigate the impacts of online informational fairness and online interpersonal fairness on patient–doctor online relationship quality in Chinese OHCs.

Beyond that, and secondly, fairness theory indicates that in di<sup>f</sup>erent situations, individuals have di<sup>f</sup>erent unfairness perceptions on the interactions in a social exchange relationship (Huppertz, Arenson & Evans, 1978; Chen, Brockner, & Greenberg, 2003). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Chen et al. (2003) posit that the di<sup>f</sup>erent relative status and expectations of the interactions induce di<sup>f</sup>erent fairness perceptions among individuals. To unravel the inconsistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings on interactional unfairness in the prior literature, the relative status di<sup>f</sup>erences between stakeholders and expectations of interactions are critical factors pertinent to the stakeholders unfairness perceptions. With that in mind, the doctors’ professional seniority re<sup>fl</sup>ects the status di<sup>f</sup>erence between doctors and patients in the OHC context as it indicates the professional-knowledge asymmetry in online interactions. On the other hand, the patients’ disease severity represents their expectations from and the valued importance of online patient–doctor interactions. Accordingly, the second research objective of this study is to explore the contingent roles of the doctor’s professional seniority and the patient’s disease severity on the efects of online interactional unfairness on online patient–doctor relationship quality.

To achieve the research objectives previously mentioned, this study develops a theoretical model to investigate how online interactional unfairness in<sup>fl</sup>uences patient–doctor relationship quality (manifested by relationship-quality incentive) and the moderating roles of the two boundary factors on the links. The research model is tested using a large objective dataset on patient–doctor interactions from a well-known OHC platform in China.

This paper makes several theoretical contributions. First, although the patient–doctor relationship has been a hot research topic recently, most prior research has focused on o<sup>fl</sup>ine patient–doctor relationships or Western research contexts. Our research is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst that empirically explores the role of online interactional unfairness in online patient–doctor relationship development in the Chinese health care industry, among which online interpersonal relationships serve as critical resources for disease management. Second, although ICT-based platforms enable to develop roughly equal online relationships (Ou, Pavlou, & Davison, 2014), this study suggests that interactional unfairness is still a critical challenge for relationships developed in the online context. This study develops a multidimensional perspective of online interactional unfairness to investigate the underlying mechanism of developing patient–doctor relationships in OHCs. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it contributes to the literature of online social interaction by demonstrating the distinct roles of online informational unfairness and online interpersonal unfairness. Third, accompanied with the contextual e<sup>f</sup>ects of a doctor’s professional seniority and a patient’s disease severity, this research contributes to clarifying the inconsistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings on interactional unfairness in the prior literature. The study also makes certain practical contributions. On one hand, this study provides strategies to doctors on conducting productive online interactions with patients by balancing information unfairness and interpersonal unfairness. On the other hand, this study provides insights into patient stress management from the perspective of interactional unfairness given the doctor’s professional seniority and the patient’s disease severity.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Online health consultation

In health care, many patient-centred health care websites (eg, WebMD and haodf.com) have been developed to scramble emerging market segments (Kamis, Yao, & Kim, 2014). According to patientdirected health care activities, these websites can be broadly classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into <sup>fi</sup>ve categories: consultation, records, monitoring, information, and community (Kamis et al., 2014). OHC refers to a remote patient-and-doctor consultation through web-based instant and noninstant tools (Al-Mahdi, Gray, & Lederman, 2015). This new type of health service mode provides patients with an alternative approach to acquiring health services. Furthermore, from an individual perspective, an OHC represents an e<sup>fi</sup>cient way for patients to access doctors worldwide for medical decisionmaking (Rittenhouse & Shortell, 2009). From a macroscopic perspective, an OHC can satisfy, to a certain extent, the increasing demands of health services with a more e<sup>fi</sup>cient distribution of existing health care resources.

Despite the emergence of this new health service mode in recent years, there has been scant empirical research on it. A comprehensive literature review was conducted by screening papers in the leading IS journals, including the senior scholars’ basket of journals, resulting in fewer than 20 relevant papers (please refer Appendix A). From the review, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that even though research on online health care has been conducted in di<sup>f</sup>erent cultures, there is no research focused on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c cultural setting. Among them, Lu et al. (2011) investigated why low-income breastcancer patients use OHC services and found that the services could be an e<sup>f</sup>ective complement to traditional health care. Their study also demonstrates that the use of an OHC can increase the patients’ roles in health care participation and medical decisionmaking and enhance their relationship with doctors. Yi, Yoon, Davis, and Lee (2013) examined how patients develop trust in health information from online consultations from the perspectives of information quality and source expertise. Yang et al. (2015) investigated the roles of doctors’ online and o<sup>fl</sup>ine information in patients’ selection of doctors with whom they consult. Additionally, Yang et al. (2015) explored the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of two critical aspects of the online patient–doctor interaction process – interaction frequency and response speed – on patients’ satisfaction with doctors in an OHC.

The outcomes of OHCs and the corresponding determinants have been previously studied, such as service quality, satisfaction, and trust. However, there is not a universally accepted construct proposed to manifest the overall outcomes of this interactionbased online service. As a service, the concept of service quality may be relevant. However, prior lit erature posits that service quality is not su<sup>fi</sup>cient for interaction-based services because successful interactions can also lead to an enduring customer–provider relationship (Crosby et al., 1990). Therefore, relationship quality has been proposed to measure the overall outcomes of interaction-based services (Athanasopoulou & Giovanis, 2015; Crosby et al., 1990). High relationship quality theory infers that customers rely on the providers’ integrity and have con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the providers’ further performance based on their evaluation of their past performance (Crosby et al., 1990). As relationship quality is di<sup>fi</sup>- cult to measure by objective data, this paper draws on relationship-quality incentive (ie, the incentives provided by patients to strengthen relationship with doctors during the online interactions) to manifest whether the relationship quality is high or not. In the OHCs, the relationship-quality incentives are provided during the online interactions, which not only re<sup>fl</sup>ect the patients’ satisfaction with their doctor but also indicates patients’ trust in the doctors’ future performance. Therefore, we rely on relationshipquality incentive to conceptualise the outcomes of online patient–doctor interactions in OHCs.

## 2.2. Healthcare ICTs in China

ICT-based health services began to emerge in China in 2000, when there were a plethora of health-related websites launched, including haodf.com and dxy.cn (Wu & Po, 2016). In 2014, the market share for these web-based services was about \$1.6 billion in China, with an expected growth to \$2.9 billion by 2017 (Wu & Po, 2016). This market includes health portals, health ecosystems, online communities, OHCs, and mobile-device-based services such as healthinformation apps, mHealth devices, and drug-choice apps. As an e<sup>f</sup>ective approach, health care ICTs are widely used by di<sup>f</sup>erent health care providers to enable remote patients to contact doctors or access real-time health services (Wu & Po, 2016).

The increasing popularity of health care ICTs in China assists in signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly relieving the unbalanced allocation of health care resources. In China, about 80% of health care resources are allocated in the urban areas, especially in several large cities (Liang et al., 2012). This unbalanced allocation makes it di<sup>fi</sup>cult for patients to receive high-quality health services from highly ranked hospitals. On the other hand, there is a wide di<sup>f</sup>usion of ICTs across China. There are more than 0.75 billion Internet users in 2017, and 26.70% of them reside in the rural areas (CNNIC, 2017). The popularity of the Internet provides a viable approach to reallocating health care resources through health care ICTs such as online health platforms and communities. The report shows that those who used such tools for health purposes account for about 22% of the Internet population in China (CNNIC, 2016). Health care ICTs provide these users with new ways to obtain health information or manage their health conditions by enabling them to consult doctors from nationwide hospitals (Wu & Po, 2016). Prior evidence shows that such health care ICTs can relieve rural–urban health-care-resource disparities (Mein Goh, Gao, & Agarwal, 2016).

Other than the popularity of health care ICTs in China, the role of such ICTs is also unique in Eastern culture. Prior literature has posited that culture plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the use of ICTs, especially emphasising the di<sup>f</sup>erences between Western and Eastern cultures (Warkentin, Charles-Pauvers, & Chau, 2015; Zhou, Jin, Fang, & Vogel, 2015). Zhang, Lowry, Zhou, and Fu (2007) argue that Western culture focuses on individualism, whereas Eastern culture emphasises collectivism. In contrast to individuals in Western countries, Chinese people tend to develop strong interpersonal relationships (ie, guanxi) with others and exhibit high personal trust (ie, xinyong) in those with whom they have strong relationships due to the country’s emphasis on collectivism (Lowry et al., 2010). This cultural di<sup>f</sup>erence characterises the decision-making or trust-building process of Chinese based on interpersonal relationships in adopting ICTs (Pb et al., 2010) and using ICTs for interaction with others in order to develop interpersonal relationships (Cui, Zhang, & Lowry, 2017; Martinsons, 2008). These relationships developed through ICTs are more important in situations of information asymmetry (Martinsons, 2008). The unique role of ICTs in Eastern culture leads health care ICTs to enable a new approach for patients to develop relationships with doctors, such as guanxi (Chib, Phuong, Si, & Hway, 2013) in China. For instance, using OHCs in China, patients and doctors can develop swift online relationships for mutual understanding, reciproca favours, and relationship harmony (Ou et al.,

2014). With these swift relationships, doctors are able to be better understood by patients and receive more incentives and o<sup>fl</sup>ine visits from patients. Meanwhile, patients can also have their health conditions better diagnosed by doctors and obtain more information about their health. Hence, health care ICTs (eg, OHCs) enable patients to develop online interpersonal relationships with doctors in China, and such relationships are critical resources for patients’ disease management.

Even though it has become common for the Chinese to use ICT-based health services, ICT-based health care is not immune to unfairness issues. Chinese patients often have feelings of unfairness regarding the health services they receive and the medical costs they pay (Tucker et al., 2015). The reasons are threefold. First, most Chinese hospitals are public, and doctors are more bureaucratic, leading to inadequate attention to individual patients preferences, needs, and satisfaction (Hsiao, 2007- ). Second, as the hospitals are public, doctors’ salaries are relatively low and have to rely on kickbacks and commissions for higher income (Tucker et al., 2015). However, in<sup>fl</sup>ated medical costs give patients the impression that doctors accord higher priority to their side incomes than to healing patients (Tucker et al., 2015), thereby defeating the purpose of public hospitals. Third, due to the unbalanced distribution of health care resources in di<sup>f</sup>erent areas, many doc tors are relatively busy and have limited time to spend on each patient in the hospital. It is more common for Chinese patients to feel that doctors are largely driven by economic pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and that the medical information or treatments are not worth what they have paid (Tucker et al., 2015). Accordingly, they will feel unfairly treated, and this unfairness perception has led to numerous protests of Chinese hospitals. In this sense, patients’ unfairness perceptions are more common in China than in Western countries. This situation of unfairness is still prominent in online patient–doctor interactions, where the interactions happen mainly through texting, with limited verbal cues.

In sum, even though Chinese patients perceive unfairness when interacting with doctors, they still tend to develop quality relationships with the latter in the hope for better medical treatment. Health care ICTs provide new approaches for patient–doctor interaction; however, little is known from prior works about the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online interactional unfairness for the success of OHCs.

## 2.3. Online interactional unfairness

Perceived fairness is an individual’s judgement on whether the exchange events he or she faces is fair considering di<sup>f</sup>erent dimensions (Turel &

Connelly, 2013). Fairness theory proposes three dimensions of fairness: distributive fairness, procedural fairness, and interactional fairness (Alexander & Ruderman, 1987; Bies & Shapiro, 1987). Distributive fairness is the perception of fairness in the resource allocation and outcome distribution of an event (Adams, 1965). Procedural fairness refers to the perception of fairness in the outcomedistribution processes between stakeholders in an event (Thibaut & Walker, 1975). Interactional fairness re<sup>fl</sup>ects the perception of fairness in the interaction process, such as whether they are treated equally when interacting with others (Bies & Moag, 1986). In particular, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne online interactional unfairness as the perception of unfairness in online interactions.

The antecedents and consequences of fairness dimensions were initially studied in organisational contexts (Colquitt, 2001; Lowry, Posey, Bennett & Roberts, 2015; Roch & Shanock, 2006). Beyond that, Rupp and Spencer (2006), in one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst studies to analyse fairness in a service encounter, examined how interactional fairness between emotional labour and consumers a<sup>f</sup>ects employees’ emotions. Carr (2007) adopts fairness theory to explore the e<sup>f</sup>ect of fairness between service, consumer, and provider on service satisfaction. In the online context, Turel, Yuan, and Connelly (2008) demonstrate that fairness factors (informational, distributive, and procedural fairness) contribute to consumers’ reuse of e-services through trust. Fang and Chiu (2010) also observe the mediating role of trust on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of fairness perceptions on online contributions in online communities. Additionally, Shi, Hong, Wang, and Pavlou (2012) adopt this theory to explore user performance in online social-referral incentive systems.

According to Lowry et al. (2015), individuals mainly draw on information from a speci<sup>fi</sup>c event for judging fairness of the event (eg, an online patient–doctor interaction). Hence, during the event of an online patient–doctor interaction, what matters for the patient’s fairness judgement of the event is the interaction process rather than the distribution of outcomes or the distribution process. Drawing on the interactional fairness perspective of fairness theory, this study focuses on how online interactional unfairness between patients and doctors in<sup>fl</sup>uences their relationship development in OHCs.

Given the two distinct aspects of the interaction process (ie, what is communicated and how it is communicated), interactional unfairness consists of interpersonal unfairness and informational unfairness (Greenberg, 1993). In particular, interpersonal unfairness refers to perceptions of unfairness on the treatment that people receive in the interaction, such as whether they are treated without politeness, dignity, or respect (Greenberg, 1993; Colquitt, 2001). In the OHC context, online interpersonal unfairness indicates patients unfairness perceptions regarding whether they are personally treated by doctors in contrast to their perceptions of how they treat the doctors. Informational unfairness captures perceptions of unfairness on the ratio of information provided by both sides in the interactions to convey the outcome allocation (Greenberg, 1993; Colquitt, 2001). In the OHC context, online informational unfairness means patients provide more information about their health conditions compared with what they receive from doctors. As the main purpose of conducting online consultations is to obtain enough health-related information for medical advice, this online informational unfairness will leave patients health demands unsatis<sup>fi</sup>ed. Prior studies have shown that informational unfairness is di<sup>f</sup>erent from interpersonal unfairness (Greenberg, 1993; Karriker and Williams, 2007; Roch & Shanock, 2006), although most previous studies still view interactional unfairness as a unidimensional construct.

Online informational unfairness and online interpersonal unfairness are especially relevant and important in the ICT-based interactions. In o<sup>fl</sup>ine patient– doctor interactions, it is impossible for patients or doctors to record all details to <sup>fi</sup>gure out the actual information provided by di<sup>f</sup>erent stakeholders. When there is a lack of information, it is easier for patients to consult the doctors further. However, in online patient–doctor interactions, there are accurate and complete records of all interactions, and both doctors and patients are able to retrieve their historical interactions at their convenience. Thus, online informational unfairness is an important feature pertinent to online patient–doctor interactions. Furthermore, doctors usually respond quickly to patients’ queries in o<sup>fl</sup>ine interactions (face to face), and patients do not need to wait a long time for responses, even when there are new questions. However, in online patient–doctor interactions, it is common for patients to wait a long time, which more easily evokes feelings of online interpersonal unfairness. Therefore, online informational unfairness and online interpersonal unfairness are prominent in online patient–doctor interactions and re<sup>fl</sup>ect the ICT features of interpersonal interactions.

The impacts of interactional (un)fairness of interaction outcomes had been broadly studied. Positive e<sup>f</sup>ects of interactional fairness were found on the relationship quality between supervisor and employees (Karriker & Williams, 2007; Masterson, Lewis, Goldman, & Taylor, 2000), continued use of e-services (Turel & Connelly, 2013), and consumer satisfaction (Ambrose, Hess, & Ganesan, 2007) among others. Other studies found insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant relationships between interactional (un)fairness and its outcomes (Leung et al., 1996; Zahra & Newey, 2009; Frenkel et al., 2012; Ucho, 2014), especially in organisational contexts (Orth, 2002; Del Río-Lanza, Vázquez-Casielles, & Díaz-Martín, 2009; Kwortnik & Han, 2011; Kuo & Wu, 2012). For instance, Kuo and Wu (2012) <sup>fi</sup>nd that interactional fairness is not related to consumers’ positive emotions on websites. These inconsistent results may be attributable to the missing examination of di<sup>f</sup>erent subdimensions of interactional fairness (Ambrose et al., 2007) and the potential boundary conditions (Treadway, Witt, Stoner, Jansen Perry, & Shaughnessy, 2013).

Fairness theory indicates that in di<sup>f</sup>erent situations, such as those where there are di<sup>f</sup>erent relative statuses and di<sup>f</sup>erent outcome expectations, individuals will have di<sup>f</sup>erent fairness perceptions in a social-exchange relationship (Huppertz et al., 1978; Chen et al., 2003). In the OHC context, the relative status of doctors and patients is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the professional seniority of a doctor because patients are generally in similar situations with respect to health care (information seekers). Outcome expectations are embodied in disease severity because patients with high-severity diseases generally have high expectations (Weinfurt et al., 2003). These two factors are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and unique in health care but have received scant research attention in fairness literature. Thus, this study draws on interpersonal unfairness and informational unfairness to scrutinise interactional unfairness from its subdimensions and also examines the boundary conditions of a doctor’s professional seniority and disease severity under which online unfairness perceptions may contribute to patient–doctor relationship quality di<sup>f</sup>erently in an OHC.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Figure 1 shows the research model of this study. We explore the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of online interpersonal unfairness and online informational unfairness on relationship-quality incentive between patients and doctors and how such in<sup>fl</sup>uences are contingent on the doctors’ professional seniority and patients’ disease severity. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we test six hypotheses in this research.

Individuals who have been treated unfairly by other people are motivated to terminate the relationship and reestablish a new, fair relationship with others (Blau, 1964; Posey, Bennett, Roberts, & Lowry, 2011). When patients experience online interpersonal unfairness in an OHC, they will negatively evaluate the interaction with doctors (Van Dijke, De Cremer, Bos, & Sche<sup>f</sup>erlie, 2009). This process is particularly prominent among Chinese patients as they rely more on interpersonal relationships. Hence, they are less likely to improve these online relationships by providing incentives in the future. On the other hand, when patients experience politeness, dignity, and respect in online interactions with doctors, they will have higher satisfaction with previous interactions, trust the doctors, and develop quality online relationships with the doctors by sending incentives in the online interactions (Oliver & Swan, 1989). Therefore, we propose the following:

H1: Patients’ online interpersonal unfairness is negatively associated with relationship-quality incentive in OHCs.

Unlike interpersonal unfairness, informational unfairness in health care leads to “the setting up of a relationship of trust and con<sup>fi</sup>dence” (Arrow, 1963, p. 965). Given that patients do not have the same health knowledge as doctors (ie, information asymmetry; Rochaix, 1989), they evaluate the interaction process by their own perceptions of a doctor’s abilities instead of direct observations on the interaction process (Arrow, 1963). Then, they will believe that the doctor is doing his or her best to help them (Kolstad &

Chernew, 2009). When patients receive unfair response information from the doctor in an OHC, they will attribute this situation to reasons from their own perspective rather than complaining or questioning the doctor (Wang & Mattila, 2011). In particular, as doctors need patient information to make medical decisions (Parks, Xu, Chu & Lowry, 2017), online informational unfairness triggers patients to rethink whether the information they provided is su<sup>fi</sup>cient or not for the doctors. They thus do not treat this unfairness as a sign of a bad relationship but as an indication of a need to make informational e<sup>f</sup>orts, such as giving incentives to doctors. Given the importance of interpersonal relationships in China and that patients rely on and place more value on interpersonal relationships for problemsolving, they will tend to improve their online relationships with the doctors to obtain further information by providing incentives to the doctors. Thus, we hypothesise the following:

H2: Patients’ online informational unfairness is positively associated with relationship-quality incentive in OHCs.

A doctor’s professional seniority is represented by his or her clinical title. In the Chinese health care system, doctors have four hierarchical clinical titles (ie, resident doctor, attending doctor, associate chief doctor, and chief doctor) that indicate their professional abilities and experiences in hospitals. In OHCs, patients are of a di<sup>f</sup>erent relative status from the doctors due to the bureaucracy of Chinese doctors; thus, patients perceive themselves as having a lower relative status to the doctors. A lower status makes them pay more attention to whether they are treated with politeness, dignity, and respect in online interactions with the doctors (Chen et al., 2003). Accordingly, patients care about whether or not they are treated equally by the doctors. When consulting doctors with high seniority, patients usually experience a greater status di<sup>f</sup>erence, which impels them to care more about the interpersonal treatment from OHCs. Therefore, we present the following hypothesis:

H3: The negative relationship between online interpersonal unfairness and relationship-quality incentive is strengthened by doctors’ professional seniority in OHCs.

As mentioned previously, when Chinese patients experience online informational unfairness, they will evaluate the consultation through their own perceptions of the doctor’s abilities instead of through direct observations (Arrow, 1963). When consulting with high-seniority doctors who have high professional abilities and rich clinical experiences, patients will feel that the doctors are better able to address their diseases compared with consulting with low-seniority doctors. Thus, when patients experience online information unfairness about their diseases because of unfair information from the OHCs, they are more likely to be motivated to obtain further information by enhancing their relationships with high-seniority doctors rather than low-seniority doctors through providing incentives. Therefore, we propose the following:

H4: The positive relationship between online informational unfairness and relationship-quality incentive is strengthened by doctors’ professional seniority in OHCs.

Disease severity refers to the extent to which patients perceive the seriousness of their disease (Weissfeld, Brock, Kirscht, & Hawthorne, 1987). Disease severity implies the patient’s perceived potential loss resulting from the disease and serves as a contingent boundary of their evaluation of the consultation process (Jha & Balaji, 2015; Yang et al., 2015). Patients with high disease severity have a stronger desire to deal with their disease and thus they will have high expectations for online consultations (Weinfurt et al., 2003). When evaluating the online patient–doctor interactions, they rely more on the outcomes of the consultations and care less about whether they were treated with politeness, dignity, or respect. Hence, when patients have high disease severity, the negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online interpersonal unfairness decreases. Therefore, we suggest the following:

H5: The negative relationship between online interpersonal unfairness and relationship-quality incentive is weakened by patients’ disease severity.

Patients with high disease severity have high expectations of online consultations with regard to obtaining adequate medical and health information (Weinfurt et al., 2003). Given the experienced unfair information from a doctor during an online consultation, patients with higher disease severity have more urgent information demands than patients with lower disease severity. As information asymmetry exists between patients and doctors (Rochaix, 1989), such patients tend to believe in the professional ability of doctors and rely more on their online relationships for solving their disease problems. Accordingly, these patients are more proactive in providing incentives to enhance their online relationships with the doctors to obtain future and fair information through further OHCs. Therefore, we propose the following:

H6: The positive relationship between online informational unfairness and relationship-quality incentive is strengthened by patients’ disease severity in OHCs.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Data collection and variables measurement

To avoid the self-reporting bias of survey data, this study drew on an objective data set to test the theoretical model (Straub, Limayem, & Karahanna-Evaristo, 1995). We selected Good Doctor Online (haodf.com), a leading OHC platform in China, as the research setting. The platform hosts more than 100,000 doctors from di<sup>f</sup>erent hospitals all over China, and it has served more than 100 million patients up to 2016. The platform charges patients approximately US\$3 for each consultation. Each doctor has an exclusive community within the platform and patients can post their questions in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c doctor’s community by typing their inquiries via the platform. Doctors will reply to patients’ questions in their communities. Upon receiving the replies, patients can buy virtual gifts for doctors (at the price of about US\$2.50). The interactions between patients and doctors are kept and displayed at the doctors’ pro<sup>fi</sup>les in the platform.

This platform provides a unique setting to explore the online patient–doctor relationship in two ways: (1) most of the patients had not visited the doctors in person, so the relationships were purely developed through online consultation, and (2) the platform provides a personal community for each doctor, enabling patients to have private interactions with him or her. Based on this context, this study explores the online relationship development at the consultation level (involve multiple rounds of interactions) between one patient and one doctor.

A JAVA-based web crawler was developed to collect data from the website. To ensure validity, the respondents were con<sup>fi</sup>ned to patients su<sup>f</sup>ering from two diseases, namely lung cancer (ie, a common cancer) and diabetes (ie, a common chronic disease with low severity). The data set included two parts: the doctors’ home pages and the online consultation records. We collected online consultation data on the two diseases from January 2014 to December 2015. We excluded patients’ consultations with no more than one round interaction (ie, a question and an answer) as these consultations are ine<sup>f</sup>ective for patients’ disease management. Finally, a total of 83,553 consultations with 93 doctors were collected. The home pages provide an overall description of the doctors, including their o<sup>fl</sup>ine information (eg, hospital, department, area of expertise, and education) and online information (eg, patient ratings, the number of gifts from patients, and the number of online patients). The online consultation pages include detailed information on the online consultation process. Figure 2 provides an example of the online consultation page.

As the payments for the gifts are beyond the o<sup>fi</sup>- cial payment channel (ie, an unnecessary payment for the patients), the virtual gifts are informal payments from patients to doctors (Lewis, 2000), which is similar to the popular “red packet (hongbao)” in the Chinese health care system (Yang, 2013). Gifts given during the online consultation mean that these payments can be both preservice red packets and postservice red packets. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, postservice gifts indicate that the patient is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the doctor’s replies and wants to show his or her gratefulness to the doctor (Ward & Broniarczyk, 2011). On the other hand, giving a preservice gift also means that a patient trusts a doctor and plans to maintain and strengthen their relationship for further potential interactions (Liu, Lu, Liang, & Wei, 2010). Therefore, gift giving means patients have con<sup>fi</sup>dence in doctors’ further performance based on their evaluation of their past interactions, indicating a highquality relationship (Crosby et al., 1990). Further, we test whether the online consultation is di<sup>f</sup>erent based on whether there is gift giving or not. The results show that those consultations with gift giving have more rounds of interactions (ie, 8.222 rounds for gift giving vs. 3.471 rounds for non–gift giving; $t ~ = ~ - 3 3 . 9 2 4 )$ and more replies from doctors (ie, 541.771 words for gift-giving vs. 246.209 words for non–gift giving; t = 29.957) than those that do not, which also shows a quality relationship. Thus, we choose gift giving as a proxy for relationship-quality incentive.

In the online service context, response time is an important predictor of service performance (Cooper, 1983). A rapid response implies that the doctor paid more attention to the patient’s questions and that the patient will therefore perceive politeness, dignity, and respect from the doctor (Bies & Moag, 1986). Otherwise, if the patient waits a long time for a single response from a doctor, he or she will perceive being treated unfairly in the consultation. Thus, adapting from online service-management literature (Cooper, 1983), the ratio of a patient’s total waiting time to a doctor’s total waiting time before gift giving in one consultation is used as a proxy for online interpersonal unfairness. Online informational unfairness refers to the perceptions of the unfairness in the unbalanced information amount provided in online consultation (Greenberg, 1993; Colquitt, 2001). As the patients have to evaluate the knowledge and competence of the doctors remotely, it is di<sup>fi</sup>cult for them to evaluate the quality of the online health information (Akter, D’ambra, & Ray, 2010), and therefore the amount of information can be a critical element for patients. Compared with a response of a few words, a reply with more words likely represents more health information. Otherwise, if a patient asked a very detailed question with plenty of words and received a very simple reply with only a few words, then the patient would feel that his or her inquiry was not well addressed and would perceive information unfairness. For example, if a patient wrote about 100 words to consult the doctors but only got a 5-word reply, whether the reply addressed his or her question or not, he or she would feel the situation was more unfair than if he or she had received a 50-word reply. Therefore, we used the ratio of the number of words in all questions to the number of words in all replies before gift giving in one consultation to capture online informational unfairness. The o<sup>fl</sup>ine attributes and online experiences of doctors were used as control variables, including hospital level, the number of thank-you letters, the number of total gifts received, and the number of online patients. ## 4.3. Robustness checks

We checked the robustness of the results in three di<sup>f</sup>erent ways. First, we tested the robustness of Model 2 by dropping all of the control variables (Shane, 2002). Model R1 in Table 5 shows the results are consistent with Model 2. Second, instead of measuring relationship-quality incentive by gift giving, we used switching from online consultations to a phone consultation as another proxy of relationship-quality incentive. As a phone consultation is based on the previous online consultations and more expensive, a phone consultation also indicates that the patient is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the online consultation and trusts the doctor (Dick & Basu, 1994). The Check 2 in Table 5 shows that most results are consistent with our previous results, except for the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects of disease severity. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, $H _ { 5 }$ was supported while $H _ { 6 }$ was not. The slight di<sup>f</sup>erence may be due to the di<sup>f</sup>erence between gift giving and a thank-you letter. When patients have a high-severity disease, they will be more likely to make informal payments to overcome informational unfairness. However, for interpersonal unfairness, they can choose a less expensive way, such as sending a thank-you letter. Therefore, we can conclude that most of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are robust.

## 5.2. Theoretical implications

First, our research is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst to explore empirically the patient–doctor relationship in an ICT-based context from a fairness perspective. Although the patient–doctor relationship has been a hot research topic in recent decades, most prior research has focused on the traditional o<sup>fl</sup>ine contexts. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings extend existing knowledge on ICT-based services that involve two parties who possess di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of knowledge, such as OHCs, online teaching, and online business consultations. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight the e<sup>f</sup>ects of online interactional unfairness in the online context, which has rarely been studied in the IS discipline. Most previous studies have focused on the role of interactional unfairness in workplaces (Cropanzano et al., 2001; Greenberg, 1993; Roch & Shanock, 2006; Ucho, 2014) and consumer behaviour (Rupp & Spencer, 2006; Jha & Balaji, 2015; Oliver & Swan, 1989; Turel & Connelly, 2013; Turel et al., 2008; Kuo & Wu, 2012). However, to the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst to extend the multidimensional perspective of interactional unfairness in ICTbased service research.

Second, we found that online informational unfairness plays a positive role in relationship development in ICT-based health care services in China. Although ICT-based platforms enable the development of online relationships (Ou et al., 2014), few prior studies have investigated the mechanism of interactional unfairness in the process of developing high-quality online relationships, especially in the Chinese health care context. Chinese society mainly relies on interpersonal relationships (ie, guanxi) for solving problems and ICT-based health care platforms provide patients with the ability to develop enduring relationships with doctors through online patient–doctor interactions. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the positive e<sup>f</sup>ect of online informational unfairness on relationship quality demonstrates the unique health-related behaviours of Chinese patients. Given that the main goal of an OHC is to obtain enough information from doctors and develop a relationship with the doctors, online informational unfairness can play a unique role in this type of service. When patients cannot obtain fair information from an online consultation, they will engage in enhancement approaches (eg, gift giving) to develop high-quality relationships with doctors for the purposes of compensating information unfairness. This interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding contributes to the knowledge of patients social capital development at ICT-based platforms in China and provides a theoretical foundation for future health care research in China.

Third, this research provides several plausible explanations for the inconsistent results in fairness research. As previously mentioned, the prior literature has elaborated inconsistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings on interactional fairness, calling research attention to clarifying this black box. Drawing on the multidimensional perspective and the boundary-conditions view, this study opens the black box. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this study speci<sup>fi</sup>ed interactional unfairness as informationa unfairness and interpersonal unfairness and further explored the boundary conditions of doctor seniority and disease severity. What is inside the black box is that online informational unfairness and online interpersonal unfairness play di<sup>f</sup>erent roles in developing patient–doctor relationships at ICT-based Chinese health care platforms, and these roles are also contingent on the speci<sup>fi</sup>cities of doctors (eg, professional seniority) and patients (eg, disease severity). Therefore, this study not only enriches knowledge in the interactional-fairness literature by providing explanations of the mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings but also contributes to developing theoretical foundations of ICTbased service research in China.

Finally, this study also contributes to the unfairness literature by developing measurements of online informational unfairness and online interpersonal unfairness by using objective data from online patient–doctor interactions. Interactional unfairness is the result of actual interactions between di<sup>f</sup>erent stakeholders embedded in the interactions (Bies & Moag, 1986). The self-report method of survey data may arouse the unfairness perceptions of respondents (the respondents may not perceive unfairness until conducting the survey), which may bias the e<sup>f</sup>ects of unfairness. This study adopts an objective method to measure online unfairness and its objective outcome and therefore provokes new insights into the unfairness literature such that we can focus on unfairness phenomena and their objective outcomes from an objective perspective, instead of studying them from the cognitive perspective and thus remain con<sup>fi</sup>ned to the subjective level.

## 5.3. Practical implications

This study also provides some practical implications. First, in the ICT-based health care context, informational unfairness and interpersonal unfairness have positive and negative e<sup>f</sup>ects on relationship quality, respectively. From the online interpersonal-unfairness perspective, service providers should make their doctors aware of the importance of treating their patients with politeness and respect even in an online context, such as by responding quickly to their questions. Furthermore, service providers can design a website mechanism that monitors doctors responses to patients’ questions in a timely, polite, digni<sup>fi</sup>ed, and respectful manner. For example, service providers can set a limited time for doctors to reply to patients’ questions. From the online informational-unfairness perspective, service providers can guide doctors on how to provide information in the online context. For instance, when a disease is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to explain in an online consultation, doctors can provide less information to entice patients to engage in quality relationships, which is more e<sup>fi</sup>cient in helping patients and enables the provider and doctors to earn higher compensation.

A doctor’s seniority strengthens the positive e<sup>f</sup>ect of online informational unfairness and moderates the negative e<sup>f</sup>ect of interpersonal unfairness. Thus, for doctors with low seniority, service providers should encourage them to provide more information to patients. Disease severity strengthens the e<sup>f</sup>ect of informational unfairness, suggesting that patients with high disease severity are more likely to develop a quality relationship. Thus, service providers should pay more attention to patients with high disease severity to increase the possibility of developing quality relationships with doctors by maximising the positive e<sup>f</sup>ect of online informational unfairness.

## 5.4. Limitations

This study should be interpreted with the following limitations. First, it only focuses on online interac tional unfairness. The other components (ie, distributive unfairness and procedural unfairness) can also play signi<sup>fi</sup>cant roles in the online patient–doctor relationship. Thus, future studies can explore the unfairness issues in an ICT-based context from the perspectives of the distributive unfairness and procedural unfairness. Second, from a more rigorous test of our theoretical model, we collected data on two types of diseases (ie, lung cancer and diabetes). More diseases should be considered in future studies to increase the generalisation of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings or calculate an index of disease severity. Third, calculating DoctorTime (ie, how long a patient waits for the doctor’s next reply) to measure online interpersonal unfairness could be biased since waiting time is uncertain if the patient has posted several questions (or even repeated some). With the limitation of our current data set, we cannot address this potential bias. Future research can focus on potential alternatives to measure interpersonal unfairness based on the online interaction process. Finally, to better measure the online unfairness phenomenon and avoid the bias of self-reported data, we measured unfairness and relationship quality based on objective data. Even though we adapted the measures meticulously based on the current literature, the general limitation of using objective data remains in this study, such that it may jeopardise the match between the theoretical constructs, and the objective measures, such as gift giving and thank-you letters, are used to measure relationship-quality incentive. Further research could improve the consistency between de<sup>fi</sup>nition and operationalisation of variables by using more speci<sup>fi</sup>c objective measures, such as neuro-response data and semantic analysis data.

## 6. Conclusion

The growing popularity of OHCs has changed the previous power structure between patients and doctors in health care, especially in China. Even though health care ICTs provide Chinese patients a new approach to developing relationships with doctors, and patients have high authority in ICT-based contexts, online patient–doctor interactions are still demonstrated to be unfair given the professional knowledge asymmetry between them. However, few previous studies have explored this phenomenon in ICT-based contexts. In addition, for the e<sup>f</sup>ects of interactional unfairness, the prior literature has evoked many mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings that are left unexplained. Therefore, this study adopted a multidimensional perspective of online interactional unfairness in ICT-based Chinese health care such as OHCs. In particular, a theoretical research model that investigates the e<sup>f</sup>ects of online interpersonal unfairness and online informational unfairness on relationship quality and two moderators – a doctor’s professional seniority and disease severity – was proposed and empirically veri<sup>fi</sup>ed using an objective data set from a leading OHC platform in China. This study contributes to ICT-based health care research, fairness theory, and patient–doctor relationship management in China.
