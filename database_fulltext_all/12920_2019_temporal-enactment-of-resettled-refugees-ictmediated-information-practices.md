R E S E A R C H A R T I C L E

# Temporal enactment of resettled refugees' ICT‐mediated information practices

Antonio Díaz Andrade | Bill Doolin

Business Information Systems, Auckland University of Technology, Auckland, New Zealand

Correspondence

Antonio Díaz Andrade, Business Information Systems, Auckland University of Technology, Auckland, New Zealand. Email: antonio.diaz@aut.ac.nz

## Abstract

“In this paper, we explain how resettled refugees use information and communication technology (ICT) to respond to their changed circumstances and, in doing so, enhance their well‐being and effective participation in a new society. Focusing on three modes of ICTmediated information practices (ie, orienting, instrumental, and expressive), we identify eight patterns of ICT use: learning about a new environment, keeping informed, transacting online, communicating with others, managing everyday life, sustaining support networks, maintaining transnational ties, and expressing cultural identity. Further, we draw on a temporal theory of human agency to explain how current dilemmas and contingencies, cultural identities and connections to the past, and future expectations and aspirations shape resettled refugees' enactment of these patterns of ICT‐mediated information practices. We show that, as resettled refugees move between multiple and overlapping temporal‐relational contexts, ICT use makes a difference to managing their bifurcated lives.”

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Today, millions of individuals are forced to abandon their homes due to political struggles, clashes between riva factions, occupation by foreign forces, ethnic conflicts or criminal violence. Following the exodus, they transitio to refugee camps in other countries until their eventual relocation to a recipient state―usually an economicall advanced yet alien society. It is against this background that we study how resettled refugees engage in using information and communication technology (ICT) to exercise a degree of control over their changed circumstances post‐settlement and to manage the consequences of dislocation from their countries of origin. Studying the use of ICT by refugees resettled in a new country offers an interesting opportunity to understand how patterns of IC use emerge in specific contexts, what shapes these patterns, and how such ICT use fulfils specific needs and goals for the users.

Recognizing that access to and use of ICT is an essential enabler for participation in the emerging information society (Mansell, 2002), we examine the use of ICT by individuals of refugee background who have been relocated to New Zealand. These individuals were uprooted from their known environments, transplanted into an unknown situation, and required to navigate through an unfamiliar information environment in order to participate in their new society (Kennan, Lloyd, Qayyum, & Thompson, 2011). However, what resettled refugees are able to achieve through ICT use involves consideration of how ICT becomes incorporated in their attempt to exercise control over their circumstances. The intentional use of ICT occurs within the possibilities and constraints of the existing socia arrangements that resettled refugees inhabit.

Because refugees relocated to a new country are in a transitional stage, in which their connections in and to their host country are not yet fully established (Weil, 2001), they undergo a process of adaptation and settlement domi nated by particular information and communication needs (Caidi, Allard, & Quirke, 2010; Lloyd, Kennan, Thompson, & Qayyum, 2013). Further, they have the right to acquire the capabilities necessary for participation in an informatio society (Jääskeläinen & Savolainen, 2003), which arguably New Zealand is.\* In particular, we are interested in under standing the mechanisms by which a group of refugees relocated to New Zealand use ICT to exercise a degree of control over their changed circumstances post‐settlement and to manage the consequences of dislocation from their countries of origin. In order to offer a meaningful explanation of this phenomenon, we build upon the notion of “infor mation practices”―“the ways people seek, use, and share information in everyday contexts” (Savolainen, 2008, p. vi) ―to focus on how these practices are mediated by ICT. We draw on Emirbayer and Mische's (1998) conceptualizatio of human agency to explore how individuals' temporal orientations shape their engagement with ICT and their enactment of ICT‐mediated information practices.

Accordingly, our overall research question is:

How do resettled refugees use ICT to respond to their changed circumstances?

Answering this question requires ascertaining ICT use among resettled refugees, understanding the mechanisms that shape particular ICT use practices, and the implications of such practices for the refugees. Hence, three specifi research questions structure our study

1. In what ways do resettled refugees use ICT in their information practices?

2. How are particular ICT‐mediated information practices enacted?

3. What is achieved through these ICT‐mediated information practices?

To answer our research questions, we adopted a qualitative research approach, collecting our data through in‐depth interviews, and then combining inductive analysis of that data with abductive reasoning using the theoretica concepts of ICT‐mediated information practices and temporal agentic orientations. Such an approach enables us to develop a deeper understanding of what is a complex and multi‐dimensional phenomenon

This paper is organized in seven sections. Following this introduction, the next two sections provide a theoretica background, first presenting the concept of ICT‐mediated information practices and, second, outlining the theory of human agency that we use to inform our analysis. These two sections are followed by a description of the research methods that we used to collect and analyze our data. We then present our findings on how the enactment of particular ICT‐mediated practices is influenced by the temporal dimensions of human agency. This is followed by a discussion of our results, in which we focus on how these ICT‐mediated practices assist resettled refugees to repair their disrupted lives. The paper concludes with the theoretical contributions and practical implications of our study.

## 2 | ICT USE AND REFUGEES

At a fundamental level, information is an essential tool that helps individuals cope with their lives (Dervin, 1977). In everyday contexts, individuals seek, use and share information in more or less established ways, which Savolainen (2008) terms “information practices.” These practices are based on particular activities that “a person may engage in when identifying his or her own needs for information, searching for such information in any way, and using or trans ferring that information” (Wilson, 1999, p. 249). Prior research suggests three broad categories of information prac tices: orienting, instrumental and expressive

Orienting practices involve active scanning or non‐directed monitoring (McKenzie, 2003) of information sources for information with which to orient oneself to one's (potentially new or unfamiliar) environment: “it may be under stood as an attempt to continually construct a cognitive environmental map to facilitate the need to cope with uncer tainty” (Savolainen, 2008, p. 84). Instrumental practices involve actively seeking and using information to make sense of a problematic situation, solve a specific problem or perform a particular task. Instrumental practices tend to address information needs that are episodic, situationally based and context-dependent (Caidi et al., 2010: McKenzie. 2003 Savolainen, 2008). Expressive practices reflect the social and communicative dimensions of information use and shar ing among individuals, groups, and networks for the purposes of social interaction and identity expression (Caidi et al., 2010). The expressive dimension of information practices also draws our attention to how information and commu nication are intimately related. As Castells (2009) observes, “communication is the sharing of meaning through the exchange of information” (p. 54)

In today's information society, information practices are fundamentally and pervasively mediated by ICT. Essentially, ICT creates an “information ecology”―a new ecology of relations in which information becomes the central element (Kallinikos. 2006). As a digital artefact. ICT has various attributes—eg, editability. interactiy ity, reprogrammability, and distributedness (Kallinikos, Aaltonen, & Marton, 2013), which form the basis for various functionalities that both facilitate and constrain opportunities for action. For instance, a smartphone enables a user to take a digital photograph and share it nearly instantaneously with others located virtuall anywhere in the world. In practice, however, the viewing of that photograph may be constrained by the band width of the digital network used to transmit the data and the screen resolution and size of the receiving mobile device. In their situated use of ICT, users interpret and enact its functionalities in relation to their goals and needs. From the range of possible actions that ICT affords in a given situation an individual will realiz particular actions based on how they perceive the technology enables them to achieve their intended aims (Majchrzak & Markus, 2013; Stoffregen, 2003).

The action potential of ICT goes beyond the attributes and functionalities of any specific device. Because ICT tools are always embedded in an existing social system, they are.“susceptible to being deeply modified by its socia practice, and leading to a whole range of potential social outcomes” (Castells, 2001, p. 50). In other words, the same technology may afford different patterns of technology use and consequences in different social contexts (Majchrzak & Markus, 2013). This social embeddedness of ICT calls for an understanding of the degree of malleability of technology. As Orlikowski (2000) elaborates, “the more a particular technological artifact is integrated into a larger system, network, or technological configuration, the narrower the range of alternative uses that may be crafted with it. Thus the use of a stand-alone personal computer in my home is likely to be more malleable than the use of a workstation by an air traffic controller” (p. 409). Orlikowski's (2000) scenario reflects the relatively low‐end ICT set‐up provided to the participants in our study―a technology with a high degree of malleability that offers multiple potential uses in their everyday lives.

Resettled refugees share some of the experiences of more general migrants to a new country in that they “move away from their familiar environments, social alignments, information sources and ways of knowing” (I loyd, 2014, p. 48), As such, they are likely to engage with a range of information sources and practices that collectively constitute a new information landscape. Migration and resettlement are often constructed as an information problem, where navigating an unfamiliar and potentially complex environment may involve satisfying information needs around, for example, language, employment, housing, health, education, transportation, banking, and compliance with local regulations (Caidi & Allard, 2005; Caidi et al., 2010;). In such situations, ICT can be an important tool with which to identify and use information to address everyday needs. Prior research suggests that migrants are also likely to use ICT to follow events in the places they left behind exchange information and maintain transnational social ties with family and friends in their countries of origi or located elsewhere (Benítez, 2012; Caidi et al., 2010; Hiller & Franz, 2004). In this way, ICT can mediate practices involving information sharing and communication within social networks. Finally, the consumptio of cultural content through Internet‐based resources can offer “an emotional shelter …, a tool for inter‐gener ational cultural transmission and a site for exploring various aspects of new identity formation” (Elias & Lemish, 2009, p. 534). In researching ICT‐mediated information practices, individuals should be understood from their own perspectives, situations, and intentions (Dervin, 1989). This involves understanding “the ways in which people who accomplish everyday information practices construct them as meaningful” (Savolainen, 2008, p. 5). In such an analysis, it is important to understand both how particular time‐space‐bound contexts and networks of social relations orient and shape people's ICT use, and how individuals engage agentically with these structur ing contexts (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998).

## 3 | HUMAN AGENCY AND ICT ENACTMENT

Individuals exercise agency in order to obtain a degree of control in their everyday lives (Sewell. 1992). A focus on human agency recognizes that human actors are capable of intentionality, forethought, self‐regulation, and self‐reflectiveness (Bandura, 2001), and that, when confronted with unplanned events, they attempt to “exercise some measure of control over their self‐development and life circumstances” (Bandura, 2001, p. 11). Nevertheless, human agency operates within particular contexts, conditions, and relations that provide a variety of socio‐structural influences on that agency and that enable and constrain human action (Giddens, 1984; Sewell, 1992). Understanding how human actors exercise agency in their interaction with their environment requires the scrutiny of people's goals and their available tools in a particular context (Engeström, 1999). The possible desires and intentions, and the resources available to individuals, within different social contexts means that agency is exercised variably (Sewell, 1992). For instance, occupying a particular social position, such as that of being a resettled refugee, shapes the interests and needs that might be salient to an individual, as well as, the kind and amount of resources available, including ICT, for exercising agency.

Individuals do not engage with ICT as passive subiects without a sense of purpose. They exercise their judgement through critical consciousness in order to assess how and to what extent the functionalities of ICT afford possibilities for action that can help them address their needs and achieve their goals in the specific relational and tempora conditions in which they live (Faraj & Azad, 2012; Markus & Silver, 2008; Robey, Raymond, & Anderson, 2012). Thus, from a practice perspective, technology use is “influenced by users' understandings of the properties and functionalit of a technology” (Orlikowski, 2000, p. 409), “influenced by specific interpretations and particular institutional contexts, and shaped by a diversity of intentions and practices to collaborate, solve problems” and so on (ibid., p. 420)

In explaining why ICT users respond to the particularities of their circumstances with specific and changing practices of technology use, a number of authors have utilized a temporal view of agency proposed by Emirbayer and Mische (1998). For example, Boudreau and Robey (2005) employ this theory of human agency to explore users' vary ing enactments of an enterprise resource planning system, Cousins and Robey (2005) use it to explain variations in nomadic computing practices, and Howard‐Grenville (2005) explores how agency shapes the persistence and flexibil ity of organizational routines. More recently, Chu and Robey (2008) use the theory in explaining changes in work practices following the implementation of an online learning application, and Richardson and Benbunan‐Fich (2011) use it to consider patterns of behavior in after‐hours work connectivity

Emirbayer and Mische (1998) argue that time is a critical consideration in analyzing the agentic dimension of social action: “The ways in which people understand their own relationship to the past, future, and present make a difference to their actions” (p. 973; emphasis in the original). For Emirbayer and Mische, agency is relational in that it involves social engagement with the constraining and enabling structures that constitute different contexts of action. However, they suggest that these contexts of action are not just relational but also temporal fields: “multiple, overlapping ways of ordering time toward which social actors can assume different simultaneous agentic orientations… toward the past, the future, and the present” (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998, pp. 963–964; emphasis in the original). These three temporal agentic orientations represent how actors responses to problematic situations involve “the interplay of habit, imagination, and judgment” (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998, p. 970). Human agency is:

…informed by the past (in its habitual aspect), but also oriented towards the future (as a capacity to imagine alternative possibilities) and towards the present (as a capacity to contextualize past habits and future projects within the contingencies of the moment) (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998, p. 963).

In order to analyze the complex recursive interaction between actors and their circumstances, Emirbayer an Mische (1998) distinguish three constitutive elements of agency that broadly correspond to these three tempora agentic orientations: iterational, projective, and practical‐evaluative.

The iterational element of agency is oriented to the past and involves “the selective reactivation by actors of past patterns of thought and action, as routinely incorporated in practical activity, thereby giving stability and order to social universes and helping to sustain identities, interactions, and institutions over time” (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998, p. 971). The iterational element acknowledges that actors' agentic responses to emergent events may reflect internalized predispositions to certain activities developed through past experiences. In Cousins and Robey's (2005) study of how nomadic computing users manage the boundaries between their personal and work lives, iteratio explains why some individuals prefer using somewhat obsolete technologies over newer ones, even though th former are in the process of being phased out.

The projective element of agency is oriented to the future and involves “the imaginative generation by actors of possible future traiectories of action. in which received structures of thought and action may be creatively reconfigured in relation to actors' hopes, fears, and desires for the future” (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998, p. 971). The ambiguities that actors face in life encourage them to hypothesize future scenarios and possibilities that inform their conceivable actions. Projectivity reveals the aspirations of agents in the “space of possibles” (Bourdieu, 1993, p. 64), where agents subjectively assess their chances to achieve their goals. For example, projectivity may be reflected in a individual's willingness to acquire and embrace new mobile technologies in the expectation of enhancing his or he work performance (Cousins & Robey, 2005).

The practical‐evaluative element of agency is oriented to the present and involves “the capacity of actors to make practical and normative judgments among alternative possible trajectories of action, in response to the emerging demands, dilemmas, and ambiguities of presently evolving situations” (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998, p. 971). The continuously changing contexts of action require actors to adjust their actions based on an evaluation of current conditions and “the exercise of situationally based judgement” (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998, p. 994). For instance, the synchronization of different mobile devices to overcome the difficulty of keeping track of all of them can be considered a practical‐evaluative response to an emergent problematic situation (Cousins & Robey, 2005).

The authors argue for analytically distinguishing the three elements, suggesting that actors “may be primarily oriented toward one or another of these within any one emergent situation” (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998, p. 964). Exploring which orientation(s) predominate in a particular situation, enables an examination of how different forms of social action are “more (or less) engaged with the past, more (or less) directed toward the future, and more (or less) responsive to the present” (ibid., p. 972), and how actors switch between differ ent temporal orientations as they navigate multiple and overlapping “temporal‐relational contexts of action” (ibid., p. 970).

Emirbayer and Mische's (1998) perspective on human agency provides the conceptual elements for our analysis of the research problem at hand. A temporal conceptualization of human agency is particularly relevant to our participants' ICT‐mediated information practices given the strong temporal dimension to the journeys that they have experienced. their dislocation from familiar contexts, and their relocation and transition to a new and unfamiliar environment. If agency involves interpreting and mobilizing available resources using one's knowledge of the schemas that inform social life, then new contexts may demand the application of new schemas or transformation of existing ones (Sewell, 1992). Emirbayer and Mische (1998) suggest that individ uals have the self‐reflexive capacity to reconstruct their temporal orientations in response to being positioned between the old and the new: “Actors who face changing situations that demand (or facilitate) the reconstruc tion of temporal perspectives can expand their capacity for imaginative and/or deliberative response” (p. 1006)

In this paper, we examine how our participants' enactment of ICT is shaped by their desires, intentions, and the resources that they encounter, as they come to terms with settling in a new country; how these ICT‐mediated information practices comprise forms of action that are variously oriented towards past experiences, future possibilities and present contingencies; and what is achieved by such practices

## 4 | RESEARCH METHODS

In this section, we present our participants and explain the data collection process that we followed. We then elaborate on the inductive‐abductive analysis we used to identify how temporal agentic orientations shape the ICT mediated information practices of our participants

## 4.1 | Participant background

The participants in our study, individuals who had no option other than abandoning their homes and possessions because the situation in their countries of origin became untenable, come from different parts of the world and have different cultural backgrounds and exile back‐stories. However, they all share the experience of being hosted by a new country, New Zealand where they were granted residency status. The contrasting living conditions between New

Zealand and the participants' countries of origin have not gone unnoticed. Participants not only highlighted the mate rial progress they witness in their host country (eg, roads, hospitals, schools) but also the less tangible aspects of a stable democracy and largely tolerant society, characterized by a high sense of fairness and respect for human rights However, no matter the infrastructure and social services that their host country has to offer, entering it as a refugee is not a trouble‐free experience. All participants had to leave their belongings, hopes, and dreams as well as friends and families behind to resettle in a new and unfamiliar environment.

Upon their arrival in New Zealand, refugees spend six weeks at a resettlement center in Auckland where the receive elemental English lessons as well as learn about local culture, their rights and the services offered by govern ment agencies, before being relocated to state houses. While this assistance is intended to make the resettlement pro cess smooth, the transition imposes serious challenges. Above all. language represents the maior barrier for many participants. For instance, while in their countries of origin they used to approach government agencies whenever they needed to, New Zealand largely operates under a system of appointments, which are mainly made on the telephone. This practice, intended to simplify an everyday life activity, imposes a stressful experience on the new arrivals, who lack the confidence to have a conversation in English

The participants in this study constituted a purposeful sample (Denzin & Lincoln, 1994) and, collectively, a rich case that manifests the phenomenon under study intensely (Patton, 2002). All were recipients of a governmentfunded initiative, Computers in Homes―Refugee Programme, aimed at improving their digital skills. This initiative offers recently arrived families with school‐aged children a 30‐hour basic computer‐training course, which covers topics such as logging on to a computer, using a web browser, creating and using an email account, and basic word processing. Upon the successful completion of the course, the participants are given a refurbished desktop computer and software, as well as broadband Internet connection and technical support free of charge for one year. Our participants received the computer‐training course at different stages: most did it within three years of their arrival, although three did the training six years after entering New Zealand.

Even though all participants shared the experience of being resettled in a new country, they are far from consti tuting a homogeneous group. The participants' previous familiarity with ICT can be roughly classified into three levels: no experience at all, some experience, and a high degree of expertise. Two participants are illiterate and had never touched a computer before the training course. Other participants had some experience with computers, for example using email while living in refugee camps. The participants with the highest level of computer expertise hold universit degrees or had computer training because of their previous job responsibilities. At the time of the fieldwork, most participants were employed in diverse occupations (eg, store operator, government officer, housekeeper, interpreter gardener, checkout operator), while others were studying―mainly English; ten were unemployed.

In general, participants expressed their overall satisfaction with ICT. Those who lacked much computer expertise before the computer-training course in New Zealand were especially proud of their newly acguirec abilities. In most cases, the refurbished computer provided upon completion of the training course occupied a prominent position in the participant's house; often sitting on a desk in either the main living room or the dining room. In many cases, a newer model had replaced the originally provided computer or additiona ICT devices had been acquired so that multiple family members could participate in ICT use. In those households where the first year of paid broadband had already expired, individual arrangements were made in order to maintain Internet access.

## 4.3 | Data analysis

Data collection and data analysis were conducted iteratively. Interview notes and audio‐recordings were uploaded into NVivo as the data collection progressed; we used it as a tool for managing the data and for listening to the audio‐recordings. NVivo supported our initial inductive thematic examination (Braun & Clarke, 2006) of the rich dataset, from which we identified participants' patterns of ICT‐mediated information practices. Then, we followed an abductive reasoning (Reichertz, 2007) to map these emergent patterns against each of the orienting, instrumental and expressive practices explained in Section 2 and summarized in Table 1.

The inductive‐abductive scrutiny of our participants' use of ICT for addressing their information and communication needs allowed us to uncover the not so obvious conceptual associations between their patterns of ICT‐mediated information practices and their temporal agentic orientations. The constant move between data from the field and existing theories (Miller, 2003) revealed how the temporal agentic orientations towards the past, present, and future―as explained in Section 3 and summarized inTable 2―shape each of the identified patterns of ICT‐mediated information practices. We subscribe to Klein and Myers's (1999) reasoning that “no understanding of new “data” is possible without relying on some pre‐understanding” (p. 87). Appendix 2 presents a diagrammatic representation of our inductive‐abductive analysis. In addition, consistent with Klein and Myers's (1999) principle of contextualization, we paid special attention during the analysis to the social context in which the participants' ICT‐mediated information practices were performed

## 5 | FINDINGS

In this section of the paper, we present the results of our analysis of the ICT‐mediated information practices of the participants in our study. In presenting our results, we included some information related to our participants, such as age, education, gender, marital status, number of children, and employment situation. This information is not intended to imply any cause‐effect relationship between these variables and the ability to use ICT or specific ICT use patterns. We include this information to present a more vivid, broader description of our participants' lives wit the intention to enhance the authenticity of our analysis (Lincoln, Lynham, & Guba, 2011)

We use our earlier categorization of such practices as orienting, instrumental, and expressive to organize our findings. Our analysis identifies patterns of ICT use within each of these categories of practices. Appendix 3 summarizes the distribution of these patterns across our 53 participants; this distribution reflects ICT uses that were salient in our participants' accounts. Drawing on Emirbayer and Mische's (1998) theorization of tempora agency, we also analyze how different temporal agentic orientations―ie, practical‐evaluative (present), iterationa (past), and projective (future)―shape the enactment of the various ICT‐mediated information practices.

## 5.1 | Orienting information practices

As former refugees, resettled in a new country, our participants face the need to find and interpret information that assists them in navigating the unfamiliar environment in which they now find themselves. Unsurprisingly, when confronted with such information needs, they utilize the computer training that they have received, in conjunction with the ICT that is available to them, to enact a range of orienting information practices that assist them in transitioning and integrating into their host country. We could discern two different patterns of ICT‐mediated orienting practices that were prevalent in our data: “learning about a new environment” and “keeping informed.”

## 5.1.1 | Learning about a new environment

A commonly observed practice among our participants, as residents in a new country, involves using ICT to learn about aspects of New Zealand culture and ways of life. For instance, Thi<sup>‡</sup> (a mother of three children) and Nyan (a father of two), both from Burma, explained that information available online has allowed them acquire informatio about their host country. Biraj and Deepeka, a Bhutanese couple with three children who entered New Zealand i 2010, provided an interesting aspect of using ICT for learning more about their host country. Bibek, who works as a recycling operator, explained that he uses to Google to find information about “how New Zealand children are taught by their parents.” These instances illustrate how our participants rely on online information to reduce the state of disorientation generated following their relocation to an unfamiliar environment.

Similarly, Amahoro, a Rwandan solo mother of two teenage boys, who works as caregiver at a health care provider said that when she wants to learn more about New Zealand, “I go to the Internet and do research to find it out.” As Amahoro's prior experience with computers was limited to basic email use, this instance of ICT use reflects the applica tion of a new (to her) schema based around the notion of ICT-enabled research, The possibilities afforded by the com puter/Internet/search engine assemblage that she has access to enable her to inform and expand her understanding o everyday life in New Zealand. In this sense, she is responding in a deliberate way to the contingencies of her new context.

In another example, Armand, a Congo‐born student, who spent ten years in a refugee camp in Uganda before entering New Zealand with his mother and nine younger siblings, explained how his use of computer technolog contributes to his understanding of New Zealand culture:

For instance, these Māori<sup>§</sup> people… There are many things they do… We are new to here, so it is hard for us to [understand] their culture. So if I want to learn more about their culture and the things they do, we go and search [on the Internet] to understand their culture

Obtaining this type of information does not address a specific problem confronting Armand. Rather, it enhances his understanding of the cultural history of New Zealand. As with Amahoro, this example of Armand's ICT use reflects the application of an acquired schema. As another participant put it, “We can learn new things through the Internet.”

Another form of learning about the new environment in which our participants find themselves involves regula monitoring of that environment in order to identify the patterns that govern events there and to observe trends that may influence various aspects of an individual's life. For instance, Ganesh is a university‐educated, former Bhutanese government employee, who lived in a refugee camp in Nepal for 18 years, where he married and had three children Ganesh described how, even though he lacks the financial deposit needed to secure a home loan, “We use [the] Internet … we browse everyday [a website that lists properties for sale] … We are trying to find the house that is suitable to us.” His use of the Internet and his computer to scan the housing market in New Zealand is not focused on a present need for accommodation. Rather, in doing so, Ganesh is anticipating a time when he and his family will be able t purchase their own home.

Similarly, Ganesh, who currently works for a New Zealand government agency, is also attentive to opportunities for other employment, using the Internet to monitor the job market and to understand what is involved in applying fo particular types of jobs:

When [a position] was advertised, I went [to] the Internet and prepared myself for the interview. I got the information from there to prepare for the interview … There was no reason to look for a job, but I still go there because it is two and a half years now that I have been working in the same job. So, I sometimes Google to see if there are suitable jobs around the country, where I can fit in. But it is not acute for me now; I have not gone in depth. Maybe one day, if I become unemployed, I will have to see in depth … It is a useful tool for job hunting as well as preparing for the job.

What is clear from his comments is that, similar to his scanning of the housing market, Ganesh is not presentl looking for a job (any more than he is presently able to purchase a house). He is enacting this ICT‐mediated practice because he wants to increase his chances of success when he does find himself in a position to need to find a new job In these orienting information practices, Ganesh is imagining future possibilities for action in response to the uncertainties of his situation. His agency is projective, temporally directed at the future rather than responsive to the contingencies and demands of the present.

## 5.1.2 | Keeping informed

While the examples presented above demonstrate ICT use to obtain and assimilate knowledge about their host coun: try, our participants also enact ICT‐mediated orienting information practices in ways that reflect their internationa backgrounds. Often this involves monitoring local and international current events using various ICT‐mediated information sources. Kim, a man in his early 30s, who entered New Zealand with Hayma―his wife―and three children spent ten years in Thailand following an exodus from his native Burma. Kim, who did not complete primary school now studies English and, through an interpreter, described his practice of monitoring world news on the Internet in his mother tongue: “We can know the news from around the world… I watch news every morning on the com puter.” Kim's routine monitoring of the international news reflects the importance to him of being informed. Nyunt and his wife Shein, who also spent ten years in Thailand after leaving Burma before entering New Zealand in 2011, relied on an interpreter to explain that they “listen to BBC News in Burmese language on a regular basis.” While some other participants may be able to maintain a conversation in English with some limitations, they still pre fer to use ICT to access the news in their own language. This is the case of Myo, a nursery farm worker originall from Burma, and his wife Aom, a woman in her late 20s born in Thailand of Burmese parents. They regularly “listen to BBC news Burmese channel” over the Internet. This existing schema of keeping up‐to‐date with what is going on informs the ICT use of these participants. Where they may have used television, radio or newspapers in their past lives, ICT offers them access to international news in their own languages in their current circumstances, overcoming their limited understanding of English.

In a similar example, Nishva and her husband Ugyen, who spent 19 years and married in a refugee camp in Nepal after being forced to leave Bhutan in 1992, explained how they use the Internet to: “listen to… a Nepalese radio ever Saturday. It is a radio of what is happening in the world.” Again, given their limited English language skills, ICT is essen tial to their accustomed practice of monitoring of current events. In comparison, Htun, who earned a Bachelor's degree in mathematics in his native Burma, spent four years in Malaysia before being relocated to New Zealand with his wife and four children, and now works as a high school relief teacher, has a better command of the English lan guage. He is able to consume English‐language news on television. Nevertheless, this is not a trouble‐free exercise, and he sometimes relies on ICT to access supplementary online information that enhances his understanding of news items presented on television:

Sometimes we cannot get [it] … They speak very fast on TV … If [the topic] is interesting, we can type [it] on the Internet … For example, in Al‐Jazeera News, they say ‘If you need more information, go to the website.

Bijay, a Bhutanese married man in his 30s who lived in a refugee camp in Nepal for 19 years before entering New Zealand, said that he reads the news online “in both Nepali and English, to learn more English.” His monitoring of cur rent events through Internet‐based news is not just a practical‐evaluative response to his present desire to keep informed. By then making the effort to read the same content in English, Bijay is also trying to improve his grasp of the language of his new host country. This suggests a projective element of agency, oriented to the future and sug gestive of an intention to become more integrated into New Zealand society.

For many of our participants, using ICT to “keep informed” involved more than a present concern with keeping up‐to‐date with current events in New Zealand or around the world. Often it involved following political and socia events in their countries of origin, as well as the places where they had stayed during their (often long) journey to New Zealand. For example, Maung, an ethnic Karen from Burma in his late 30s, married and father of four children (two of whom were born in New Zealand), told us, “Because we are far from our country, we go on to the Internet, we look in the news, so we know everything about our country … The war is in the news.” Maung, who lived for ten years in a refugee camp inThailand, before entering New Zealand, also explained that by having access to ICT he can know “[what is happening] in the refugee camps.” For Naing, Yu, and Eka, three ethnically Kayan women who left Burma at a very young age, the considerable time that they spent in Thailand before entering New Zealand (19, 15 and 13 years, respectively explains their practice of following news and events in Thailand by listening to Thai radio stations on the Internet.

In monitoring events in places that they have left behind, our participants are engaging with the past. Their actions are influenced by an iterational temporal agentic orientation that reproduces past patterns of thought and reflects an affective need to maintain a connection with their pasts; as Maung explained, “Because we are far from our country.” This sense of nostalgia for former places and lives was particularly poignant when Maung describe viewing on the Internet images of the Burmese village he was forced to leave in 1992:

When I go to Google Maps and look at the place where I was born… I can see a photo … I am interested because my village has changed a lot. Someone cut the forest … I am very sad. When I was child … the places I went around … I can remember. When I see the photo … [it's] a [big] change … Very sad … Why do we have to leave our village?

In a similar way, Shwe, an ethnic Chin man who spent three years in Malaysia after leaving Burma, remains interested in keeping informed about his Burmese village's situation. Shwe relies on his membership of an email group to monitor:

What happens in my village, what happens in my place, I see it on email … We have a group for the Chin community … Some people live in the US, some people from own country … They include my village in the world.

Although Shwe is located in the present in a new country that demands his attention, he still retains a powerfu need to connect to his past―still “my place.” His orienting information practice around this is a testament to the use of ICT to transcend geographic distance and extend geographic reach―“They include my village in the world.” Of course, people, as well as places, are important. Shwe's compatriot, Zaw, who did not complete high school in his native Burma and now works at a local supermarket, uses the same Chin email group and Facebook to keep informed about his extended community. He explained:

I belong to a Facebook Chin group, with members all over the world … For example, if something is happening, they write ‘blah, blah, blah’ … I go to the group and learn what happened … Some Chin people who live in America get married, you know, some people die.

Zaw also described how members of the Chin diaspora use ICT to share their impressions of living conditions in different countries: “We talk about general things … Their jobs. We do share … ‘How is life there? How is life here?’” Here, Zaw's temporal orientation has shifted to a concern more with the present than the past. Comparing work and life in their various locations reflects a practical response to the contingences of being uprooted and dispersed around the world. Adan, who spent 18 years in a refugee camp in Nepal (where he met his wife and had two children) after being displaced from Bhutan, uses regular Skype conversations to compare his current circumstances in New Zealand with that of his two brothers and two sisters who were relocated to the United States:

I ask, ‘How are you? How is it going?’ ... They are better than me because they got jobs [even though] thei English is not better than me … Maybe because there are many factories [over there] … So many things are produced in America; not here.

Adan, who at the time of his interview had been unable to get a job in New Zealand, was clearly frustrated. H had applied for many jobs and in each case had been told that his English was not good enough. Yet his siblings, whose English skills are no better according to Adan, have been able to secure employment because of the greater opportunities offered by their location in the United States. As with Zaw, Adan's use of ICT in this particular wa reflects a situationally based evaluation of his current conditions and an orientation towards the present

## 5.1.3 | Temporal agentic orientations on ICT‐mediated orienting information practices

The examples described above reveal the variety of ways in which our participants enact ICT‐mediated orienting information practices using multiple digital media. These orienting information practices contribute to easing the burden of involuntary relocation to a new country, with ICT providing a technological resource for both learning about their new environment and keeping informed about what is going on with the places and people that matter to them. Although their ICT use occurs in the present time, the way our participants enact these orienting practices demonstrates how they may be shaped by not just present circumstances, but also past experiences and future possibilities Even though the practices illustrated above may appear mundane, they reflect the development and deployment of capabilities around ICT use that enable the resettled refugees' participation in contemporary New Zealand society, while simultaneously fulfilling their wider information needs as international citizens, their desire to remain connected with former places and people, and their interest in imagining future aspects of their lives. In all cases, their social position as resettled refugees influences their use of ICT and makes particular interests and information more salient Orienting ICT‐mediated information practices contribute to an understanding of the broader context in which individual lives are immersed. As resettled refugees, the lives of our participants unfold in different localities. In this contex of transnational dislocation, ICT provides the vehicle for fulfilling the need to make sense of multiple relevant contexts.

## 5.2 | Instrumental information practices

The instrumental information needs of our participants arise from the particular situations and contexts in which they find themselves as resettled refugees in a new country. In seeking and using information to solve emergent problems or perform specific tasks, our participants often turned to ICT as a readily available and increasingly accessible source of relevant information for them. Arguably, simply living in an information society predicates the use of ICT to perform everyday information activities, whether making a doctor's appointment, paying a utility bill, filing a tax return, preparing for the driving test, or finding the address and directions for a service provider. Many of our participants were also eager to take advantage of the perceived opportunities afforded by ICT in their everyday lives. We categorized our participants' predominant ICT‐mediated instrumental information practices into three broad patterns: “transacting online." “communicating with others." and “managing everyday life”

## 5.2.1 | Transacting online

Online shopping is a common ICT‐mediated instrumental practice that was highlighted by our participants. It is a convenient example of how these resettled refugees have developed skills and behaviors involving ICT use that enable their full participation in New Zealand society. For instance, Zaw explained how he uses the Internet not only to check his bank account, but also to find deals for his household appliance needs: “If I want to buy a phone, a computer, or things … I go to [local stores'] websites [to see] what is on special, like ‘half price’ or ‘40% off’ … Then, I compare prices.” Online price comparison involves the learning of a new schema for Zaw―a particular way of thinking and act ing about purchasing everyday goods made possible by ICT.

Thaw, a school bilingual assistant and supermarket supervisor who left Burma in 2006 and entered New Zealanc after one year in Malaysia, is an enthusiastic ICT user. He uses one of the three computers he shares with his children at home for at least 30 minutes every day. Besides the usual online transactions (eg, Internet banking, paying bills online) Thaw explained that he buys air tickets for his twice-a-year travels within the country because. “Lown a credit card and have Internet access … That is very quick, useful, and easy.” Thaw's experience illustrates the convenience o having a credit (or debit) card to do online shopping; however, this is not always the case. For instance, Yamin, a Zomi speaking Burmese solo mother of two adopted teenagers, described how she was able to circumvent the requirement of a credit card by using an online retailer that offered a range of payment options, including money orders and bank transfers: “I do online shopping … I do not have a credit card … I pay from my bank … and then they [the online store] send it to me.” This series of transactions represents Yamin reorienting herself in an unfamiliar information environ ment through a new way of knowing shopping and negotiating payment. It is a practical response based on an evaluation of her current conditions, including her need to purchase a particular item and her recognition of the opportunities available to her for doing so.

Applying for jobs is another common ICT‐mediated practice described to us by our participants. For instance, Maung, who currently works as a forklift operator, confided that he was interested in getting another job: “I am doing a job search in New Zealand … using computers. [I] try to send [my] CV by email … It is very good for finding a job. Some jobs are advertised on the Internet. It is good!” Here, a familiar activity―changing jobs―is transformed by new information sources and ways of acting. Not only can the job search be performed online, but the application process itself is online. Maung is clearly responsive to the perceived benefits of online job hunting and is adjusting to lif in an information society.

While the transactional instrumental practices typically presented by our participants were related to information needs firmly grounded in their present circumstances, some examples of ICT use reflected more of an orien tation towards the past. We explained previously how Shwe is particularly attentive to what happens in the Burmese village that he left behind many years ago. However, his interest goes beyond just monitoring his village's situation:

Many people from my village live in the United States, Denmark, Sweden, Norway, and Australia. We do something for our village … We have a leader who is in the US. He organizes the work we have to do fo our village, like water―because in my country it is hard to get [drinkable] water. So [every family] has to pay 200, 300 dollars, or something like that … We [send] the money to our country, so they [can] buy the pipe and get water … We do the same for electricity in our village.

Here, Shwe provides an interesting example of how ICT is used by the Burmese transnational diaspora to overcome geography in coordinating their efforts and transferring funds to Burma to help those they have left behind. In this way, Shwe is able to maintain his identification with and connection to the people and places from his past in a tangible and meaningful way.

## 5.2.2 | Communicating with others

One of the most salient problems confronting many of our participants was communicating in a new or unfamiliar language, particularly the need to interact and organize activities with English‐speaking New Zealanders. For these participants, the structured and asynchronous nature of email communication offers an alternative, less stressful communication channel. Sachita was forced to leave Bhutan as a child and lived for 20 years in a refugee camp i Nepal before entering New Zealand with her husband and two children. She explained how a simple tool such as emai is important in both arranging her activities as a school cleaner and communicating with her five‐year‐old son' teacher and refugee education coordinator:

I get emails from her about my older kid … especially about English … She is the teacher. The schoo interpreters translate them [from English] into Nepali … ‘He has to read this book’ … Sometimes, he messages are just for me because I am the cleaner, so she asks me ‘Please [take care of the] staff room.

Bibek and Shristi, who left Bhutan as teenagers, completed high school and married in a Nepalese refugee camp before entering New Zealand with two children (a third was born after resettlement). Bibek and Shristi, who ofte mediate between their fellow refugees and various service providers and government agencies, prefer the higher degree of certainty offered by email communication. Shristi, who works as a Nepali interpreter at a local hospital explained: “[We email] service providers like the community nurse, GP [general practitioner] sometimes, banking staf … Others sometimes ask us to do some help [as interpreters] and we reply [to] them by email.”

Email is a common and accepted method of communication in New Zealand, and individuals and organizations will often assume that online communication is available and accessible as a norm. Cecile fled violence in her native Congo and spent almost four years in a refugee camp in Uganda before entering New Zealand with her daughter. Cecile, who has French and Swahili as mother tongues and became fluent in Kiganda during her stay in Uganda, conceded that her command of English is not good. To overcome this, she uses online dictionaries to complement her use of email in everyday communication. For example, as a member of her church choir, she often receives emails from th church community:

Everything they send is through email … ‘We are going to meet that date, that time’ … Sometimes I cannot reply to them. Sometimes I can reply to them, but I am using my [online] translator. Computers still help us … A lot!

Cecile's ICT use in interacting with others is a practical response to the contingent demands placed on her as a non‐fluent English speaker in a modern information society. As Bibek emphatically asserted:

Computer technology and Internet is important for people of refugee background in New Zealand. New Zealand is a computerized world; not a lot of people in Bhutan or Nepal use computers. No computer, no job can be done in New Zealand.

Our participants underline the importance that ICT offers for bridging the language barrier. Some of them see the potential of ICT and express their wish of having a wider range of capabilities to fulfil their needs. This is the case of Aung, an ethnically Karen Burmese widow who is currently studying English that entered New Zealand with her onl daughter in 2011. She expressed that it would be useful to have a “Karen‐English‐Karen online translator” in order to be able to communicate with government agencies.

## 5.2.3 | Managing everyday life

For some of our participants, their use of ICT offered opportunities to manage their everyday lives. The multiple ways our participants use ICT for managing their everyday lives go from checking tide times and charts online for go fishing (as Bijay does), using online maps to look for a specific address (as Cecile does), planning a camping with the family (as Thaw does) to preparing for the driving test (as Thaw did). Furthermore, ICT use by our participants also involves deal ing with work related activities in their host country. For instance, Carmen, a solo mother of three who spent a few months in Ecuador after fleeing from drug‐related violence in her native Colombia, relies on her mobile phone to organize her work as a cleaner. Yamin offers a more striking illustration of how ICT helps resettled refugees carry out their activities in a new country. She ascribes a high value to her refurbished computer; besides using its calendar applica tion for recording her appointments, she uses it to plan and organize her work schedule as a bilingual assistant at th local primary school:

It is just like my notebook, my diary … Every appointment, I put there and then check, ‘Oh! Tomorrow I have appointments’, or whatever … ‘Today I have two classes … [so] what kind of things [do] I have to try, to teach’ … ‘OK, today I have to try children writing, something like that, I have to try reading today’ … I put everything in there.

In this description of her ICT use, Yamin draws an analogy with a notebook or diary. Here, we see an existing schema―use of a familiar, paper‐based medium to organize one's activities―transformed into a new way of acting in response to the contingencies of Yamin's current circumstances and the possibilities afforded by the ICT availabl to her.

Other problems related specifically to a newcomer's situation, such as trying to interact with others on an everyday basis in an unfamiliar language, may prompt our participants to use ICT to assist the transition to a new country For example, Hamid, an Eritrean man fluent in multiple languages, who spent eight years in Sudan before entering New Zealand with his wife and children, expressed his frustration at his perceived limited skill with the English lan guage. He felt that this was preventing him from finding employment in his new country, despite previously representing Eritrea as an Olympic swimmer and having worked as a swimming instructor at a foreign embassy i Sudan. Hamid uses his computer for taking daily English lessons, as well as emailing his English teacher on a regular basis. He emphasized, “I want to perfect my British English … The computer is like a teacher at home.” Similarly Kyi, a politically active Burmese man who spent 16 years in Thailand before being relocated to New Zealand wit his wife and two children, explained how he uses various online resources to supplement the English lessons that he is taking: “I do not understand English … I saw in the website [an] English learning course … speaking, grammar.”

Arguably, these participants' use of ICT to improve their ability with the English language reflects their intention to participate more fully in New Zealand society. Their dedication and perseverance with learning English is an imaginative response to the frustrations of their somewhat problematic current circumstances as resettled refugees. Thei agency here has a projective element, oriented towards an anticipated future in which the English language is not a barrier to their social inclusion.

For other participants, becoming familiar with ICT and developing capabilities in ICT use were considered essential to attaining a desired future life in New Zealand. Some of these individuals anticipated that ICT skills would make them more employable or help them to set up their own businesses. Similarly, many of our participants expressed their satisfaction that their children are learning to use ICT, ultimately enabling them to have the same opportunities as others in an information society. Nabin, who studied for a degree, married, and had three children during the 19 years he spent in a refugee camp in Nepal after leaving Bhutan, empha sized the importance of his children being competent ICT users, “in order to flow with the same technology―otherwise they will be lagging behind.” Considering the importance of ICT to his and his family's future, Prem, who left Bhutan as a teenager and spent 18 years in Nepal before entering New Zealand, concluded: “[Without ICT] we would be switched off from the world.”

## 5.2.4 | Temporal agentic orientations on ICT‐mediated instrumental information practices

The examples presented here demonstrate how resettled refugees respond to the particular circumstances of their settlement in a new country. As would be expected, many of their ICT‐mediated instrumental information practices are a deliberative response to the emergent demands and contingencies of their present situation, in which ICT is used to seek specific information or perform a particular task. These instrumental practices facilitate their participation and functioning in a new and unfamiliar environment. While the predominant agentic orientation in such practices is practical‐evaluative, we observed that our participants also enact ICT use in ways that reflect an orientation towards the past, as they maintain their identification with people and places left behind, as well as towards the future, as they self‐reflexively anticipate their prospective lives in their host country. These practices demonstrate how the three temporal constitutive elements of human agency are intertwined in how they shape the ways in which ICT is instrumentally used by resettled refugees for transacting, communicating, and managing their resettled lives.

## 5.3 | Expressive information practices

The traditional view of refugee resettlement as an information problem leads to a focus on orienting and instrumental information practices that involve directed and non‐directed information seeking and use in navigating an unfamiliar environment. By contrast, expressive information practices focus our attention on the social and communicative dimensions of information use and sharing among individuals and groups. For our participants, ICT‐mediated communicative or expressive activities were clearly connected to their emotiona well‐being, cultural identity, and quality of life in a new country as we demonstrate in the next sub‐sections We identified three patterns of ICT‐mediated expressive practices in our data: “sustaining support networks,” “maintaining transnational ties,” and “expressing cultural identity.”

## 5.3.1 | Sustaining support networks

For our participants, ICT‐mediated expressive information practices often involve communication with othe members of formal and informal support networks in New Zealand. Given the specific circumstances or our partici pants as resettled refugees, these support networks are typically organized around particular ethno‐cultural groups, who offer a degree of familiarity in a new country. For example, Cecile describe how the expatriate Congolese com munity in New Zealand communicates and exchanges information using an email group set up for that purpose:

I got emails from the president … the one who looks after the Congolese community … He sends us emails… about meetings or something … It is helpful… Sometimes he sends [emails] in French, but most of the times he sends in English because he says we must learn more English … If we do not learn English, life is going to be hard.

This kind of ICT‐mediated communication has a dual purpose in sustaining social links among the resettled Congolese people and providing information and support that assists in their resettlement (such as the need to be competent in English in order to fully participate in their host country).

Undoubtedly, ICT offers advantages in helping resettled refugees maintain social connections with other members of their ethno‐cultural community. However, our participants recognize the limits of what technology can do in a particular social setting. In this situation, they resort to a hybrid mode of social interaction that combines ICTmediated connection and face‐to‐face communication. Madhu, who experienced major life events during the 19 years she spent in a refugee camp in Nepal (she met and married Bijay, and gave birth to her two children there), illustrates this hybrid mode of social interaction. Madhu, who currently works as a bilingual assistant on a part‐basis, explained that she relies on ICT―specifically Skype and email―to learn about her Bhutanese friends in the United States and New Zealand. Then, she shares this ICT‐mediated information through face‐to‐face encounters with other members of the local Bhutanese community for two practical reasons: “Some of them do not use email and I do not know how to type in Nepalese on a computer keyboard.” Nabin, who is a committee member of his local Bhutanese community in a different location, describes a similar approach. He uses his computer and desktop publishing software to prepare a community newsletter that he distributes to the Bhutanese community in his area. Again, we can see how this par: ticular practice helps to coordinate the network and sustain the social links among its members (including their infor mation about their activities and the sharing of creative content):

I do some community jobs also here … I prepare documents. I do the newsletter … Community updates … and everything we have done, what we are doing, what the committee is doing. And what things are going around, and some work of people, like pieces of writing.

In another example, Thaw, as the Christian leader of a Chin Burmese community, uses one of the three computers that he has in his home to type his sermons. Thaw, who is planning to study for a Bachelor of Theology, also produces and prints the church newsletters, as well as distributing announcements by email.

Although how Cecile, Madhu, Nabin, and Thaw may engage in ICT use in different ways, they all have a similar for mulation. Their use of ICT is intended to participate and sustain social relations within a particular ethno‐cultural group

## 5.3.2 | Maintaining transnational ties

Using ICT to sustain ethno‐cultural support networks is a practical response to their present life in a new country for our participants. However, as resettled refugees, they have also left family members and friends behind in various countries around the world. We have previously seen how our participants use a range of digital media, such as emai groups, Facebook and Skype, to monitor and keep informed about their extended communities. ICT‐mediated communication also offers opportunities for more expressive practices that contribute to the maintenance of emotional connections with friends and family separated by time and geography. For instance, Sandi, a Burmese woman who entered New Zealand in 2011 after 23 years in Thailand following her exodus from Burma in 1988 as a young teenager, relies on email communication to keep in touch with her relatives and friends in Thailand.

In another example, Sachita explained how her family uses a combination of ICT functionalities and devices (including a conventional landline) to organize a virtual rendezvous:

My mother and father are still in Nepal … There are people who have computers there. My mom and dad pay money there, then we talk [to] each other, we look [at] each other, and write messages … My dad calls me on the landline; he tells me ‘We want to talk at this time’. At that time, we stay at home, so we talk to each other.

What is clear from Sachita's explanation is that an Internet‐based video chat session offers a more satisfying emo tional connection than a simple phone call, Similarly. Cecile emphasized the importance of experiencing a visual con nection with loved ones left behind: “They are very far [away], but through the computer we can see them. When you see their face, we are very happy. We are like we are together.” Cecile, who fled her village when it was raided by one of the various belligerent groups in Congo, uses Facebook to help her track the whereabouts of people that she lost touch with, including close family members:

The computer is helping me a lot, because I lost my contact with my sisters and brothers. One of my friends, on Facebook, he's telling me, ‘Cecile, we found your sister.’ The first time I talked to my sister it was through Facebook, and she said, ‘Yes, I'm here, I'm still alive.’ I was nearly, like, to hug the computer!

Continued contact with the significant people in one's life is an important aspect of human social existence. In th examples described above, our participants are oriented to the past and exercise an iterational agency in using ICT to maintain valued interactions and connections over time and space. In turn, their ICT‐mediated practices contribute t reducing the uncertainty and anxiety arising from their disrupted lives.

## 5.3.3 | Expressing cultural identity

We observed that many of the resettled refugees that we spoke with use ICT as a vehicle for cultural expres sion through either the consumption or production of online cultural content. The Internet offers a source of information and entertainment from the countries that constitute a resettled refugee's history and often in his or her own language. Examples described to us include watching Nepalese or Nigerian movies, Burmese or Ethiopian comedy, Burmese boxing, Rwandan or Ugandan music television channels, listening to Chin songs, or singing Kayan karaoke. Other participants use ICT to sustain their religious practice. Prabhakar, an unem ployed man with several physical disabilities, spent 18 years in a refugee camp in Nepal before entering New Zealand with his wife and two children. Because he cannot communicate in English, Prabhakar relies on ICT use to practice his religion: “I watch religious programs in our own language, in Nepali … program that my friends put in YouTube.” Similarly, Cecile explained: “Sometimes, when I want to listen to the Word of God, I can go to YouTube and look at this preacher, who is preaching.”

Using ICT to consume cultural content is not always about entertainment or religion; sometimes it is about general interest content. For instance, Than, an ethnic Zophei Burmese man who spent four years in Malaysia before entering New Zealand in 2003, created an online forum where the Zophei community spread over different countries can share content in their own language. He explained how he consumes content in Zophei language from the forum he created, “if [members of the forum] put stories of famous people, I want to read [it].” In another example, Aom―Myo's wife born in Thailand―explained that she often “goes to Thai websites to look up Thai recipes.” In al these examples, the consumption of ethnic, religious or general content in their mother tongue through ICT use reproduces past patterns of thought and action that help to sustain cultural identities and provide some stability in the lives of these individuals who were forcibly displaced

ICT is also used by our participants to produce cultural content that helps them to maintain a continued connection with their country of origin or the places that they left behind. For instance, Ashmi, who only completed seven years of education, is now in her 30s, and works as a Nepali bilingual tutor at a local primary school in New Zealand, confided: “I use the computer to write a journal about my country.” By “my country” she refers to Nepal, not her country of birth, Bhutan, which she left when she was a small child. Her use of ICT to record her past expe riences reflects a desire to maintain a connection with and memory of the place she left behind.

When Zaw was resettled in New Zealand. he was in his mid-20s, and was accompanied by his wife anc one child. Since then, the family has grown, with two more children born in New Zealand. Zaw described his intention to produce a website that would document his family history, preserving his family's cultural and ethnic roots for his descendants, who are going to be New Zealanders: “In the future, my grandson or grand daughter can read about it on the web.” Here, Zaw is reflexively reorienting his thinking from the past towards the future, and imaginatively considering how ICT affords the production and sharing of information in a particular way―one that will record his family's exceptional journey and preserve their cultural legacy and identity for future generations.

## 5.3.4 | Temporal agentic orientations on ICT‐mediated expressive information practices

The enactment of ICT‐mediated expressive information practices by our participants–whether in response to the need for support in their present circumstances, the maintenance of their past experiences and connections, or imag ined scenarios for cultural continuity–reveals the disrupted and bifurcated lives of resettled refugees. Such patterns of ICT use reflect the importance of establishing stability and order in a new stage of life, as well as the value attached to maintaining and expressing a cultural identity in a new country

## 6 | DISCUSSION

In presenting our findings, we distinguish between three categories of ICT‐mediated information practices: orienting, instrumental, and expressive. In practice, the three categories are often intertwined and the boundaries between them blurred. Indeed, the information needs confronting our participants are complex and may often extend across orienting, problem‐solving, and information‐sharing activities (Caidi et al., 2010). Nevertheless, distinguishing these three categories enabled a more detailed analysis of the practices that we observed in our data (Savolainen, 2008). First, we were able to identify specific patterns of participant ICT use within each category of information practices. Thus, the predominant patterns constituting ICT‐mediated orienting practices among our participants involved learn ing about the new environment in which they have been resettled, and keeping informed about what is going on i their host country and with the places and people that matter to them. ICT‐mediated instrumental practices involved transacting, communicating, and managing their lives in an information society. ICT‐mediated expressive practice were implicated in sustaining social and cultural support networks, maintaining transnational ties, and expressing cul tural identity. With regard to the latter, the use of ICT to produce and consume content that essentially comprise cultural symbols, reinforces cultural identities that are “ultimately rooted in a more or less invariant network of socia relations” (Bauman, 1999, p. 87)

Second, by delving deeper into the data associated with specific ICT‐mediated information practices we were able to track differences in participants' temporal orientations when they described using ICT in various ways. We suggest that how individuals orient themselves variably to the present, past, and future, influences how they enact particular ICT‐mediated information practices. The conventional understanding of providing ICT training and access to refugees frames resettlement as an information problem. From this perspective, ICT is useful in overcoming th challenges of settling in a new country about which individuals have little or no prior information. This view empha sizes a practical‐evaluative temporal agentic orientation where ICT is used in responding to the practical contingen cies of the present, and certainly we observed many instances of such ICT use across all three categories of information practices. However, as resettled refugees, our participants inhabit more complex temporal-relational con texts than their present circumstances in a new country. In reality, their unique personal and social histories, togethe with the prospects of imagined future scenarios, are intertwined with the pressures imposed by the current and constantly changing conditions. These overlapping temporal‐relational contexts present them with an on‐going stream of information needs (Cousins & Robey, 2005), and it is against this background that they perceive the possibilities offered by the ICT available to them as a vehicle for solving those needs. While grounded in the present, their enactment of ICT-mediated practices also involves “working through emerging choice situations with eves on the past anc the future” (Chu & Robey, 2008, p. 83). Thus, our participants' differential use of ICT in various ICT‐mediated infor mation practices reflects all three temporal agentic orientations: practical‐evaluative, iterational, and projective Figure 1 summarizes the interweaving of these temporal agentic orientations and their shaping of the patterned ICT‐mediated information practices that our participants enacted.

When participants' agentic orientations are predominantly practical-evaluative in nature. their ICT-mediatec information practices are markedly responsive to challenges that arise in their present lives. Accordingly, the practi cal‐evaluative orientation shapes, our participants' patterns of ICT use in a way that satisfy their immediate information and communication needs. Their assessment of the current conditions governs the patterns of ICT use that are concerned with addressing the uncertainties of unfolding events in order to navigate an unfamiliar setting and achieve a degree of stability in previously disrupted lives. Thus, their ICT use variously involves actions to engage with thei context of settlement; to deal with everyday problems, communicate, and perform tasks in a new environment i which ICT is pervasive; and to develop and sustain social connections and support networks in their local communities. Contextual knowledge and understanding of a new society, effective interaction with one's environment, and being socially connected and supported, are all aspects that address our participants' anxieties and insecurities (Elias & Lemish, 2009), and thus enable their fuller participation in society. Such participation satisfies an individual's need for competence and a sense of empowerment and agency in his or her life, and is an important contributor to th social inclusion of resettled refugees (Díaz Andrade & Doolin, 2016)

However, our participants' lives are not restricted to engaging with a new information environment and connecting with a local community (Lloyd et al., 2013; Wilding, 2009). While they are mindful of their current circumstances and respond to the present challenges of life in a new environment, the trauma of having been forced to leave their countries of origin―in some cases, in a rather abrupt and violent way―create a need to sustain a coherent identity and to repair disrupted lives. Accordingly, our participants engage in ICT‐mediated information practices in a way that is anchored in their lives prior to resettlement, which allow them to maintain a connection with their past and to reaffirm who they are. These practices are attuned to and influenced by past experiences―shaped by an iterationa agentic orientation in ways that are cognitively and emotionally related to contributions that support their cultura identities and affective interactions over time. For instance, ICT use features significantly in maintaining communication and transnational ties with family and friends separated by distance. The use of ICT to construct a virtual connectedness that can substitute for physical co-location, however imperfectly or temporarily, reflects the repetition of pre‐existing and familiar practices of communication for our participants (Wilding, 2006). Such ICT‐mediated practices provide a source of emotional support and psychological comfort, in turn reducing the stress and social isolation of settling in a new country (Benítez, 2012; Caidi et al., 2010; Elias & Lemish, 2009)

Our participants also use ICT to access online news and social networking tools in monitoring (and on occasions intervening in) communities and places that they have left behind. Such ICT‐mediated practices reflect the “backward gaze” (Hiller & Franz, 2004, p. 741; see also Elias & Lemish, 2009) of resettled refugees and the need to maintain connectivity and identification with their origins—even if a return is not contemplated or indeed possible. Similarly, the consumption or production of online cultural content creates a symbolic sense of closeness with their past and acts as an expression of cultural identity (Caidi et al., 2010). ICT use like this enables our participants to re‐establish the sense of community that has become disrupted in their forced displacement (Wilding, 2012). This, in turn, contributes to the resettlement of refugees by emphasizing and rebuilding continuity between their past and present circumstances (Hiller & Franz, 2004). The use of ICT to sustain, and even empower, a prior cultural identity allows resettled refugees to manage the bifurcated nature of their lives as “alien outsider[s] in a strange land” (Hiller & Franz, 2004, p. 747). As Gifford and Wilding (2013) suggest, “Core to settlement is coming to belong in a new host country while at the same time, maintaining and creating new forms of belonging in a globalized world” (p. 15).

Finally, being positioned between the old and the new produces imaginative responses to their particular circumstances by our participants. Here, they use ICT to unlock the prospects to bring about betterment and help realize per sonal aspirations in a new social environment. Their predominant agentic orientation is projective, as they engage in ICT‐mediated information practices that anticipate future scenarios and lives. Such practices include actively scanning their environment for information and trends relevant to situations not yet materialized but considered attainable, and acquiring the language, educational, employment, and technical skills that would allow them to enhance their life prospects in the future. There was also some evidence to support the possibility of ICT being used to create vehicles for the expression of cultural or family histories, identities, and narratives (Benítez, 2012), and their preservation for future generations. We would argue that in these ways, ICT offers important possibilities for resettled refugees in envisioning and constructing meaningful lives in new circumstances

## 7 | CONCLUSION

In this paper, we have sought to offer a theoretically informed explanation (Gregor, 2006) of how resettled refugees use ICT to respond their changed circumstances In doing so we analyzed their enactment of differ ent ICT‐mediated information practices―orienting, instrumental, and expressive―as they exercise their agency in everyday situations in their new country. In a society where the production and transmission of information through ICT plays a central role, these ICT‐mediated information practices collectively augment the opportunities for resettled refugees to make informed decisions for the attainment of their desired outcomes. By using a temporal theory of agency to disaggregate human agency into three constituent elements oriented towards the present, past, and future (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998), we draw attention to how resettled refugees enact ICT‐medi ated information practices that encompass current dilemmas and contingencies, reinforce past connections and cultura identities, and frame future expectations and aspirations. In this way, we contribute a form of causal explanation (Avgerou, 2013) of how resettled refugees engage in ICT use to manage their lives in a country where they ended up living as a result of circumstances beyond their control, and how their past experiences, current circumstances, and imagined future make a difference to their ICT use. When resettled refugees are focused on the present, they exercise a practical‐evaluative form of agency in using ICT to minimize the uncertainties of the unfamiliar information environment that they have to navigate. When their past experiences give rise to an iterative agentic orientation, their ICTmediated information practices are focused on reaffirming and upholding their cultural identity through, as Dertouzous (1991) prophetically described, “the formation of electronic tribes that can span physical distance” (p. 69). When their ICT‐mediated information practices are shaped by a projective agentic orientation, their engagement with ICT is directed at imagined future scenarios in preparing for anticipated events and situations. Our findings provide support for Emirbayer and Mische's (1998) assertion that actors who find themselves in the problematic present of unsettled situations often expand their capacity for deliberative response while developing new ways to integrate past and future perspectives.

Capturing the richness of ICT use by resettled refugees requires acknowledging that they inhabit multiple and overlapping temporal‐relational contexts. As they respond to this contextual complexity, individuals are constantly switching between different temporal orientations depending on their changing needs and goals in any given circumstance. Consequently, their ICT‐mediated information practices vary as their temporal orientations change. While w have focused on predominant temporal agentic orientations in discussing the various ICT‐mediated information prac tices that we observed in our study, all three temporal orientations may be implicated in a particular emergent situation (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998). In many cases, distinguishing a predominant orientation for a particular ICT mediated practice may be difficult or a problematic simplification of a complex reality

Our findings highlight the importance of ICT use for people with refugee backgrounds in closing information and social gaps as they seek to alleviate the burden of forced displacement and disrupted lives (Elias & Lemish, 2009; Gifford & Wilding, 2013). Certainly, ICT use facilitates effective participation in a society of which resettled refugees have little or no prior knowledge, providing a vehicle with which they can exercise agency in regaining control over their everyday lives. However, the resettlement of refugees is more than an information problem; their ICT use also involves communicative and expressive activities that contribute to their emotional well‐being and stability by reducing the uncertainty and anxiety arising from their involuntary relocation in a new country. In a globalized world, in which ICT use transcends geographic distance, resettled refugees are able to maintain meaningful connections to people and places in multiple relevant locations. The value derived from being able to sustain and express a distinct cultural identity in a new host country is an important part of managing the bifurcated selves of resettled refugees

The causal explanation we provide here constitutes an interpretation of the complex interaction between pat terns of ICT‐mediated information practices by resettled refugees and the temporal agentic orientations that shape those practices. In this way, our analysis renders a theoretical, meaningful explanation of a real‐life phenomeno (Walsham, 1995). Rich insights can be derived from this explanation and applied in other contexts. An alternative approach may focus the analysis on how resettled refugees' demographic variables (eg, age, level of education, marita status, employment status) predict their particular ICT‐mediated information practices. This type of analysis can b supplemented by incorporating the time elapsed in the country as another variable.<sup>¶</sup> In addition, the influence of temporal agentic orientations on ICT use can be fully scrutinized with a different group of actors where the living experience may delineate their engagement in ICT use (eg, senior individuals, teenagers). This analysis can be conducted i diverse, non‐mandatory environmental backgrounds (eg, family, entertainment) in order to capture the influence of the contextual conditions.

Our study has practical implications for the formulation of policy around the settlement of refugees, in particular in relation to the provision of access to ICT against the background of the information society. Our findings demonstrate the importance of devising policies intended to build digital skills among resettle refugees; without being able to use ICT, their options for functioning and inclusion in their host countries would be seriously curtailed. In doing so, it is important to acknowledge the complexity of compliance and communication in a society where the primary information media and modes of interaction with government and service providers are digital. Such complexity is intensified by the prevailing cultural assumptions and schema that underlie ICT use in such societies, and which may be unfamiliar to resettled refugees. Thus, relevant policy needs to ensure that support is provided for a longer period until these individuals are able to construct new ways of knowing and develop a degree of “informational self‐reliance” (Kennan et al., 2011, p. 207). Our findings also emphasize that the needs of resettled refugees go beyond those intended to address their immediate problems or to increase their prospects of becoming merely productive members in their host country. ICT policies for these individuals should transcend the goal of simply making them employable; ICT policies should recognize the importance of emotional well‐being, including their need to maintain affective and social links with those left behind, and to express a sense of belonging and identity in their resettlement.

While our research focuses on a particular group of ICT users, our examination reveals a fine‐grained perspective of the mutual interaction between ICT use and social practices, and simultaneously offers a causal explanation, albei incomplete, of an information systems phenomenon (cf. Avgerou, 2013). Our findings are grounded in everyday ICT use and demonstrate that the malleable nature of ICT (Orlikowski, 2000) is not given but constantly negotiated (Kallinikos, 2002) and heavily dependent on specific users' needs and goals in situated and temporal‐relational contexts (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998; Lamb & Kling, 2003). We invite information systems researchers to continue to examine this process in which users respond to their environment(s) in light of the perceived properties and functionalities of the ICT available to them. Further and deeper analysis will give us a better understanding of the opportunities for action associated with ICT use in relation to users' needs and goals
