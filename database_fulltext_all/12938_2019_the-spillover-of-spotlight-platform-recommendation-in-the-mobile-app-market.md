# The Spillover of Spotlight: Platform Recommendation in the Mobile App Market

Chen Liang,<sup>a</sup> Zhan (Michael) Shi,<sup>b</sup> T. S. Raghu<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Operations and Information Management Department, University of Connecticut, Storrs, Connecticut 06269; <sup>b</sup> W. P. Carey School of Business, Arizona State University, Tempe, Arizona 85287

Abstract. Many e-commerce platforms offer editor-curated recommendations to help consumers overcome the difficulty of discovering and evaluating new products. Whereas it is well recognized that the spotlight generated by editor recommendations has a positive effect on the sales of featured products, there is much less understanding in the literature on whether the spotlight influences the sales of other products that are not featured but are related to the featured products and how this externality varies depending on the specific relationship between the featured and nonfeatured products. By leveraging a novel data set collected from the mobile app market, this paper systematically investigates the spillover effect of platform-provided editor recommendations on three groups of related apps: apps by the same developer, apps with similar functionality, and the same apps marketed on a different platform. We distinguish the two mechanisms that potentially drive the externality: exposure spillover and quality endorsement spillover. We find that the overall spillover effect is positive for all three groups, but the underlying mechanisms are different. For the apps by the same developer and the same apps on a different platform, we find a significantly positive spillover of exposure, but the spillover of quality endorsement is weaker across platforms than within the same platform; for the functionally similar apps, the evidence on spillover of exposure is weaker than that for the other two groups, and the spillover of quality endorsement is statistically insignificant. In addition, we also find that the strength of the spillover effect depends on the salient characteristics of the featured apps such as price and user rating

## 1. Introduction

The tremendous increase in the number of products available on e-commerce platforms makes it difficult both for new products to stand out and for consumers to find new products that meet their specific needs. Effective product discovery via “organic” channels such as sales rank- or collaborative filtering-based recommender systems typically requires a sizable customer base, which most new products lack. Thus, to bootstrap growth and promote diversity, many platforms offer editor-curated recommendations to help consumers overcome the difficulty of discovering and evaluating new products. For instance, Amazon hires editors to review and recommend new books. Apple’s App Store actively maintains a list of “Apps We Love Right Now” (formerly “New Apps We Love”) and highlights the list at a prominent place on its homepage to attract user attention.

Although it is well recognized that the spotlight generated by platform editors’ recommendations can boost the sales of featured products, there is much less understanding in the literature on the spillover of platform spotlight, that is, whether and how platformprovided editor recommendations also influence the commercial performance of other products that are not featured. Formally, this paper investigates the following research questions:

1. Do platform-provided editor recommendations influence the sales of other products that are not featured but are related to the featured products?

2. If so, how does this spillover effect vary depending on the specific relationship between the featured and nonfeatured products? More important, what are the mechanisms that drive the spillover? How does the spillover effect depend on the characteristics of the featured products (e.g., price and user rating)?

Given the ubiquity of platform recommendations in large online markets, these research questions have both practical and academic significance. Platform operators have recently ramped up investment in optimizing store design and providing humancurated discovery mechanisms including editorial recommendations to improve user experience and market efficiency. One recent example is Apple’s redesign of its iOS App Store in September 2017. Its emphasis on improving editorial recommendations is especially noteworthy: “Apple decided that the best way to ensure that valuable apps are getting discovered is through editorial content—shifting even more weight away from algorithm-based suggestions and moving toward human-curated lists of featured apps that Apple wants to push.”<sup>1</sup> With the goal of improving product discovery, the new store features more editor-curated recommendations and provides sellers more tools to demonstrate the functionality of their apps. Given the size and significance of platform markets, it is important to examine whether platform investments in discovery mechanisms are indeed effective in aiding consumer search and improving product discovery. From a theoretical point of view, scholars have studied not only the direct effect of platform recommendations on the featured products but also the indirect effect on consumer behavior (e.g., Cosley et al. 2003, Adomavicius et al. 2017) and market outcome (e.g., Fleder and Hosanagar 2009). Exploring the spillover of platform spotlights and investigating the underlying mechanisms that drive the spillover can inform us on the externality of platform recommendations and, as a result, help us understand more completely their overall market implications.

In a large, differentiated market, promotional activities in general have two potential effects on product sales. First, they increase consumers’ awareness of the featured products. Promotions reduce the consumer search cost, and products featured in promotions enjoy more exposure and are more likely to enter the consideration set of consumers (e.g., Goeree 2008, Fleder and Hosanagar 2009). Second, depending on the specific context, promotions may also directly influence consumers’ purchase decisions by altering the evaluation of products in their consideration set (e.g., Ackerberg 2001, Clark et al. 2009, Adomavicius et al. 2017).

Both the awareness and choice effects have potential externality on other products that are not featured but are related to the featured products. For example, promotional activities not only may attract consumer attention to the featured products but also may prompt consumers to explore functionally similar products that they would not have otherwise considered. Moreover, in markets of experience goods where consumers face considerable uncertainty about product quality prior to consumption, a recommendation from a credible source should increase consumers’ expectations of product quality, which may, in turn, affect the ordering of other options in consumers’ consideration set. Therefore, to develop a deeper understanding of the spillover mechanisms of the product promotional activities, we need to sepa rate their effects at the awareness and choice stages.

Our research specifically focuses on platform provided editor recommendations, which have sev eral salient features that make their spillover effects a both the awareness and choice stages potentially different from those of other forms of product pro motion that have been studied in the literature. First producer- or seller-initiated promotions, such as advertising, are often costly and can be prohibitively so for small and independent producers and sellers Presumably, they are also perceived differently by consumers (Benlian et al. 2012) because seller self interest should play a bigger role in advertising than in platform-provided recommendation. Moreover, whereas platform-provided editor recommendations typically focus on the featured product itself, there is much more heterogeneity in terms of the information provided in advertisements, which can range from directing consumers to other products of the same seller to comparing the advertised product with its rivals. Thus, the market effects of the two forms of pro motion are expected to be different. Second, platform initiated, sales- or ratings-based promotional or recommendation approaches such as best-seller lists and collaborative filters require products to already have sizable user base. For example, a commonly used collaborative filtering approach is to use information about a product’s existing customers by correlating thei purchase histories with those of others who have not purchased the focal product yet. For such systems to work effectively, the existing customer base needs to exceed a certain threshold, which can be difficult for new products to achieve organically, hence the term cold-start problem. As such, editor recommendations curated by “experts,” are less dependent on customer purchase data and are used by many platforms to complement collaborative filters to promote new products or underexplored niche products, for which consumers face greater uncertainty in evaluating quality. Another difference is that whereas collaborative filters leverage purchase choices made by other consumers and employ personalization, editor-curated recommendations can be seen by all platform users and are provided by platform-recognized experts who are supposed to be more informed and experi enced in the product domain and less biased in evaluating the quality of products, as well as to have test used the products for an extensive period of time Therefore, an editor-curated recommendation might serve as a stronger endorsement of quality than a collaborative filtering–based recommendation for less known products (Senecal and Nantel 2004).

The fact that editorial recommendations are curated by humans and may potentially aid consumers product choice decisions makes it also important to compare editorial recommendations with expert reviews (also called professional reviews in the literature), which share the same two characteristics. A key distinction between editorial recommendations and expert reviews is that at the conceptual level, expert reviews are better categorized as pull-type information cues and recommendations as push-type information cues (Xu et al. 2009). The consumption of expert reviews generally requires consumers’ voluntary search, and consumers typically search for and read expert reviews after they become aware of the product and when they need more information to resolve uncertainty about the product. This implies that expert reviews would mainly affect consumers at the product choice stage but much less so at the awareness stage. In contrast, platform recommendations are by nature highlighted at the platform level and are often “pushed” to consumers, so their influence on consumer decision making is likely to take place at both the awareness and choice stages. Second, the direction and extent of the influence on consumer choice can be very different between platform recommendations and expert reviews. Whereas platform recommendations can simply be a curated list of products without any detailed product information (which is the case in our empirical context), the essence of expert reviews is in their informational content—their effect crucially depends on the informativeness and sentiment of the review.

The preceding discussion on the differences between editorial recommendations and other promotional activities suggests that their market implications and specifically spillover effects can be quite distinct. This paper empirically investigates the spillover effect of editor-curated recommendations in the context of the mobile app market. The mobile app market has grown rapidly during the last decade, and the total size of the app economy is projected to reach \$101 billion by 2020. Although the market is becoming more competitive, the distribution of app downloads is highly skewed—one report suggests that over 90% of apps are downloaded less than 500 times per day.<sup>2</sup> As such, app discovery channels including editorial recommendations are recognized to be crucial for both platform owners and app developers. Hence, studying the effects of editorial recommendations will help platforms understand which apps benefited from the spotlight, how they benefited, and to what extent. Moreover, app developers commonly adopt multihoming (an app is released on multiple app markets; Idu et al. 2011, Ghose and Han 2014, Cennamo et al. 2018) and “freemium” pricing (versus paid pricing) business models (Bawa and Shoemaker 2004, Lee and Tan 2013, Liu et al. 2014, Deng et al. 2018, Bond et al. 2019). Both phenomena provide a unique angle for examining the nuances of the spillover of platform spotlight (see more details in Appendix A of the e-companion)

To conduct the analysis, we construct a novel data set on platform recommendations and product sales by merging both platform-provided and handcollected data of the two major mobile app distribution platforms, Apple’s iOS App Store and Google’s Play Store. The specific editor recommendation channel we choose to focus on is the “New Apps We Love” column on the iOS platform, where Apple’s store editors select newly released or recently updated apps to feature at a prominent place on its home page (see the Figure A2 in Appendix B of the e-companion).<sup>3</sup> Using our data set, we first establish the fact that editor recommendations have a positive impact on the sales of the featured apps. We then study the spillover effect of recommendations on three distinct groups of related apps: (1) iOS apps by the same developer, (2) iOS apps with similar func tionality, and (3) the same apps’ Android versions marketed on the Google Play platform. We choose to focus on these three groups of related apps for two reasons. First, as will be elaborated in Section 3, our conceptual framework posits the likely paths along which the spillover might take place for these groups of related apps. Second, there is evidence that clearly shows the theoretical paths of spillover that we postulate indeed exist for the three groups of related apps in our empirical context (see more discussion in Appendix B of the e-companion). In our analysis, we first use reduced-form regressions to examine the overall spillover effect on each of the three groups of related apps. The reduced-form regressions provide evidence on the existence and size of the spillover effects, but they do not inform us on how the effects play out across the awareness and choice stages. To delve into the underlying mechanism of spillover, we propose and discuss a two-stage framework of the product discovery and purchase process, and then we use the framework to motivate a bivariate probit model with partial observability (Poirier 1980, Sh et al. 2014) that allows us to separate the spillover effects at the awareness and choice stages.

We find that the spillover effects at both the awareness and choice stages can drive the externality of editor recommendation, but their strength and relative importance vary depending on the specific relationship between the featured and nonfeatured products. Furthermore, our heterogeneity analysis suggests that the strength of spillover effect also depends on the salient characteristics of the featured products including price and user rating.

Our work complements and extends the extant literature in exploring the spillover mechanisms of promotional activities. Whereas the informational role of promotion has been recognized in prior studies, to our knowledge, extant research has not simultaneously examined the awareness and endorsement spillover effects of product promotion activities. Moreover, our work systematically analyzes three distinct groups of related products and shows how the externality is different across the three groups by disentangling the spillover mechanisms in an empirical model. Our results regarding the heterogeneity in the spillover mechanism also bring useful insights to similar contexts where the “freemium” pricing model and producer multihoming phenomenon are popular.

## 2. Related Literature

Our study is related to several strands of literature on e-commerce and platform economics. The phenomenon of demand spillover has been studied in many different contexts, for example, in brand commitment (Ahluwalia et al. 2001), information policy (Gal-Or and Ghose 2005), sponsored keyword strategy (Lu and Yang 2017), and advertisements (Garthwaite 2014, Lewis and Nguyen 2015). The stream of research that is closest to this paper studies the spillover effect of product promotion activities such as advertising and recommender systems. The spillover effect of promotions on products sold by the same producer or similar products has been examined in several empirical studies. For example, Garthwaite (2014) found that advertisements have a positive effect on the sales of other books written by the endorsed authors. Lewis and Nguyen (2015) found that search-engine advertising increases the cumulative searches for competitors more than for the advertised product, which indicates a strong positive spillover effect on similar products. Using Amazon’s copurchase network data, Carmi et al. (2012) found that an exogenous demand shock brought by an endorsement on “The Oprah Winfrey Show” had a significantly positive effect on the sales of other books, most of which were either books written by the same author or books on a similar topic. Also using Amazon data, Oestreicher-Singer and Sundararajan (2012) and Jabr and Zheng (2013) studied the effects of collaborative filters on similar products in a copurchase network. In addition, there is evidence for cross-platform spillover in the contexts of sales ranking (Ghose and Han 2014) and product reviews (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006).

However, the existence and extent of spillover of editor-curated recommendations do not directly follow from the prior studies because, as discussed in Section 1, editor-curated recommendations differ from advertising and collaborative filtering–based recommender systems in several important aspects, including their source, method, and the way they are delivered to and perceived by consumers. Moreover, we further the investigation on the spillover effects in this study by examining the mechanisms that drive the phenomenon. To our knowledge, this paper is the first study to provide a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms of spillover at both the awareness and choice stages. We empirically document how the two stages differ for different types of related products. Table 1 provides a summary of the differences between this research and the related previous literature in terms of the type of product promotion studied, the mechanism of spillover examined, and the group of related products considered.

As discussed in Section 1, editor-curated recommendations are also related to professional reviews (although reviews are generally from independent experts as opposed to curated recommendations provided by platforms). The previous literature has found that expert reviews tend to have a positive impact on the demand for experience goods (Reinstein and Snyder 2005) and may serve as contingent moderators in the impact of general user reviews (Chakravarty et al. 2010). In the information systems literature, Zhou and Duan (2016) found that expert reviews could both directly and indirectly influence software downloads, wherein user-generated word of mouth serves as a mediator. This stream of research has shown the important role of expert reviews in reducing consumer uncertainty on experience goods and is consistent with our discussion in Section 1 that expert reviews primarily aid consumer decision making at the product choice stage. In the context of this paper, we examine the effects of editorial recommendation on both product awareness and choice. Moreover, whereas the studies of expert reviews have mainly measured the direct effect and mediators of the direct effect of expert reviews, we investigate the externality of editorial recommendations (i.e., the existence of spillover and its underlying mechanisms).

A key novelty of our research is in separating out the spillover effects at the awareness and choice stages, and the two-stage model we use resembles those in several prior papers that study the role of information in consumer demand (Roberts and Lattin 1991, Wu et al. 2005, Goeree 2008, Fleder and Hosanagar 2009, Hendricks and Sorensen 2009, Masatlioglu et al. 2012, Sahni 2016, among others). Given consumers’ limited cognitive resources for product search and evaluation, consumer behavior research has long postulated the importance of awareness (or, closely related, the role of the consideration set) in determining product choice (Hauser and Wernerfelt 1990, Kardes et al. 1993, Bettman et al. 1998). Roberts and Lattin (1991) found that a two-stage model of consideration and choice performs better than a one-stage choice model. In simulating the market effect of recommender systems, Fleder and Hosanagar (2009) used a two-stage consumer decision model where the first stage captured product awareness and the second stage was choice based on preferences. By including recommender systems in the simulation model, these authors found that most popular recommenders tend to reduce product diversity, though it is possible to overcome the problem by avoiding recommendations mainly based on popularity or reviews. Sahni (2016) used randomized field experiments to study the spillover effect of online advertisements on nonadvertised restaurants of the same category. His theoretical model similarly distinguished between awareness and choice and showed the two competing effects of advertisements at the awareness stage. On the one hand, advertising has a positive direct effect by increasing the probability of consumers considering advertised products. On the other hand, it also leads to a negative effect on the advertised products by reminding consumers of similar products that are not advertised. In the prior studies, the effect of the variable of interest (i.e., advertising) is assumed to be only on awareness. In contrast, the two-stage awareness–choice model in this paper allows editor recommendation to influence choice as well as awareness because recommendations may be perceived as a quality endorsement from the platform. Additionally, we use the two-stage model directly in the empirical analysis to disentangle the effects of editor recommendations on the two probabilities.

Our paper also joins the literature in showing the importance of search cost in shaping market outcome on large e-commerce platforms. Consumer search plays a critical role in crowded marketplaces, and search cost has been recognized to be instrumental in determining product variety, market share, price dispersion, and firm strategy (e.g., Bakos 1997, Brynjolfsson et al. 2011, Overby and Forman 2014). High search cost is a prevalent issue in many e-commerce settings (e.g., Smith et al. 2000, Chen et al. 2004, Ghose et al. 2012), and it has been found that recommendations are an effective decision-aid tool for consumers (Häubl and Trifts 2000). Recommendations enhance the quality of consumer decision making by facilitating consumers’ search efforts, reducing search costs, and expanding the consideration set. To our knowledge, extant research has not examined the impacts of platform recommendations on consumer search efforts and market outcomes in the mobile app market. The previous literature on the mobile app market has studied the demand effects of version updates (Lee and Raghu 2014), information cues (Lee and Raghu 2016), mergers and acquisitions (Li and Agarwal 2016), and app characteristics (Ghose and Han 2014). Our paper contributes to the literature by showing the externality of platform recommendations. At the product level, we show how the reduction of search cost introduced by the platform spotlight drives discovery of a broad set of related apps and increases their sales. In addition, our paper also complements the literature on the cross-platform effect of reviews or user activity (Idu et al. 2011, Ghose and Han 2014, Koh and Fichman 2014) by examining the magnitude and mechanism of the spillover effect of editorial recommendations across platforms.

## 3. Potential Spillover Mechanisms of Platform-Provided Editor Recommendations

3.1. A Two-Stage Process: Awareness and Choice In a large, differentiated market, consumers typically consider only a small subset of the products when making their purchase decisions. The existence of a positive search cost prohibits consumers from searching the whole market (Kim et al. 2010). Even if consumers were aware of all the products, the large number of alternatives would render a complete evaluation and comparison impractical because of the huge cognitive burden (Hauser and Wernerfelt 1990). Given the combination of search cost and consumers limited cognitive resources for product evaluation, the literature on consumer demand in both the offline (Hauser and Wernerfelt 1990, Kardes et al. 1993, Bettman et al. 1998) and online settings (Wu and Rangaswamy 2003, Wu et al. 2005, Fleder and Hosanagar 2009, Sahni 2016) has suggested that the consumer purchase decision can be modeled as a two-stage process. In the two-stage decision process, a consumer becomes aware of a subset of available products (or a subset of products that a consumer is aware of becomes salient), thereby forming a consideration set in the first stage, and in the second stage, the consumer decides which product(s) in the consideration set to purchase by ordering the options according to his or her preference. Following the previous literature (Fleder and Hosanagar 2009, Sahni 2016), we term the first stage the awareness stage and the second the choice stage and illustrate the process in Figure 1. The probability that a consumer buys a particular product is then the probability that he or she is aware of the product multiplied by the probability that he or she purchases the product conditional on awareness.

The two probabilities are functions of many factors, including product characteristics, marketing efforts, information technology, word of mouth, and social learning. Many of these factors have been studied in the literature, and the focus in this research is on platform-provided editor recommendations. In an experience goods market, platform-provided editor recommendations can influence the purchase decision through both awareness and choice probabilities. First, editor recommendations reduce the search cost for the featured products and increase the probability that an average consumer would be aware of the featured products. Editor recommendations also promote the featured products to a more prominent position on the platform, thus making these products more salient to an average consumer. Second, when consumers are uncertain about product quality in the choice stage, they leverage available information to form expectations about product quality and make the purchase decision according to the expectations. As argued in Section 1, a platform-provided editor recommendation may be perceived as a strong endorsement from a credible source. As such, every thing else equal, the recommendation might increase a consumer’s expectation of the recommended product’s quality. Editor recommendations therefore can directly change the ordering of product options in the consideration set and contribute to an increase in the purchase probability.

## 3.2. Spillover of Exposure and Spillover of Quality Endorsement

As illustrated in Figure 2, both the awareness effect and choice effect of editor recommendations can potentially spill over to other products that are not featured but are related to the featured products. However, the underlying spillover mechanisms may be different depending on the specific relationship between the featured and nonfeatured products. Our discussion here focuses on three distinct product groups: products by the same producer, products with similar functionality, and the same products on a different platform.

The awareness effect has potential externality on the three groups of products, creating an exposure spillover. First, platform-provided editor recommendations cause a featured product to have a larger customer base, and possibly a subset of the new customers will become familiar with the producer and also consider the other products in the producer’s catalog. Then the other offerings by the producer will become salient to a group of potential customers who would otherwise not have considered these products (Carmi et al. 2012, Garthwaite 2014). This is thus spillover of exposure to products in the same producer’s catalog. Second, platform-provided editor recommendations may also prompt consumers to explore other products that are functionally similar. For example, a consumer who has tried the featured product may find that he or she needs the features provided in the product but does not like the specific implementation or design of the featured product. The desire for its features and the dissatisfaction with its implementation would probably prompt the customer to spend more effort in searching for functionally similar products (Carmi et al. 2012, Oestreicher-Singer and Sundararajan 2012, Jabr and Zheng 2013, Lewis and Nguyen 2015). Third, editor recommendations may also have a spillover effect on the awareness of the same products sold on a different platform. There are several mechanisms that potentially drive the cross-platform spillover. For instance, there is a direct effect through the (probably small portion of) consumers who are multihoming on different platforms (for our empirical context, this means users who use mobile devices of different operating systems). Additionally, the new users of the featured product on the focal platform may initiate word of mouth that spills over to consumers on a different platform. For example, opinion leaders on social media and reporters on mass media may share information on those trending products, which may encourage customers from another platform to search and try out the same products. For a product that exhibits a network effect, the increased user base on the focal platform can also make users of a different platform more willing to check out the product. Therefore, mechanisms such as word of mouth, network effect, and consumer multihoming may drive the spillover of exposure across platforms.

Editor recommendations on the platform can also directly influence the choice probability for the three groups of related products. We call the effect the spillover of quality endorsement. First, because an editor recommendation is a positive signal of a product’s quality, consumers might infer a higher capability of its producer and even extrapolate the quality endorsement to other products by the same producer. Hence, we expect the quality endorsement of editor recommendations to have a positive spillover effect on the other products by the same producer. Second, editor recommendations should have a negative spillover effect on the choice probability of other similar products because the platform editors have chosen to recommend the featured product over another product. As such, products with similar functionality can experience a negative effect on their relative position in a consumer’s consideration set. Third, for a product sold on more than one platform, being recommended by one platform may also increase consumers’ expectations of its quality on the other platform through mechanisms similar to those discussed for products by the same developer. How ever, the size of the spillover may exhibit great variation if there are significant differences in the competitive environment.

Taken together, for other products sold by the same producer and the same product on a different platform, we expect positive spillover on both the firststage awareness and the second-stage purchase choice. The relative importance of these two mechanisms (awareness and choice) may, however, vary. For similar products sold by other producers, platform-provided editor recommendations may have a positive spill over effect on the first-stage awareness but a negative effect on the second-stage purchase choice. Next, we test the existence of spillover and investigate the mechanisms for these related products empirically in the context of the mobile app market.

## 4. Data

We collect our data from the two major app distribution platforms: Apple’s iOS App Store and Google’s Play Store (hereafter the iOS Store and Play Store, respectively). The main data set is from the iOS Store, and it consists of three parts. The first part contains apps that were recommended by the store’s editors in the “New Apps We Love” column between February 1, 2016, and October 31, 2016. We call these apps the featured apps. Two important points need to be made about the “New Apps We Love” column First, the column is the major channel of new app recommendations at the store level, and apps from all different categories can be selected to be featured in this column. Second, although the exact procedure and criteria for choosing featured apps are not public information, side evidence suggests that it is not (or at least not purely) based on popularity. Apple’s official guidelines for developers state that in selecting recommended apps, the store editors consider factors including design, accessibility, innovativeness, and uniqueness. Number of downloads is not listed among the factors.<sup>4</sup> Moreover, we surveyed industry reports discussing app recommendations in the iOS Store, and the consensus is that (1) the impact of platform featuring is real and significant, and (2) it is virtually impossible to predict what apps will be featured and when (e.g., see Eadicicco 2015, Yip 2016, Blacker 2017). Thus, being selected for recommendation is not merely a relabel of popularity. Nonetheless, the evi dence is not conclusive, and there are still challenges to identification. We will address the related concerns after presenting the first set of results.

Because the iOS Store does not provide an appli cation programming interface (API) endpoint for retrieving the list of featured apps programmatically, we first took screenshots of the “New Apps We Love” web page every day and manually identified the featured apps from the images. To rule out human error in recording the featured apps, we checked each identified app on App Annie, a mobile app market intelligence website, to make sure that the manually identified featured app matched with the archived information provided by App Annie. We found that all the manually recorded featured apps were validated by the App Annie data. The “New Apps We Love” column typically features an app for a number of days. For each featured app, the recommendation window is the time period from the first day to the last day when the app was featured. During the data-collection period, the recommendation window ranged from 3 to 17 days, with a mean equal to about 10 days. We excluded featured apps whose recommendation window started before February 1, 2016, or ended after October 31, 2016, to ensure that our data contain the complete recommendation window. In total, we identified 416 distinct featured apps, and none of them was featured for more than one episode. For these apps, we provide descriptive statistics of selected app attributes and the distribution over app categories in Appendix C of the e-companion.

The second part of the iOS Store data set comes from the listing information provided by the store APIs and a proprietary data archive that contains information about iOS Store apps as of January 2016. First, for each featured app, we identified the other apps by the same developer and apps of similar functionality. Apps by the same developer were identified by the unique developer ID. In total, we found 1,789 related apps by the same developers. Apps of similar functionality were selected by analyzing the textual data of app descriptions in our data archive. The app description (see an example in Appendix D of the e-companion) is a piece of text provided in each app’s listing information that summarizes the app’s functionality—apps of similar functionality tend to have similar descriptions. Thus, for each featured app, we measured the similarity between the featured app’s description and all other apps’ descriptions in our data archive. In particular, we used the term frequency–inverse document frequency similarity measure (Manning et al. 2008) to retrieve the 10 most similar apps and then excluded those that were not in the same category as the focal featured app. More details about how we identify the similar apps can be found in Appendix D of the e-companion. Overall, we found 1,223 related apps with similar functionalities. Next, we combined the featured apps and these two groups of related apps. For each app in the combined set, we collected both the static listing attributes, such as title, developer, category, and recommended age group, and the dynamic listing events, including version updates and price changes (for paid apps). The third part of the iOS Store data set is the store’s “Top Free” and “Top Paid” charts, which we tracked daily from January 1, 2016. For each day, we observed the 100 top-ranked free apps and 100 top-ranked paid apps in the whole store, as well as in each of the 24 categories. Note that the iOS Store has country-specific versions, and our programs and manual data-collection tasks were all implemented in the United States. Also note that in this main data set, we do not directly observe the apps’ sales in terms of download volume. As in many prior studies (Carare 2012, Garg and Telang 2013, Lee and Raghu 2014, Wen and Zhu 2017), we use entering the top charts to proxy the sales performance.

The main data set is complemented by information collected from the Play Store. For each featured app identified in the iOS Store’s “New Apps We Love” column, we searched whether it had an Android version in the Play Store. We developed a computer program for the search and match task and then had a research assistant manually validate the results. Only a subset of the featured apps was multihoming— we identified the Android version for 131 of the featured apps. We also collected these Play Store apps’ static attributes, dynamic change events, and sales performance (from the Play Store top 100 charts) data.

The collected data are then used to construct four samples for empirical analysis, one for the featured apps themselves and one for each of the three groups of related apps: apps by the same developer, apps with similar functionality, and the same apps marketed on a different platform. In the first sample, for each featured app, we compute daily observations for the period from 30 days prior to the recommendation window to 30 days after the recommendation window. On each day, we observe whether the app was listed on the top 100 chart of its category, whether it was featured, and its version age and price. Figure 3 plots the daily proportion of featured apps that entered the top 100 during the observation window. The plot shows that the proportion of top 100 apps increased significantly after recommendations started. For the three samples on the related apps, the data structure is the same. Take apps by the same developer, for example. Suppose that j is a featured app and i is a different app by the same developer of j. For examining the spillover to i when j is featured, we compute daily observations for the period from 30 days prior to j’s recommendation window to 30 days after j’s recommendation window. The daily observations include whether i was on the top 100 chart, whether j was featured, and i’s price and version age. For the descriptive statistics of these four data samples, please refer to Appendix C of the e-companion.

We do not report on the time-invariant variables because we control for app fixed effects in the empirical analysis, so the timeinvariant variables (such as category and the paid dummy) will not be included.<sup>5</sup> The dependent variable we will model in our main analysis is $t o p _ { i t } ,$ the dummy variable indicating whether app i is in the top 100 on day t, rather than app i’s actual rank on the top chart. This is because the ranking information is available only for the top 100 apps, so if an app did not make into the top 100 chart on a particular day, its rank on that day would be missing in our data set. Given that for each app we would need both a sufficient number of observations to estimate a fixed effect and also consecutive observations to allow for state dependence (which we explain in the next section), using the top 100 ranking as the dependent variable would lead to severe attrition of observations. Instead, modeling the binary outcome allows us to use as many daily observations as possible so that we can control for unobserved app heterogeneity without making restrictive assumptions. The drawback is that we will only have estimates of the extent to which the potential spillover effect will change the likelihood of an app entering the top 100 chart, but not how much it will change the downloads or ranking. To complement the main analysis, we managed to obtain a data set of app downloads for a portion of the apps in our sample from a mobile app analytics company. We report the details of the supplemental data and related analysis in Section 7.

We note several points regarding some other timevarying factors that we do not include in the main model. First, though rating score and count are in theory time varying, most of the related apps in the sample do not have any ratings. For related apps whose ratings data are available to us, we find that neither the existence of ratings nor the rating scores of related apps influence their download rankings after controlling for app fixed effects (results available on request). We believe that this is due to the limited within-app variation of ratings in the observation window. Second, we do not include time fixed effects in the main model because the download ranking is a relative measure. One might worry that the difficulty of entering the top 100 chart increases as the number of apps in the market increases. This is less likely to be a concern for us because each app in our sample has its own (relatively short) observation window, and the app fixed effect partially captures the level of difficulty in entering the top 100 chart during the window. That said, we show that our results are robust to controlling for weekly dummies in Section 5. Third, in one of our robustness checks, we will include Google search volume to control for the general interest in the app. We do not include Google search volume in the main model because it is available for only a subset of our data. We were unable to obtain reliable Google search volume data for apps whose names are too common or too rare.

We first study the impact of recommendations from “New Apps We $\mathrm { L o v e ^ { \prime \prime } }$ on the featured apps themselves. To estimate the effect, we use the following reduced-form specification:

$$

where I · on the right-hand side is an indicator function that takes value 1 if the condition in the argument holds and 0 otherwise. Therefore, it is a binary outcome regression model, and we will estimate the linear probability, probit, and logit versions of it and present the results. Several points are noteworthy regarding the specification. First, there is a high degree of serial persistence in an $\mathsf { a p p ^ { \prime } s }$ sales performance, as indicated by entering the top 100 sales chart $( t o p _ { i t } ) .$ , and our specification allows two distinct sources of this serial persistence. The first source is the unobserved app characteristics that affect the choice probability. This is thus app-specific heterogeneity that is constant over time and is captured by $\alpha _ { i } .$ . The second source is called “state dependence” in Heckman (1978, p. 229), and consistent with previous literature, we model it by including $t o p _ { i t - 1 }$ in the specification (Liu 2006, Zhou and Duan 2016). The rationale for using the lagged variable in our context is that the top-ranking chart is used by some consumers as an app discovery channel $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , see Arment 2013, Daniel 2017), so being listed on the top 100 chart in the previous period enhances the app’s exposure to consumers, thereby increasing its awareness probability in the population. With $\alpha _ { i }$ and $t o p _ { i t - 1 }$ included, our specification is a binary outcome model with both heterogeneity and state dependence. Second, $X _ { i t }$ includes observed app characteristics including price and version age . The time-invariant app characteristics such as category and developer are not included because of the way we control for $\alpha _ { i } .$ . By the same logic, an app will be excluded from estimation if $t o p _ { i t } = 0$ in all periods or $t o p _ { i t } = 1$ in all periods. Last, unlike the typical panel-data setting, where the number of observations per individual is small, our data set contains a large number of time periods for each app i (30 days before to 30 days after the recommendation window), so we can estimate the specification by taking $\alpha _ { i }$ as a fixed effect and using the joint maximumlikelihood method (Wooldridge 2002).

Con sistent with the intuition, app sales is negatively associated with version age. We do not find a statistically significant effect for $p r i c e _ { i t } ,$ probably because there is very little variation during the observation period. ## 5.2. Spillover Effects of Editor Recommendations on Related Apps

We use a similar reduced-form specification to test the existence of spillover and estimate the overall spillover effect on each of the three groups of related apps: apps by the same developer, apps with similar functionality, and the same apps on a different platform We rewrite the regression specification here:

$$
t o p _ {i t} = I (\alpha_ {i} + \delta t o p _ {i t - 1} + \theta f e a t u r e _ {j t} + X _ {i t} \beta + \varepsilon_ {i t} \geq 0),\tag{2}
$$

where i denotes a related app and j the featured app that app i is related to, and feature indicates whether j is recommended at time t. Therefore, a nonzero θ will indicate the existence of spillover from the recommendation of app j to the sales of app i. Again, α and δ capture individual heterogeneity and state dependence, respectively.

We estimate the specification under the probit assumption for each of the three groups of related apps and report the results in Table 4. As shown in the three columns, we find that the editor recommendations of “New Apps We Love” have an overall positive spillover effect (θ > 0) on all three product groups. Moreover, θ is statistically significant at the 1% level for all three groups of related apps. When conditioning on the related app entering the top 100 chart at least once during the observation window, the partial effect of the featured app’s recommendation on the probability of the related app entering the top 100 list is estimated to be 5.6% for an app by the same developer, 2.5% for a functionally similar app, and 4.8% for the same app sold in the Play Store. The finding that the positive spillover effect is larger for apps by the same developer than for apps of similar functionality is consistent with our expectation. Note that price is not included in the Play Store app group because very few price change events happened in the observation window, so price is highly correlated with the fixed effects. These effects are the average effects across pairs of featured and related apps. We also find that the magnitude of the spillover effect is weaker when the similar apps had previously entered the top 100 chart. Additionally, the cross-platform spillover is stronger when the featured app is in the social-networking category or when its Google search volume is high. The heterogeneity analyses are reported in Appendix E of the e-companion.

## 5.3 Potential Threats to Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and Robustness Analyses

Before delving into the mechanisms of spillover, we focus on addressing potential identification concerns regarding our analysis of the spillover effect in the preceding subsection. Given that the iOS Store only publishes a general guideline on the featured apps (cited in Section 4) and that the exact criteria for selecting the featured apps remain opaque, there is the important concern that unobserved factors can be correlated with both selection of the featured apps and the download performance of the related apps.

To begin with, we note that under our fixed-effects specification, all the time-invariant factors that may be used for selecting featured apps will not pose a threat to our identification of the spillover effect. For example, app quality is a factor considered by store editors in selecting featured apps. However, unobserved app quality of either featured or related apps will not cause an endogeneity problem because app quality of both featured and related apps can be considered to be constant during the observation window (given that we also control for version updates), so the effect of app quality is absorbed into the fixed effects. The critical threat to the validity of our spillover estimates comes from the possible presence of time-varying factors that could simultaneously cause a featured app to be selected for recommendation and the sales of its related apps to increase. Below we consider various possibilities and provide an overview of our robustness analyses for alleviating the concerns.

5.3.1. Unobserved Marketing Effort. Although app developers in the industry generally consider it impossible to predict what apps will be featured and when (as we discussed in Section 4), there might be the concern that the store editors could be influenced by the unobserved marketing efforts by developers. If marketing effort caused both an app to be selected for recommendation and the demand of its related apps to increase, then the spillover effect we reported would be spurious. To alleviate this concern, we collect from Google Trends the daily search volume data for the featured apps during the observation window. The rationale of using Google Trends is that during the data-collection period, the opportunity for marketing within the iOS Store was limited,<sup>6</sup> and most app marketing happened outside the store. The search volume time series reflects the trend of market interest, and it should exhibit a noticeable increase during the recommendation window if significant marketing were taking place at the same time. With the Google Trends data, we show the robustness of our results after controlling for the search volume of featured apps during the observational window. Note that the increase in Google search volume is not necessarily due to marketing. It is also possible that the sharp search volume increase during the recommendation window is merely the consequence of editor recommendation. We cannot distinguish the true cause. Nevertheless, we estimate the spillover with the control for the change in Google search volume. In this sense, our robustness check here tends to provide a conservative estimate of the spillover effect.

5.3.2. Trend at the Store Level. There could be a concern that the recommendation of featured apps and increase in popularity for related apps are driven by some common trends at the store level. We address the concern about potential common trends at the store level by including weekly dummies in our specification to explicitly control for time fixed effects. As Table 5 shows, after controlling for Google Trends and weekly dummies, the results are highly consistent with those reported earlier.

5.3.4. Potential Selection on Unobservables. One might still have the concern that there are other unobserved events that could simultaneously affect recommendations and demand for related apps. This concern is fundamentally about the selection on unobservables issue, that is, whether some time-varying factors that store editors would consider in selecting apps for recommendation could also appear in the error term in the equation of demand for related apps (in other words, the feature dummy could be correlated with the error term in Equation (2)). Although it is difficult to think of a factor that could influence editors’ recommendations and simultaneously increase the demand for all three groups of related apps in a systematic way, we formally assess the sensitivity of our spillover estimate to the selection issue. We use the method of selection on unobservables to address the selection issue (Altonji et al. 2005, Oster 2019). The key idea of the method is that the degree of selection on observables can be used as a guide to assess the degree of selection on unobservables. In other words, the approach allows an assessment of the sensitivity of our spillover estimate to the selection issue. We find that both the upper and lower bounds of the spillover effect on all three groups of related apps are greater than zero, still supporting the existence of a positive spillover effect. Please refer to Appendix G of the e-companion for details. Additionally, we also perform simple cross-sectional robustness checks to show that the existence of positive spillover is not due to the specific panel structure of our sample setup (see more details in Appendix H of the e-companion). Together, all the robustness analyses lend support to our main findings. We summarize these analyses in Table 7.

Finally, we note that the focus of our study is on the spillover effect of recommending apps that are likely to be selected by platforms. Because platforms tend to recommend apps from the subpopulation of apps with high quality, our point estimates of the spillover effect may not be directly applied to the situation when a random set of apps is being recommended. Nevertheless, this does not degrade the credibility or importance of our findings. First, as mentioned earlier, the selection of featured apps does not pose a threat to identification of the spillover effect under the fixed-effects specification. More important, our findings have high policy relevance because platforms are most interested in the market effects of curated apps that they would want to recommend. In contrast, investigating the spillover effect of recommending low-quality apps or randomly selected apps might be theoretically interesting, but such practice is highly unlikely to be adopted by platforms. Similarly to our study, existing research on the spillover effect of events that are not under the producer’s control generally focuses on a nonrandom subpopulation that is policy relevant (among others, on celebrity endorsement, see Garthwaite (2014); on negative online chatter, see Borah and Tellis (2016)).

## 6.1. Spillover Mechanisms, Reduced-Form Probit Model

Considering again the awareness–choice two-stage model discussed in Section 3, the results from the regression model in Section 5.2 provide evidence for the existence of spillover effects of the recommendations of “New Apps We Love,” as illustrated on the left side of Figure 4. Though the results show that the size of the spillover effect varies across the three related app groups, whether the difference is due to the spillover of exposure or the spillover of quality endorsement cannot be distinguished based on the regressions.

Our discussion in Section 3 reveals that the key channel for the exposure spillovers of editor recommendations for a related app is through the increased popularity of the featured app. The recommendation of featured app j increases the number of users for app j, and some of the new users will become aware of or be reminded of the other apps by $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ developer, apps that share similar features with j and $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ Android version, if any. As such, the key mediating variable in the spillover of exposure is the sales of app j. This motivates us to include the variable $t o p _ { j t } ,$ a proxy of app j’s sales, in the regression specification, because we have shown that feature is positively associated with top . Specifically, $t o p _ { j t . }$ , the mediator, satisfies the two conditions for the specification of a mediation model (Baron and Kenny 1986): (1) as discussed in the empirical context and supported by the results in Table 3, our main variable, editorial recommendation of featured app $\textit { j } ( f e a t u r e _ { j t } )$ , influences the mediator $( t o p _ { j t } )$ but not vice versa; (2) based on our earlier discussion, the mediator $( t o p _ { j t } )$ influences the outcome variable, the spillover of exposure from featured app j to related app i, given that the exposure spillover mainly depends on the number of consumers who download featured app j. If our theoretical discussion is to be confirmed by data, we expect the variable $t o p _ { j t }$ should capture a sizable, if not the whole, portion of the exposure spillover effect, and meanwhile, the remaining effect of feature will be smaller, mainly reflecting the spillover of quality endorsement. ## 6.2. Spillover Mechanisms, Two-Stage Bivariate Probit Model

In this subsection, we specify a bivariate probit model to separately estimate the spillover of exposure and the spillover of quality endorsement.

The motivation of our bivariate probit model is the two-stage awareness–choice decision process discussed in Section 3, and it is based on the idea that a new user must be aware of an app before he or she can download or buy it. ## 7. Heterogeneity of Spillover Mechanisms and Long-Run Spillover Effects

In this section, we first investigate how the spillover mechanism depends on the characteristics of the featured apps, including pricing model and user rating. Second, we use the subset of our data where daily download information is available to supplement our main analysis. We also use the estimates to calculate the long-run spillover effects on the daily downloads of the related apps.

## 7.1. Heterogeneity of Spillover Mechanisms with Respect to Featured App Characteristics

The results in Section 6 suggest that both the spillover of exposure and the spillover of quality endorsement play an important role in influencing the related apps sales. Given that there is considerable heterogeneity among the featured apps, we further leverage the variation in our sample to explore whether and how the spillover mechanisms may differ depending on the characteristics of featured apps. Two salient characteristics of the featured apps are their pricing model (paid versus free) and user rating, both of which can potentially affect the degree to which consumers who are exposed to them would want to explore related apps. Specifically, when a featured app is a paid app, consumers who are very price sensitive may turn to exploring other related apps instead of downloading the featured app. In addition, the high user rating of a featured app, which is a signal of high quality, may also increase consumers’ intention to explore other apps by the same developer while decreasing their willingness to explore similar apps by other developers.

Thus, we examine the heterogeneous spillover effects with respect to whether the featured app is paid or free (paid<sub>j</sub>) and whether the featured app is highly rated or not (high rated ).<sup>7</sup> We change the specification of Model (4) by including the interaction of feature<sub>j</sub> and paid in both stages (Model (6)) and including the interaction of feature and high rated in both stages (Model (7)). The estimation results are reported in Table 11. As the results show, the spillover effect of exposure on apps by the same developer is higher when the featured app is a paid app or is highly rated. The spillover effect of exposure on similar apps by other developers is lower when the featured app is highly rated. Moreover, the spillover on the same app marketed on the other platform is stronger when the featured app is a paid app. These results on heterogeneity are all consistent with the expectation. Additionally, it appears that the spillover of quality endorsement does not depend on whether the featured app is paid or highly rated.

## 8. Discussion and Conclusion

In this research, we analyzed the potential externality of platform-provided editor recommendations. We empirically examined the existence and mechanisms of such externality in the mobile app market using data collected from Apple’s iOS App Store and Google’s Play Store. Our main finding is that platform-provided editor recommendations have an overall positive spillover effect on three groups of related apps: apps released on the same platform by the same developer, apps with similar functionality on the same platform, and the same apps on a different platform. Specifically, for the apps by the same developer and the same apps on a different platform, we find a significantly positive spillover of exposure, but the spillover of quality endorsement is weaker across platforms than within the same platform. For the similar apps, however, we find the editor recommendations have a weak positive effect on their awareness probability and an insignificant effect on their choice probability. The evidence documented by our research suggests that both the spillover of exposure and the spillover of platform endorsement can drive the externality of editor recommendations, but their strength and relative importance vary depending on the specific relationship between the featured and nonfeatured products. Furthermore, the spill over effect also depends on the characteristics of the featured products, including pricing model and consumer ratings.

Our research not only furthers our understanding but also provides salient managerial implications for the mobile app market. Editor recommendations can help consumers discover new apps and help the platform diversify consumer traffic and potentially reshape the distribution of sales. Recommending highquality apps that have had a difficult time attracting enough consumer attention could encourage developer innovation and increase platform profitability in the long run. We found that platform-provided editor recommendations not only increase the sales of recommended apps but also increase the sales of other apps by the same developer and those with similar functionality. The result implies that platform managers could leverage editor recommendations to promote lesser-known high-quality developers or even a whole market niche that is underexplored by featuring just a small number of apps in the relevant catalog. Our finding on cross-platform spillover also sheds light on a potential benefit of developer multihoming—a positive demand shock on one platform could generate a feedback loop that will create a multiplying effect on aggregate sales.

We note some caveats in interpreting our findings on the spillover effect. First, in discussing the spillover mechanisms, we posited that platform recommendations attract new users to the featured apps, and then some of these users will explore other related apps. Here we emphasize that for the spillover effect to exist, it needs only a portion of users to follow this path. The result of spillover certainly does not rely on the assumption that all users will explore other related apps or even the average user will explore other related apps. In the real setting, there are many special situations that may affect the size or even existence of spillover. For instance, though it did not occur in our data-collection period, the same app could be featured multiple times, and the size of the spillover effect during the different recommendation episodes might be different. Another situation is that two related apps might be featured concurrently or very close in time. In that case, the size of the spillover effect from one to the other can vary. Another moderator of spillover is recommendation intensity. If a featured app is recommended in multiple places, either inside or outside the store, or an app is related to multiple featured apps, the spillover effect should also be different. Analyzing all these possibilities is beyond the scope of this research, but they are important for practitioners to consider in the real-world setting.

This study on platform-provided editorial recommendations can be extended in several directions. Our study has examined the impact of editor recommendations on the sales of related products. For the group of similar products, it will be interesting to investigate whether the spillover is driven by complementarity or substitution and how their long-term effects differ. To answer this question, more data need to be collected on user engagement after purchase. Researchers can also investigate whether and to what extent the existence of editor recommendations will change consumers’ product search habits. If editor recommendations alter consumer search behavior in a significant way, then one can try to incorporate editor recommendations into a canonical consumer search model to systematically study their effects on the emergent market structure, such as equilibrium price and sales distributions. On the supply side, it will also be interesting to study the long-term impact of editor recommendations on strategic decisions of entry, pricing, product design, and innovation.

These questions are also very important from the standpoint of the platform owner because, as a twosided market, it needs to design market institutions including recommendations in a way that optimizes and balances two objectives: encouraging consumer search and promoting producer innovation. Specifically, regarding editor recommendations, many practical questions still exist. For example, what is the best way to feature editor recommendations and how frequently should they be updated? Furthermore, how should the mix of product offerings in the editor recommendations be selected, and what should the objective function in the selection decision process be? There are other important open research questions related to the complementarities between sales- or ratingsbased product lists, expert-curated recommendations, and artificial intelligence–powered search and recommendation systems. Addressing these open questions can greatly improve our understanding of the longterm sustainability of product offerings in platform markets.
