# Preconditions for public sector e-infrastructure development

## a b s t r a c t

Interoperability in e-government has been recognized as a key factor in the quest of administrations at national, local and international level to achieve the provision of one-stop services to citizens and businesses. Interoperability in e-government should enable ef<sup>fi</sup>cient information exchange between applications from different agencies with the help of IT-services. Interoperability in networked IT-service delivery is accomplished by e-infrastructures. However, knowledge of how to develop e-infrastructures in the public sector is still limited. In this paper we report a design science case study of the initial stage of the design of a public sector e-infrastructure in the social welfare sector, especially addressing the bootstrap problem, i.e. the initial problem of starting-up the development of an e-infrastructure. Six types of preconditions for the development of the e-infrastructure are examined and explained: legal, economical, organizational and the installed base of the as-is e-infrastructure consisting of technical, informational and contractual preconditions. From a design and action oriented perspective we claim that a basic understanding of the design context, within which the six types of preconditions have to be analyzed and understood, are essential for e-infrastructure development in an e-government context. The paper highlights the fundamental role of regulations as a precondition for the design, and the fundamental role of lawyers as designers of e-infrastructures in the public sector.

## 1. Introduction

Interoperability in e-government has been recognized as a key factor in the quest for administrations at national, local and international level to achieve the provision of one-stop services to citizens and businesses (Charalabidis, Panetto, Loukis, & Mertins, 2008). Success in e-government requires agencies to work together across traditional boundaries to improve services signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and to reduce operating costs. This implies that different applications have to be able to exchange information, which has shown to be problematic. The main cause is that their design scope remains con<sup>fi</sup>ned within an organizational context. However the deployment of information systems over the last 30 years or more has resulted in the need for opening up closed applications and connecting them. Such an interoperable, networked and heterogeneous structure is called an information infrastructure (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2004, 2010), or e-infrastructure (Edwards, Bowker, Jackson, & Williams, 2009). E-infrastructures typically form only when various applications merge allowing dissimilar applications to be linked into networks. This means quoting Hanseth and Lyytinen (2004) that “II [information infrastructure, e-infrastructure] design must recognize how the installed base of the as-is infrastructure and its scope and complexity in<sup>fl</sup>uences the ongoing design”. This means that the design of e-infrastructure never starts in a green-<sup>fi</sup>eld situation, and that design is “path-dependent and shaped by neighboring infrastructures, existing IT capabilities, user and designer learning, cognitive inertia, etc.” (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010 p. 4-5). The central problem is therefore how to integrate existing applications, which are locally controlled by different organizations into an interoperable distributed e-infrastructure of IT capabilities, i.e. potentially useful software and information (Edwards et al., 2009). The knowledge in regard to how to accomplish this is still limited. According to Sahay, Monteiro, and Aanestad (2009, p. 400), “we have little empirically based knowledge about the interplay of the political and technical con<sup>fi</sup>gurations that arise during attempts to integrate multiple ICT systems.”

E-infrastructures development implies distributed control because no single designer or organization is in full control of the design as is assumed in traditional information systems design literature (Edwards, Jackson, Bowker, & Knobel, 2007; Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010). This means that appropriate preconditions for distributed control have to exist, or have to be developed, in order to support cooperation in e-infrastructure development. The objective of this paper is to investigate the preconditions for e-infrastructure development for e-government, especially for the initial stage of e-infrastructure design. The problem at this stage is to be able to bootstrap, i.e. to initially develop a new IT capability with a minimal amount of resources, and to make it useful for a small user group, and after that sustain a cumulative adoption (ibid). The main empirical base is a longitudinal and still ongoing development effort called “The Social Allowance Project”. This project has the aim of connecting the applications of six national authorities with the applications of the municipalities (all together 290) in Sweden, in order to provide IT-services, i.e. useful IT capabilities, to the of<sup>fi</sup>cers at social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>ces. The main purpose of the paper is to investigate the process of e-infrastructure development in e-government from a design action perspective, focusing of the initial stage, and clarify different enabling and constraining preconditions in the context of the public sector. This will contribute with knowledge distinctly for e-government but also knowledge that is valuable for e-infrastructure development in general. The research approach is a combination of action research and design research. The researchers have participated in the design and construction of IT artifacts (design research) and also participated in change in different practice settings (action research). The paper proceeds as follows: Section 2 describes related research and basic concepts. The research approach is described in Section 3. After that in Section 4 the research process and design case study is presented. Section 5 analyses the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the case study and presents six types of preconditions which are grouped into four main categories: the as-is e-infrastructure, economical, organizational and legal preconditions which can all function as enablers and obstacles for 1) engaging in design and 2) conducting design, 3) the results and features of the design. In emerging theory for e-infrastructure design will be presented. Finally, in Section 7 the research contributions are summarized.

## 2. Related research and basic concepts

Below, the notions of interoperability and e-infrastructure, and obstacles and enablers for the distributed control of e-infrastructure development are explored. The emphasis is on e-government literature, but some other related research on e-infrastructure development is also discussed.

## 2.1. Interoperability in e-government

The importance of cooperation between agencies and the need for interoperable applications is frequently addressed in e-government literature (e.g. Binz-Scharf & Lazer, 2009; Charalabidis et al., 2008; Sarantis, Charalabidis, & Psarras, 2008). E-government requires development of consistent work-practices and IT-services across agencies, and consistent protocols for presentation and collection of information in order to make e-government more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient (Binz-Scharf & Lazer, 2009). However, cross-agency communication and consistency, in e-government is very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult because of the agency-based structure and the oversight institutions within both the legislative and executive branches (Binz-Scharf & Lazer, 2009).

Interoperability may be generally de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the ability to exchange information and mutually to use the information which has been exchanged” (CEC, 1991). This means that interoperability in e-government should enable ef<sup>fi</sup>cient information exchange between applications from different agencies in order to provide high quality services to citizens and businesses.

## 2.2. E-infrastructures

Interoperability in networked IT-service delivery, is accomplished by information infrastructures or e-infrastructures. Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010) de<sup>fi</sup>ne this notion in the following way:

“a shared, open (and unbounded), heterogeneous and evolving socio-technical system (which we call installed base) consisting of a set of IT capabilities and their user, operations and design communities.”

Hanseth and Lyytinen (2004) also de<sup>fi</sup>ne three types of vertical e-infrastructures, using the scale and scope of the e-infrastructure as the main classi<sup>fi</sup>cation criteria: 1) universal service infrastructure, 2) (business) sector infrastructure (this can also be a public sector e-infrastructure used for e-government), and 3) and corporate information infrastructure.

A sector e-infrastructure (type 2 e-infrastructure), which is focused in and as we de<sup>fi</sup>ne it in this paper is an application infrastructure and an IT-service service network based on the principles of a Service-Oriented Architecture (SOA-architecture) on the Internet. SOA de<sup>fi</sup>nes standardized system-to-system services (interfaces) that are loosely coupled (Mankad and Sajja, 2010), because the IT capabilities interoperate with a set of messages, which often are de<sup>fi</sup>ned in an Extensible Markup Language (XML) format. As a consequence a sector infrastructure (the application level) is always built on top of a universal service infrastructure (type 1). Universal service infrastructure offers data transmission (data transport) on the global scale, access and storage services, and the typical example is the Internet. A sector infrastructure enables electronic markets and collaborative information sharing within a business sector or among larger business communities. These structures typically evolve in an incremental fashion when previously unconnected applications are integrated. Sector e-infrastructures are networks that enable locally controlled and maintained applications to interoperate more or less seamlessly (Edwards et al., 2007, p. 11).

System-to-system services, adapters and gateways are key elements in sector e-infrastructure design, and designers have to develop these elements in order to connect several dedicated applications (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010). An adapter is often described as a translator of different formats, i.e. an interface between two different protocols and standards. In the case of sector e-infrastructure development adapters are used to translate data from different applications in the installed base into a standardized XML-interface. Edwards et al. (2007) claim that there is a clear need for gateways to bridge between applications with different properties in order to enable such applications to interoperate. Gateways permit information and functionality from multiple applications to be integrated, though rough patches often remain that must be smoothed over by user action (Edwards et al., 2009). At the user level gateways can be described as IT-capabilities connecting various dispersed applications and work practices. This implies that although these gateways are developed using the Internet, their actual use is dependent upon, and linked with local work<sup>fl</sup>ows and communities of practice. In this paper a gateway is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an IT capability that permits the user to interact with at least two applications.

## 2.3. Enablers and obstacles for e-infrastructure development

Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010) claim that one common reason for e-infrastructure design failures is that e-infrastructure design does not conform to traditional top-down IS design guidelines, because the anticipated design context for e-infrastructure design is quite different compared to traditional IS design. E-infrastructures are never built from scratch, which means that the design is path-dependent, and they are often bootstrapped by experimenting and enrolling new communities. In order to provide a better understanding of the design context in e-infrastructure design a number of preconditions have been described in the research literature, which can both enable and hinder the bootstrap and further development of e-infrastructures. These can be categorized into general preconditions (GPre) see Table 1, which are assumed to enable and hinder e-infrastructure design in general, and preconditions that are speci<sup>fi</sup>c for business sectors (BSPre) see Table 2.

## 2.3.1. General preconditions (GPre)

In this section a number of general preconditions are presented which is described in the literature (See Table 1 below). The as-is installed base (GPre 1) is the basic precondition that all e-infrastructure development endeavors must consider. Tensions (GPre 2) imply that the access and control of resources are always present and must be considered as a precondition for e-infrastructure development. Finally standards (GPre 3) are considered to be the most important enabler for e-infrastructure development. These preconditions will in Sections 6.3.1–6.3.4. be compared with the preconditions found in the Social Allowance case study.

2.3.1.1. Installed base (GPr 3). The evolution of infrastructures is both enabled and hindered by the installed base, which is the existing con<sup>fi</sup>guration of IT capabilities (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010). Newly developed IT capabilities needs to be integrated into the installed base. This sets up demands for horizontal and/or backwards compatibility, and imposes constraints on what can be designed. The design theory of Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010) is focused upon how the as-is installed base could be used as an enabler for promoting the bootstrapping, and further development of the e-infrastructure. The bootstrap problem is oriented towards the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage of e-infrastructure design, which should create a momentum of growth, and it includes three design principals. 1) Design initially for usefulness, which guides the designers to develop a simple IT capability that is directly useful for a small user group. 2) Build on the installed base, which guides the designers to use the installed base, especially the existing universal service infrastructures, as an enabler for e-infrastructural growth. 3) Expand the installed base with persuasive enrollment tactics, which means that after the <sup>fi</sup>rst small user group have started to use it and <sup>fi</sup>nds it useful, the designers have to focus on sustained growth. To achieve this designers have to continuously persuade users to adopt the IT capability, and not add functionality that complicates its implementation.

However, Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010) also recognize that the installed base can be an obstacle for the development process due to lock-in effects or technology traps, because e-infrastructures that face early lock-in and obstacles will not scale up. The designers have to come up early with solutions that persuade users to adopt while the user community still is small. However, early design decisions based on the requirements of a small user group may later constrain the further expansion of the e-infrastructure. To quote Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010, p. 7) “blind early design decisions can later block the expansion as new communities join, or technological trajectories change, and create adverse constraints for growth. Such adverse constraints we call technology traps”.

Edwards et al. (2007, ibid. p. 17–18) describe how the as-is infrastructure both can serve as an enabler and obstacle, because technological change is always path dependent, which means that “Today's choices constrain tomorrow's possibilities. Yet they also create new possibilities, i.e. directions that could not have been taken in the absence of technology X”. The installed base is an enabler because it creates network effects, which means that the value of certain kinds of IT capability increases exponentially with widespread adoption. The path dependency of the installed base can be an obstacle because of localized learning which means that once individuals have made an initial investment: they adapt themselves to that investment rather than considering alternatives. Path dependency also creates an irreversibility problem because if an IT capability becomes dominant, choosing an alternative to this dominant technology becomes too costly.

2.3.1.2. Tensions (GPre 2). Edwards et al. (2007) also discuss preconditions for e-infrastructure development in terms of tensions, based on analyses of the development of e-infrastructures in general, and cyber infrastructures in particular. Cyber infrastructures are e-infrastructures that provide IT services over the Internet supporting research activities. Tensions are important to consider because of the potential that they have to upset or remake previously accepted relations and practices. Practices, organizations and individuals can take advantage of, or not, the new possibilities and challenges posed by the infrastructure. In particular this is true for e-infrastructures that are in the bootstrapping phase. According to Edwards et al. (2007) can tensions be regarded both as enablers and obstacles for infrastructural development, and they describe three such tensions: (1) interest and exclusion, (2) ownership and investment models, and (3) data culture and data tensions.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst tension, interest and exclusion (Gpre 2.1), is concerned with the short-term experiences of gain and losses of individuals and institutions. This will shape the climate, and the incentive structures within which infrastructures will be bootstrapped. Some actors will act as allies supporting the development process bringing in their resources and innovative capability. Others actors will be hostile or simply refuse to participate.

The second tension, ownership and investment models (GPre 2.2), is concerned with property regimes, public/private investment models, ancillary policy objectives, etc. Investment models are important and a complicated matter in infrastructure development because this is a collective endeavor and it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to come to an agreement of how costs and revenues should be shared among the actors that have to co-operate in developing the infrastructure. Therefore, many of the traditional infrastructures that have been in place for a long time, e.g, railroads and roads have been financed publicly. In recent decades public investment models have been criticized, and there has been an increasing demand for investment models that can partner with industry in order to promote ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and innovation. Intellectual property rights are also important to consider in e-infrastructure development, because no one has the incentive to contribute to the development of the e-infrastructure, if others who have not contributed can enjoy the results. On the other hand, if many actors contribute to initial phase and further development of the e-infrastructure it will reduce the costs.

The third tension, data culture and data tensions (Gpre 2.3), is related to the lack of metadata and techniques for data sharing between databases implemented at different organizations using different equipment and data formats. It also relates to the lack on incentives for data sharing. Questions that have to be answered and resolved are who decides on rules and conventions for sharing, storing, and preserving data, and frictions between localized routines and cultures that stand in the way of effective inter-organizational collaboration.

2.3.1.3. Standards (Gpre 3). Standards are considered to be the key enabler for e-infrastructure development. Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010, p. 16) de<sup>fi</sup>ne standards as shared and agreed upon e-infrastructure speci<sup>fi</sup>cations among a set of communities. ICT-standards can be divided into technical (horizontal) and semantic (vertical) standards. Horizontal standards like XML and SOA-services convey technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations that dictate how IT capabilities should work together. These standards are important in that they allow for the design of interoperable modular systems. Vertical standards, in contrast, prescribe information and data structures and their de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, document formats, and business processes for particular domains or industries (Markus, Stein<sup>fi</sup>eld, Wigand, & Minton, 2006). The key role of standards as the enabler for e-infrastructure design has also been acknowledged in the e-government literature, where they have been discussed under the label of interoperability frameworks. These frameworks reference basic technical and semantic speci<sup>fi</sup>cations that all agencies relevant to the e-government strategy implementation should adopt (Guijarro, 2006). Standards could also be divided into “De jure” (prede<sup>fi</sup>ned) and “De facto” (emerging) standards. De jure standards are standards developed by standardization bodies, and de facto standards are emerging industry standards a typical example of “technology X”, as described above by Edwards et al. (2007). Standards function as “attractors” (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010) that contribute to bootstrapping the e-infrastructure and initiating growth. Standards are considered as a very important mechanism for e-infrastructure growth because they produce network effects. The reason for this is that the value of a standard de<sup>fi</sup>ning an IT capability, or an IT capability itself which becomes a de facto standard, depends on the number of users having adopted it, and the more users there are the more valuable the standard gets. This makes the standard even more attractive which leads to increased adoption that further increases its installed base.

The important role of emerging and prede<sup>fi</sup>ned standards as a key enabler for e-infrastructure development also explains why standards research constitutes a major part of e-infrastructure research (cf. Bowker, 1996; Bowker & Star, 1999; Edwards et al., 2007; Hanseth & Monterio, 1998a; Markus et al., 2006). Standards are considered to be the key enablers for interoperability and information sharing (Bowker, 1996; Bowker & Star, 1999; Edwards et al., 2007, Guijarro, 2006). The problems and obstacles that are related to prede<sup>fi</sup>ned vertical (semantic) standards implementation have also been recognized in research literature. For example Hanseth and Monterio (1998a), Bowker (1996) and Bowker and Star describe socio-technical complexity of establishing an e-infrastructure using prede<sup>fi</sup>ned standards. To quote Libicki (1995:35): “Information technology standards have been touted as a means to interoperability and software portability, but they are more easily lauded than built or followed”. Especially the development and implementation of prede<sup>fi</sup>ned semantic standards have showed to be a tedious, costly and failure prone process. Damsgaard and Truex (2000) claim that pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned semantic standards with their enforced grammar could be seen as “procrustean beds”, and thus incapable of resolving the trade-offs between global and local requirements. The key problem with pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned global standards is that they embrace a set of a priori generic structures that may serve poorly in some local contexts.

## 2.3.2. Business sector preconditions

Markus et al. (2006) have addressed how the problems of implementing pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned semantic standards (Vertical Information Standards, (VIS)) have been successfully addressed in the development and implementation of sector e-infrastructures in the mortgage industry. This study is of particular importance because it addresses obstacles and enablers for business sector e-infrastructure development. Although public sector e-infrastructure development is not the same as standardization in a business sector. the case studies have much in common. They both address the task of sector e-infrastructure development. The organizations that constitute the sector and its users, not the IT-vendors, should drive the development. The organizations in the sector have diverging interests and assignments but they have to cooperate in order to create sector-wide interoperability. As a consequence it is of interest to analyze the enablers and obstacles found in the case study presented in Markus et al. (2006), and we will compare these preconditions with the preconditions found in the Social Allowance case study in Section 6.4.

Markus et al. (2006) claim that development and implementation of standards are interlinked and that the whole standardization process (both development and adoption) must be analyzed together and be regarded as a collective dilemma. There is a dilemma in that all relevant groups in the business sector should participate in order to create sector-wide interoperability. However, an obstacle (BSPre 1) is that the heterogeneity of interests among participating user organizations, threatens the success of standards development by inhibiting collaboration. Another precondition that could obstruct standards adoption is (BSPre 2) if user groups that are key to standards diffusion are excluded from standards development, or are allowed to in<sup>fl</sup>uence standard development at the expense of other user groups. As a consequence a number of enablers must exist, or have to be developed that promote cooperation and network organizing. They speci<sup>fi</sup>cally address the bootstrap problem by suggesting that (Markus et al., 2006, p. 461) “In order to achieve successful diffusion of industry-wide VIS standards, each VIS standardization initiative must employ a set of tactics that jointly solves its particular standards development dilemma, without jeopardizing the solution to its particular standards diffusion dilemma.”Different tactics/preconditions are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed like moral suasion, rules for governance, IPR agreements, and technical decisions regarding the scope of the standard. Moral suasion (BSPre 3) implies that the endeavor must have an explicit moral support of the key actors in the sector, in this particular case two government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs) that were key actors in the mortgage industry. Rules for governance (BSPre 4) means that there have to be policies and procedures that ensure fair representation of organizations from all industry segments. IPR agreements (BSPre 5) ensured that IT vendors did have the ability to make pro<sup>fi</sup>ts from commercial products, but that they did not have the ability to make pro<sup>fi</sup>ts of the standards themselves. Technical decisions regarding the scope of the standard (BSPre 6) concerns the ability to make the right decisions in order to develop a standard that is as simple as possible rather than developing a perfectly developed technical standard.

## 3. Research approach

The purpose of the paper is to investigate the process of e-infrastructure development in the public sector focusing of the initial stage (the bootstrap phase), and clarify different enabling and constraining preconditions in the context of the public sector. This has been addressed in a longitudinal case study, i.e. an e-government development case in the social welfare sector. The research approach can be characterized as practice research (Goldkuhl, 2011) comprising both action research (AR) and design research (DR). A main idea of practice research (PR) is to contribute to local practices (through intervention and design) and also through abstracted and generalized knowledge to the research community and to “general practice”, i.e. other practices of similar kinds. In this case general practice should comprise e-government practices and partially also other practices involved in sector e-infrastructures development. Another important feature of PR is to conceive the empirical field as a set of inter-related practices (ibid; Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Schatzki, 1996). The conducted case study can be characterized as exploratory and theory generating. A key feature is the combined action and design research approach with in<sup>fl</sup>uences on preconditions for successful evolution of e-infrastructure.

A key feature in PR is to address a topic of great practical relevance and importance (Benbasat & Zmud, 1999). Topic selection emerges continually and integrated with choice of an appropriate case study. The case study was chosen since it consisted of demanding design challenges, a complex e-infrastructure and a large number of stakeholders. Two researchers (the authors of this paper) have participated in a social allowance project (see Section 4 below) as action researchers and design researchers. The roles of the two participating researchers have been to actively conduct development tasks (like process modeling, information modeling, user-interface design, XML schema design, program coding, testing and deployment) besides traditional research tasks like data collection and analysis, which will be described in more detail in Section 3.2 below.

## 3.1. A combined action research and design research endeavor

We characterize the research process as a combination of action research (Davison, Martinsons, & Kock, 2004) and design research (Hevner, March, Park, & Ram, 2004). How these two research approaches have been integrated in this project has earlier been described in more detail in Goldkuhl (2012). The research is clearly design research since we have been actively participating in design and construction of a new IT artifact. It is action research since it has worked with intervention and change in several practice settings. The DR activities of build and evaluate has been integrated into the AR phases according to the canonical AR cycle model (Davison et al., 2004; Susman & Evered, 1978). This will be explained below. We have not developed or applied any speci<sup>fi</sup>c model of integrated action design research, like e.g. Sein, Henfridsson, Purao, Rossi, and Lindgren (2011). AR and DR were well integrated within the research context of practice research (Goldkuhl, 2011). Sein et al. (2011) pose important criticism against DR for separating building and evaluating. Although possible to analytically distinguish, these types of activities need to be performed in continual alternation and iteration. The speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, design and construction of the IT artifact have been interwoven with continual formative evaluations within the project group. After deployment of the IT artifact, evaluative studies concerning its use have been conducted.

The process has followed the phases of canonical AR. How many AR cycles have been conducted? It is not straightforward to determine the number of cycles due to the iterative and incremental nature of the development work and the successive deployment of artifact releases. It is not critical for the analysis of data to determine the number of cycles and iterations.

The project started with analyses of the state of the art of the installed base mapping the applications used at the municipalities and at the state agencies. At the start of the project a number of modeling seminars were also performed where the existing case handling processes at the municipalities were described. This as-is analysis included a more detailed investigation of the functionality and the conceptual models implemented in existing applications (enterprise systems) used for case handling of social allowances in the municipalities The modeling of the installed base and the work processes had the role of an initial diagnosis; as the <sup>fi</sup>rst step of the action research cycle (Davison et al., 2004). The initial diagnosis was a basis for action planning (second step of action research) where a changed work process and IT capabilities were sketched and decided on. The next step was the building and formative evaluation of IT artifact: the design and implementation of a gateway called the Multi-query (i.e. action taking; the third step of action research). The use of the new IT artifact among social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>cers has been studied and evaluated by the researchers (the fourth step of action research). In order to further improve the designed e-infrastructure and to put new demands on other stakeholders, the practitioners and researchers in the project have been engaged in inquiring what was learnt (the last step in the action research cycle).

The work with process modeling, conceptual modeling, prototyping and user-interface design was theoretically informed through all parts of the research process. Improvements of the case handling process were proposed and implemented. Intervention and change was conducted through process improvements and the introduction of a new IT artifact.

## 3.2. Data collection and data analyses

The main source of the data collection has been 18 project meetings, and all the activities and interactions performed in the design process (see Section 4.2 below). There have been 14 project meetings until now, 13 one-day and 1 two-day physical meetings, and 4 two-hour telephone meetings. The data collection has been performed through discussions and interactions with social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>cers and systems administers at project meetings. At some of these meetings representatives of vendors and state agencies have participated. These meetings have been recorded on tape and documented through minutes, which describe the main events and decisions taken at the meetings. The case handling systems have been demonstrated and the way the social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>cers work with their systems. On site visits have been made at three of the national authorities: The Social Insurance Agency (SIA), The Board for Study Support (BSS) and The Federation of Unemployment Insurance Funds (FUIF). The interaction in the design process also includes hundreds of telephone conversations and about 1200 recorded e-mails. All documentation generated through the development process and the different versions of the Multi-query application also constitute key data as a basis for data analysis. The time period for the data collection has been from December 2008 until December 2010, and the researchers have been complete participants in the development project (Oates, 2006).

Our data analysis strategy is mainly inductive, following principles of grounded theory — GT (Corbin & Strauss, 2008; Glaser & Strauss, 1967). We have studied the e-infrastructure literature prior to our empirical design work (Sections 2.2 and 2.3 above). However, in the spirit of GT we have bracketed extant categories of enablers and obstacles in order to have fresh and open minds in the data analysis and category generation. We have applied an inductive manner of data analysis, but not applied a strict GT coding process, which will be explained below.

The data analysis strategy was designed to describe and understand the design situation and to investigate the preconditions for e-infrastructure development in e-government (see Section 1). During the development the main categories emerged as the design team experienced that some state agencies were willing to co-operate in the design process while others seemed to be uninterested. Thus, during the design process it became clear that there were certain preconditions that hampered the design process respectively, and these preconditions were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the actual development work. Repeatedly the project team recognized that the actors put forward similar arguments explaining why they wanted to participate or not, and that these arguments were categorized into four main categories: legal, economical, organizational and e-infrastructure. During the actual design of the information exchange in co-operation with two state agencies, the project team also realized that it was impossible to make certain design decisions dependent on how the laws were written and interpreted by the state agencies. The design team also discovered how the installed base enabled and hindered the design. This research process (founded in action and design research) of how to generate important categories is quite different compared to a conventional GT approach and other similar qualitative data analyses methods, which are based on a massive coding of collected data. Instead the collected data material has in our case been used as a source for validating and retaining development and design experiences. Confer a similar approach in Baskerville and Pries-Heje (1999) where some elements of grounded theory have been appropriated into an action research approach. One important strength of our approach is that when involved in the development process the researcher obtains experiential data (personal experiences) through direct social interaction with other actors; e.g. in the process of trying to engage different actors in the design and conducting design with fellow actors. Another strength of our approach is that the actual construction of an IT artifact gives a direct experience and understanding of the materiality of the installed base, of the real-world conditions under which the design and construction is conducted. This type of access and experience is impossible to attain only by observing or interviewing someone. Such experiences are also helpful in order to understand what is important and unimportant in the development process. The risk of using such an approach is of course that the researcher becomes less objective and that the results get biased (Oates, 2006, p. 210). However, this risk can be overcome by comparing the <sup>fi</sup>ndings with other sources and theory. For example, arguments for the preconditions presented as obstacles and enablers in Section 5 are presented in an independent evaluation report written by The Swedish National Audit Of<sup>fi</sup>ce (Riksrevisionen. 2010) as a result of the workshop that was held in June 2010. which have been reviewed by state agencies that participated, and did not participate in the development of the public sector e-infrastructure.

Theoretically we have abstracted the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the case study by presenting a model for public sector e-infrastructure development (see Section 6.1) describing preconditions, stages, actors and results for public sector e-infrastructure development. The preconditions for e-infrastructure development have also been abstracted into theoretical clauses (see Section 6.2). We have also compared the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the case study with general preconditions for e-infrastructure found in the literature, and the “Design theory for dynamic complexity in information infrastructures” (Hanseth & Lyytinen, 2010) (see Sections 6.3 and 7). We have also compared the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the case study, which describes the design context of public service e-infrastructure development, with the design context presented in Markus et al. (2006) that describe the context of e-infrastructure development in the business sector, in the case of the mortgage industry.

## 4. The design case study: the social allowance project

## 4.1. Background

A municipality of Sweden is obliged by the Social Services act to provide social services. An important part of individual and family services concerns community members who require social allowance to maintain a reasonable standard of living (Socialstyrelsen, 2010).

If the household, a family or an individual, needs income support a request has to be made to the social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>ce. Today requests are sent in as paper documents. Forms to be <sup>fi</sup>lled in can be downloaded from the municipalities' websites, or handed over to the applicant when he/she visits the social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>ce. These requests generate a lot of administrative work because it is not only the request form that has to be handled but also a number of supporting documents. The side effect of this is that a lot of time is spent on paper routine work. An important part of the case handling process is to gather information about the economic situation of the members of the household from a number of state agencies. In 2009 this was done through telephone calls, which takes a lot of time both for the municipalities and the state agencies. There also exists one batch IT-service that is used for exchange with the Social Insurance Agency. There are primarily six state agencies that the social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>cers have to interact with:

• The Social Insurance Agency (SIA)

• The Board for Study Support (BSS)

• The Public Employment Service (PES)

• The Tax Agency (TA)

• The Federation of Unemployment Insurance Funds (FUIF)

• The Pensions Agency (PA).

The information exchange between municipalities and state agencies is constrained by several legal regulations. There are regulations for preserving the privacy of the clients (i.e. the members of the household). Therefore a new statute (SFS, 2008:975) to make electronic information transfer easier to conduct between the state agencies and municipalities (the social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>ces) was issued in the beginning of 2009. As a consequence, there was a need for development efforts in order to transform the as-is e-infrastructure where only one state agency SIA was connected to the municipalities via a batch service into an e-infrastructure providing direct access to all of the six state agencies (Fig. 1).

A project group was formed and a project leader was assigned from Sambruk, which is an association with the aim of supporting the joint development of e-government in the municipalities of Sweden. It consists of approximately 100 municipalities. Eight of the municipalities joined the project group, and each municipality had 1 to 4 representatives in the project group. The representatives were system administrators and social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>cers. The authors of this paper, and a technical consultant constituted the design team.

Gateways, adapters, standardized messages and direct-access services were needed in order to connect and expand the installed base. The Social Allowance Project started in December 2008 to address this need for infrastructural development.

## 4.2. The design process

## 4.2.1. Diagnoses

The main goal for the project was to develop a direct-access query function (a gateway) that could be used by the social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>cers in their interaction with the state agencies. This was expected to have an immediate effect on the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the case handling process, on the quality of the social welfare allowance service, and it would also reduce the number of incorrect payments. At a later stage it was also decided to develop an e-service that makes it possible for the applicants to submit their applications electronically to the municipality.

In order to perform the design activities the installed base had to be considered. Municipalities use different case handling systems for their social welfare case handling. There are 7 different types of systems (from external vendors) installed in most of the 290 municipalities, which means that each type of application is instantiated in several municipalities. The investigation of the case handling process and applications used at the municipalities also revealed that there was no single standardized work process among the 8 municipalities. A number of ambiguities in the conceptual models implemented in the case handling systems were also detected. For example, important concepts like “open case” and “household” were ambiguously de<sup>fi</sup>ned and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. This was a major obstacle for the information exchange, because the new statute (SFS, 2008:975) was very clear that the social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>cers could only ask fo information about members of households with open cases.

At SIA there were about 25 different IT applications, which contained information that was of interest to the municipalities. SIA also had a batch service that should be replaced by a new direct-access service. At BSS there were three application systems that were of interest. There were also several other systems at the other state agencies that contained information of interest to the municipalities.

## 4.2.2. Action planning

In order to analyze which type of gateway should be developed the project contacted the state agencies and the IT vendors in the beginning of the project. SIA had already, due to the new statute, started a project developing a direct access service in order to replace their existing batch service. BSS was just about to start a project developing a similar direct access service. The other state agencies had no development plans. They referred to budgetary, organizational and technical restrictions. The vendors of the case handling systems used by the municipalities did not present any exact plans when and how to implement the demanded information exchange. This led the Sambruk project group to start developing their own gateway (“the Multi-Query gateway”) according to the infrastructural model in Fig. 2. The “Multi-query” was intended to be a temporal solution until the vendors had caught-up. This implies that the “Multi-query” should be simple and easy to implement, in order to get a pay-off from the investment since the intention was that it should only be used for a limited period of time.

## 4.2.3. Action taking

The speci<sup>fi</sup>cation work of the Multi-query started in March 2009. This work included:

• a further re<sup>fi</sup>nement and standardization of work processes and conceptual models;

• negotiations with the state agencies about the meaning of the statute (SFS, 2008:975) and other statutes that govern the information exchange;

• design of XML-<sup>fi</sup>les; and

• the design of the user interface of the Multi-query gateway, which was carried out through building a prototype.

The speci<sup>fi</sup>cation work was <sup>fi</sup>nished in June. In August 2009 the programming work started. The gateway was developed in NET using Visual Basic and XSL-scripts. The programming and test activities were <sup>fi</sup>nished in November. The developed public sector e-infrastructure is displayed in more detail in Fig. 2.

During 2010 the Multi-query gateway has been deployed at 8 municipalities. Besides the continuous work of trying to engage the other four authorities in the design, maintenance and support activities with new releases are now regularly carried out.

## 4.3. The designed IT-artifact — an operational public sector e-infrastructure

Today the operational public sector e-infrastructure consists of two direct-access services that are implemented at SIA (this service is called Lefi) and BSS (this service is called Kommunfraga). There are 8 nodes at municipality side (Sundsvall, Botkyrka, Skellefteå, Luleå, Bollnäs, Kumla, Eskilstuna and Borlänge), and there are over 130 users at these 8 nodes using the Multi-query gateway. The Multi-query was developed as a stand-alone IT capability because the IT vendors of the case handling systems used at the municipalities were not interested in opening of up their systems to allow direct-access interaction between their systems and the Multi-query gateway. The use of the Multi-query and the developed e-infrastructure is described below.

The case handling process starts with an applicant sending in the request for social welfare allowance. The social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>cer opens a case for the household in the case handling system. In order to retrieve the information from the state agencies the social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>cer then uses the Multi-query by typing in a number of parameters into a screen document: the case identi<sup>fi</sup>er, the household identi<sup>fi</sup>er, the PID numbers of the household members. time period. and chooses the state authorities that queries should be sent to. The direct-access services at SIA and BSS receive the queries in XML <sup>fi</sup>les, search the databases at each agency, and then provide the answer in XML <sup>fi</sup>les which is sent back to the gateway at the municipalities. As soon as one of the answers from the two agencies has arrived the user can present the information on a number of screen-documents.

## 4.4. Evaluation and learning

The fourth and <sup>fi</sup>fth steps of action research (evaluation, re<sup>fl</sup>ection and learning) have taken place continuously during the whole project. The users assert that they save a lot of time using the gateway although not all state agencies are connected to the gateway, because they do not have to make so many telephone calls to SIA and BSS as they used to. It is a great advantage for the case handling process that social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>cers can get the answers without delay. This is also in line with the statements made by the representatives in the project group. These results will be more deliberatively presented in further research.

The Swedish National Audit Of<sup>fi</sup>ce (Riksrevisionen) have also performed an independent evaluation of the implementation of the information exchange based on statute (SFS, 2008:975). The evaluation report is based on a workshop that was held in June 2010. The workshop was initiated by Riksrevisionen in order to <sup>fi</sup>nd out the reasons why SIA and BSS had been able to implement the information exchange, and why the other agencies had not. Four state agencies (SIA, PES, TA, FUIF) participated in the workshop, and from the Social Allowance Project the design team, and representatives from two of the municipalities of the project group took part in the workshop.

## 5. Analysis: preconditions and stages

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the case study have given rise to a deeper understanding of the context for public sector e-infrastructure development. In this section we will present and analyze these <sup>fi</sup>ndings based on two main constructs that have emerged through our data collection and analysis: Preconditions and stages. We have made a principal division of e-infrastructure evolution into three stages (Fig. 3):

• Deliberation stage (compare the action planning phase Section 4.2.2)

• Design stage (compare the action taking phase Section 4.2.3)

• Operational stage (compare action results and operational e-infrastructure Section 4.3).

Performing these three stages has created the <sup>fi</sup>rst version of a national public sector e-infrastructure for direct-access information exchange for social welfare allowances. The design of e-infrastructural constructs like gateways, system-to-system services, and adapters was preceded by a deliberation stage and succeeded by an operational stage. The case study shows that there have to be some analysis, planning and decision before the organizations, i.e. state agencies, municipalities and IT Vendors, engage or not in design. This is what we call the deliberation stage. In the deliberation stage an organizational actor (i.e. one or more humans acting on behalf of an organization) perceives and interprets the action context in order to participate or not in the design. We have investigated reasons for engaging in design and reasons for not engaging in design. This stage was followed by a design stage where the enrolled organizations, cooperated and developed new IT capabilities. Finally there was an operational stage where the designed IT capabilities were installed, operated and used. There are certain preconditions for the deliberation and design stages and for the results and features of the design.

## 5.1. Legal preconditions

(1) Fundamental for enabling this e-infrastructure development has been the existence of old and new regulations. The new statue (SFS, 2008:975) assigns certain roles to the welfare boards of the municipalities and to some state agencies. The state agencies obtain the liability to deliver certain information about the members of the households to the municipalities, and the municipalities obtain the right to request and receive such information. This means that roles, actions and information content are de<sup>fi</sup>ned through the regulations. The way to transfer information is also stated through regulations. The municipalities do not have an obligation to request and receive information; they have a right to do so However, with the obligation to pay welfare allowances to households follows obligations to control the economic situation of their members.

Although there are strict obligations for the state agencies to deliver information and thus to arrange for IT-service delivery, some of the state agencies have postponed their efforts to do so. They have referred to other than legal reasons for this delay to comply with the law, which means there are other preconditions that act as obstacles (see Sections 5.2–5.4). It is thus not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to have legal regulations; it seems to be necessary that other preconditions also are available to move from deliberation stage to design stage. (2) A major problem is that there are no of<sup>fi</sup>cial bodies working with enforcement of the regulations, and as consequence the state agencies can violate the law without any sanctions.

Another obstacle was that the new statute (SFS, 2008:975) that should boost the interaction between municipalities and state agencies turned out to be cumbersome to apply in practice. (3) There were different legal interpretations between the state agencies and municipalities (in the project group) about how the information exchange should be technically implemented (which delayed the design and implementation). The project group had taken it for granted that the information exchange should be based on direct-access technology but when the project group met a representative from BBS at a project meeting on the 27th of January 2009, it became clear that the notion of direct-access was unclear. The statute SFS, 2008:975 did not explicitly prescribe the technology that should be used, and there were other statutes e. g. the statue (SFS, 2009:321) that hindered the use of direct-access technology. When this was discovered, one of the researchers argued (quote from the minutes of the meeting): “Direct-access is preferable” and the representative of BSS responded “The word of the act is unclear especially for BSS”. BSS eventually launched a direct-access service on the 1st of January 2010 but they had to await a change in the statute (SFS, 2009:321), which took effect on that date that allowed the use of direct-access technology for the information exchange.

Another problem occurred when the two <sup>fi</sup>rst municipalities (Botkyrka and Sundsvall) were about to connect the Multiquery gateway to the system-to-system service (Kommunfraga) at BSS in January 2010. In this case it was differing interpretations of the notion of technical obstacle that was the problem. In the preparatory work of the statute SFS, 2008:975 it was stated that there must be technical obstacles that prevented the Social Welfare Of<sup>fi</sup>cers to submit questions without open cases. The lawyers at BSS were hesitant to allow the municipalities the use of their service (Kommunfraga) because it was technically possible to submit a query using the Multi-query without <sup>fi</sup>rst opening a case in the case handling system used at the municipality, although all queries sent from the Multi-query were logged in order to prevent misuse. The view of the project group was that a log function was suf<sup>fi</sup>ced as a technical obstacle to prevent misuse, but the lawyers at BSS did not share this view.

The quote below is from an email sent from a representative of the project group representing the municipality of Sundsvall to members of the design team. In the email he refers to a telephone conversation he had just had with a lawyer at BSS:

“The problem is that BSS maintains that the preparatory work of the statute prescribes that there must be technical obstacles that prevent this from happening (sending a query without an open case). However, I think that I succeeded in getting the person who contacted me (from BSS)….to be realistic about this. When we <sup>fi</sup>nished our conversation he promised to investigate if The Data Inspection Board could support our interpretation of the law.”

Eventually the municipalities of Sundsvall and Botkyrka (in January 2010) were allowed to connect to the system-to-system service (Kommunfraga) at BSS. However, a year after the services had been connected the Data Inspection Board made an inspection of the system-to-system service (Kommunfraga) at BSS, and the Multi-query at Botkyrka, and they concluded that a log of the questions sent from Multi-query gateway was not enough (Datainspektionen, 2011) and they urged BSS “to stop giving out information about individuals by direct-access” until they had reassured that the municipalities had implemented technical obstacles, which really means that the Multi-query must be started or called from within the case handling system, which was a major problem because the vendors of the case handling system at <sup>fi</sup>rst did not allow this. The problem was eventually resolved by a allowing the design team to build an adapter to the case handling system that was used in Botkyrka.

(4) There were also disputes between the organizations concerning the speci<sup>fi</sup>ed information content (in the new statute): the exact meaning of certain information items, and if the information requirements of the municipalities were really covered by the statute. For example, the Social Welfare Of<sup>fi</sup>cers wanted to know how many days a client had left of his/her parents' allowance. At a meeting held on the 8th of October 2010 however, the representatives from SIA answered that “this requirement is not prescribed in the statute 2008:975, which means that it has to be changed”. Nevertheless the statute was not changed and the information was never provided to the municipalities.

## 5.2. Organizational preconditions

The ability to engage in inter-organizational e-infrastructure projects depends on the competence and experience of the organization for governance and performance of such projects. (5) Most large public agencies have a long tradition and experience at developing their own intra-organizational IT systems. In recent years their competence in developing e-services aimed for citizens has increased. (6) However, experience of working with other organizations in collective e-infrastructure design projects seems to be limited. This is obvious in our case. Some state agencies (TA, PES, FUIF) seem, surprisingly, to be ignorant and uncertain of how to develop the requested IT capabilities. They refer to challenging technical demands (see Section 5.4) and also to an organizational uncertainty, as to how to interact with all the 290 municipalities both in design and operation. There is obviously a lack of knowledge and competence of how to arrange IT-service delivery to other organizations and how to conduct a development project with many other stakeholders.

(7) A major obstacle is that there is no institutional body at the operational level that all state authorities and municipalities can turn to in order to get help with the organizing of these types of projects. There were for example two major initiatives taken to start the development; one initiated from the municipality side by Sambruk, and one initiated from the state authority side by SIA. Sambruk tried to contact all the state agencies and vendors, and SIA tried to contact all the municipalities and the vendors, but at the start these two initiatives were unaware of each other. Another problem is that Sambruk only represents 100 of the 290 municipalities and SIA only represents itself as a state authority, which implied that the job of implementing the information exchange was not fully coordinated. The problem is that Sambruk is not fully acknowledged as the one representative for the municipalities this was obvious in a meeting between the design team and the CIO at FUIF in June 2009. This quote is taken from the <sup>fi</sup>eld notes: “She (the CIO) called for one part to talk to, she said that she did not want to talk to a lot of un-coordinated municipalities. We claimed that Sambruk was that party but she wanted a more production-oriented partner and was not really convinced by us.”

The roles of public agencies are changing. As indicated above, there is a growing need to act as a network organization. (8) However, authorities are traditionally organized as hierarchical autonomous units and thus they have a long tradition to work as an independent authority. This has created an ingrained self-image of a self-contained organization (9). The formal organization structure and the organization culture implies that these authorities do not see themselves as network organizations, and there is no explicit organization assignment issued by the government that the state agencies should consider themselves as network organizations. The National Audit Of<sup>fi</sup>ce (Riksrevisionen, 2010, p. 10) recommends the state agencies to “create a forum where the of<sup>fi</sup>cials can discuss issues that concern information exchange and create communication channels which makes it possible to focus on real problems”. Nowadays networking is implied from other assignments to the public agencies; for example the new statute (SFS, 2008:975) stating that a public agency should be an information supplier, which is a new assignment added to the agency's primary duties to serve their external clients (citizens). A new role as a network organization is evolving and different public agencies have been more or less fast and successful in learning this new organizational role (10).

## 5.3. Economic preconditions

There are economic motives for engaging in e-infrastructure development and economic motives for not doing so. The state agencies have got an obligation to deliver information electronically, but they do not get any revenues for this delivery. Today, this kind of information is delivered mainly through telephone services. (11) The transformation from telephone services to e-delivery will decrease costs. The development of the services (made by SIA and BSS) has been governed by both legal obligations and economic calculations. These state agencies consider their investments in IT-service provision for the municipalities to be pro<sup>fi</sup>table. Thus, they claim not only legal motives, but also economic reasons for engaging in design.

As described above, there are state agencies (TA, PES, FUIF, PA) that have not yet worked out any IT capabilities for the requested electronic information delivery. The state agencies claim, among different motives, (12) budget restrictions for postponing their IT investments. For example at the meeting on 27th of January the representative from TA said: “We made a cut in the development budget some weeks ago because no municipality had asked for the service”. In large state agencies there are bureaucratic budget procedures and if there is no budget record for an IT project it cannot be commenced. (13). Furthermore, they do not have any good statistics of how much their manual telephone services cost and (14) they have no direct revenues for being an information supplier. As a consequence these agencies claim that they do not know if this is a good economic investment.

(15) The present <sup>fi</sup>nancing model for e-government development projects in Sweden is designed for the <sup>fi</sup>rst generation of e-government projects, aimed at making the single organization more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient (SOU 2009:86). The idea is that each organization evaluates the IT investment and decides whether there will be a proper payback in the future. However, this single organization cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t model is not adapted for e-infrastructure development projects, because in these projects the costs are distributed between the information suppliers and receivers, and often it is the receivers/users that get the greatest bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the investment. There are not economic incentives enough for the information suppliers to embark on joint e-infrastructure development. For example, FUIF has estimated (Riksrevisionen, 2010) that to develop a direct-access service would cost 4.5 million SEK. The operational cost would be 3.75 million SEK each year. But the estimated cost reduction would only be 1.26 million SEK each year thus the cost reduction would only be 25% of the development cost and the cost for operating the service each vear would be three times higher than the cost reduction

(16) It is also clear that the reason the IT vendors had no clear plans for when to develop new interfaces in order to integrate the enterprise systems with the state agencies was strictly commercial. One representative of a major IT vendor said that “so far no municipality had requested that kind of functionality, however they intended to watch the SIA on-line project, and they will have a direct access service ready for their new case-handling system”.

## 5.4. Preconditions of existing e-infrastructure: technical, informational and contractual

An e-infrastructure exists as an installed base, which acts as a precondition for deliberation and design. Investigations, in the deliberation stage conducted by the different actors, covered the assessment of which IT capabilities were available and already used, and the feature of these capabilities. This was done in order to see how the existing e-infrastructure could be used as a base for an integrative design.

In the project there were two different technical options to transport the information between the nodes in the e-infrastructure. One option was to use web services another was to use the SHS-protocol. SHS is a technical infrastructure for information exchange to be used by authorities in the public sector in Sweden for high security information exchange across the Internet. SIA, had already chosen SHS, because the information exchange demanded high security solutions. This security demand was stated in the regulations and was due to needs for strict privacy. In the design phase, (17) the Sambruk project group <sup>fi</sup>rst argued that the same protocol (SHS) should be used by all parties, since this made the data transport both secure and easy for all parties. However it was soon discovered that web services also could provide an acceptable security level so BSS decided to use this technology to enable the exchange. (18) Another enabling prerequisite for creating services and gateways at both sides is the existence of common standardized identi<sup>fi</sup>ers (like the Swedish PID-number and The Swedish organization number). The PID-number makes it possible to integrate and retrieve information from different databases knowing that the right household member is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. The organization number enables the security capability in the SHS-protocol to make sure that it is the right organizations that request the information and that the answer is delivered to the right organization. (19) Although the application infrastructure at SIA and BSS was far from perfect (see below) the information was accessible and the Social Welfare Of<sup>fi</sup>cers could understand it.

(20) Most of the authorities had no experience of using direct-access techniques for information exchange. They had used SHS but only for asynchronical (batch) <sup>fi</sup>le exchange. For example the representative for the TA stated that “Today there exist e-transport SHS and <sup>fi</sup>le exchange, direct-access does not exist, it would be more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult” (Minutes, 2009, Jan 27). (21) Another obstacle also emerged during the technical installation of the Multi-query at the municipalities because of problems with <sup>fi</sup>rewalls and technical infrastructure that involved coordinated troubleshooting from the IT departments and IT vendors providing the SHS software, and this delayed the installations.

The application infrastructure at the state agencies had a strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the design of the XML-structures of the services. As a consequence the structure of the XML-<sup>fi</sup>les had to be adapted to the databases at SIA and BSS. (22) This was a problem because the content of the XML-<sup>fi</sup>les was constrained by the installed base, and because of that they could not contain all the information that was speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the statute (SFS, 2008:975), and nothing was done to change the applications at the state agencies to provide it. Some of the XML-<sup>fi</sup>les were of low information quality, and the basic cause of that were low quality information structures in the installed base. In addition, one of the agencies (FUIF) have postponed its efforts to develop a service because of a very messy application infrastructure. They claim “the application systems must be adjusted which is a workload that is hard to estimate without a feasibility study” (Riksrevisionen, 2010). Consequently, they cannot estimate the cost for adjusting their application infrastructure in such a way that the information speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in (SFS, 2008:975), can be delivered, and based on the fact that at the moment it is too dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for them to design a direct access service. Metadata has also been a problem because it has been hard to understand the data structures and information of the installed base both for the design team and the users due to the lack of comprehensible semantics. (23) The metadata has not been produced for external use but for intra-organizational use only.

## 6. Theorizing and discussion

In this section an emerging theory for e-infrastructure design will be presented. In Section 6.1 a model for e-infrastructure design is presented, in Section 6.2 the preconditions are abstracted and de<sup>fi</sup>ned, and clauses are presented that can be used to guide the developers' attention to important enablers and obstacles that are essential in public e-infrastructure design. Finally, in Section 6.3 the presented model and the preconditions are discussed and compared to the existing literature presented in Section 2.

## 6.1. A model for public sector e-infrastructure design

Fig. 4 below shows a model that has three stages and six types of preconditions. The model is based on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings presented in Section 5. It is also based on pragmatist ontology where an action is divided into phases of pre-assessment, intervention and post-assessment (Goldkuhl, 2012; Mead, 1938). In order to act in the world the actor needs to interpret and (pre-) assess its instrumental qualities as action affordances and then utilize these affordances in the interventive phase of the action. Such external instrumentalities direct the intervention through its enabling and constraining functions (Wertsch, 1998). External elements can be of both a material and social character (Leonardi, 2011), i.e. the affordances of other actors that have to be considered. The actor does not only assess these external circumstances in a situated action, also internal aspects, like “what counts as appropriate actions when being an actor/organization of this kind” in<sup>fl</sup>uences the decision of what course of action to pursue (March & Olsen, 1984). Many of the preconditions below is typically external enablers or obstacles, but there are some that are internal. For example, an internal in<sup>fl</sup>uence occurs when actors within a public agency follows a restricted organizational self-image of not being a net-work organization. Such a view may limit the engagement in inter-organizational infrastructure endeavors.

The <sup>fi</sup>gure shows that public sector e-infrastructure development implies the development of adapters, standardized messages, system-to-system services and gateways, and in order to do that a number of public agencies (e.g. state authorities and municipalities), IT vendors and other organizations have to be engaged in the design. These actors should form different project teams that can work with different parts of the e-infrastructure, but the work of the different teams has to be coordinated. The actors that engage in the design stage will in<sup>fl</sup>uence the following: the formation of the project groups and their coordination, the development process, as well as gateways, adapters, standardized message and services designed, because they have power and they bring resources and competence into the design stage. Thus, the features of the enhanced e-infrastructure will depend on both the actors that engage in the design stage and those who do not. The preconditions described in the <sup>fi</sup>gure have a strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the design process because they act both as enablers and obstacles for e-infrastructure engagement and design. They shape the context within which the e-infrastructure will be designed. As a consequence, it is important to analyze these preconditions because they will have a strong impact on the design process and the enhanced e-infrastructure. These empirically generated categories are described in Table 4 below.

## 6.2. Theoretical clauses

In Table 4, the different enablers and obstacles discovered in the case study (see Table 3, the numbers in brackets refer to Table 3 and Section 5) have been further abstracted into theoretical clauses. This has been done for the purpose of theorizing and making the knowledge less bound to the studied case and thus more easily transferrable to other cases and situations in the public sector. In this abstraction process the four groups of preconditions have been generally de<sup>fi</sup>ned, and a number of clauses have been formulated. The formulation of the different clauses has been adapted to two general formats. The clauses on enablers follow this standard form: b Enabler > may enable the evolution of e-infrastructure. The clauses on obstacles follow a corresponding standard clause: b Obstacle > may inhibit the evolution of e-infrastructure. “Evolution” covers both to join (deliberation stage) and to conduct (design stage). In this evolving theory on public sector e-infrastructure design these clauses can inform designers of important aspects that have to be considered in the design process. The word “may" in the clauses is very important, In this socio-technical theorizing there is no deterministic natural science like explanations. The typical situation is that the existence of an enabling precondition may contribute to the actors' possibilities to participate in the evolution process. The existence of an obstacle precondition may inhibit (stop, obstruct or discourage) the actors to participate in the e-infrastructure evolution. Walsham, (1995 p 79) describes this type of explanation in the following way: “Thus, generative mechanisms identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for phenomena in the social sciences should be viewed as ‘tendencies’, which are valuable in explanations of past data but are not wholly predictive for future situations”.

We will make some comments on the theoretical clauses below. These comments also comprise considerations made in the abstraction process when going from case-dependent formulations of preconditions to generalized de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and clauses.

The legal precondition means “regulations for electronic exchange”. Enabler (1) corresponds directly to clause, “Le1. Regulations with clear specifications for electronic exchange may enable the evolution of e-infrastructure”. Three obstacles (2, 3, 4) are grouped together in the clause “Lo1. Regulations with unclear or contrarious speci<sup>fi</sup>cations for electronic exchange may inhibit the evolution of e-infrastructure”. Obstacles (2) and (4) concern the lack of detailed speci<sup>fi</sup>cations in regulations concerning information exchange (4) or measures if not complied with. We include also the existence of different legal interpretations, and the fact that regulations can be contrarious (3) within this clause (Lo1). Such diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed interpretations are seen as a consequence (and thus an intermediate phenomenon) of insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient clarity in legal formulations.

The organizational preconditions are grouped into the category of “organizational capabilities for interorganizational cooperation”. The notion of organizational capability follows a broad resource-based view of the organization (Wernerfelt, 1984). Organizational capability may comprise organizational assignments (10), competence/experiences (5, 6), external supporting organizations (7), organization structure (8), organizational self-image (9) as well as other possible ingredients. In this context it is the capability of inter-organizational co-operation that is focused. The clause “Oe1. Clear and strong organizational capabilities for inter-organizational cooperation may enable the evolution of e-infrastructure” is based on enabler (10), and the clause “Oo1. Weak organizational capabilities for inter-organizational cooperation may inhibit the evolution of e-infrastructure” is based on obstacles (5, 6, 7, 8, 9).

The economic precondition is categorized as “tangible economic revenue and information about economic circumstances". This category includes revenues or information about other economic circumstances like the possible cost reductions and available economic resources. The enabler is formulated as “Ee1. Expected economic cost reductions from altering to electronic exchange may enable the evolution of e-infrastructure” (11). The three obstacles are “Eo1. Lack of information about economic cost reductions from altering to electronic exchange” (13, 16), “Eo2. Lack of economic resources for altering to electronic exchange” (12, 14, 15), and “Eo3. Lack of tangible economical revenue from altering to electronic exchange” (16).

## 6.3. Preconditions for public e-sector e-infrastructure development versus general preconditions

The literature review presented in Section 2 shows that designers must be aware of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c design context that designer of e-infrastructures face due to the path dependency and distributed control characteristics of these types of endeavors. In the e-infrastructure literature <sup>fi</sup>ve types of general preconditions have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, that are considered to be crucial for e-infrastructure development:

• (GPre 1) Installed base,

• (GPre 2 Tensions

➢ (Gpre 2.1) Interest and exclusion,

➢ (Gpre 2.2) Ownership and investment models

➢ (Gpre 2.3) Data culture and data tensions

• (GPre 3) Standards.

These preconditions can be compared to the six types of preconditions presented above

## 6.3.1. Legal Precondition, “regulations for electronic exchange”

The legal precondition is fundamental for public sector e-infrastructure design. It is regulations for information sharing that enable the information exchanges in the public sector by regulating who should deliver what information. and who has the right to request. But the regulations are also an obstacle for information exchange when there are no sanctions that can be used if it is not complied to. Regulations are also an obstacle when they are not well written; if they are too vague or ambiguous, leaving room for different interpretations; or too detailed not giving room for thoughtful IT designs. We claim that the legal preconditions referred to in this paper could be generalized within the whole public sector.

The fundamental role of the legal precondition for public sector e-infrastructure development is something that has not been noticed in previous research. In e-government and e-infrastructure literature semantic information standards (Gpre 3) have been viewed as the most important precondition for sector e-infrastructure development, and a lot of interest has been devoted to semantic standards development and implementation (Damsgaard & Truex, 2000; Guijarro, 2006; Hanseth, Monteiro, & Hatling, 1996; Hanseth & Monterio, 1998a; Markus et al., 2006). What does not seem to be recognized either in the e-infrastructure, or in e-government research texts (Janssena, Chunb, & Ae, 2009) is that regulations that specify how information should be shared, stored or preserved, are even more important as a precondition for public sector e-infrastructure development because they are compulsory while standards are only voluntary. It is these regulations that really govern the information exchange in the public sector and not semantic standards.

## 6.3.2. Organizational preconditions, “organizational capabilities for inter-organizational cooperation”

The organizational preconditions suggest that there is a need for new organizational capabilities, structures and assignments that enhance the engagement and cooperation in the deliberation, design and operational stages. For the last decade there has been a growing insight into the fact that the information technology leads to the emergence of a new kind of networked information society as a whole. In this new society the traditional vertical lines of power and of organization based around authority, ownership, territory and place, are overlaid by new forms of horizontal emergent network organizing which cut across the traditional vertical tiers of control (Benington, 2000). This puts demands on how to organize both development and operations in new networked ways. New attitudes must also follow. Public agencies no longer remain as self-contained organizations. There is a need for a shift in perspective towards “a whole society approach”. Public agencies must start to see each other as service providers and customers in an IT service provision network in joint case handling processes that ultimately should be of bene<sup>fi</sup>t to the client and society at large. The organizations in the public sector must learn how to organize these types of inter-organizational projects and how to design appropriate adapters, services and gateways. We claim that the organizational preconditions presented in this paper can be generalized within the whole public sector.

The organizational precondition is related to the general preconditions, interest and exclusion (Gpre 2.1) and data culture and data tensions (Gpre 2.3), described by Edwards et al. (2007). These tensions will shape the climate, and the incentive structures within which infrastructures will be bootstrapped and affect inter-organizational collaboration. Edwards et al. (2007, p. 28) conclude that “The careful nurturance of infrastructural change, and attending to the tensions that emerge from it, is a managerial and political skill of the highest order.”. Furthermore, in the infrastructural literature, the development of e-infrastructure has been considered not only as dif<sup>fi</sup>cult manage, but even unmanageable. Instead mechanisms have been sought for emergent structures and for how the evolution process could be self-organized. The design theory of Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010) and the use of Actor Network Theory (ANT) (Latour, 2005) to explain e-infrastructure evolution, are examples of that. According to Hanseth and Monterio (1998b) “Stability and social order, according to actor network theory, are continually negotiated as a social process of aligning interests. As actors from the outset have a diverse set of interests, stability rests crucially on the ability to translate, that is, reinterpret, represent or appropriate, others' interests to one's own. In other words, with a translation one and the same interest or anticipation may be presented in different ways thereby mobilizing broader support.” We agree on that, however the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the public sector show that there is a need for new inter-organizational structures that support networking and collaboration in the public sector, which are not top-down controlled. These are organizational structures that are driven by service orientation and the use of modern information technology but you cannot expect that these structures just emerge and self-organize. They have to be intentionally designed and implemented.

## 6.3.3. Economic preconditions, “tangible economic revenue and information about economic circumstances”

The economic preconditions are decisive in order to engage in the e-infrastructure design process. Successful e-infrastructure design always includes <sup>fi</sup>nancial elements (Edwards et al., 2007). The economic preconditions can be compared with (GPre 2.2) ownership and investment models and this precondition is well recognized in the e-infrastructure literature. We claim that the economic precondition is a general a precondition for e-infrastructure development, and in particular there is a need for new <sup>fi</sup>nancial models as well as an enhanced understanding of network economics in public sector e-infrastructure design. To quote (Edwards et al., 2007, p. 30) “targeted research on the political economy of infrastructure may help present-day infrastructure builders re-imagine and re-articulate the appropriate form, focus, and rationale for collective investment in infrastructure.”

## 6.3.4. The installed base precondition

In the e-infrastructure literature it is generally recognized that the installed base can be both an enabler and obstacle for e-infrastructure design.

6.3.4.1. The technical precondition, “technical capabilities in the installed base”. Useful technical capabilities in the installed base (Te1) relate to the transport (data transmission) and service infrastructure. The service infrastructures provide necessary support for addressing, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and service property discovery. Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010) claim that an enabling precondition for e-infrastructure growth is that the designer should use existing transport and service infrastructures (that include transmission, addressing and security capabilities) that the targeted user groups already use. However the transport and service infrastructure can also be an obstacle “(To1) Weak technical capabilities may inhibit the evolution of e-infrastructure.”. For example, if there is no one capable of using the transport and service infrastructure, which point to the need for technical skills in developing e-infrastructures.

6.3.4.2. The informational precondition, “informational constructs and content”. Established information constructs (Ie1), and available information (Ie2) at the application level enable the evolution of the e-infrastructure, and inaccessible information constructs, and content (Io1) is an obstacle. It is important to notice how important high quality informational constructs at the application level are in order to enable the information exchange. Especially it is important to emphasize the enabling power of identi<sup>fi</sup>ers for e-infrastructure development (Eriksson & Ågerfalk, 2010) e. g. the Swedish PID and organizational numbers, since identi<sup>fi</sup>ers and institutional objects form critical bounadary objects that make information exchange between different contexts possible. However, infrastructures at the application level can also be an obstacle when decades of systems development and maintenance have led to fragmented and ill-designed information constructs and content of low quality as well as incomplete and incomprehensible metadata. The as-is application infrastructure including its documentation has also been developed to support in-house developers and users inside the organization, not to support external users and designers. As a consequence the information becomes dif<sup>fi</sup>cult or even impossible to share.

6.3.4.3. The contractual precondition, “contractual circumstances”. The contractual precondition can be related to ownership and investment models (Gpre 2.2), because policies for intellectual property rights are important preconditions for e-infrastructure development, because they regulate the possibility to develop and use the IT capabilities of the e-infrastructure. Expected usefulness of contractual opportunities (Ce1) may enable the development, e. g. the use of open or community source. However, the usefulness and benefits of existing contractual situations (Co1) may be an obstacle. For example, propriety licenses and what Edwards et al. (2007) call “localized learning.” Localized learning implies that once an organization or users have made an initial investment, they adapt themselves, rather than (re)consider alternatives.

## 6.4. Vertical information systems (VIS) standardization versus public sector e-infrastructure development

Finally, if we relate the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our case study to the case study of Markus et al. (2006) in the mortgage industry, there are a number of striking similarities and dissimilarities. In Section 2.3.2 we described a number of preconditions that hindered and enabled the development of the e-infrastructure in the mortgage industry. Obstacles: (BSPre1) Heterogeneity of interests, (BSPre 2) Key user group in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Enablers:(BSPre 3) Moral Suasion, (BSPre 4) Rules for governance, (BSPre 5) IPR agreements, (BSPre 6) Scoping decisions. Below these preconditions will be compared with the preconditions presented in Section 6.2.

## 6.4.1. Similarities

There are many similarities between business sector standardization and public sector e-infrastructure development. In both cases there is a sector collective action dilemma that has to be solved. Thus we can rephrase the proposition made by Markus et al. (2006) in the Social Allowance Welfare case by stating that “there is a collective action dilemma in that all relevant groups: IT-vendors, public agencies and other organizations should participate in order to create sector-wide interoperability.” The importance of collaboration is also re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the fact that both case studies high-light the importance of the organizational precondition. Markus et al. (2006) have found two major obstacles to standards adoption (see Table 2 Section 2) (BSPre1) heterogeneity of interests and (BSPre 2) key user group in<sup>fl</sup>uence. The <sup>fi</sup>rst obstacle (BSPre 1) threatens collaboration, which is a necessity because collaboration between all relevant structurally different groups is necessary to ensure a solution that meets sector inter-operability. The second obstacle (BSPre 2) suggests that if user groups that are key to standards diffusion are excluded from standards development, or are allowed to in<sup>fl</sup>uence standards development at the expense of other user groups, it will jeopardize the standardization process. These obstacles could be described under the label of “Oo1. Weak organizational capabilities for inter-organizational cooperation may inhibit the evolution of e-infrastructure.” The <sup>fi</sup>rst enabler Moral suasion (BSPre 3) implies that the endeavor must have an explicit moral support of the key actors in the sector. The second enabler (BSPre 4) rules for governance, means that there is a need for structures that guarantee fair representation of organizations from all relevant actors within the industry, which encourage broad participation use of the developed standard. This could be compared to the enabler “Oe1. Clear and strong organizational capabilities for inter-organizational cooperation may enable the evolution of e-infrastructure.”.

The third enabler (BSPre 5) IPR agreements IPR agreements which ensured that IT vendors did have the ability to make pro<sup>fi</sup>ts from commercial products, could be compared to enabler, “Ce1. Expected future usefulness of contractual opportunities may enable the evolution of e-infrastructure”.

## 6.4.2. Dissimilarities

Although there are many similarities between business and public sector e-infrastructure development. there is a major difference. In the business sector and in communities around more formal standardization initiatives, it is commonly believed that there is one way to successfully build sector infrastructures: By bringing together all stakeholders, which should agree upon a set of standards. The infrastructure is then assumed to be built by each stakeholder (or may be vendor) implementing the standards. These assumptions are not applicable in public sector e-infrastructure. A public sector e-infrastructure development process has to be focused on the legal and the installed base preconditions rather than de<sup>fi</sup>ning and implementing standards. This also implies that the last enabler (BSPre 6) “Technical decisions that limit the scope and complexity of the standard” could be compared to the legal obstacle, “Lo1. Regulations with unclear or contrarious speci<sup>fi</sup>cations for electronic exchange may inhibit the evolution of e-infrastructure.”. In public sector e-infrastructure design it is the regulations not semantic standards that constitute the most important speci<sup>fi</sup>cations for the information exchange, and these regulations must have the right scope and be well-designed. Global semantic standards (or ontologies which is the term used in software engineering) are not useless in public sector design but there is an exaggerated belief that they can be used to develop application infrastructures independent of the installed base (Ure et al., 2009). To quote Goguen (2005, p. 1), “many ontologists seem to believe in the possibility of a single uni<sup>fi</sup>ed ontology that attracts consensus because it ‘re<sup>fl</sup>ects the real underlying reality' of a domain."

## 7. Conclusions and future research

The objective of this paper is to investigate the process of sector e-infrastructure development as a collective design endeavor, and clarify different enabling and constraining preconditions in the development context of public sector e-infrastructure development. The endeavor has been focused or bootstrapping a public sector e-infrastructure for information exchange.

In retrospect we can conclude that we followed the guidelines suggested by Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010) to bootstrap the e-infrastructure, although our initial purpose was not to put these design principles to the test.

(1) Initially we designed, and developed a simple IT capability that was directly useful for a small user group. (2) We used the installed base, especially the existing universal service infrastructures, as an enabler for e-infrastructural growth. (3) We created a user community that offered feedback and learning. The project has been successful if we look at it as a traditional IS project. The social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>cers (the users) are very satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the Multi-query because it saves them time and they get a lot of useful information. The managers are also satis<sup>fi</sup>ed because they can see that the social welfare of<sup>fi</sup>cers both save time and can make more informed decisions about the cases based on the information from the Multi-query. However, from an e-infrastructure perspective the success is questionable. Only two state agencies and eight municipalities have joined the network up to this date. However, in February 2013 the Pension Agency (PA) will provide information using the direct-access service provided by SIA (PA was until December 2009 a part of SIA). In August 2013 the Tax Agency will launch a direct-access service. Thus there will be four state agencies out of six, which will have joined the network, and the development of a version 2 of the Multiguery have started that will be launched in August 2013. It will be of interest to watch if this extension of the network on the state agency side will make Multiquery more attractive for the municipalities.

The theory of Hanseth and Lyytinen (2010) has been useful for understanding “the bootstrap problem”. But as the authors notice (ibid., p. 15) “all theories are incomplete (Weick, 1989) and so is our proposed theory.” Thus, the theory does not tell us much about the preconditions for application e-infrastructure design in the public sector except for the e-infrastructure precondition. From a design and action oriented perspective we claim that a basic understanding of the design context where the six types of preconditions presented in Table 3 and their abstractions in the theoretical clauses (in 6.2) above, are essential for e-infrastructure development in an e-government context. These preconditions emerge as basic enablers and obstacles for the development process when the design process is experienced and analyzed from within the design process, and an action design perspective. In retrospect we should have put more effort in trying to overcome the legal, economical, organizational and installed base obstacles described in previous sections. It is important that designers of public sector e-infrastructures are made aware of these preconditions in order to be able to act in the in the right way, and we maintain that these preconditions have not previously been analyzed and understood from a design action perspective. However. we think that it is hard at this stage of the research process to suggest general “enrollment tactics” or policy recommendations. To quote Edwards et al. (2007) “the quiet politics of infrastructure emerge as politics of a more recognizable and sometimes uncomfortable type. Such instances of tension and resistance may constitute important sites of infrastructural learning and improvement, provided we can produce mechanisms that reliably surface and honestly report on dif<sup>fi</sup>culty, limitation, and failure (not a simple prescription, given the incentive structures prevailing among funders, sponsors, and builders of infrastructure).” We claim that our case study is an example of such an honestly report of a rather, but not completely successful e-infrastructure development project in the public sector. At this stage the important advice we can give to developers of e-infrastructures in the public sector is to use the six types of preconditions as guidelines in the deliberation stage to analyze the context within which the e-infrastructure will be designed. Based on such an analyses enablers and obstacles can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in a particular endeavor, and well informed decisions could be made concerning which speci<sup>fi</sup>c measures and tactics should be used in order to succeed.

This Social Allowance case study deals with public e-infrastructure development that compromises government bodies (authorities) at the central state level, and local agencies (the municipalities). Thus, the case study has implications for public e-infrastructure development because it provides a rich insight of the design context of such endeavors. However, there are also other sector e-infrastructures where actors from the public and business sector collaborate, such is the case with map and location based data services. These are interesting combinations of public and private business e-infrastructure development. It would be of interest to evaluate how the preconditions presented in this paper and the preconditions described by Markus et al. (2006) apply in such a mixed business-public design context.

Furthermore we agree with Hanseth and Lyytinen (2004) that e-infrastructures are not designed by an omnipotent designer and that the order of e-infrastructure emerges from e-infrastructure growth. However, this does not mean that there is no need for pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned order/structures or that there are a no powerful designers that can in<sup>fl</sup>uence and control the design more than others. The fundamental role of legal precondition entails that lawyers are powerful designers of public sector e-infrastructures. That is, some lawyers formulate the law on behalf of political executives, and other lawyers at different authorities interpret the law. In the design projects it is often lawyers who have the power to decide when there are con<sup>fl</sup>icts and ambiguities concerning legal interpretations. An important conclusion from “The Social Allowance Project” is that the sections of the regulations that have been of interest to the project, (both the old and new) and their interpretations, are not harmonious with, and even contradictory, to the goals of a modern e-government approach, which comprises a whole-of-society perspective.

Finally, the design action perspective presented in this paper represents an alternative and complementary view to the design theory presented by Hanseth and Lyytinen and that both perspectives must be considered in the actual design work. As a consequence of that the next step in the research process will be to further develop the model presented in Section 6.1 to a full-blown design theory for public sector e-infrastructure design. This includes a further validation of the preconditions and may also include suggestions for “enrollment tactics” or policy recommendations for public sector e-infrastructure design.
