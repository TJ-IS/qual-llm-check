# SURGE PRICING AND SHORT-TERM WAGE ELASTICITY OF LABOR SUPPLY IN REAL-TIME RIDESHARING MARKETS<sup>1</sup>

Shu He School of Business, University of Connecticut, Storrs, CT 06269 U.S.A. {shu.he@uconn.edu}

Liangfei Qiu Warrington College of Business, University of Florida, Gainesville, FL 32611 U.S.A. {liangfei.qiu@warrington.ufl.edu}

Xusen Cheng School of Information, Renmin University of China, Haidian District, Beijing 100872 CHINA {xusen.cheng@ruc.edu.cn}

The prominence of real-time ridesharing services, such as Uber and Lyft, has dramatically changed the landscape of traditional industries. This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the short-term wage elasticity of labor supply in real-time ridesharing markets using data from a major ridesharing platform in China. By exploiting an exogenous shock from uneven driving restrictions as an instrumental variable, we find a negative labor supply elasticity for ridesharing drivers, suggesting that drivers tend to drive less during days with a higher average hourly wage. Specifically, a percent increase in hourly wage will lead to a 0.931 percent decrease in daily working hours. This surprising finding is consistent with the behavioral income-targeting model based on the theory of reference-dependent preferences: Drivers have heuristic daily targets for total earnings and are more motivated to supply labor when they are below their income target than when they are above it. Therefore, they work less on days when earnings per hour are high and quit the market once their income target is reached. In addition, we find that taxi drivers are more rational and have positive labor supply elasticity, which implies that drivers are more rational when they have repeated opportunities for learning. Estimating labor supply elasticity is critical to understanding the economic efficiency of various surge pricing algorithms and driver subsidization programs for ridesharing platforms and policymakers. Our research suggests that a uniform price surging or driver subsidization approach for all ridesharing drivers may not incentivize the labor supply of drivers effectively.

Keywords: Labor supply elasticity, ridesharing, income target, reference-dependent preferences

## Introduction

## Motivation

The unprecedented growth of ridesharing services, such as Uber and Lyft, has reshaped the traditional taxi industry by matching passengers and drivers in real-time. About 3.9 million drivers actively drove with Uber as partners by the end of 2018, and they have completed 10 billion trips worldwide.<sup>2</sup> The number of active Uber drivers approximately doubled every six months from the middle of 2012 to the end of 2015 (Hall and Krueger 2018). In 2016 the estimated number of Uber and Lyft drivers in San Francisco was 45,000, while the San Francisco Municipal Transportation Agency issued only 2,026 taxi medallions (Jiang et al. 2018). Similarly, from 2013 to 2018, Uber and Lyft had 490,000 trips on average per day in New York City, while Yellow cabs had less than 300,000 trips.<sup>3</sup>

A unique feature of innovative ridesharing services or, more broadly, the new gig economy, is the flexibility on the labor supply side (Burtch et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2019; Huang et al. 2020; Kanat et al. 2018). The traditional taxi industry is heavily regulated: In many cities, the number of taxi drivers is limited by a licensing regime (e.g., the number of medallions that are issued), and taxi fare prices are often set by regulatory bodies (Cramer and Krueger 2016; Jiang et al. 2018). In contrast, a ridesharing platform is open to a large group of drivers. Also, thanks to the mobile information technology (IT) and mobile applications, which facilitate matching between the supply side and the demand side, ridesharing platforms offer partner drivers the flexibility in both setting a customized work schedule and adjusting it throughout the day: Drivers can enter and exit labor markets freely and adjustably (Chen et al. 2019). In addition, the flexible labor supply helps the gig economy attract workers who have different labor supply preferences. Workers who cannot provide labor supply regularly as taxi drivers may also contribute to the ridesharing platform.

Because of the flexibility of labor supply in the gig economy, ridesharing platforms typically use surge pricing algorithms to induce travel service supply and reduce demand during periods of high demand (Chen and Sheldon 2016). Specifically, when the platform detects situations of a supply shortage in areas, the surge pricing algorithm will hike the price in increments. Similarly, other gig economy platforms and online labor markets that enable flexible work arrangements, such as Freelancer and Upwork (Huang et al. 2020), have considered lowering the commission fees as a monetary incentive to recruit workers. Conventional wisdom suggests that surging prices (or lower commission fees) can increase labor supply and lead to more efficient markets. For example, considering the ridesharing platform: On one hand, the price increase would encourage more drivers to join the platform or motivate drivers to work longer; on the other hand, because the price is higher, customers who are willing to pay a lower price will leave the platform first. Hence, the customers who value the riding service more will be served first, and the overall social welfare will increase. However, the potential economic efficiency of a surge pricing mechanism depends critically on the supply-inducing effect. The key empirical question is whether a temporary higher wage level can stimulate the market’s supply side effectively in the short-term. If the supply-inducing effect is not significant, then surge pricing is primarily a way of extracting consumer surplus. A New York Times article called surge pricing “high-tech gouging. 4 In light of this view, estimating the short-term wage elasticity of the labor supply precisely is both theoretically and practically important.

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the shortterm wage elasticity of labor supply in the new real-time ridesharing services using data from a major ridesharing platform in China. For a permanent wage increase, a driver will be able to adjust the labor supply decision accordingly in the long term. The surge pricing, on the other hand, only temporarily changes a driver’s hourly wage for a few hours, which may not be fully incorporated in drivers’ labor supply decisions. A driver may stick to the same daily income goal despite the varying surge prices in the short term (Doran 2014). We also dig deeper into the heterogeneous effects of driving experiences and driving patterns on labor supply elasticity and offer researchers and practitioners insights regarding how to effectively implement price surging algorithms and driver subsidization programs based on driver characteristics and driving patterns.

## Research Questions and Contributions

Prior literature has offered two different frameworks for workers’ labor supply decisions: the rational life cycle model and the behavioral income-targeting model (Camerer et al. 1997). The predicted sign of labor supply elasticities from these two models are opposing, and each has gained empirical support to a certain degree. The classical (fully rational) life cycle model of labor supply predicts a positive price elasticity in the labor supply: As wages increase, individuals supply more labor. The underlying logic is that workers maximize their lifetime utilities in a multi-period optimization problem. A temporary wage increase will cause workers to supply more labor now in exchange for future leisure when the opportunity cost of leisure is lower. In contrast, the behavioral incometargeting model, rooted in the reference-dependent preferences theory in behavioral economics (Tversky and Kahneman 1991), predicts a negative price elasticity of labor supply. The theory postulates a kink in an individual’s utility curve around a contextual reference point, where losses relative to this point are weighted more heavily than gains. In an influential and seminal study, Camerer et al. (1997) empirically find a negative price elasticity of labor supply in the taxi industry: As wages for a day increase, taxi drivers drive fewer total hours. This surprising empirical result can be justified in the framework of income-targeting. Taxi drivers have daily, heuristic targets for total earnings. Because losses are weighted more heavily than gains, taxi drivers are more motivated to supply labor when they are below their income target than above it. Therefore, on days when the wage rate is lower, taxi drivers tend to work more to reach the target, and on days when the wage rate is higher, taxi drivers tend to work less and quit the market once the target is reached. This explanation is consistent with the theory of referencedependent preferences (Ordóñez et al. 2009; Tversky and Kahneman 1991).

The prior empirical literature has not reached a consensus on the direction of labor supply elasticity in the traditional taxi industry. Camerer et al., Chou (2002), and Crawford and Meng (2011) find support for income targeting (referencedependent preferences) using taxi driver data from Singapore and New York City. However, Farber (2005, 2008, 2015) questions the empirical implementation of Camerer et al. and concludes that income targeting does not play an important role in determining taxi drivers’ labor supply in New York City.

On ridesharing platforms, drivers’ short-term labor supply elasticities might be different because of the IT artifact, such as mobile IT and mobile apps. On one hand, the IT artifact allows workers in the gig economy to provide their labor in a more flexible way. Real-time ridesharing mobile applications lower the barriers to providing rides, allowing inexperienced and part-time drivers to enter the market. For (experienced) taxi drivers, the same labor supply decision happens every day, and they tend to learn to overcome the bias of narrow framing over time. Inexperienced drivers, who may be focused on earning pin money, tend to be less rational and more likely to be affected by narrow framing and income targeting. On the other hand, mobile IT may make daily income level more salient and further amplify (irrational) behavioral income targeting. In other words, the convenience of real-time ridesharing mobile applications allows ridesharing drivers to make labor supply decisions in a smaller time unit than taxi drivers. Taxi drivers typically make their labor supply decisions daily (Camerer et al. 1997). Meanwhile, ridesharing drivers can enter and exit markets several times a day as they make labor supply decisions based on driving sessions, which might last an hour or several hours. Hence, compared with taxi drivers, ridesharing drivers make labor supply decisions more frequently using a narrower framework.

A handful of previous studies (e.g., Angrist et al. 2017; Chen and Sheldon 2016; Sheldon 2016) find that the labor supply elasticity for Uber drivers is positive, suggesting that Uber drivers are not affected by income targeting. However, we believe that it is still necessary to evaluate ridesharing drivers labor supply elasticity. There is a crucial difference between Uber and the platform studied in this paper: Uber has already realized the issue of income targeting and has implemented behavioral interventions to address it. In contrast, the Chinese ridesharing platform in our study has not implemented any intervention to correct income-targeting behavior. Therefore, studying the income targeting phenomenon using data from our ridesharing platform is more appropriate than using data from Uber. Even if Uber drivers initially exhibit income targeting behavior, the effect may become insignificant because of the behavioral interventions implemented by Uber.<sup>5</sup> Therefore, we believe more analyses need to be done to understand labor supply elasticity in the new ridesharing service and the heterogeneity of labor supply elasticity, as well as to compare elasticities of labor supply for ridesharing drivers and for taxi drivers using data from the same city and the same period.

To systematically bridge this research gap, we begin by asking our first research question: (1) How do we precisely estimate the short-term wage elasticity of labor supply for ridesharing drivers? To alleviate the potential endogeneity issue in estimating labor supply elasticity, we adopted an innovative instrumental variable (IV), an exogenous shock from uneven driving restrictions in Beijing.

We find a negative labor supply elasticity for ridesharing drivers, suggesting that ridesharing drivers tend to drive less during days with a higher average hourly wage. Specifically, when the hourly wage increases by 1 percent, a ridesharing driver will reduce the daily working hour by 0.931 percent. Therefore, the behavioral income-targeting model based on the theory of reference-dependent preferences seems to provide a better explanation for ridesharing drivers’ short-term labor supply decisions. Using conditional Logit and survival models, we also show that the likelihood of quitting the market for ridesharing drivers is significantly associated with the income already earned, which provides additional evidence for the behavioral income-targeting model.

Past research has not directly compared elasticities of labor supply for ridesharing and taxi drivers using data from the same city and the same period, which leads to our second research question: (2) What is the difference between the labor supply elasticity for ridesharing drivers and for taxi drivers? As mentioned earlier, the literature has not converged on the labor supply elasticity for taxi drivers. Using our IV, which is based on uneven driving restrictions, we find that taxi drivers’ labor supply elasticity is positive. In other words, taxi drivers are more rational. It confirms our proposition that IT-enabled features lead ridesharing drivers to be more affected by narrowing framing, which is an important prerequisite of the income targeting model (Kahneman 2003). To gain a deeper understanding of driver heterogeneity in labor supply elasticity, we ask our third research question: (3) How does labor supply elasticity vary with ridesharing drivers’ characteristics and driving patterns? The rational life cycle model and the behavioral income-targeting model predict opposing signs of labor supply elasticity. Both cases are theoretically plausible and have gained support from empirical studies in different contexts. We suspect that driver heterogeneity plays an important role: Different types of drivers may exhibit different behaviors. Our empirical results provide a more complete picture of labor supply elasticity in ridesharing markets by reconciling these two different models in a unified framework: We find that the labor supply elasticity is less negative for more experienced and engaged ridesharing drivers. For some experienced and engaged ridesharing drivers, the labor supply elasticity could be positive. Also, in terms of driving patterns, labor elasticity is less negative for pattern drivers who regularly work at certain times during a week than it is for random drivers. Overall, our findings bridge the two perspectives in prior studies and suggest that ridesharing drivers who are new to the market are more likely to have reference-dependent preferences and to set a behavioral income target. However, setting income targets is inefficient because these drivers are systematically leaving “money on the table”: They work less at high-wage times and work more at low-wage times. We find that the behavior of more experienced ridesharing drivers is more consistent with the rational life cycle model, possibly because of repeated opportunities for learning.

## Literature Review and Conceptual Development

Our paper contributes to the gig economy literature. Specifically, our paper relates to labor supply elasticity in the gig economy, as well as in other industries with a flexible working schedule. In addition, our findings help platform owners better design incentive mechanisms to improve market efficiency. In this section, we summarize previous studies on the gig economy and compare our paper with them to emphasize our contributions. Then, we provide a conceptual framework to highlight the IT artifact in our study.

## The Gig Economy

The gig economy, which relies on digital marketplaces and IT-related technology, is different from traditional industries, and researchers are interested in how to improve the gig economy’s market efficiency. Using data from Airbnb, Fradkin (2015) finds that search frictions still exist in the gig economy and that a personalized ranking algorithm may increase the matching rate by up to 10%. Similarly, Mo et al. (2018) develop a framework to recommend tasks to potential solvers on crowdsourcing platforms and prove that such a system improves solvers’ success rates. Information asymmetry is another potential issue in the market. Horton (2019) investigates how a signaling feature would effectively increase market surplus by driving work invitations to high capacity workers.

Furthermore, studies have shown that other designs on a platform, such as reputation systems (Lin et al. 2018) and reviews (Cui et al. 2020), can alleviate information asymmetry and discrimination in the gig economy. In online labor markets, Liang et al. (2016) explore how the implementation of IT-enabled monitoring systems for time-based projects can mitigate moral hazard. Using data from crowdsourcing contests, Jian et al. (2018) find that the use of prize guarantees and in-process feedback attract more submissions. In a ridesharing platform, Guda and Subramanlan (2019) find that using surge pricing strategy in zones where supply exceeds demand can increase total platform profit across zones. Mejia and Parker (2021) find that removing riders’ gender and race information may reduce bias at the ride request stage, but racial bias and lesbian, gay, bisexual, and transgender (LGBT) bias are still persistent after acceptance based on the canceling information through a large field experiment.

Another popular stream of studies in the gig economy is its socioeconomic impacts. Theoretically, Benjaafar et al. (2019) build up an equilibrium model to see how the existence of the sharing economy influences ownership and usage levels among consumers. Based on Uber’s entry time and urban mobility data, Li et al. (2021) empirically find that Uber’s entry significantly reduces traffic congestion and its related costs in urban areas of the United States. Related to this study, Babar and Burtch (2020) demonstrate the heterogeneous treatment of] the ridesharing services on demand for different public transit services. In addition, the empirical results of Greenwood and Wattal (2017) show that the entry of ridesharing services can significantly reduce the rate of alcohol-related motor vehicle fatalities. Other papers explore the relationship between ridesharing services and other business activities. Zervas et al. (2017) show a negative causal effect of Airbnb entry on incumbent hotels’ revenue, and the effect is larger for lower-priced hotels. Similarly, Burtch et al. (2018) demonstrate a negative and significant relationship between Uber entry and entrepreneurial activities, especially for those of lower quality. Our study finds that the labor supply in ridesharing markets tends to be reference dependent, which is critical for understanding the economic efficiency of various surge pricing algorithms in ridesharing markets.

## Labor Supply Elasticity in Industries with Flexible Nature of Work

This study explores drivers’ labor supply decisions both in the taxi industry and in the emerging gig economy. Compared with the taxi industry, with its traditional employment relationships, understanding labor supply decisions in the gig economy is probably more important because it has significant implications for the market’s long-term success. The advantage of the gig economy is that workers, with the help of IT technology, can freely adjust their working schedules; however, the workers also might suffer from a self-control issue because they may be tempted to quit earlier. Income targeting can serve as a commitment device. Therefore, a number of studies examine the reference-dependent prefer ences model in industries where the nature of the work is flexible, and the studies find evidence in support of this model. Fehr and Goette (2007) conduct field experiments in a setting where workers were free to choose hours worked and effort per hour (i.e., a bicycle messenger service). They find experimental evidence favoring the model with referencedependent preferences over the rational life cycle model. Using pear packers’ labor supply data in response to various wage shocks, Chang and Gross (2014) find strong evidence for reference-dependent preferences in the labor supply of a large pear-packing factory. With the help of constructed cash needs and randomized cash payouts, Dupas et al. (2016) show that bike taxi drivers in western Kenya have referencedependent preferences around a daily earned income target. Hammarlund (2018) studies the labor supply of Swedish Baltic cod fishermen and finds support for referencedependent preferences. Our paper contributes to this stream of literature and highlights the role of IT-enabled features in reference-dependent preferences. We find that IT-enabled features lead ridesharing drivers to be more affected by reference-dependent preferences than taxi drivers.

## Other Factors Influencing Labor Supply in the Gig Economy

In addition to wages, other factors may also affect drivers labor supply decisions in the gig economy, such as motivations, flexible schedules, compensation arrangements, and alternative options. Alam and Campbell (2017) find that personal, collective, and external factors all motivate volunteers to contribute to online projects without monetary compensation, and the effect of these factors evolves over time. By estimating drivers’ reservation wages, Chen et al. (2019) estimate the surplus of Uber drivers generated from work flexibility. They show that, compared with the traditional industry, Uber drivers’ surplus is more than twice that of taxi drivers. Using a randomized field experiment, Angrist et al. (2017) examine the economic value of ridesharing work opportunities for drivers by focusing on differences in the compensation arrangements available to traditional taxi and ridesharing drivers. Huang et al. (2020) establish a significant positive association between offline market unemployment and online labor supply in the same county. Our paper focuses on estimating the labor supply elasticity and the heterogeneous effects of drivers’ characteristics and driving patterns on the labor supply elasticity in ridesharing markets.

## Individual Driver Behavior

Our paper investigates the difference between taxi and ridesharing drivers’ labor supply decisions. Prior literature on this topic has primarily focused on estimating taxi drivers’ labor supply elasticity, and there is no clear conclusion in these studies (Camerer et al. 1997; Crawford and Meng 2011; Farber 2005, 2008, 2015). There are primarily two models to explain drivers’ labor supply decisions: the rational life cycle model and the behavioral income-targeting model based on the theory of reference-dependent preferences. The former model assumes that individuals select a combination of work and leisure hours that optimizes their utility (Farber 2015). With this assumption, a taxi driver will work for a longer period during days with a higher hourly wage. The theory of reference-dependent preferences, rooted in “loss aversion” (Ordóñez et al. 2009; Tversky and Kahneman 1991), assumes that drivers set a daily target level. A driver’s marginal utility of income is significantly lower when the income level is greater than the target level (Farber 2008). As a result, a driver’s working hours will be shorter during days with higher hourly income. In particular, Camerer et al. (1997) find significant negative elasticities of cabdrivers using three samples of trip sheet data from New York City. However, in Crawford and Meng (2011) and Farber (2015), the researchers find substantial heterogeneity and uncertainty in drivers’ labor elasticities using a larger sample of New York City taxi drivers and the additional discrete choice model. More recently, researchers have empirically explored Uber drivers’ labor supply decisions and find a positive labor elasticity (Angrist et al. 2017; Chen and Sheldon 2016; Sheldon 2016).

Overall, the results seem to support the neoclassical model of labor supply more. In our study, we also empirically test which model fits our data better in terms of both taxi drivers and ridesharing drivers, and we find negative labor supply elasticity only for ridesharing drivers.

## Conceptual Development and the IT Artifact

The identity of information systems (IS) research has been tightly bound with the notion of the IT artifact, which is conceptualized as “the application of IT to enable or support some task(s) embedded within a structure(s) that itself is embedded within a context(s)” (Benbasat and Zmud 2003, p. 186). Our key research question of labor supply is influenced by the IT artifact: mobile IT and mobile apps. Specifically, the ridesharing platform, which is supported by mobile IT, provides drivers an opportunity to make flexible labor supply decisions. Zhang et al. (2011) classify different types of core IT artifacts in IS studies, and one of them is mobile IT and mobile apps. Orlikowski and Iacono (2001) develop a conceptual framework showing different roles for IT artifacts conceptualized by IS scholars. According to their framework, the main role of our IT artifact (mobile IT and mobile apps) is an information processing tool: IT as managing, storing, and/or controlling the flow of information, and access to it. Specifically, the essential feature of the gig economy is that mobile IT allows users to share and process information about their unused capacity in a more flexible way to improve social efficiency. Our work shows that mobile IT in the gig economy provides more flexible work opportunities to a different group of workers, who may not be willing to provide their labor supply in traditional markets.

The difference in labor supply elasticities for ridesharing and taxi drivers critically depends on our IT artifact (real-time ridesharing mobile applications). There are two necessary conditions for income targeting:

(1) Reference-dependent preferences (loss aversion): There is a kink in an individual’s utility curve around a contextual reference point, where losses relative to this point are weighted more heavily than gains.

(2) Narrow framing (Kahneman 2003): Drivers consider a sequence of decisions (labor supply decisions on different days/sessions) separately rather than as a portfolio. Essentially, drivers intuitively simplify their labor supply decisions by isolating them from the entire stream of decisions in which they are embedded. Kahneman argues that narrow framing occurs when decisions are made intuitively, rather than through effortful reasoning. Since intuitive thoughts are by nature spontaneous, they are heavily shaped by the features of the situation at hand that come to mind most easily. In deciding how long to work in a given day/session, drivers fall into a cognitive trap of narrowly thinking about their earnings one day/ session at a time, which leads them to make the mistake of driving less when the wage is high. The IT-enabled features lead ridesharing drivers to be more affected by narrow framing for the following two reasons.

Extensive Margin (how many people work): Real-time ridesharing mobile applications lower the barriers to entry of providing rides, allowing inexperienced drivers to enter the market. Taxi drivers, who are professional drivers, tend to have more experience providing rides and are more motivated to maximize their life-cycle income and to make more rational decisions because this work is their only or primary income. In contrast, many ridesharing drivers are part-time drivers and focus on earning pin money.

Intensive Margin (how much time people work on average): Real-time ridesharing mobile applications offer drivers flexibility: Drivers can enter and exit labor markets freely and flexibly. Compared with taxi drivers, ridesharing drivers make labor supply decisions more frequently in a narrower framework: Using mobile apps, they can more easily keep track of their earnings at an hourly level (or across several hours, which is defined as a driving session) rather than at a daily level. Therefore, ridesharing drivers are more affected by narrow framing, which is an unintended consequence of work flexibility caused by IT-enabled mobile applications.

Our study contributes to the existing IS literature by revealing that mobile IT (the IT artifact) is a double-edged sword (more flexible working schedules versus less rational labor supply behaviors) in the gig economy. In other words, our research highlights the unintended consequences of mobile IT in the gig economy labor supply: Mobile IT may lead gig economy workers to make less rational labor supply decisions and to be more affected by behavioral income targeting.

## Data

In this section, we explain our use of data on trip information of Beijing taxi and ridesharing drivers to explore the relationship between hours that drivers choose to work each day and the average daily wage. Overall, our data include 20,286 taxi drivers’ and 2,941 ridesharing drivers’ trip information within one month.

For ridesharing drivers, we obtained a novel panel dataset from one of the major ridesharing companies headquartered in China. The ridesharing service was launched in China in 2015. Like Uber and Lyft, the platform matches consumers and private drivers based on its dispatch system and route planning, allowing individuals to provide customized transportation services using their own cars. In 2016, the company had more than 15 million registered drivers. One of the main motivations for drivers to join the platform is to make money on the side to subsidize household income. Therefore, we expect these private drivers’ labor supply to be more flexible. Specifically, our data cover drivers’ join date, car information, and daily trip information. Because drivers need to log on to the mobile application to receive an order from customers on the platform, we use the period that each driver has been logged on as a measure of this driver’s labor supply. Drivers’ total revenue per day was calculated by adding up the fares from all the trips a driver delivered. The average hourly wage was calculated by dividing total revenue by hours worked.

In addition, we also constructed the average speed of all trips each ridesharing driver had in one day or one session. We collected an approximate distance value for each recorded trip based on the Baidu Maps APIs. Baidu Maps is the most widely accepted map service in minland China; it provides developers service to calculate the distance and time of the route in batches based on the geographic coordinates of origins and destinations.<sup>6</sup> It is common for taxi drivers or ridesharing drivers to follow the guidance of Baidu Maps to deliver their customers to destinations. We calculated the ratio between the retrieved distance for each trip and its real travel time as the inferred “speed,” which can be regarded as a measure of road congestion conditions. A larger value in this measure indicates less traffic on the road for a specific trip.

Regarding taxi drivers, there were about 66,600 cabs in Beijing by the year 2015, and the number had not changed significantly in the previous 10 years.<sup>7</sup> There are two types of taxi drivers in Beijing at present: lease-drivers and ownerdrivers. As only about 1,000 of them are owner-drivers in 2011,<sup>8</sup> the majority of taxi drivers (more than 98%) need to pay their taxi company a deposit at the beginning of the contract, which is about half of the car’s market value. In addition, these drivers also need to pay their taxi company a fixed administrative fee. Taxi drivers tend to have more driving experience, and their labor supply is less flexible because this is their full-time job. During our analysis period, a GPS had been installed in a random sample of taxis in Beijing, and it recorded the focal taxi’s status every 30 to 60 seconds. Each record includes the following information: the latitude and longitude coordinates, whether a taxi car is occupied or not, and the real-time speed. Note that with the permission of these taxi drivers, the data were collected by an agency that allowed the data to be used in the authors’ scientific research. The data have been anonymized and contain no personal information. The same taxi data in other periods have been used in other papers (Yuan et al. 2011; Zhang and Wang 2016).

To generate taxi drivers’ labor supply data, we have gone through similar procedures as in Zhang and Wang (2016) to estimate the number of trips, trip distance, and daily income of each driver. First, based on the information about whether a taxi is occupied or not, we were able to infer the number of trips each taxi has in a day and the time duration of each trip. In addition, using one taxi’s driving track, we calculated each trip’s distance. In Beijing, the taxi service has a fixed-price rule.<sup>9</sup> Specifically, for a trip within 3,000 meters, the fixed price is 13 RMB. Thereafter, the price ratio is 2.76 RMB per kilometer for midnight trips (from 11:00 p.m. to 5:00 a.m.) and 2.3 RMB per kilometer for other trips.<sup>10</sup> Hence, we were able to accurately approximate the daily income of each taxi using the price rule and the distance of each trip. Finally, we constructed the speed information for each trip based on the ratio of each trip’s distance and time duration.

To better characterize ridesharing drivers’ driving patterns, we generated a mobility variable based on the radius of gyra tion, indicating whether the activity space of the vehicle is large or not. The radius of gyration has been widely used in geographical literature to represent the spatial dispersion of an individual’s daily activities (Gonzalez et al. 2008). Specifically, we collected geographic coordinates of all trips’ points of departure and destination. We then obtained the geometric center (centroid) by calculating the arithmetic means of all latitude and longitude coordinates. The mobility measure for driver i is created using the following formula:

$$
\text { mobility } _ {i} = \frac {1}{N _ {i}} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N _ {i}} \sqrt [ 2 ]{\left(\text { longitude } _ {i n} - c \_ l o n g _ {i}\right) ^ {2} + \left(\text { latitude } _ {i n} - c \_ l a t _ {i}\right) ^ {2}}
$$

where c\_long and c\_lat are the coordinates of the geometric center and $N _ { i }$ is the number of trips per driver in our dataset.

Finally, we collected Beijing’s daily weather and air pollution information during our analysis period, including high and low temperatures, wind speed, precipitation, visibility, air quality index (AQI), and PM 2.5.<sup>11</sup>

One important caveat of our data set is that taxi drivers may use the ridesharing app at the same time. To exclude the potential confounding effect, we try to identify potential taxi drivers in the ridesharing data. To alleviate both air pollution and travel congestion, the Beijing municipal government started implementing driving restrictions within the 5th Ring Road (excluding the 5th Ring Road) one weekday per week from 7 a.m. to 8 p.m. Specifically, vehicles are categorized into five groups based on the last digit of the license plate: 0&5, 1&6, 2&7, 3&8, and 4&9. Each group is assigned to one weekday every 13 weeks for the driving restriction. However, restrictions do not apply to taxies. As a result, we could examine each ridesharing drivers’ order data to see if any of them have orders on each weekday during the restricted time. Overall, we found 890 potential taxi drivers among the ridesharing drivers, and we dropped their order data in our main analysis.

The summary statistics of the key variables are presented in Table 1. Drawing on the summary statistics, we find some interesting patterns. First, taxi drivers tend to drive 8.50 hours per day on average, which is higher than the 5.40 hours per day for ridesharing drivers. In addition, taxi drivers have more trips per day and certainly higher daily income. These findings are in line with our expectations because many ridesharing drivers have other full-time jobs, and ridesharing is their way to earn pin money. For the weather information, most of the 32 days in our data set did not have precipitation. However, there were multiple days with severe air pollution. Actually, Beijing issued the first-ever pollution “red alert” in December 2015.<sup>12</sup> AQI is a weighted average of the concentrations of five major pollutants: $\mathrm { O } _ { 3 } , \mathrm { N O } _ { 2 } , \mathrm { S O } _ { 2 } ,$ PM 2.5 (particles with an aerodynamic diameter less than 2.5 ìm), and PM 10 (particles with an aerodynamic diameter less than 10 ìm). AQI ranges from 0 to 500, with a higher value indicating a greater level of air pollution.

We first use the raw data to explore the relationship between drivers’ hours worked and hourly wage. For each of the two data sets, we calculate the simple correlation between (log) hours and (log) hourly wages. The correlation for taxi drivers is 0.1061 and for ridesharing drivers is -0.1446. We observe different behavioral patterns among taxi and ridesharing drivers using the raw data set.

## Empirical Model and Results

There are two empirical challenges in identifying the true elasticity of labor supply for drivers. First, the results in earlier studies (Camerer et al. 1997) may suffer from measurement errors (Farber 2005, 2008).<sup>13</sup> One advantage of our ridesharing data is that due to the development of IT, the ridesharing mobile application accurately monitors drivers activities in real-time, and the measurement error in recording working hours should be minimal in our context.

Second, a typical way of estimating labor supply elasticity is based on a regression of daily working hours on the average hourly wage (the effect of wages on working hours). However, drivers’ wages are endogenous. When the wage rate of driving is high, it is likely to be on a holiday, when drivers want to enjoy quality family time. Therefore, the usual estimates of labor supply elasticity may be misleading, and we may observe a spuriously negative elasticity: a negative correlation between working hours and wages. In this study, we exploit an exogenous shock from uneven driving restrictions as an innovative IV to address the endogeneity of drivers’ labor supply. The rationale of our IV is based on the exogenous shock from government regulation: Each vehicle is not permitted to drive in Beijing one day a week. Interestingly, the exogenous driving restrictions are not uniform across weekdays, and more vehicles are permitted to drive on one particular weekday. This exogenous variation can be an IV for an hourly wage of drivers who are not restricted on these days because it is negatively correlated with drivers hourly wage: (1) With fewer ridesharing drivers being restricted, the supply in the ridesharing market increases, and (2) with fewer private cars being regulated, people are more likely to use their own cars as a travel option rather than using ridesharing services.

## Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Analysis

We use the following regression to estimate the labor supply function:

$$
Y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \times w a g e _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} X _ {i t} + \theta_ {i} + t i m e _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}
$$

The dependent variable $Y _ { i t }$ in our model is each driver’s natural log number of hours being logged on the system (based on the ridesharing platform or GPS) in a day. The main independent variable of interest is $w a g e _ { i t } ,$ and it is the natural log hourly wage for each driver on a day when he or she chooses to work. $X _ { i t }$ represents other control variables, including drivers’ tenure information (for ridesharing drivers), weather information, and average speed of all trips each driver has in period t (one day). To further control for driverlevel and temporal unobservable characteristics, we include driver fixed effects and day of week fixed effects. The standard errors are corrected to account for driver level clusters. The regression results are reported in Table 2. Overall, based on the estimated coefficients of log hourly wage, we find positive labor supply elasticity for taxi drivers and negative labor supply elasticity for ridesharing drivers.

Specifically, when the hourly wage increases by one percent, the taxi drivers’ working time increases by 0.092 percent while the ridesharing drivers’ working time decreases by 0.159 percent. This means that ridesharing drivers tend to drive less during days with a higher average hourly wage. In other words, income reference-dependent preferences may have been a better theory (Camerer et al. 1997) to understand ridesharing drivers’ daily labor supply decisions.

This finding is different from those in previous studies using Uber data (Angrist et al. 2017; Chen and Sheldon 2016; Sheldon 2016), which show positive labor supply elasticity for Uber drivers. As mentioned earlier, a possible reason is that Uber has already implemented behavioral interventions to correct income-targeting behavior. Another plausible reason is that institutional factors specific to contexts may also influence the elasticity of labor supply. In reality, ridesharing drivers are likely to be affected by both the behavioral income-targeting and the rational life cycle models. The sign of labor supply elasticity depends on which effect dominates: If the behavioral income-targeting effect (rational life cycle effect) dominates, the labor supply elasticity is negative (positive).

An immature financial market in China may cause a weaker rational life cycle effect and lead to the fact that the behavioral income-targeting effect dominates the rational life effect for the following two suggestive reasons. First, because financial markets in China are closer to state-owned enterprises than to serving individual borrowers (Huang et al. 2017; Song et al. 2011), it is much harder for drivers in China to obtain personal loans from banks or other financial channels with acceptable interest rates. As a result, individual ridesharing drivers have more difficulty rationally allocating their working time throughout their life to maximize their utility. Essentially, the rational life cycle model is about the trade-off between work and leisure. With a mature financial market, rational workers can smooth their working and leisure time throughout their life cycle (Aiyagari 1994; Marcet et al. 2007). In other words, financial markets in developed countries (e.g., the United States) facilitate labor supply smoothing over time, but the immature financial markets in developing countries (e.g., China) present challenges to the intertemporal substitution that is central to the rational life cycle model of labor supply. Therefore, workers in China are less affected by the rational life cycle model, which predicts a positive shortterm wage elasticity.

Second, the overall labor supply elasticity is a weighted average of the elasticity of workers who follow the rational life cycle model (the sign is positive) and that of workers who follow the behavioral income-targeting model (the sign is negative). For workers who follow the rational life cycle model, the labor supply elasticity of those (who are more riskaverse) in developing countries is smaller than that of those in developed countries (Chetty 2006). It means that the rational life cycle effect is weaker (i.e., the supply elasticity is smaller) for these workers in China. For the workers who follow the behavioral income-targeting model, the labor supply elasticity is likely to be the same for those in developed countries and developing countries, as no prior theory or empirical studies have demonstrated that their labor supply elasticity would be affected by the degree of risk-averse. Therefore, the overall labor supply elasticity may be more likely to be negative in China.

We also have interesting findings for other control variables. For weather variables, the results show that rain will decrease drivers’ daily driving time. This finding can be explained by the fact that rain may decrease drivers’ enjoyment of their driving experience and increase the possibility of traffic accidents, which is also reported in the existing literature (Farber 2015). In addition, in more severe weather conditions (e.g., high wind speeds, low temperatures), the length of time that taxi or ridesharing drivers provide service for customers tends to be longer. Severe weather conditions may drive more people to choose cabs or ridesharing car services rather than alternative transportation methods, such as walking and subways.

## Instrumental Variables

One main concern of the basic empirical results is the potential endogeneity problem of the log hourly wage. There may be measurement errors, such as drivers forgetting to log out of their mobile applications at the end of their working session. It is also possible that other unobservable variables or events are influencing both the hours drivers work and the average hourly wage. This challenge could be alleviated by using IVs that are correlated with hourly wage but not directly related to the decision of labor supply. In this study, we adopt two sets of IVs: (1) summary statistics of other drivers’ hourly wage distribution, and (2) uneven driving restrictions.

## Instrumental Variable I: Summary Statistics of Other Drivers’ Hourly Wage Distribution

One widely accepted set of IVs in the literature is summary statistics of other drivers’ hourly wage distribution on the same day (Camerer et al. 1997; Farber 2015). Specifically, we use the mean and the 75<sup>th</sup> percentile of hourly wage distribution of other drivers on the same day as our IVs. On one hand, these IVs should be correlated with one individual’s average hourly wage on the same day since many environmental variables tend to affect all drivers on the road. On the other hand, a focal driver is unlikely to make the labor supply decision directly based on other drivers’ income. We have further controlled for other variables that we think would potentially affect both the dependent variable and the IVs to exclude potential endogeneity issues, such as weather conditions, road congestion conditions, and day of the week.

With the help of IVs, we use the two-stage least squared method (2SLS) to estimate the labor supply function, and the results are reported in Table 3. We can see that the first-stage F statistics for both regressions are above 30, indicating that our IVs are not weak. In addition, we conduct Sargan– Hansen tests because the number of IVs is larger than the number of endogenous variables. The Sargan–Hansen statis tics are 0.887 with a p-value of 0.3464 for taxi drivers and 0.532 with a p-value of 0.4658 for ridesharing drivers. In both cases, we cannot reject the over-identification restriction. The results are quite interesting. For taxi drivers, the coefficient of log hourly wage is significantly positive. When taxi drivers’ hourly wage increases by one percent, the driving hours will increase by 0.670 percent. This result means that taxi drivers are making labor supply decisions based on the rational life cycle model. In contrast, for ridesharing drivers, we still find significant negative labor supply elasticity: A one percent increase in hourly wage leads to a 0.374 percent decrease in labor supply. The observed difference in the behavior of taxi drivers and ridesharing drivers can be explained by the following reasons: Taxi drivers tend to have more experience in providing rides and are more motivated to maximize their income because taxi driving is their only or main income. In contrast, many ridesharing drivers are parttime drivers and focus on earning pin money. As a result, compared with ridesharing drivers, taxi drivers can make more rational labor supply decisions. Ridesharing drivers, meanwhile, are more likely to have reference-dependent preferences: They have a daily income target, and an increase in hourly wage makes them reach their targets sooner and quit driving for the day (Camerer et al. 1997). The results also echo the findings of existing studies on other industries tha allow for flexible work (Chang and Gross 2014; Fehr and Goette 2007; Hammarlund 2018).

This exogenous variation can be an IV for drivers’ hourly wage from both supply and demand perspectives: (1) With fewer ridesharing drivers being restricted, there are more ridesharing service providers available, leading to an increase in market supply; and (2) with fewer private cars being regulated, people who own cars are more likely to use their own cars for traveling, resulting in a demand reduction. Combining both the supply and demand effects, we expect log hourly wage to be lower on Mondays during this period, which is validated by our data analysis in Columns 1 and 3 of Table 4. Therefore, we use the weekday when 4&9 is restricted as an alternative IV.

Our results with the alternative IV are reported in Table 4, which are qualitatively equivalent to our main results. Note that we control for the weekend dummies instead of the day of week dummies in this IV analysis. If we control for weekday dummies in our 2SLS daily level analysis, the IV will be absorbed in the analysis (i.e., the IV is perfectly correlated with the weekday dummies). We also address the concern that the IVs may affect the decision to supply through expectation below.

## Session Level Analysis

One concern of using each driver’s daily working hours and income as an analysis unit is that a typical workday may not correctly represent drivers’ supply choice. Since there are no organizational constraints on working hours for the gig economy, ridesharing drivers have the flexibility to choose when to start and stop driving (Chen et al. 2019). For example, one driver may choose to work from 10 p.m. to 2 a.m. because of the high demand among people who leave nightclubs downtown (Chen and Sheldon 2016). Similarly, a driver may serve as a ridesharing driver twice a day—for example, during the morning and evening peaks. Please note that using hourly data may not be a good choice because ridesharing drivers typically do not decide whether to quit driving every hour. Our data show that ridesharing drivers generally do not start and end a working period many times throughout the day. As a result, to precisely estimate the labor supply elasticity, we complement the analysis with daily sessions, following a modified approach used in Chen and Sheldon.

We define a session as a cluster of trips that take place without a break of more than four hours.<sup>15</sup> The “length” of a session is defined as the time interval between the start time of the first trip and the end time of the last trip. And the income of a session is calculated by the sum of all trips within the session. Table 5 shows the summary statistics of sessionlevel variables.<sup>16</sup> Similar to the daily data analysis, for the IVs of each session, we use the distribution of hourly wage for trips that happened in the session from other drivers and whether the session happened during the restriction time on Monday. The results using session data are reported in Table 6, which are qualitatively consistent with our main findings: The estimated coefficient of the log hourly wage is negative and significant. Specifically, a percent increase in hourly wage results in a 0.931 percent decrease in driving time. The magnitude of the estimator is larger, indicating that when we look at a shorter timeframe, we are more likely to observe ridesharing drivers’ income targeting behavior, which is consistent with our expectation and further confirms our conclusion that a flexible working schedule supported by mobile IT in the gig economy may be a reason for the observed different labor supply decisions between taxi and ridesharing drivers.

## Nonlinear Setting

Based on our main linear regression and 2SLS analysis, we find that ridesharing drivers are more likely to have referencedependent preferences. In other words, drivers may have a daily target, and they have less motivation to drive after they have reached their daily targets. Therefore, we include both the linear and squared terms of log hourly wage in the following model:

$$
\begin{array}{c} Y _ {i t} = \alpha_ {2} + \alpha_ {1} \times w a g e _ {i t} + \alpha_ {2} \times \left(w a g e _ {i t}\right) ^ {2} + \\ \alpha_ {3} X _ {i t} + \theta_ {i} + t i m e _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}
$$

If drivers do have reference-dependent preferences, we would expect the coefficient of the linear term to be positive and the coefficient of the squared term to be negative (after a threshold, a wage increase may lead to a decrease in driving time). The results are reported in Table 7. We visually show the relationship between log hourly wage and log driving time using the 2SLS models in Figure 1, where the X-axis represents the different values of log hourly wage in the data set (wage ), and the Y-axis represents the overall effect of log hourly wage on the driving time, controlling for other variables. The figures show that the relationship between wage and driving time is an inverted-U curve. In particular, we find that (1) when the log hourly wage is smaller than a threshold value (3.5), an increase in hourly wage would lead to increased driving time; and (2) when the log hourly wage is larger than the threshold, an increase in hourly wage would lead to a decrease in driving time. Because we use the natural logarithm to transfer both the dependent variable and the independent variable, the threshold for the hourly wage should be 33.12 Chinese Yuan per hour $( e ^ { 3 . 5 } \ = \ 3 3 . 1 2 )$ Therefore, a professional ridesharing driver who works eight hours a day and five days a week earns about 5,300 (= 33.12 $\times 8 \times 5 \times 4 )$ Chinese Yuan a month in Beijing. The average disposable income of people who live in Beijing is $4 { , } 4 0 4 { . } 9$ Chinese Yuan in 2015.<sup>17</sup> Thus, we believe that the estimated hourly wage threshold is as expected.

Specifically, drivers’ behaviors can be classified into two cases. In case (1), the hourly wage is low, and drivers likely have not reached their income targets. Therefore, drivers tend to respond to the monetary incentives and drive longer when the wage is higher. In other words, the classical (fully rational) life cycle model applies in this case. In case (2), the hourly wage is high, and drivers are more likely to be closer to their income targets or to have reached them. Therefore, drivers tend to drive less when the wage is higher due to the reference-dependent preferences. In other words, the behavioral income-targeting model applies in this case. In summary, we find that the effect of wage on drivers’ labor supply is contingent on the level of wage (which is closely related to income targets), suggesting that the behavioral incometargeting model is likely to be a plausible explanation.

## When to Stop Driving

Another analysis that can be complementary to the existing ones is to estimate a driver’s decision to continue or stop driving after a trip within a session. If a driver has a reference-dependent preferences, he or she is more likely to end the session when the existing cumulative hourly wage is higher.

To verify this, we use both the conditional logit model and Cox survival model to analyze the data. For the conditional logit model, we use a dummy variable to indicate whether the trip is the last one within a session. For the Cox survival model, we use the time until a driver ends the driving session as the duration. The results in Columns 1 and 3 of Table 8 show that when the cumulative hourly wage is higher, drivers are more likely to end a driving session. This empirical result is in support of the reference-dependent preferences model. We also include the last trip’s wage to see how it influences drivers’ decisions in the analysis, and the results are reported in Columns 2 and 4 of Table 8. The estimated coefficients of the cumulative hourly wage are still positive and significant.

## Moderating Effects of Drivers’ Characteristics and Driving Patterns

There are multiple potential reasons that explain the observed difference in labor supply elasticities between taxi drivers and ridesharing drivers. First, taxi drivers and ridesharing drivers are different types of people who have different motivations. Taxi drivers generally regard providing taxi service as their main income source, while many ridesharing drivers earn only pin money from the platform. Hence, taxi drivers are more likely to maximize their life-cycle income. Ridesharing drivers, in contrast, may not perfectly predict their labor supply decisions in the future. Second, taxi drivers, who are professional ride service providers, have more experience in how to optimize their income in the market (Zheng et al. 2020). To further validate our proposed reasons, we investigate whether ridesharing drivers with different characteristics on this platform behave differently, especially for the engagement measures. Specifically, following the two proposed reasons, we expect ridesharing drivers who are more engaged and more experienced on the platform to have similar labor supply preferences as taxi drivers.

Specifically, we have different measures of ridesharing drivers’ engagement levels: online time, earned income, and the vehicle’s mobility space. The three engagement measures represent different perspectives of drivers’ usage patterns. Drivers who spend more time on the platform are frequent users of the app, but they may not necessarily be the ones who provide the most rides. Drivers who have earned more income by providing more services have more experience on the platform. Similarly, drivers whose geographic mobile space is larger are more likely to be professional ridesharing drivers, compared with regional drivers restricted to local areas. We created engagement dummies based on whether drivers’ total online time, total earned revenue, or mobile space in this one month is larger than the median values among all ridesharing drivers. Then we include the interaction between log hourly wage and the corresponding engagement dummy in our baseline model. The results reported in the first three columns of Table 9 show that the coefficients of the log hourly wage are significantly negative, and the magnitudes are larger than those in Table 2. The coefficients of the three interactions are positive and statistically significant. These findings indicate that, compared with irregular, lower-earning, and regional ridesharing drivers, the labor supply elasticity is less negative for drivers who regularly participate in providing ride service, who earn more income, and who have a larger activity space. In fact, the estimated labor supply elasticity for highly engaged drivers is not statistically different from 0. In other words, ridesharing drivers who are more experienced and professional are more rational and less likely to have reference-dependent preferences to leave “money on the table.” In addition, it aligns with our conclusion that taxi drivers tend to have positive labor supply elasticities because they are professional drivers with more experience.

In addition to the drivers’ engagement level, we also explore other driver characteristics. Based on the ridesharing company’s business report, more than 30% of the drivers believe that this platform is an excellent opportunity for them to earn pin money to subsidize their main jobs. At the same time, more than 25% of the drivers worked full time on the platform in 2016. Drivers with different driving patterns may also have different preferences: Drivers who regard ridesharing as their main income source and regularly work on the platform may maximize their income from a multi-period optimization problem. These drivers may choose to lengthen working hours in the current period when the current wage increases (Farber 2015): Drivers who randomly use this platform to earn pin money are more likely to have an income target for a particular day or session. These drivers may choose to quit driving early when the hourly wage is higher. Hence, we apply a k-means cluster analysis of the hours worked by drivers (Chen et al. 2019), attempting to classify drivers based on their driving patterns. Specifically, we constructed a 168- degree vector for each driver, with each component representing the number of minutes the driver was active during each one hour of the week. We then used a k-means clustering analysis for all the ridesharing drivers in our data set and set the number of clusters at 3, based on the elbow method.<sup>18</sup> We ran the k-means clustering using k from 1 to 20, respectively, and for each value of k, we calculated the sum of squared errors (SSE). All the SSEs are reported in the line chart in Figure 2. We can see that 3 is the “elbow” on the arm. This indicates that adding another cluster after 3 does not improve the modeling of the data.

Based on the presented drivers’ working patterns within a week, we can put all drivers into two groups: random drivers and pattern drivers. For drivers in cluster 1, the average working time is significantly less than other drivers in clusters 2 and 3. Also, compared with drivers in the other two clusters, those in cluster 1 do not have a clear pattern for the average fraction of drivers being active in each hour of the week. As a result, we label the drivers in clusters 2 and 3 as pattern drivers (Pattern driver = 1) and drivers in cluster 1 as random drivers (Pattern driver = 0). We expect to see the coefficient of the interaction between the log hourly wage and pattern driver dummy to be positive. The results are reported in Column 4 of Table 9, which are consistent with our expectations.

Another driver feature is the type of registered automobile on the platform. In our data set, drivers’ registered vehicles range from low-end economy cars to luxury SUVs. Drivers with luxury cars or economy cars have different motivations on the platform. As the operating cost to provide ride service is much higher for luxury vehicles, full-time drivers are less likely to use luxury vehicles on the platform. Therefore, drivers with more expensive cars are more likely to have reference-dependent preferences as they focus mainly on earning pin money. Based on the car model information in our original data set, we collected the Manufacturer’s Suggested Retail Price (MSRP) of each vehicle online. We define a vehicle as a luxury one if the MSRP is higher than the median value among all ridesharing drivers.<sup>19</sup> The estimated coefficient of the interaction between log hourly wage and luxury vehicle dummy are reported in Column 5 of Table 9. As expected, drivers with luxury vehicles are more likely to have reference-dependent preferences.

So far, we have used OLS to explore the heterogeneity among drivers’ labor supply elasticity. Considering the endogeneity issue, we apply the same IVs discussed above and their corresponding interactions in the 2SLS. Our estimated results are shown in Columns 6 through 10 of Table 9. Overall, the results are consistent with linear regressions. The labor supply elasticity for more engaged ridesharing drivers is significantly less negative, based on their online time, earned revenue, and mobility space. In contrast, amateur drivers’ labor supply elasticity can be as negative as -1.619 (see Column 7 in Table 9), indicating that they have stronger reference-dependent preferences and are prone to work less during days with higher hourly wages: A percent increase in hourly wage leads to a 1.619 percent decrease in labor supply. In our main analysis, we investigate the labor supply elasticity using both daily data and session-level data. Similarly, we explore how drivers’ labor supply elasticities vary with different characteristics using session-level data with both linear regressions and 2SLS. The corresponding results are shown in Table 10. For the results using 2SLS, all the interactions coefficients have the expected signs. In addition, we explore how drivers’ characteristics influence their decisions to stop a session, and the consistent results are reported in Table 11. These estimators provide evidence that our results are robust to various specifications. To provide a more in-depth discussion about how the platform can derive drivers’ labor supply elasticity based on their characteristics, we have built up a regression that includes all factors and the corresponding interactions, which we have analyzed in the main paper. The results are mostly consistent and reported in Appendix E.

As a summary, in this and the previous subsection, we dug deeper into our data and used the approach of “coherent pattern matching” (Shadish et al. 2002) to demonstrate that our results are more likely to be driven by income targeting than by other confounding factors. Shadish et al. (p. 105) point out that “the more complex the pattern that is successfully predicted, the less likely it is that alternative explanations could generate the same pattern, and so the more likely it is that the treatment had a real effect.” Essentially, our income targeting interpretation provides a coherent explanation for the complex pattern of our findings on the decision of when to stop driving (hitting the targets early has a positive and significant impact on the probability of stopping work) and for the moderating roles of drivers’ experience and driving patterns, whereas other confounding factors do not. In particular, many of our findings on moderating effects of drivers’ characteristics are consistent with income targeting but cannot be explained coherently and consistently by other confounding factors. Following the approach of “coherent pattern matching” (Shadish et al. 2002), a coherent pattern of multiple empirical findings (not just a single finding) suggests that our results are less likely to be driven by other confounding factors. In the next four subsections, we explain four robustness checks used to provide empirical evidence to support our conclusions.

## Drivers on the Platform for at Least One Month

In the dataset, a small group of drivers recently joined this platform. These drivers may behave differently from existing ones because the platform has different promotions overtime to attract new drivers. For example, drivers who bought a specific car would receive a ¥10,000 reward after they accepted 10 orders in December 2015.<sup>20</sup> In addition, new drivers have not obtained much experience on the platform, so they may need some time to “learn” the market and to form their own strategy. Hence, to avoid the potential confounding effect, we conduct another robustness check without activity data from drivers who joined the platform after October 2015.

Both OLS and 2SLS results, reported in Table 12, are qualitatively equivalent to those with the full sample. In terms of the magnitudes of the estimated labor supply elasticities, we find that most of them are less negative. This echoes our heterogeneous treatment effect results showing that less engaged drivers are more likely to have reference-dependent preferences. After we removed the least experienced drivers data, we expected the remaining drivers with more engagement on the platform to show less negative labor supply elasticities.

## Excluding Data from Holidays

During holidays and weekends, people may have different travel demands compared with that of a weekday. On one hand, people who used to go to work using a ridesharing service do not need to request a ride on holidays, leading to a reduction on the demand side. On the other hand, as people spend holidays in different places of entertainment, such as parks, cinemas, restaurants, and malls, travel needs may increase as well. Furthermore, on the supply side, drivers may prefer to enjoy the holiday season with their families, hence reducing the labor supply.

During our observation period, there were two main holidays: Christmas Day and New Year’s Day. To exclude the potential shocks of holidays, we include another robustness check using data excluding holidays and weekends. The results, reported in Table 13, are consistent with our main results.

## Analysis Using All Ridesharing Data

In our main analysis, to exclude the confounding effect of taxi drivers who use the ridesharing app at the same time, we identify potential taxi drivers based on the driving restrictions and exclude their data from our analysis. However, it is possible that we accidentally removed a ridesharing driver because of measurement errors. As these excluded drivers tend to be more active on the platform, these exclusions may lead to a potential selection issue. Therefore, we conduct a robustness check with all available ridesharing drivers’ data. The results, reported in Table 14, are qualitatively equivalent to our main findings. As shown in Column 1, the magnitude of the labor supply elasticity is smaller compared with that using the whole sample. It aligns with our finding that more engaged drivers are less likely to have reference-dependent preferences. With more active drivers’ data included in our analysis, the average labor supply elasticity is closer to zero.

## Ruling out Alternative Explanation: Autocorrelation of Income

The main finding of our study is that ridesharing drivers tend to drive less when the wage is high. An alternative explanation for the finding is the negatively autocorrelated intraday hourly wage rates. Even if drivers are perfectly rational, they may quit when the wage is high because of a negative wage autocorrelation: A high current wage suggests a low wage in the next few hours. Following the literature (Camerer et al. 1997; Chen and Shelton 2016), we explored the within-day wage temporal autocorrelation to rule out alternative explanations. We show the autocorrelations between the average (median) hourly wage at hour t and hours t-1, t-2, t-3, and t-4 in Table 15. Following the method in Camerer et al. (1997), we regress the average or median value of the hourly wage at hour t on the corresponding lagged average or median hourly wage. All the correlations are positive and statistically significant. Similarly, we divide a day’s trips into two parts and regressed the second half’s average (median) wage on the corresponding first half’s value.<sup>21</sup> The estimated coefficients are 0.918 for the average value and 1.116 for the median value, respectively. Both are statistically significant. These results indicate that when a driver has a high wage at the beginning of a day, he or she will probably continue to earn more in the rest of the day. In other words, negative wage autocorrelation is unlikely to be an alternative explanation in our context. We provide additional suggestive evidence that favors the behavioral income targeting model in Appendix C. Another concern of our study is that the driving restriction IV may affect the decision to supply through expectations. The logic is that, conditional on the current income, drivers may expect the income to evolve differently on Mondays. To address this concern, we regress median (mean) hourly wage at hour t on median (mean) hourly wage at previous hours and its interaction with the Monday dummy. In Table 16, we find that the coefficients on interaction terms are not statistically significant, suggesting that the autocorrelations between the median (mean) hourly wage at hour t and the median (mean) hourly wage at previous hours on Monday are not different from those on other weekdays. Hence, we believe that drivers are not likely to expect to earn less if they drive one hour longer on Monday, conditional on their current wage.

## Managerial and Policy Implications

The prevalence of the gig economy has dramatically changed the landscape of traditional industries and reshaped the distribution of unused or underused assets through digital platforms (Gong et al. 2018; Hong and Pavlou 2017; Mayya et al. 2021; Sundararajan 2016). Our results have broad managerial and policy implications for platforms, as well as for policymakers. Quantifying labor supply elasticity is vital for ridesharing platforms to design platform strategies (Song et al. 2018), such as price-surging algorithms and driver subsidization programs. A key purpose of price surging in the ridesharing market is to stimulate drivers’ labor supply. However, our findings suggest that a uniform price surging to all drivers may not be effective because the overall labor supply elasticity is negative: When prices surge, some ridesharing drivers reach the income target sooner and are less motivated to drive. Our results remind small and emerging ride-sharing platforms about the possibility of drivers’ income targeting behavior. Although Uber has recruited a large team of economists to understand drivers’ income targeting behavior, many emerging ride-sharing platforms (e.g., Via<sup>22</sup> and Gett<sup>23</sup>) may not have the resource and capability to invest as Uber does.

In addition, we find that labor supply elasticity varies with drivers’ characteristics and driving patterns. More experienced and patterned drivers tend to drive more when the price surges. Specifically, our empirical analyses indicate that drivers’ historical total income, mobility space, driving pattern, and registered car are personal characteristics that the platform may consider to influence the effectiveness of the price surges. Drivers whose earned income is higher, whose mobility space is larger, whose car is cheaper, and who is regularly providing service on the platform are more likely to increase their labor supply when the hourly wage rises (i.e., they are less affected by behavioral income targeting). As a result, when a platform wants to increase drivers’ labor supply, it should target drivers with these characteristics to alleviate drivers’ income targeting behavior. According to conventional wisdom, when the demand is high, the platform may try to motivate drivers who are less active because they have the potential to dramatically increase the labor supply. However, because of the unintended consequence of the IT artifact, incentivizing the labor supply of the less active drivers may not be the optimal approach because they may operate based on behavioral income targeting: They tend to quit the platform earlier when the price is higher because it is easier for them to reach their daily target income.

Our research also has implications for driver subsidization programs of ridesharing platforms. Major ridesharing platforms have announced driver subsidization programs in various formats: (1) extra bonuses if a certain number of rides are completed,<sup>24</sup> and (2) a reduced commission fee for drivers.<sup>25</sup> Both of these subsidization programs may increase drivers’ real income, aiming to encourage drivers to work more by providing monetary incentives. In this way, the platform increases the actual wage for these drivers. However, according to the same logic, a uniform driver subsidization program for all drivers may not be effective because of the negative labor supply elasticity. Our research suggests that a more effective approach is to launch subsidization programs targeting drivers who have more experience and more income, who regularly provide service, and who own a cheaper car on this platform. Note that our suggestions on the subsidization programs are based on the similarity between an increase in the wage of ridesharing drivers and the driver subsidization programs. Therefore, these managerial implications are tentative and suggestive, and we recommend caution in generalizing our findings to driver subsidization programs.

Our finding that labor supply in ridesharing markets could be reference-dependent has significant policy implications. Evaluations of government policy regarding tax and transfer programs in ridesharing markets rely on estimates of the sensitivity of labor supply to wage rates. Specifically, for policymakers, estimating labor supply elasticity is critical to understanding the economic efficiency of various surgepricing algorithms in ridesharing markets. If labor supply elasticity is significantly positive, then surge-pricing algorithms can increase the efficiency of ridesharing markets. However, we find a negative labor supply elasticity, which may significantly reduce the economic gains from a uniform surge-pricing algorithm.

## Discussion and Conclusions

Ridesharing services, such as Uber and Lyft, have substantially reshaped the traditional taxi industry by matching passengers and drivers in real-time. At the same time, the IT artifact, which efficiently matches the supply and demand sides of the ride service, is a double-edged sword in the gig economy. On one hand, it attracts more drivers to the market and provides flexibility on the labor supply side. On the other hand, it may make daily income levels more salient and amplify (irrational) behavioral income-targeting behavior. In the present study, we conduct a comprehensive empirical analysis to understand short-term labor supply elasticity in the new ridesharing services, factors that may moderate the labor supply elasticity, and its comparison with taxi drivers’ labor supply elasticity. Specifically, we take advantage of the accurate data of drivers’ activities recorded by ridesharing mobile applications, as well as policy IVs, to investigate these questions. Overall, we find a negative labor supply elasticity for ridesharing drivers. A percent increase in drivers’ hourly income will lead to a 0.931 percent decrease in the daily working time. We also find a positive association between the likelihood of quitting markets for ridesharing drivers and the income already earned, which suggests a behavioral incometargeting model based on the theory of reference-dependent preferences to be a better explanation for ridesharing drivers daily labor supply decisions.

Another theoretical contribution of our study is the investigation of the boundary conditions of the reference-dependent preferences theory, which enriches our further understanding of the theory. The standard reference-dependent preferences theory has not focused on investigating when the referencedependent preference is more likely to hold (Tversky and Kahneman 1991). Our paper explores this question in the context of the gig economy labor supply. We explore the heterogeneity in labor supply elasticities among drivers with different characteristics and driving patterns. Our empirical results indicate that labor supply elasticity is less negative for more experienced, engaged, regular ridesharing drivers, and for those with a cheaper car. In other words, not all drivers have reference-dependent preferences. The behaviors of more experienced and more engaged ridesharing drivers are consistent with the classical rational life cycle model. In reality, ridesharing drivers are likely to be affected by both the behavioral income-targeting model and the rational life cycle model. The sign of labor supply elasticity depends on which effect dominates: If the behavioral income-targeting effect (rational life cycle effect) dominates, the labor supply elasticity is negative (positive).

Finally, we also show some empirical evidence that taxi drivers are less influenced by the reference-dependent preferences model using data from the same period and city. Our paper makes significant contributions to both the literature and industry. A better understanding of drivers’ labor supply decisions helps the platform design price surging and driver subsidization programs. At the same time, our research should be of interest to policymakers investigating this issue.

It is worth noting that through specific behavior interventions, income targeting is less likely to be observed on Uber. Therefore, Uber may not be an ideal platform to study income targeting. Our study is based on a major ridesharing platform in China, which has not implemented behavioral interventions to address the issue of income targeting. Our research can provide more reliable empirical insights into the debate on the labor supply elasticity of ridesharing drivers.

Our paper has several limitations. First, although the data in this study are novel and from a major ridesharing provider, we only have a random sample of drivers from one city in one month. Hence, we could not observe how drivers’ preferences evolve. Right now, our empirical results show that drivers who are more engaged in the platform tend to be less influenced by the reference-dependent preferences. It would be interesting to investigate further how drivers’ preferences evolve by observing drivers’ activities for a longer period. In addition, it is generally difficult to obtain a causal relationship using observational data. Future research should precisely quantify the causal relationship using field experiments. Last but not least, we do not have each vehicle’s license plate information, and we predict the last digit number of each driver’s license plate by analyzing the trip data. Similarly, we do not observe additional stipends provided by the ridesharing platform through drivers’ subsidization programs.

## Uber’s Behavioral Interventions

According to a New York Times article, “top officials at Uber and Lyft have certainly concluded that many of their drivers set income goals. ‘Others are motivated by an income target for sure,’ said Brian Hsu, the Lyft vice president in charge of supply.”<sup>26</sup> Employing hundreds of social scientists and data scientists, Uber has a long tradition of solving practical problems by using psychological inducements and other techniques of behavioral economics. Uber has alleviated the issue of income targeting by setting new earnings goals: Drivers are alerted that they are close to hitting a new target when they try to log off. Specifically, when Uber drivers try to log off (reach their original income targets), many of them receive a message on the Uber app with the headline “Make it to \$XXX.” The text then explains: “You’re \$XX away from making \$XXX in net earnings. Are you sure you want to go offline?” Below are two prompts: “Go offline” and “Keep driving.” The latter is highlighted (see Figure A1 for an example). Essentially, Uber tries to alleviate the issue of income targeting by setting a new (higher) income target to replace drivers’ original income targets.

Another related behavioral intervention to address the issue of income targeting is that Uber has concocted an algorithm similar to a Netflix feature that automatically loads the next program, which many experts believe encourages binge-watching. In Uber’s case, this means sending drivers their next fare opportunity before their current ride is even over.<sup>27</sup> Both Uber and Lyft have this forward dispatch algorithm that dispatches a new ride to a driver before the current one ends. Forward dispatch causes drivers to stay on the road substantially longer during busy periods (high wage periods), which leads drivers to be less affected by income targeting. Forward dispatch is a default setting. To disable it, drivers need to pause it every time they pick up a new passenger.

## Appendix C

## Suggestive Evidence for Daily Income Targeting

An import pillar for the behavioral income targeting model is narrow framing (Kahneman 2003): drivers consider a sequence of decisions (labor supply decisions on different days) separately rather than as a portfolio. In deciding how long to work on a given day, drivers fall into a cognitive trap of narrowly thinking about their earnings one day at a time. In other words, thinking about their earnings one day at a time is much more accessible than intertemporal choices

Essentially, if (1) ridesharing drivers who experience a negative or positive income shock on the current day do not change their daily income targets on the following day, then it suggests that ridesharing drivers do not use income targets beyond the daily level. In other words, the narrow framing effect is stronger (narrower framing) in this case. On the other hand, if (2) ridesharing drivers who earn significantly lowe on the current day (negative shocks) make up the shortfalls on the following day, or ridesharing drivers who earn significantly higher on the current day (positive shocks) reduce their labor supply on the following day, then it suggests that ridesharing drivers use income targets beyond the daily level (e.g., weekly or monthly targeting, or even longer horizon, which is more consistent with the rational life cycle model). In other words, the narrow framing effect is weaker in this case (an extreme case is the rational life cycle model that assumes an income target at the entire life cycle level). Therefore, case (i) is more consistent with the behavioral income targeting model due to a stronger narrow framing effect.

Following Agarwal et al. (2015), we test which case (case 1 or 2) is supported by empirical evidence and select ridesharing drivers who experience a negative or positive income shock based on income ratio: their actual income for each day in the last week of our sample period divided by their daily income target. The daily income target is measured by the average daily income of ridesharing drivers in the first three weeks of our sample period. Ridesharing drivers in the bottom 25<sup>th</sup> percentile of the income-ratio distribution on a particular day are identified as experiencing a negative shock in their income. Similarly, ridesharing drivers who are in the top 25<sup>th</sup> percentile of the income-ratio distribution are defined as those who experience a positive shock in their income.

In Figure C1, for these two groups of drivers, we track the ratio of their actual income to their reference daily income on the current day and the next day following the shocks. Each graph in the figure plots the kernel density of the income-ratio for ridesharing drivers on each of the seven days (Sunday–Saturday) in the last week of our sample period. There are seven series, and daily kernel density series are represented by different colors. Figure C1(a) represents the distributions of the income ratio on the current day with positive shocks (the current day could be Sunday, Monday, …, or Saturday). These distributions peak to the right of 1.0, indicating that this sample of ridesharing drivers earn substantially above normal on the current day. Figure C1(b) shows that although these ridesharing drivers experience a positive shock on the current day, the income distributions revert to the reference income target level on the following day (around income ratio 1.0). Figures C1(c) and (d) demonstrate a similar pattern, which indicates that regardless of whether ridesharing drivers experience a negative or positive shock on the current day, the labor supply of ridesharing drivers on the following day is determined largely by adherence to daily income targets. This pattern provides additional evidence for a strong narrow framing effect, supporting the behavioral income targeting model.
