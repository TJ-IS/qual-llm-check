# Assessing the importance of industry in the adoption and assimilation of IT: Evidence from Italian enterprises

Paolo Neirotti \*, Emilio Paolucci

Politecnico di Torino, Department of Production Systems and Business Economics, corso Duca degli Abruzzi, 24, 10129 Turin, Italy

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 6 December 2009 Accepted 2 November 2010 Available online 20 July 2011

Keywords: IT adoption IT assimilation IT business value Industry effects Capabilities

## A B S T R A C T

We analysed the role of industry in determining the diffusion and business value created by IT. Data was collected for this purpose by surveying 192 large enterprises in Italy. Our research revealed three findings. First, in the material services and non-hi-tech manufacturing industries, firms had a relatively limited adoption of IT, resulting in little business impact. Second, firms’ IT spending behaviour depended on their industry type and not on their IT capabilities. However their capabilities were more important than industry in explaining why firms achieved benefits from IT adoption that depended on the previous accumulation of IT resources and other capabilities. Third, industry type determined the degree to which IT affected profitability and its effectiveness in helping firms to defend their competitive advantage. Specifically, the slower the adoption of IT in an industry, the greater its impact on the firm’s profitability.The implications of these findings for managers and policy-makers are discussed

## 1. Introduction

Industry type is an important factor in affecting firms’ decisions to invest in IT. However, apart from some exceptions [5,16], it has received limited attention in IS research. There are a number of reasons for studying the effect of industry type on firms’ IT adoption strategies. First, reports often highlight systematic differences in IT spending in different industries. Second, common needs for IT often emerge within particular industries due to their specific information requirements and environmental conditions. Third, as a result of this fact, many IT vendors have targeted only particular sectors and have provided them with an array of specialised ‘‘vertical’’ solutions. This has generated industry-level differences in the (relative) costs companies invest in developing new IT. Fourth, industries have different levels of human capital and exhibit different timing in the adoption of organisational and managerial innovations (OMI) – such as empowerment or business process re-engineering [1,2].

The lack of research into industry effects on firm IT adoption strategies may be due to a shortage of cross-sectoral data [4]. This has led to many studies assessing the effect that firms’ internal characteristics (size, capabilities, etc.) and environmental conditions have on IT adoption within only a few industries (for a review, see [12]). Studies that have investigated broad sectoral populations have approached the IT construct as a ‘‘black-box’’, focusing only on expenditures [18] and treating IT as a monolithic entity rather than as an array of many technologies with different maturity levels, flexibility, and functional scope. Therefore, they did not investigate in detail how the use and benefits of IT may vary across industries. For the same reason, they failed to capture the evolving nature of IT. While the commodity-like IS are affordable to every firm by being packaged into cheap standardized solutions, many systems require firm-specific capabilities and extensive organisational learning in order to be effectively incorporated into business processes. RFID systems for inventory management are a good example of a commodity-like technology: the costs for tags and readers are affordable even to small enterprises, the system can be easily implemented with little customisation, and it can easily reduce picking errors and improve inventory control. However, the use of RFID for tracing products along the supply chain requires firms in the chain to coordinate their RFID-relevant technologies (i.e., middleware and inter-firms information systems for supply chain management). Similarly, in certain industries, despite the fact that basic customer relationship management (CRM) systems are now used, few companies have developed sophisticated analytic approaches to build an integrated view of the customer based on data mining technology.

The evolving nature of IT has therefore led to increased interest in ‘‘opening the black box’’ to study how firms in different industries adopt and use IT to leverage their potential. To do this, we investigated the effect of firm capabilities and industry on IT adoption and on the degree of its assimilation, which reflects the development of new capabilities or improvement of pre-existing ones. According to resource-based view, industry may strongly influence strategic decisions on IT that are easily observable and imitable such as the IT spending level or the adoption of commercial ISs. The type of industry may influence the impact of IT on firm profitability, depending on the intensity of competition and the ability of competitors to respond to other firm’s investments. We therefore sought to assess:

## 2. Industry, firm capabilities and IT adoption: theory and hypotheses

## 2.1. The role of industry characteristics on IT adoption

Industry type influences firm IT adoption decisions in several ways. First, within a sector, firms share the same environmental characteristics, such as competition, support for growth, and rate of change of technology and products (dynamism). For example, in munificent industries, firms are expected to invest more heavily in IT because of there being more resources available and a need for standardised ISs to scale up their operations. In a dynamic environment, as highly competitive conditions are likely, IT strategies that may improve market-based or product development capabilities are likely to be more successful than strategies that focus on improving operational efficiency [6]. Second, within a sector, firms deal with the same institutional concerns causing them to be susceptible to fads. Under conditions of uncertainty, managers consider the actions of industry peers in setting their own firm’s strategy. Furthermore, the fact that in the same industry most firms share the same consulting companies produces spillover effects of management practices from one firm to another [9]. Third, industry effects on IT adoption can be due to similarities that firms share in organisational and technological factors, such as size, human capital, information processing requirements, and the complexity and information intensity of products, production methods, and supply chains. Firms in a complex and turbulent environment deal with greater uncertainty in their operations and have more information processing requirements [17], and thus a greater need for IT.

These arguments suggest that for both manufacturing and services we can discern two industry categories that capture differences in environmental, organisational and technologies conditions that are relevant to the way IT is used in firms (see Table 1). In manufacturing, ‘‘hi-tech’’ industries generally have greater financial resources. Specifically, in science-based, specialised-suppliers industries, and scale-intensive sectors with complex products and supply chains firms are likely to consider IT to be a strategic asset because of their greater operational complexity and their large number of complementary resources such as human capital and OMI.

In services, information services firms have greater information processing requirements and IT has a more important role in supporting the business models and processes. Specifically, information services sectors include scale-intensive information networks (telcos, financial services, call centres) and specialisedsuppliers and science-based services (business services, software firms, etc.). These industry clusters use IT for industrialising and delivering services in new ways, automating and de-integrating processes and outsourcing commodity tasks [13]. This applies to financial services, software development, and transaction-based services like call centres, and even for more traditional services such as professional education, legal advice, and tax assistance. As IT is the core technology for delivering services, most of the applications and infrastructure technologies used in these industries are strategic necessities. However, in material services industries IT has a more limited operational and strategic impact because the service requires the delivery of a physical product (e.g., in hotels, restaurants, construction, etc.).

## 2.2. The role of firm capabilities on IT adoption

While many firms invest in IT, not all are able to use it effectively. This is because of the different ways that companies manage their IT projects and adapt their organisational structure and business processes to use IT effectively. Such capabilities are firm-specific, heterogeneously distributed and difficult to replicate, because they originate through change processes that are ambiguous, pathdependent, and socially complex. Specifically, IT management capabilities are needed to convert financial investment into IT assets in an economical manner [11,22,23]. Organisational capabilities may play a key role in IT assimilation because they favour effective usage processes for IT assets. Earlier studies [3] identified such capabilities in the organisational capital embedded in routines and practices like a broader use of decentralised decision-making, greater attention to human capital investment, and broader use of horizontal and vertical coordination to aid the flow of knowledge through the organisation. These characteristics are expected to be more common in firms considering IT to be a strategic asset, rather than merely automating routine tasks.

## 2.3. Hypotheses

The extent of IT adoption might be more pronounced in information services and the hi-tech industry. These industry classes might also exhibit a broader diffusion of organisational and managerial capabilities that are complementary to IT. Such companies can obtain different returns on IT investment because of differences in their competitive environment. We formulated five hypotheses drawing on these ideas (see Fig. 1).

We assumed that the industry type had an effect on the development of Key IT capabilities (IT management practices and the organisational capital). Institutional isomorphism (i.e., companies imitating the managerial and organisational practices of successful competitors) may explain industry level differences in IT-related capabilities and time compression diseconomies [14] in their accumulation may explain the persistence of such differences. Thus:

H1. Organisational capital and IT management capabilities are greater in hi-tech and information service industries.

Industry type may result in differences in IT expenditure, because annual spending behaviour is affected by strategies that can be readily observed and imitated by firms. We thus expected that firms of the same industry type would exhibit similar IT spending behaviour. Such considerations suggested that hi-tech and information services firms would invest more resources in IT. Also that lack of correlation between capabilities and spending in IT may be due to the fact that companies with superior capabilities do not need to invest more than their competitors, but invest more effectively or have already provided a good base in the past and do not need to continue at a high rate in the future. Thus:

H2.A. The IT expenditure behaviour is associated with industry type, with hi-tech and information services industries exhibiting a higher IT expenditure per employee ratio.

H2.B. The IT expenditure behaviour is not associated with a firm’s organisational capital and IT management capabilities.

Unlike IT spending behaviour, we expected that management capabilities affect the extent of a firm’s IT adoption. A firm’s IT adoption extent may also depend on industry type as similarities in information requirements in the same industry type may lead to similar adoption patterns. Thus:

H3.A. The extent of IT adoption depends on industry type.

H3.B. The extent of IT adoption depends on the firm’s IT management capabilities.

The effect of firm capabilities on IT assimilation may be less straightforward than those effects that influence the decision to adopt IT. We expected that industry type would have a limited impact on the assimilation technologies that are deployed in firmspecific business activities. Successful assimilation of these technologies is likely to depend on long learning cycles and be socially complex [19]. Improved collaboration in supply chain relationships, evolution of organization structures towards more organic configurations, superior cross-functional coordination and knowledge usage in new product development and marketing activities are example of the capabilities that firms develop as a result of such processes. In light of their characteristics, these capabilities are expected to have a low diffusion and to be more likely in firms combining IT with innovative organisational practices and having a vision of IT as a strategic factor. As a result, we postulated:

H4.A. Assimilation of non-commoditised types of IT depends on a firm’s organisational capital and strategic IT vision.

H4.B. Assimilation of non-commoditised types of IT does not directly depend on industry type.

The successful assimilation of IT resources may increase profitability if the industry competitive environment is favourable; e.g., due to weak competition, low market turbulence, and slow response of competitors to match IT decisions. Consequently:

H5. Industry moderates the relationship between IT assimilation and profitability growth, with the relationship being stronger in sectors that exhibit lower adoption and assimilation of IT.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. The sample

To test these hypotheses, in 2006 we carried out a survey of firms doing business in Italy. We focused on the following industries: textiles; food products; chemical and pharmaceutical products; manufacture of transport equipment; manufacture of machinery and equipment; manufacture of computers and electronics components; utilities; wholesale and retail trade; hospitality and tourist assistance services; logistics services; telecommunications; financial services; business services; publishing; and private healthcare. To guarantee a homogeneous sample with regard to IT requirements, we did not survey industries that are known to use IT in highly specific ways, such as multimedia, software, and IT services. Other excluded industries were agriculture, mining, and construction, whose potential use is inherently limited by the characteristics of their business.

The population of firms was defined using the Bureau Van Dijk’s AIDA database (see http://www.bvdep.com/en/AIDA.html), which contains financial statements for all the Italian firms. Companies with more than 250 employees were included in the population, consistent with the definition of a large enterprise adopted by the European Union. Mail surveys were sent to 1519 CIOs, who had been contacted by phone to be selected as appropriate respondents. The survey had a potential population of 1894 firms; of these, 375 companies were dropped because their CIOs stated that they were not well-informed about IT assimilation in their company.

Follow-up calls were made to any CIO who had not returned completed surveys within 30 days of their being sent. A total of 214 questionnaires were returned (corresponding to an 11.3% response rate) but only 192 were usable for the purpose of this research (see Table 2 for the sample distribution). To prevent CIOs from reporting personal feelings, we asked them to express positions that were shared with the top management.

To check for non-response bias, we compared respondents with non-respondents by using contingency tables, Kruskall–Wallis non-parametric tests, and a logit analysis; it was tested on the basis of size, industry type, profitability, fixed assets and value added per employee. None of these revealed any sample bias.

## 3.4. Key IT capabilities

To represent organisational capital (ORG\_CAP), we considered the presence of:

1. horizontal coordination through extensive use of cross-functional work teams and knowledge-sharing activities among workers;

2. vertical communication between line employees and their managers about strategic goals and operations;

3. extensive use of incentive-based compensation linked to individual or group performance;

4. resources for recruiting candidates’ based on their interpersona skills and for direct involvement of line managers in the process; and

5. a high percentage of employees being offered continuous special training.

We asked CIOs to evaluate the extent to which their company employed these practices using a five-point Likert scale, where 0 corresponded to a neutral position between strong disagreement (-2) and strong agreement (+2).

PCA results showed that these practices had high loadings on a single factor (explaining approximately 40 percent of their variance). We did not expect that the practices included in this factor would capture more than a small portion of the firms operating philosophies, but we expected that they would identify a firm’s tendency to move towards a more modern configuration.

We measured IT management capabilities (IT\_MAN) by considering the use of the following:

1. financial or qualitative indicators to measure ROI due to IT;

2. methods used to prioritise IT projects and assess their alignment with business strategy;

3. task force and other ways to communicate between business and IT departments to identify emerging IT needs; and

4. training to encourage acceptance and effective use of new ISs. We then used a PCA approach to confirm high loadings on a single factor.

## 3.5. Strategic IT vision

We measured strategic IT vision (STR\_VIS) by asking CIOs to identify the top managers’ vision that best described the role of IT in their firm. We defined a scale that could measure four levels of strategic importance of IT:

1. IT does not play an important role in the firm’s core activities;

2. the role of IT is to improve the efficiency of administrative activities by automating repetitive tasks;

3. the role of IT is to improve the efficiency of primary activities in the value chain; and

4. the role of IT is to promote competitive advantage, enabling the development of new products and services.

## 3.6. IT expenditure

IT expenditure (IT\_EXP) was measured as the IT expenditure per employee, as the mean (on a logarithmic scale) of the annual budgets for IT investments declared by the companies between 2002 and 2005.

## 3.7. IT adoption extent

We developed a list of 14 types of IT and asked the CIOs to state how many were used as of the end of 2005 in their firm; this was used as the measure for IT adoption (IT\_ADO). Consistent with the notion of IT assets used, we considered enterprise systems used to support value chain activities (ERP, CRM, SCM, and PLM/PDM) and infrastructure resources (EDI, Intranet, Extranet, e-learning platforms, business continuity systems, and data warehousing technologies). Though this measure provides an aggregate view on IT adoption, it is robust and consistent with our goal of identifying the properties of organisations that use IT to innovate in a variety of settings [8].

## 3.8. IT assimilation

To deal with its multi-dimensionality, we avoided measures that would aggregate the impact of IT across different types of benefits but used a metric that was based on four types of IT impact: improved product/service development capability; transformed business processes; enriched knowledge of customer behaviour; and more flexible and adaptive organisational structure. CIOs were invited to evaluate, on a 5-level Likert scale, whether IT led to significant impact on a set of items involving these four areas. To help them report objectively, we asked them to base their assessment on any impacts observed in the four previous years (2002 through 2005).

To measure IT assimilation in new product/service development processes, we considered whether IT contributed by reducing time-to-market, mitigating product development risks, and increasing the revenue from new products or services developed during the past four years. These three items were grouped into a single factor termed improved product development capabilities (NPD).

To measure IT assimilation in business processes, we asked respondents to evaluate the effect of IT on:

1. the efficiency of administrative activities (simplification of payroll and accounting);

2. operations (improvements in inventory management, throughput, order cycle time, and product or service quality); and

3. supply chain relationships (through more effective procurement operations, improved collaboration with suppliers, or in product development).

In a PCA carried out on these items, we found that they did not load significantly on a single factor. Therefore they were included as three distinct variables (ADMIN, OPER and SCM).

To measure the IT-based improvement of knowledge about customers (CUST\_KN), we measured whether IT allowed firms to improve their knowledge about four types of costumer behaviour: purchasing habits; primary needs and expectations about new products or services; values and lifestyles (of consumers); and satisfaction with the products and services.

Finally, to measure the degree to which firms were transformed into more flexible and adaptive organisations, we considered 14 itemsshowinghow ITled to organisationalchange between 2002 and 2005. PCA showed that there were two distinct factors. The first was due to the pervasiveness in the use of IT in work practices (IT\_WP), capturing how IT affected the number of end-users of IT applications and the frequency of their IT training, the degree of internal coordination through the use of videoconferencing tools, email, and groupware applications, and the use of IT tools supporting front-line employees and middle managers in operational and tactical decisions. The second related to changes in organisational design (ORG\_CHA), as it captured modification to the organisation structure and to the properties of tasks, measured as the way IT changed the number of middle managers and the nature of their control authority, the number of hierarchical levels in the organisation, the degree of automation of business processes through downsizing or outsourcing, and the variety of tasks and decision rights of front-line employees (i.e., their empowerment and job enrichment).

## 3.9. Control variables

To account for extraneous sources of variation in firm IT adoption and assimilation that could be associated with the size of the resources available for IT investment, we included the firm’s size (as the logarithm of the number of employees) and previous profitability (PR\_01), which was assessed as the difference in the EBITDA/revenue ratio in the year 2001, compared to a pair group composed of large firms having the same NACE 3-digit code.

## 3.10. Psychometric properties of measurement scales

For the factors identified by PCA, scales were computed by averaging the scores of the items. The psychometric properties of these scales included establishment of content validity, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and reliability. Content validity was established through interviews with seven CIOs from different industries. Considerable effort was made during this field-based validation to ensure that the scale items were generalisable across the industries in our sample. Confirmatory factor analysis was used to establish convergent validity by confirming that all scale items loaded significantly on their hypothesised construct factors. All scales exhibited acceptable reliability, achieving coefficient alphas of at least 0.70. Discriminant validity was assessed through CFA by comparing the chi-square differences between a constrained factor model with all items constrained to be part of the same construct and an unconstrained model. The assessment of discriminant validity of the IT assimilation measures confirmed a need to discriminate between seven distinct dimensions. Finally, Harman’s one-factor test was conducted to test the presence of common method bias. No single factor emerged from the factor analysis, suggesting that common method bias was unlikely to affect our results.

## 4.1. Industry-level differences in IT adoption and assimilation

ANOVA analyses and non-parametric tests showed higher IT spending, a broader extent of IT adoption, and a high assimilation of these technologies in hi-tech manufacturing and information services firms (see Table 3). They also showed significant differences in the diffusion patterns of our seven IT assimilation dimensions:

 A considerable impact of IT on the efficiency of the administrative area (ADMIN) was diffused throughout the entire sample and did not exhibit any significant difference in its intensity across industry classes; this is consistent with the commodity-like nature of the IS for administrative processes.

 The impact of IT on operations efficiency (OPE) was also broadly diffused, especially in hi-tech and information services firms.

 However, improvements in organisational design (ORG\_CHA), product development capabilities (NPD) and supply chain management (SCM) were less diffused, consistent with the fact that IT assimilation in these areas requires investing in a cluster of interrelated technologies (such as PLM and SCM) that can be considered non-commoditised given their high level of co specialisation with firm capabilities.

## 4.3. The effect of IT assimilation on profitability

To assess the effect of IT assimilation on profitability, we used a treatment-effects model with either a two-step consistent estimator or a maximum likelihood approach. This allowed us to account for endogeneity due to auto-selection bias; omitted firm-specific factors, such as unobserved aspects related to strategy decision-making and managerial capabilities may codetermine both assimilation of IT and profitability growth. The treatment-effects model involves a two-step estimator that corrects for endogeneity by estimating both the treatment (IT assimilation) effect on profitability and the determinants of the treatment by using a probit regression model.

Our measures of profitability were return on assets (ROA) and the EBITDA/Revenues ratio. We chose the following specifications for the principal and the treatment equations:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \Delta \text {Profitability} & = \alpha + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {1 4} \beta i D i + \beta_ {1 5} I T _ {-} A S S I M _ {-} D + \beta_ {1 6} T R M A N \\ & \quad + \beta_ {1 7} I T _ {-} A S S I M _ {-} D \times T R M A N + \beta_ {1 8} S I Z E \\ & \quad + \beta_ {1 6} M S E R V + \beta_ {1 7} I T _ {-} A S S I M _ {-} D \times M S E R V + \varepsilon \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} I T A S S I M \_ D & = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} O R G \_ C A P + \alpha_ {2} S T R \_ V I S + \alpha_ {3} M S E R V \\ & \quad + \alpha_ {4} T R M A N + \alpha_ {5} S I Z E + \mu . \end{array}
$$

Specifically, IT\_ASSD refers to the IT assimilation dimensions as dummy variables, MSERV and TRMAN were dummy variables denoting material services and traditional manufacturing industries, Di were the fifteen 2-digit NACE industry segments in the sample, and e and m represented the error terms of the models. The growth in profitability ratios was observed over the period 2001– 2005, for which CIOs were asked to evaluate the impact of IT on firm activities. We did not include any delay in profitability, as our way of measuring IT assimilation assumed that we already controlled for any delay (the ‘‘assimilation gap’’) between adoption of the innovation and its results.

For each IT assimilation variable the threshold used to identify a considerable IT impact was a score of 1 on a five-level Likert scale ranging from -2 (strong disagreement) to +2 (strong agreement).

Some of the hypothesised interaction effects were confirmed by the analysis, supporting, in part, hypothesis H5 and suggesting that IT assimilation produced higher positive returns on profitability for firms in traditional manufacturing and material services industries (see Tables 6 and 7). Changes in organisational design and improvements in operations efficiency and customer knowledge were found to have a positive effect on both the profitability ratios considered. Instead, improvement in product development capabilities was found to have a positive impact only on growth of sales profitability. ## 5. Discussion and conclusions

## 5.1. Key findings

Our research provided evidence of differences in the extent of IT adoption and the impact of IT on both business activities and profits among hi-tech and information services companies and more traditional sectors. By determining how IT was used and assimilated in firms, we determined how industry and firm capabilities affected firm adoption and assimilation of IT.

First, IT spending behaviour –a strategic decision that can be easily imitated – was found to be affected by industry type but not by capabilities that are complementary to IT. Second, depending on the area in which the impact of IT was studied, we found differences in the level of assimilation and their determinants. The differences reflected the composite nature of IT, which includes technologies with different degrees of diffusion and commoditisation. Improved administrative efficiency was indeed found to be independent of both firm capabilities and industry type, being a consequence of adoption of commoditised technologies that are diffused and have similar usage patterns across sectors. IT assimilation in other areas was affected by firm capabilities and type of vision of IT, confirming the existence of a side of IT that is firm-specific and requires a complementary investment in organisational capabilities.

Third, IT was found to improve profitability when used to create leaner and more flexible organisational structures and when it improved customer knowledge, operations management, and product development processes. In material services and traditional manufacturing these dimensions of IT assimilation were less common, but they had a higher impact on profitability. Therefore, the few companies in these industries that were able to successfully use IT in such areas had higher profitability returns on those investments due to low environmental turbulence and a slow response from competitors in undertaking the same IT investments. The strategic value of a given technology depends on the industry, thereby confirming the idea that industry moderates the relationship between IT investment and performance. In information services industries IT applications are likely to be a strategic necessity. With respect to studies that have debated the role of industry in determining the value of IT investments (e.g., [21]), our paper has two novel aspects. First, we considered industry effects by choosing a classification that goes beyond the aggregate dichotomies used in the past to discriminate manufacturing from services [15,20]. Second, we attempted to assess the impact of IT on performance by analyzing the multiple ways in which IT can produce business value.

## 5.2. Implications

Our findings present some important implications for both managers and policy-makers. By showing that the returns of successful IT investments are more appropriable in industries where IT penetrates more slowly, the study informs managers that, in material services and traditional manufacturing industries, isolating mechanisms are more effective in protecting competitive advantages that were built on the IT use. Path analysis gave evidence of these mechanisms as it showed that securing improvements in profitability from IT investments was a complex process that required time and previous successful investments in IT. This evidence thus highlights three types of isolating mechanisms in IT investments. First, time compression diseconomies, as the benefits of IT manifest gradually on time, and superior usage processes of IT depend on a portfolio of IT applications that cannot be rapidly imitated by competitors by accelerating the pace of annual expenditures in IT. Second, asset mass efficiencies, as benefits from IT investments increase with larger previous IT assets stocks. Third, interconnectedness of asset stocks, as benefits from IT investments increase with larger previous stocks of other type of intangible assets such as managerial expertise and organisational capital. However, in industries where IT is penetrating more slowly managers have fewer opportunities for improving business processes through IT. This is due to the fact that firms are more likely to exhibit mechanistic organisational structures and lower human capital.

The study – by corroborating the importance of organisational capital for IT investments – also confirmed that investments in such forms of capital go beyond CIOs responsibility and require them to establish an extensive coordination with the top management team.

The most important implication is that policies for accelerating IT diffusion must primarily target material services and traditional manufacturing industries, given their more limited use of IT to improve core value chain activities and to make enterprises more flexible and adaptive. Furthermore, because of the significant industry-level differences in the state of IT adoption and assimilation, in countries where traditional manufacturing sectors account for a significant proportion of the GDP this industrial structure can favour an increasing gap with nations where information-intensive and hi-tech sectors account more for the GDP.

## 5.3. Limitations

Our results must be viewed in light of four limitations. First, the focus on Italian enterprises may impact the ability to generalise our results. Second, we used a stylised and aggregate view of IT adoption that failed to disentangle the different mechanisms by which industry can impact IT adoption. Third, the inability of path analysis to validate causal linkages may raise doubts about our results. Nevertheless, the causal path identified by the regression models remains plausible and consistent with the timing and the ways in which various enterprise systems are usually adopted by companies. Finally, the data were collected from CIOs and at single point in time. This has certain drawbacks which we tried to correct through our selection of CIOs who were sufficiently knowledgeable about the business, such drawbacks cannot be completely ignored.

A fourth limitation is the risk of endogeneity due to unobserved variables that may co-determine decision to adopt IT, their organisational assimilation and their impact on performance. This risk was in part ruled out by using two-steps treatment regression models to estimate the impact of IT assimilation on profitability. Panel data would have allowed another convenient method of estimating the impact of IT adoption and assimilation on profitability accounting for unobserved firm characteristics [10]. Specifically, fixed-effects regression models would have allowed incorporating time invariant firm-specific factors that are the source of endogeneity of IT adoption and assimilation. However, even panel data may be associated with problems when focusing on innovation because certain antecedents of IT adoption and assimilation – such as the use of practices and routines - are quasifixed or evolve through very slow dynamics.
