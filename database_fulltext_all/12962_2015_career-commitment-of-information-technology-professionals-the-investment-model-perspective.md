# Career commitment of information technology professionals: The investment model perspective

Jen-Ruei Fu <sup>a,</sup>\*, Jessica H.F. Chen <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Management, National Kaohsiung University of Applied Sciences, 415 Chien Kung Road, Kaohsiung 807, Taiwan, ROC <sup>b</sup> Department of Information Management, National Chi-Nan University, No.470, University Road, Puli 54561, Nantou County, Taiwan, ROC

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 23 March 2015 Accepted 31 March 2015 Available online 11 April 2015

Keywords: Career commitment IT professionals Investment model Professional self-efficacy Threat of professional obsolescence

## A B S T R A C T

This research applied an investment model to investigate the career commitment of IT professionals. We explore why some unfavorable careers persist and why some favorable careers end. Self-reported data from MIS departments of Taiwan’s top-1000 large-scale companies were collected to test our hypotheses. The results suggested that an IT professional’s career commitment is primarily determined by career satisfaction. In addition, the threat of professional obsolescence exerted more influence on individuals with low professional self-efficacy. Finally, career satisfaction appeared the most important antecedent for individuals with positive valence. In contrast, the career commitment for individuals with negative valence was largely driven by non-voluntary dependence. The implications for practitioners and scholars are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The impact of rapid technology advancement and global competition is changing the traditional expectations of a career. Gradually, the organization cannot guarantee the development of a personal career. Changes in the psychological employment contract suggest that the long-established concept of organizational commitment, even the organizational career, may no longer be valid [21]. While this potential threat may affect most people’s lives in the era of the knowledge economy, it is immediate and prominent for IT Professionals<sup>2</sup> (hereafter, ITPs). Thus, in response to changes such as outsourcing of IT-related services, downsizing, layoffs, mergers, and acquisitions, many ITPs choose to concentrate on and commit to their professional careers [5] rather than their organizations.

Career commitment, defined as ‘‘the degree to which someone identifies with and values his or her profession or vocation [3]’’, is recognized as an important construct as it relates to work performance. Past research shows that the development of sophisticated expertise requires individuals to be constantly devoted to the relevant activities for a long period of time. Individuals with high career commitment show less intention of leaving their jobs, spend more time in developing skills, demonstrate better performance [51], and are likely to promote various relationship maintenance behaviors such as willingness to selfsacrifice, adaptive social comparison, and devaluation of attractive alternatives. Therefore, career commitment is critical to a firm’s performance because it helps individuals develop specialized skills by persisting for a sufficient amount of time and also providing the incentives to nurture professional and business relationships.

However, it is not easy for ITPs to commit to their career. Work exhaustion and stress, unexpected ever-changing user demands and unrealistic deadlines, struggling to keep up with everadvancing technology—all are reported as common occurrences in the work environments of IT professionals [45] and make a career in IT stressful. Today, IT is an occupation in which dramatically changing technologies require continuous learning to stay current in the field [31]. In addition, obsolescence is even more of a threat to ITPs’ careers [22]. Past research reveals that, while 52% of civil engineering graduates remained in the field 20 years later, only 19% of computer science graduates did so [4]. Thus, many social and economic reasons exist to influence ITPs considerations of a career change.

Currently, the retention of skilled ITPs is an important issue for recruiters and employers as well [10]. Investigating career commitment helps us realize how people make sense of, develop, and integrate their multiple work-related commitments, especially for those that go beyond organizational boundaries. To our knowledge, limited attention in the IS literature has been paid to the understanding of ITPs’ career commitment. Our purpose is to identify salient factors that prompt an ITP to consider whether to remain in or leave a career. Guided by the investment model, we propose that relationship variables (e.g., career satisfaction (SAT), career investment (INV), and availability of career alternatives (ALT)), individual factors (e.g., professional self-efficacy (PSE)), and environmental variables (e.g., threat of professional obsolescence (TPO)) play an integral part in influencing ITPs’ career commitment (CRC). In addition, the moderating role of PSE and valence were examined.

## 2. Research on career commitment of IT professionals

Career is defined as ‘‘a sequential, predictable, organized path through which individuals pass at various stages of their working lives. [15] ‘‘The terms occupational, professional, and career commitment are used somewhat interchangeably in the literature. Lee et al. [26] preferred to use occupational commitment, defined as ‘‘committing to an identifiable and specific line of work that an individual engages in to earn a living at a given point in time.’’ However, professionals may pass through a sequence of related and different jobs in different career stages (e.g., an ITP who was promoted from a programmer to a systems analyst). Therefore, the concept of career commitment seems more relevant to the experiences of a professional because it relates to an individual’s motivation to work in a particular career field.

The concept of career commitment evolved from interest in the ongoing evaluation of one’s career choices. It is characterized by the development of personal career goals, the attachment to, identification with, and involvement in those goals. One who shows high career comment will persist in pursuing career objectives despite potential barriers and misadventure that may lie ahead. Considerable existing research suggests that career commitment is positively associated with organizational commitment, job involvement, and professional identity and is negatively associated with turnover and job withdrawal cognition. For example, Hauschildt and Heinemann [19] found that career commitment is related to the development of a professional identity. Blau and Lunz [3] investigated the effect of the career commitment of 457 medical technologists and found that career commitment accounted for substantial differences between those intending to leave and those intending to stay. Thus, career commitment relates to the extent to which people feel anchored in a specific occupational domain [31].

The research has examined the issue of career change, career exodus, career abandonment, and career persistence; such topics are closely related to career commitment. A growing body of literature on the career commitment/change of professionals has emerged in other occupational fields, for example, managers [7], research scientists [23], medical careers and technologists [3]. However, in the IS/IT literature, career commitment thus far has received limited attention until recently, and a few empirical studies such as Shropshire and Kadlec [46], Cho and Huang [9] Colomo-Palacios et al. [10], Duffy et al. [13], and Fu [15,16] were published.

Investigating career exodus of IT employees, Shropshire and Kadlec [46] found that IT workers who suffer from stress, burnout, or are concerned about the security of their jobs are more likely to consider a career change. Colomo-Palacios et al. [10] investigate career abandonment intentions among software workers. They concluded that effort–reward imbalance, perceived workload, and emotional exhaustion significantly influence individuals’ intention to abandon the career. Duffy et al. [13] found that career commitment was positively related to job satisfaction. Cho et al.

[9] identified three components of commitment: affective, normative, and continuance commitment. It suggested that the impact of affective commitment on professionals’ intention to leave their organizations is moderated by the continuance component. Fu [15] found that ITPs appeared to have different career attitudes in various career stages. Finally, Fu [16] found significant differences between ITPs and non-IT employees in their decisions to change career. Threat of professional obsolescence and professional self-efficacy were found to be more salient in predicting ITPs’ career commitment compared with non-IT employees. Generally, past research lacks sound theory-based models, thereby compromising their robustness.

With reduced promotional opportunities, decreased employee loyalty, increased contract workers, and changes in job expectations, a career has become a valuable source of occupational meaning and persistency. Moreover, career commitment and organizational commitment can complement each other to understand professionals’ motivations and work performance. Scholars have learned a great deal about the antecedents of organizational commitment and turnover intention in work organizations among ITPs. However, there is a paucity of IS research on career commitment. In this article, the authors seek to address this gap in the IS literature.

## 3. The threat of professional obsolescence (TPO)

It seems ironic that ITPs who are most responsible for promoting innovative technological change are themselves greatly vulnerable to the consequences of such change. This vulnerability is manifested in the obsolescence of their professional competencies [11]. Professional obsolescence refers to the decay or decrease in the value of professional competencies. It occurs when the job incumbent’s expertise (which was sufficient to the requirements of the profession previously) is mismatched with current work demands and skill requirements owing to change in the knowledge domain.

As opposed to accumulating over time, ITP expertise is constantly depleted with the ‘‘competence destroying’’ nature of IT. Therefore, the fear of falling behind in the technology race and the ceaseless pressure to stay up-to-date through ongoing learning are extra stressors on top of already tight deadlines and a demanding workload [32]. In fact, among the 33 most mentioned stressors, four of them were related to the pressures to keep pace with developments for ITPs in the field [44]. Failure to maintain and update one’s skills can quickly make a professional obsolete. Some anecdotal observations use ‘‘ITP’s worst nightmare’’ to describe this phenomenon: ‘‘You go to bed one night secure in your technical competences, and you wake up the next morning as a technological dinosaur.’’ This phenomenon is analogous to the Red Queen’s race: ‘‘You have to run as hard as you can to stay in the same place, and to make any progress you have to run twice as fast.’’

Although professional obsolescence has been recognized as a critical issue in IT research [26,38], limited studies have inspected the threat and its consequences. However, the speed of the technology change and obsolescence can have profound psychological and physiological effects. Pazy [35] observed that obsolescence is not uniformly experienced by people. Therefore, because the effect of obsolescence has not been understood thoroughly, attempts to study obsolescence in organizations should explore its variety among individuals.

## 4. Investment model

One model that seems ideal for investigating the factors involved in the relationships between ITPs and their careers is the investment model. The investment model posits that decisions to end or to stay in a relationship are mainly determined by feelings of commitment. Two structural elements, satisfaction and dependence, characterize interdependence in a relationship [41]. Satisfaction, which refers to the extent to which a person favorably evaluates the current relationship, results from the subjective evaluation of costs and rewards. Dependence refers to the extent to which an individual relies uniquely on a particular relationship that cannot be gratified elsewhere. The stability of the current relationship will be threatened by the availability of attractive alternatives. Based on social exchange theory, Rusbult [41] further extends these notions by introducing the concept of investment, which refers to the resources that an individual has put into a relationship. Such invested resources serve as a psychological inducement to strengthen commitment because the act of investment raises the costs of terminating a relationship. In other words, an individual would be ‘‘tied in’’ a hold-up situation because an invested resource creates value on one hand, and it generates a sunk cost at the same time, which prevents the person from seeking a new relationship. In sum, a relationship continues if the individual wants to persist (feels satisfied), needs to persist (has high investment), and has no choice but to persist (possesses poor alternatives).

Part of the investment model’s strength lies in its emphasis on understanding the conditions under which individuals stay in unfavorable relationships. It asks ‘‘why do some relationships persist despite dissatisfaction?’’ Rusbult and Martz [42] used the term ‘‘non-voluntary relationships’’ to describe those in which the person is forced to stay although he would prefer not to. They found that shelter women who possessed fewer alternatives and greater investments are more likely to feel committed to current relationships. Rhatigan and Street [39] found that the investment model explains a substantial amount of variance in the intentions of victimized women to leave current dating relationships. Li et al. [29] used the investment model to explore the moderating effect of valence to understand why internet users stick with a specific website. They found that for individuals with positive valence, satisfaction was most salient in predicting commitment toward a website. However, for individuals with negative valence, the investment size and availability of alternatives explained most of the variances in commitment. In sum, the investment model may provide a rationale for why individuals remain in their careers even if they do not like them.

In the IS literature, the investment model has received empirical support in a wide range of relationships: IS professionals turnover intention, job satisfaction, commitment [25], relationship commitment [14], online consumer loyalty and retention [29], and human-avatar relationships [52]. Overall, these studies suggest that the investment model is robust and adequately predicts commitment in a variety of relationships across various contexts. Therefore, one of the objectives of this study was to examine the effectiveness of the investment model in predicting ITPs’ career commitment.

## 5. Research model and hypotheses

This study develops a theoretical conceptual model about factors that influence ITPs’ career commitment, as shown in Fig. 1. The relationships among constructs will be discussed in the following sub-sections.

## 5.1. Career satisfaction (SAT)

Career satisfaction refers to the extent to which an ITP expresses a positive orientation toward his or her career. All things considered, it is not difficult to stay committed to a satisfied relationship. Thus, satisfied ITPs are expected to demonstrate commitments to their career. Past research provides evidence that career satisfaction is important in predicting career commitment. In the IS research, Gupta et al. [18] concluded that career satisfaction is an indicator of career commitment and turnover among information center personnel. Similarly in referent fields, a survey of white-collar employees from a diverse set of occupation and organizations suggests that career satisfaction was positively associated with career commitment [37]. Therefore, it is justifiable to expect that when an ITP perceives more career satisfaction, he or she will exhibit a higher level of career commitment. Hence,

H1. SAT is positively related to CRC.

## 5.2. Career investment (INV)

Career investment refers to accumulated resources in one’s career that would be deemed worthless or lost if one decided to switch to another career. ITPs continuously invest their time and energy in studying programming languages, development tools, algorithms, system architectures, data structures, etc. However, these competencies are specialized and non-transferable and are worthwhile and imperative only in information system design and development. Therefore, the substantial volume of investment could ‘‘trap’’ an ITP in the present career even if he or she does not like it because leaving the career represents a substantial loss of investment.

More investments in specialized knowledge and techniques may cause an ITP to commit to the specific career. In contrast, a career change often implies a loss of investment in one’s professional identity and years of discipline as well. Therefore, the greater the investment an ITP has devoted to an IT career, the higher his or her career commitment is.

H2. INV is positively related to CRC.

## 5.3. Availability of career alternatives (ALT)

Availability of career alternatives refers to the perceived abundance of accessible career choices that enable pursuing a new career. It reflects how attractive other career options are perceived by people compared with the present options. If the personal goal or unmet need could be gratified by other better career options, individuals may decide to change.

In the IS field, perceptions of job alternatives were found to significantly influence turnover intention [49]. This suggested that the comparison of external opportunities and their current situation has implications for ITPs’ career decisions. In contrast, absence of attractive alternative opportunities may oblige individuals to commit to their present careers. Therefore, having an abundance of career alternatives, the individual would express a lower level of career commitment.

## H3. ALT is negatively related to CRC.

## 5.4. Perceived fairness of rewards (PFR)

The investment model suggested that satisfaction is determined by the benefits and costs that are associated with a certain relationship. Perceived fairness of rewards refers to employees’ perceptions that their rewards are comparable to the effort that is exerted in their professional career. Fair rewards signal to employees that they are valued and important contributors to the success of organizations. Therefore, when employees perceive they receive fair rewards, they should report greater satisfaction. Research in IS and referent fields has found that fair distribution of rewards contributes to career and job satisfaction [30]. Thus, when ITPs perceive their rewards are fair, they may express more career satisfaction. Hence,

## H4. PFR is positively related to SAT.

## 5.5. Organization-based self-esteem (OBSE)

In addition to the tangible costs and benefits, the intangible benefits drawn from current careers may have psychological effect on the ITPs. The career chosen by an individual is not just a way of earning a living. It is also a way of life, a social role. Professionals obtain earnings, prestige and social status from their organizations through their career lives. Organization-based self-esteem (hereafter, OBSE) is defined as ‘‘the degree to which an individual believes him/herself to be capable, significant, and worthy as an organizational member [36].’’ It is the value that individuals perceive in themselves as being capable, competent, and important actors within their organizations. Because individuals pursue their careers within an organizational context, it is difficult to separate perceptions of a career from an organization.

ITPs with strong OBSE may have a sense of having satisfied their needs through their organizational roles. In addition, those who obtain more respect and a better reputation may increase personal identification with their careers. The degree to which ITPs perceived themselves as being valued by their organization should indicate more career satisfaction. Individuals with higher self-esteem are motivated to maintain a positive self-perception. As an expression of self and of self-consistency, feeling satisfied with and committing to one’s career could have the benefits of heightening self-esteem and pride. Research has found that OBSE significantly influenced job satisfaction among IT developers [28]. In addition, Carson et al. [6] predicted that OBSE is positively related to career satisfaction. Perceived OBSE may be interpreted as a cue from the organizational environment about the value placed on the work or the professional and thus positively influences career satisfaction. Hence,

## H5. OBSE is positively related to SAT.

## 5.6. The threat of professional obsolescence (TPO)

Theory of social identity suggests that a significant source of a professional’s self-concept, pride, and self-esteem is derived from a sense of belonging to a professional group or categories. Being recognized as a professional member in the IT field enhances one’s self-identity due to the high IT professionalization. However, the professional membership of an ITP is greatly threatened by obsolescence [40]. With the tidal wave of technological advancement and the large magnitude of change, ITPs are confronted by what has been described as ‘‘never-ending competence-destroying innovation’’ [1] and ‘‘constant technical skill depletion’’ [50]. It is estimated that the half-life of techniques and knowledge is lower than two years in the IT profession [1].

Following the logic of social identity theory, a threat to the continuity of membership will impair the salience of that identity. As a result, when ITPs perceive a higher threat of professional obsolescence, they are likely to express lower career commitment.

## H6. TPO is negatively related to CRC.

## 5.7. Professional self-efficacy (PSE)

Self-efficacy refers to belief in one’s ability to organize and execute courses of action required to produce given attainments. According to social cognitive theory, individuals with higher selfefficacy are willing to accept difficult duties and are prepared to expand best efforts against setbacks and obstacles, whereas persons with lower self-efficacy may feel psychologically threatened and stay away from challenges.

Professional self-efficacy is the extent to which a person has faith in one’s capability of managing emotional, physical and intellectual resources to successfully develop within one’s profession [24]. The sense of mastery and control created by professional self-efficacy enhances their willingness to persist within a specific career choice [48], as suggested by social cognitive theory. Past research in the IS field revealed that self-efficacy significantly influences individuals’ persistence, effective problem solving, task effort, resilience against failure, self-control, and performance [2,47]. Cherniss [7] found that professional self-efficacy is critical in determining the career commitment of human service professionals. Hence, we predict that individuals with a high level of professional self-efficacy will persist in their career commitment.

## H7. PSE is positively related to CRC.

## 5.8. The moderating effects

Jex and Bliese [20] observed that self-efficacy is a moderator in their study of stress and work. With control (i.e., self-efficacy) by the subject, mere exposure to stressors (i.e., professional obsolescence) may have few adverse effects. On the other hand, exposure to stressors that preclude control on the part of the subject may lead to undesired or harmful effects. Because committing to an IT career is associated with some challenges, it is likely that the relationship between stressors and career commitment is moderated by self-efficacy [37].

Therefore, we propose that professional self-efficacy attenuates the adverse effect of the threat of professional obsolescence on career commitment. Under the threat of professional obsolescence, ITPs who have more confidence in their professions are expected to remain optimistic and view challenging situations as an opportunity for career growth, which would make career commitment more likely. In contrast, ITPs with low self-efficacy are likely to feel threatened and therefore show less commitment to their career.

## H8. PSE moderates the relationship between TPO and CRC.

Valence refers to the positive or negative emotional charge something has. A career is positively valent if an employee prefers having it to not having it. As suggested by attribution theory, valence plays an important role in the attribution process. Individuals with different valence may engage in different thinking styles to process and interpret their information and messages [29]. Therefore, it is expected that valence has a moderating effect that influences the relative impacts of some determinants on ITPs’ career commitment.

Past research implicitly suggests that ‘‘feeling good’’ is a prerequisite to predicting why some relationships continue and others do not [42]. The investment model further proposes that satisfaction is not the sole basis for sustaining relationships. Satisfaction rises and falls even in the most well-adjusted relationship, and ‘‘all relationships occasionally fall to threateningly low levels. [42]’’ Individuals may remain in relationships that are unsatisfying. Therefore, based on the investment model assertion, this study explores how valence moderates the relationships between different antecedents and career commitment.

Research has found that positive valence was related to a more heuristic thinking style [27]. People in happy situations are likely to rely more on source cues rather than on systematic assessment of messages and to perform problem-solving tasks in a more heuristic fashion. In contrast to positive valence, individuals in depressed situations appear to have more thoughtful and systematic approached, e.g., a more detail-oriented cost-benefit analysis. To interpret this phenomenon, Schwarz [43] suggests that people in difficult life circumstances may become cognitively absorbed in information-processing tasks to distract themselves from thinking about the source of unhappiness. Therefore, they are more likely to prompt cognitive actions that could afford thoughtful problem solving. Following this logic, it can be reasonably inferred that ITPs with positive valence are less likely to use systematic manners to consider career change decisions. Thus, their decisions regarding the continuance/termination of careers are based primarily on the feelings of satisfaction about the relationships. On the contrary, ITPs with negative valence toward their careers may put more weight on factors related to ‘‘non-voluntary dependence’’ and less on the feelings of satisfaction. Put differently, an ITP may consider remaining in the current career because the individual needs to persist and/or has no choice but to persist.

Despite the importance of understanding why individuals remain in unfavorable relationships, the moderating role of valence has remained less explored in the literature. To our knowledge, only one IS study specifically explored and compared the dissimilar effect of antecedents among people with different valences (e.g., [29]). In sum, we postulate that ITPs with positive valence consider career commitment based primarily on the feelings of satisfaction about their career. On the other hand, ITPs with negative valence may remain in relationships because heavy costs are associated with being in other ones. We hypothesize:

H9. Valence moderates the relationship between SAT and CRC such that the positive relationship is stronger for ITPs with positive valence than for ITPs with negative valence.

H10. Valence moderates the relationship between INV and CRC such that the positive relationship is stronger for ITPs with negative valence than for ITPs with positive valence.

H11. Valence moderates the relationship between ALT and CRC such that the negative relationship is stronger for ITPs with negative valence than for ITPs with positive valence.

## 6. Method

## 6.1. Sampling

Self-reported data from MIS departments of the top-1000 largescale companies in Taiwan were collected to test our hypotheses. We sent emails to CIOs to invite the ITPs in their departments to join our research. Eight-two CIOs consented to disseminate questionnaires to ITPs. Respondent participation in the study is both voluntary and anonymous. They were informed that any information provided would be used for academic purposes only. To make sure that subjects had sufficient working experience to form career attitudes, ITPs with a year or less of tenure were excluded.

Prior to the main study, a convenience sample of 55 participants was recruited to participate in a pretest to examine the effectiveness of the measurement. 27 part-time MBA students agreed to disseminate the questionnaires to ITPs in their respective organizations. The feedback obtained during the pretest was used to fine-tune the survey item. Finally, the modified questionnaires (in paper form) were sent to individual groups of ITPs.

## 6.2. Measurement of the constructs

The measurement items for constructs were for the most part adopted or adapted from previous related studies. The preliminary version of this questionnaire was created after conducting literature reviews and in-depth discussions with several ITPs. Then, the survey instrument was inspected by practitioners and peers in the IT industry.

Items measuring career commitment (CRC) were developed by Kidd and Green [23]. This scale comprises three factors of CRC: career identity, career planning and career resilience. The items measuring SAT are taken from Greenhaus et al. [17] with the fiveitem scale. ALT and INV are adapted from Dam [12]. Items assessing PFR are taken from Netemeyer and Boles [34]. PSE is assessed using the 4-item measure adapted from Kossek et al., [24]. OBSE is measured with the scale developed and validated by Pierce et al. [36]. The measurement of TPO is adapted from Pazy [35]. It consists of three components: time, scope, and social comparison. Finally, the bipolar 7-point semantic-differential preference scale (favorable-unfavorable, positive-negative, likedislike) developed by Miniard et al. [33] is adopted to assess valence of the career. The measurement items for constructs are given in Appendix A.

## 6.3. Data analysis and results

## 6.3.1. Samples

Of the 820 potential subjects who received mail questionnaires, 255 ITPs participated in the study. The demographic characteristics of the respondents are reported in Table 1. Within the final sample of ITPs, 142 were men and 113 were women. More than 64% had work experience of more than 5 years, and less than 20% were older than 40 years old.

## 6.3.2. Scale validation

Before testing the hypotheses, the reliability and validity of the measurement model were examined. Because two latent constructs (CRC and TPO) of this study are formative, the partial least square (PLS) approach appeared to be the most appropriate because PLS provides the ability to incorporate both formative and reflective latent constructs in one model. In addition, PLS allows researchers to perform confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and path analysis models simultaneously in a single structural equation model. Therefore, we chose PLS to assess the structural model and the psychometric properties of the scales.

A CFA was conducted by PLS to assess internal consistency (reliability), item loadings (convergent validity), and discriminant validity. Internal consistencies and item loadings are considered acceptable if they are greater than 0.70. CFA was conducted with the initial model, which consisted of 12 factors and 47 items. We eliminated one item (inv06) from career investment and one (sat05) from career satisfaction because item loadings were less than 0.7 on their respective factors. The factor structure matrix shows that all item loadings are above the 0.7 threshold on their corresponding factors, and nearly all items have minimal cross-loadings on non-target factors, which suggests adequate convergent validity. Further, the composite reliability (CR) scores in Table 2 suggested that constructs exhibit good internal consistency.

To assess discriminant validity, Chin suggested that a construct should share more variance with its assigned items than with other constructs. In Table 3, the diagonal elements represent the square root of average variance extracted (AVE). A rule for evaluating discriminant validity is that the AVE square root should exceed the inter-construct correlations, i.e., the off-diagonal elements. All constructs meet this requirement. This suggests that items load more highly on their respective construct than on others. Thus, these results provide evidence of the acceptable discriminant validity of our measures.

## 6.4. Hypotheses testing

All constructs in the hypotheses are modeled as reflective except for the TPO and CRC. For second-order formative factors, the latent variable scores were initially estimated with all of the firstorder constructs, and then, these scores were subsequently used as indicants in a second-order structural model analysis. Furthermore, the moderating effect of professional self-efficacy was examined as part of the overall structural model with an interaction term formed by cross-multiplying all standardized items of each constructs, as suggested by Chin et al. [8].

Using partial least squares (PLS), the nine hypotheses are collectively tested. Significance was evaluated using the bootstrap routine with 500 re-samples. The variance explained (R<sup>2</sup> value) by each path, and the path significance of each hypothesized association is presented in Fig. 2. Variance explained for the structural equations for CRC and SAT is 56.2% and 51.4%, respectively. In general, most of the hypotheses are supported, except for H3. CRC is primarily determined by IT professionals’ SAT (b = 0.49, p-value <0.01); thus H1 is supported. INV (b = 0.17, p-value <0.01) and PSE (b = 0.27, p-value <0.01) significantly influence CRC; thus, H2 and H7 were supported. TPO is negative related to CRC (b = -0.18, p-value <0.01). Thus, it suggests that CRC is mitigated by an ITP’s perceived TPO, thus confirming H6.

Inspection of antecedents of SAT suggested that OBSE exerts the largest effect on SAT, with a path coefficient of 0.48 (p-value <0.01); thus, H5 is supported. The positive significant relationship between PFR (b = 0.41, p-value <0.01) showed that SAT is enhanced through an ITP’s PFR, as predicted in H4.

To understand the mediating role of SAT, the CRC was regressed on OBSE and PFR in addition to other remaining variables; see Table 4. The partially mediated model suggested that SAT has a statistically significant relationship with CRC (b = 0.49, p-value <0.01). However, the effect of OBSE and PFR on CRC fails to reach significance. This suggests that SAT fully mediates the relationships between CRC and both variables. The predicting variables of the endogenous constructs and their direct, indirect, and total effects on the dependent variable are presented in Table 5.

To test the moderating effect of PSE, the path coefficient between the interaction term (PSE x TPO) and CRC is negative and significant (b = -0.1, p-value <0.01), confirming H8. To further test the interaction effect, we divided the dataset into high-low subsets using the median of PSE and TPO. The ANOVA also confirmed a significant interaction between the PSE and the TPO (F(1, 251) = 6.14, p < 0.05). The results in Fig. 3 suggested that the TPO has a stronger effect on CRC for ITPs with low PSE.

Finally, to evaluate the moderating effect of valence, we split the full sample into two groups (positive and negative) using the median of valence. Then, the structural model was conducted to estimate path coefficients on both sub-samples separately. The results are reported in Fig. 4 and Table 6. As suggested by Fig. 4, INV and PSE significantly influence CRC for the two groups. Furthermore, findings also exhibited distinct patterns between ITPs with different valence. For ITPs with negative valence, the path coefficients are all significant except for the SAT. However, for ITPs with positive valence, TPO and ALT fail to significantly predict CRC.

## 7. Discussion

This research applied and extended the investment model to investigate the determinants of ITPs’ career commitment. The significance and magnitude of relationships between constructs provide a fundamental understanding of the findings. Career satisfaction represents the most influential antecedent of whether an ITP decides to continue his/her career. Furthermore, professional self-efficacy is the second largest determinant of career commitment, followed by organization-based self-esteem, the threat of professional obsolescence, fairness perception, and career investment.

Findings in the current study partially support the investment model because the perceived career alternatives did not significantly predict career commitment. Although past research in the literature has suggested that availability of job alternatives is important in predicting turnover intention (e.g. [12]), it is likely that ITPs are pragmatic because changing careers incurs substantially more cost than simply changing jobs. Thus, a better job option is likely to encourage an ITP to switch, but having an abundance of career alternatives is not a salient factor when thinking of changing to a new career. In contrast, ITPs who perceived greater satisfaction and invested more in the IT career reported greater career commitment. Thus, ITPs with higher career satisfaction have little desire to change their careers. In addition, the sense of obligation to one’s career can be enhanced by perceived accumulated costs. This suggests that one’s career commitment can be strengthened by accumulated investment, for example, professional associations or increased participation in continuing education activities.

While career commitment can be improved by increasing career satisfaction, a means of enhancing career satisfaction is through the perception that ITPs have been rewarded fairly. We found that ITPs who perceived more fairness in being rewarded reported greater satisfaction and in turn were more committed to their present careers. This finding is consistent with equity theory, which predicts that individuals with a lower perception of fairness are less likely to have a favorable attitude toward their careers in that their efforts to continue a career in IT are greater than the rewards received. The same was true for ITPs who reported more organization-based self-esteem. Thus, the intangible benefits drawn from the current career did have a positive effect on their attitude toward their careers. Extending the original investment model with ITPs’ perception of environmental and individual factors was deemed valuable. Greater professional self-efficacy may lead to higher career commitment. Additionally, we found that the threat of professional obsolescence significantly influences career commitment, in spite of the limited amount of variance explained. The interaction analysis further reveals that the threat of professional obsolescence exerts more influence on ITPs with low professional self-efficacy. However, ITPs with high professional self-efficacy are somewhat insensitive to the threat of professional obsolescence.

Results in the univariate analysis suggested that ITPs in the negative valence group reported a significantly lower level of satisfaction than ITPs in the positive valence group. Thus, it is interesting to understand why an ITP remained in an unfavorable relationship and what social factors constrained an ITP from leaving his/her present career. The between-group analysis suggested that ITPs with different valences seem to have diverse career attitudes. Career satisfaction is the primary antecedent of career commitment for ITPs with positive valence, but it is not significant in predicting career commitment for ITPs with negative valance. In addition, availability of career alternatives and the threat of professional obsolescence significantly influence ITPs’ career commitment for those who dislike their current career, but they are not significant for ITPs with positive valence.

For the negative valence group, career satisfaction is irrelevant to the understanding of career commitment. Career commitment is positively correlated with career investments and is negatively correlated with the availability of career alternatives. This suggested that ITPs commit to their careers to the degree that they had few alternatives and to the degree that they had greater investments in their careers. Thus, career commitment appears to be largely driven by non-voluntary dependence. This suggested that ITP might feel bound to a relationship although the relationship is not very gratifying. This is consistent with the predictions of the investment model that satisfaction is not the only factor determining whether an individual remains committed to a given relationship.

In contrast, investigating the positive valence group revealed that career satisfaction and career investments appear to promote the feelings of career commitment; however, the availability of career alternatives is irrelevant to the decision to stay in/leave their careers. Thus, ITPs are entrapped by substantial investments in professional careers whether they have positive or negative valence about their career. On the contrary, the abundance of career alternatives does not act as a pull factor to attract ITPs toward leaving their careers.

## 8. Limitations and future research directions

There are a number of limitations that warrant cautious interpretations of the findings. First, although common method bias was not significant, the use of post hoc self-report design does not rule out the presence of common method variance. Instead of using post hoc statistical remedies, future research can adopt procedural methods, such as separation of measurement and scale reordering, to reduce the potential threat. In addition, it is encouraged to develop objective measures for the constructs. Second, another weakness is the cross-sectional nature of the research design. Therefore, caution must be exercised in the interpretation of these observed associations. To better understand the causal relationships among variables, future research can use longitudinal design, for example, to collect predictor variables a few months earlier than the measurement of the dependent variable. Third, a substantial proportion of the variance in career commitment remained unexplained. Thus, this suggested that some important variables are not included in our research model. Research has shown that work-related characteristics (e.g., job security, job fit, salary, autonomy, and organizational commitment) and social and environmental factors (e.g., professional socialization) are related to an employee’s attitude and behavior concerning career changes. Some researchers forecast the emergence of boundaryless organizations in which individuals pursue ‘‘boundaryless careers’’ [21]. Thus, incorporating additional variables is likely to improve our understanding of ITPs’ attitudes toward career commitment.

## 9. Implications for practitioners

Information systems have become one of the imperatives for organizations to survive and sustain competitive advantages. ITPs play a crucial role in achieving these advantages by exploiting IT to extract value and delivering the effective technological solution to business problems. Therefore, to maintain and sustain the intellectual capacity of an organization’s resources, providing ITPs with a long-term stable career is beneficial for both organizations and ITPs. In addition, with ongoing technological and organizational innovation, many issues that emerge in the IT field today are likely to appear in other fields and have impacts on non-IT workers. Issues such as professional obsolescence may represent an immediate threat for IT professionals; it is increasingly a concern for professionals in many highly expert, evolving fields. Thus, the implications of this study may provide insights for management in the general workforce of the future.

For IT/HR practitioners, the implications of this study are noticeable. First, career commitment is primarily determined by IT professionals’ career satisfaction. Satisfaction with present professional career results from the subjective reward-cost analysis of whether the ITPs can obtain commensurate benefits (both tangible and intangible) from their career. Thus, efforts to improve ITPs’ career satisfaction through factors such as OBSE and fairness of rewards will likely be effective. HR managers should ensure that ITPs perceive that they are fairly rewarded and are competent, important, and worthy as an organizational member and should make this a component of regular communication programs. Second, because ITPs have to invest their scarce resources (e.g., time, energy) ceaselessly to update their knowledge and skills so that they do not fall behind technology advancements, it is necessary for managers to realize how ITPs perceive the threat of obsolescence and what actions they take to address it. Organizations should offer ITPs opportunities to contribute to organizational objectives while at the same time fulfilling their career-related goals. Therefore, investing specific resources to help match organizational objectives and ITPs’ career requirements is crucial. Third, in recruiting qualified candidates, professional self-efficacy is suggested as a criterion for recruitment of ITPs because ITPs who possess high self-confidence in IT professions are likely to be self-motivated and to exert great effort to remain current and proficient.

In particular, professional self-efficacy is effective in alleviating the negative influence of the threat of professional obsolescence. High self-efficacy may help an ITP sustain his/her professiona career in spite of the threat of professional obsolescence. Fourth, ITPs with different valences held different career attitudes. Career satisfaction, investments, and self-efficacy were important factors in predicting the career commitment of ITPs who liked their current careers. On the contrary, remaining in an unfavorable career should be understandable in terms of an ITP’s structural dependence on his/her career in the absence of a satisfactioncommitment link. Understanding these determinants and their relative impacts on ITPs’ career commitment may help the organization to shape effective programs to attract and retain a valuable IT workforce.

## 10. Conclusion

Maintaining a qualified and stable body of IS staff has been continually ranked as one of the most critical factors for the successful functioning of IS departments. Based on the investment model, this research develops a persuasive theoretical framework to explain and understand the influential determinants of ITPs’ career commitment. Specifically, the threat of professional obsolescence and professional self-efficacy were identified to investigate the moderating effects on career commitment. The results provide insight into the relative importance of factors leading to ITPs’ career commitment.

We believe our study makes some meaningful extensions and contributions to the literature in several ways. It suggests some new and intriguing ways to think about the career commitment of ITPs. First, while the past research, which has empirically investigated the influence of psychological and contextual determinants on ITPs’ career intention, was limited, this study makes a theoretical contribution by developing a research model anchored in the investment model and extends it by incorporating environmental and individual factors to understand the career commitment of ITPs. Second, we found that the threat of professional obsolescence exerted more influence on IT professionals with low professional self-efficacy. This finding helps to clarify the seemingly small correlation between professional obsolescence and career commitment. Finally, we proposed that individuals with different valences would hold different attitudes about their current relationships. This has been implied in the prior research regarding the victimized/shelter women [39,42] or website users [29] but, to our knowledge, never explicitly hypothesized or formally tested in the career commitment literature. This research provides evidence for the investment model regarding commitment to unfavorable relationships. Such findings advance theory and contribute to the foundation for future research aimed at improving our understanding of ITPs’ career commitment. In sum, we believe such a better understanding would assist employers in planning better strategies to increase favorable outcomes.
