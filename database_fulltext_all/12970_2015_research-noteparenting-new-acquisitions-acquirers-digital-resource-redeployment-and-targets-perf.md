# Research Note

# Parenting New Acquisitions: Acquirers’ Digital Resource Redeployment and Targets’ Performance Improvement in the U.S. Hospital Industry

Kui Du

Department of Management Science and Information Systems, College of Management, University of Massachusetts Boston, Boston, Massachusetts 02125, kui.du@umb.edu

his paper examines how information technology (IT) can contribute to value creation in horizontal acquisitions. TWe propose that acquisition value can be created when an acquirer redeploys its digital resources to its newly acquired businesses and consequently improves their operations. However, not all acquirers are equally capable of redeploying their digital resources. In this study, we propose two enabling factors pertaining to an acquirer’s IT resource base: IT extensiveness and IT standardization. We argue that the magnitude of digital resource redeployment increases when the acquirer has had extensive use of IT systems within its existing business units and has standardized IT systems across its business units. Moreover, the relative strength of the IT resources of the acquirer, as compared to those of the acquired business, also affects the reuse of the acquirer’s IT resources in its digital resource redeployment activities. We empirically test these hypotheses by tracking the IT and performance changes in 108 U.S. hospitals before and after they were acquired across a seven-year study timeframe.

Keywords: acquisitions; resource redeployment; IT extensiveness; IT standardization; IT gap; hospitals; efficiency and quality of healthcare

## 1. Introduction

Industry consolidation is a widely observed phenomenon in many previously fragmented industries. In the U.S. hospital industry, for example, the number of hospitals controlled by a multihospital system has increased by 20% between 2002 and 2012 (AHA 2014b).<sup>1</sup> In this once highly fragmented industry, multihospital systems now account for 54% of all U.S. hospitals, and the trend of consolidation is continuing (AHA 2014a, Brown et al. 2012). Consolidation in the hospital industry mostly happens through acquisitions (Cuellar and Gertler 2003, Dafny 2014), a phenomenon that has also been observed in many other consolidating industries such as automotive, financial services, defense, and airlines (Bower 2001). However, acquisitions are often disruptive and routinely fail to create value for acquiring firms. Many scholars have called for more research attention to acquisition value creation mechanisms (e.g., Haleblian et al. 2009, King et al. 2004). In this paper, we investigate how information technology (IT) can enable value creation during horizontal acquisitions, or acquisitions in which the two transaction parties are from the same industry.

Studies of IT in acquisitions are growing rapidly (Hedman and Sarker 2015). However, while practitioners have highlighted the strategic value of IT in acquisitions (e.g., Sarrazin and West 2011), the extant IT literature, overall, takes a relatively tactical focus on how to address IT challenges in acquisitions rather than how to leverage IT to create value from acquisitions. Recently, scholars have begun to develop theoretical frameworks of the strategic role of IT in acquisitions (e.g., Baker and Niederman 2014, Henningsson and Carlsson 2011, Yetton et al. 2013); however, large-scale empirical validation of various theories remains scarce (Toppenberg and Henningsson 2014).<sup>2</sup> Without better empirical evidence, researchers cannot yet say how IT can contribute to acquisition value creation.

This paper reports on a study of IT-enabled value creation during horizontal acquisitions in the context of the U.S. hospital industry.<sup>3</sup> We examine how multihospital systems, as acquirers, leverage their digital resources to improve the operations of their newly acquired hospitals. Using the metaphor of parenting, we posit that a multihospital system could benefit from its acquisitions by being a good parent organization: after acquisitions, it could redeploy its own digital resources to its newly acquired hospitals (the “targets” hereafter) to nurture their development. However, not all multihospital systems are equally prepared to be good parents. Rather, only those that have the necessary digital resources and capabilities can effectively nurture their acquired hospitals. In our study, we identified two enabling factors pertaining to a multihospital system’s IT resource base that prepared it for parenting its future acquisitions: the extensive use of IT systems within its existing member hospitals and the standardization of IT systems across its member hospitals.

The U.S. hospital industry offers a valuable empirical context for our research. Health economists have identified the motivations for hospital acquisitions as both market power and economies of scale (Dranove and Shanley 1995). However, empirical findings in the hospital context support primarily the market-power motivation: after consolidating multiple hospitals, multihospital systems often charge higher prices as a result of their increased market power (Connor et al. 1997, Dranove and Shanley 1995, Melnick and Keeler 2007). On the operational side, studies have found that, in general, hospitals do not improve their clinical performance after they are acquired (Cuellar and Gertler 2005, Hayford 2012, Ho and Hamilton 2000, Huckman 2006), nor do their operations become significantly more efficient (Dranove 1998, Dranove et al. 1996, Dranove and Shanley 1995). Evidence from these studies seems to strongly suggest an absence of economies of scale from hospital acquisitions, as they, on average, improve neither efficiency nor quality of care. However, a few hospital acquirers appear to be capable of doing acquisitions exceptionally well (Tanriverdi and Du 2011), suggesting that certain resources and capabilities of acquirers might determine the operational outcomes of their acquisitions. The extant literature is unclear on what those resources and capabilities could be. Thus, in the study reported here, we took a first step to investigate when and how certain IT resources and capabilities of acquirers can contribute to the efficiency and quality improvement of hospitals after acquisitions.

In §2, we review the relevant literature and develop a conceptual model of IT in acquisitions. Then, we present our research design in $\bar { \mathsf { S } } \bar { \mathsf { 3 } } ,$ report the results in $\ S 4 ,$ and discuss their implications in $\ S 5$ . Finally, §6 concludes this study.

## 2. Theory and Hypotheses

## 2.1. The Literature on Acquisitions, Horizontal Acquisitions, and the Role of IT

From a resource-based perspective, acquisitions happen as alternatives to discrete resource exchanges (Capron et al. 1998, Karim and Mitchell 2000). If possible, two organizations that find the potential for synergy between their resources would prefer realizing those synergies through contractual relationships and marketbased resource exchanges. However, carving out some resources to exchange them can be costly, destructive, or sometimes completely infeasible (Capron et al. 1998, Karim and Mitchell 2000). Acquisitions are one way to exchange nonperfectly tradable resources by allowing firms to sell or buy these resources in a bundle (Capron et al. 1998). From an acquirer’s perspective, a desirable target either can provide similar resources to expand the acquirer’s scale, as is more common in horizontal acquisitions, or can provide complementary resources to expand its scope, as is more common in other acquisitions (Karim and Mitchell 2000).

Acquisition scholars have paid specific attention to horizontal acquisitions, the economic motivation of which often includes economies of scale. However, in practice, a bigger organization cannot always unleash greater scale benefits. Merely combining two similar entities might create a weaker whole, rather than a stronger one. Both acquiring and newly acquired units need to restructure their resources to benefit from their combined scale. Moreover, because the acquired unit is usually smaller than the acquiring one, it has greater potential to benefit from the increased scale of the combined entity, but it will also need to embrace more significant restructuring (Brush 1996). Capron and her colleagues published a series of studies of resource redeployment between acquirers and targets after horizontal acquisitions (e.g., Capron 1999, Capron et al. 1998, Capron and Hulland 1999). Collectively, this series of studies presents two major findings regarding resource redeployment during horizontal acquisitions: (1) resource redeployment from acquirers to targets, not the opposite, creates the most value; and (2) redeployment of strategically valuable resources, not resources that are readily available in the market, creates the most value. In this study, we further develop this line of inquiry and investigate whether and how redeploying digital resources can create value after horizontal acquisitions.

Both researchers and practitioners are gradually recognizing that IT can play a strategic role for value creation during acquisitions (Sarrazin and West 2011). However, the literature on IT in acquisitions thus far has largely focused on the antecedents and consequences of postacquisition IT integration decisions, with IT integration often broadly defined as “changes in IS strategy, IS structure, and systems supporting the combined IS and business units that allow them to function as a whole” (Mehta and Hirschheim 2007, p. 145). A few studies have confirmed that acquirers capabilities to integrate IT across business units can significantly contribute to acquisition performance (Tanriverdi and Uysal 2011). Moreover, a recent publication showed that, although IT integration might not always be beneficial, it is more likely to be so in horizontal acquisitions (Tanriverdi and Uysal 2015). In this study, we unpack the broad construct labeled IT integration and focus only on the movement of digital resources after an acquisition.

Yetton et al. (2013) were among the first to document how IT can improve a firm’s “acquisition readiness,” meaning that firms had developed acquisition-compatible IT strategies, built scalable IT platforms, and codified IT integration capabilities as part of their day-to-day operations. As discussed in previous studies (Henningsson and Carlsson 2011, Merali and McKiernan 1993), IT could theoretically play either a reactive or a proactive role in acquisition value creation. However, in practice, IT can rarely be proactive because of the limited involvement of IT executives during the planning and decision-making phase of an acquisition. Thus, the emerging research focus on how IT prepares a firm to be a more competitive acquirer, or in our metaphor, a better parent, might contribute to justifying the strategic and proactive role of IT in acquisitions. This study also seeks to extend this line of inquiry.

## 2.2. Parenting Targets by Redeploying Acquirers’ Digital Resources

As noted earlier, for a horizontal acquisition to be successful, acquirers need to provide targets with resources and capabilities that the targets need, but to which they previously did not have access (Capron et al. 1998, Capron and Pistre 2002). That is, an acquisition creates value for an acquirer when the acquirer can purchase underperforming targets and reinvigorate them through resource redeployment. The premise, then, is that these targets cannot gain access to the redeployed resources on their own. Thus, redeploying strategically valuable resources from acquirers to targets has been repeatedly found to be decisive for an acquisition to create value (Capron et al. 1998, Capron and Hulland 1999, Capron and Pistre 2002). In these studies, resource redeployment includes either transferring resources to the location of another business unit or using resources for the benefit of another business unit without physical transfer (Capron 1999).

Resources might include physical resources, knowledge, process standards, or other intangible assets. However, digital resources are rarely discussed specifically in the acquisition context, despite the fact that digital resources can be essential to the fusion of firms’ IT and business strategies (Bharadwaj et al. 2013). In this paper, we posit that redeploying digital resources from acquirers to targets introduces unique benefits in horizontal acquisitions, especially hospital acquisitions. We define digital resources broadly, as any resources that can be expressed in 0/1 format, including primarily software, but also data, business rules, processes, and user identities codified in software.

We argue the following two benefits from redeploying digital resources from acquirers to targets. First,

Many digital resources are hard to develop initially but relatively easy to rede ploy or to scale. A recent example in the hospital context is the development of electronic medical record (EMR) systems. As of 2009, only 11.9% of hospitals had implemented EMR systems (Jha et al. 2010). Adoption barriers include a variety of technical, economic, behavioral, and organizational issues (Agarwal et al. 2010). Many hospitals, especially small rural ones, lagged behind in this digital transformation of healthcare and thus were competitively disadvantaged. Many sought out supportive parent organizations (Beteze 2010, DesRoches et al. 2012). Parenting opportunities therefore emerge for multihospital systems that already possess rare digital resources such as EMR systems, partly because of one unique feature of digital resources: their scalability. Digital resources, compared to other types of resources, are relatively scale free (Levinthal and Wu 2010), meaning that the use of digital resources by one business unit does not necessarily preclude other business units from using the same resources. In our research context, redeploying digital resources to support one more hospital after an acquisition is likely to incur only a small marginal cost if the acquiring multihospital system has already made the initial capital investment to develop such resources. Thus, joining a multihospital system and reusing the parent’s digital resources could be at least more economically efficient than building the resources by a standalone hospital from scratch. In fact, practitioners have identified this reasoning as one of the major motivations behind recent hospital acquisitions (Beteze 2010). In addition, implementing IT systems like EMR requires not only purchasing commodity IT products and services but also defining a hospital’s user roles, administrative and clinical processes, and data recording format; codifying them into software codes or configuration tables; and setting up complementary processes and organizational structures in support of system use. A multihospital system that redeploys its digital resources can provide not only technical solutions but also the knowledge and capabilities embedded in these resources. Trinity Health, one of the largest U.S. multihospital systems, gave an example of the benefits derived from redeploying its digital resources to its newly acquired hospitals as follows:

When we pull all these new hospitals onto our 6IT7 systems, number one, you get the advantage of the intellectual property that went into the design 6of the IT systems7. A lot of these community hospitals can’t afford to have people sit down and figure out what’s the best practice, what’s the best order set, what’s the medical efficacy of using one drug versus another. So, we leverage our clinical intellectual property that’s already been used to build this system.” (Tanriverdi and Du 2011, p. 7)

Second, redeploying digital resources can foster business collaboration between the acquiring and acquired units by forming a common digital resource base that they share. In addition to redeployed digital resources, targets will likely need other resources held by the acquirers’ businesses as well. For targets to tap into these resources, acquirers and targets need to build effective sharing and collaboration mechanisms so that they can work as a unified whole. Redeploying digital resources from acquirers to targets helps build a foun dation for effective sharing and collaboration. When targets adopt acquirers’ redeployed digital resources, the two parties start to share not only software applications but also the data definitions and business processes embedded in the applications (Ross 2003) In the hospital context, scholars have documented many benefits from a multihospital system’s use of common software across its member hospitals (e.g., Venkatesh et al. 2007). Common software reduces the complexity of overall organizational operations and increases transparency among business units, or among hospitals in the context of multihospital systems (Ross et al. 2006, Tanriverdi and Du 2011). It allows parent organizations to focus on optimizing the common resource infrastructure while business units can focus on their unique value-adding activities. Moreover, a common digital resource base within a firm also serves as a platform on which process innovations can be scaled up rapidly by codifying new processes into its enterprise-wide IT systems (McAfee and Brynjolfsson 2008). In our research context, for example, when an acquired hospital migrates to the same software used by the acquiring multihospital system, it gains better access to valuable data and reports of its peer hospitals, allowing it to identify its weaker areas and to seek improvement through benchmarking and learning. Best practices encoded in or supported by software can be more easily transferred to the newly acquired hospital when it uses the same IT systems as the acquirer. Finally, many hospital acquisitions can benefit from optimized patient referrals such as referring all of the difficult cardiovascular cases to the most competent hospital in the multihospital system (Huckman 2006). In such cases, it is important for the acquired hospital to share patient information seamlessly with its sibling hospitals so that patient referrals can be better coordinated. Common IT systems across hospitals can increase the efficiency and accuracy of patient information sharing.

In conclusion, we argue that, on average, the operational performance of an acquired hospital should improve when the acquirer redeploys digital resources to it. Using our parenting metaphor, an acquirer, as a good parent, needs to be capable of providing valuable resources to its new child and nurturing the child’s growth. We hypothesize the following relationship:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> Digital resource redeployment from an acquirer to a target is positively associated with the target’s performance improvement after the acquisition.

## 2.3. Ready for Parenting: Characteristics of Acquirers’ Digital Resources

The redeployment of digital resources after an acquisition is a deliberate management decision that reflects both the availability of acquirers’ resource stocks and the cost and benefit of the redeployment. Previous studies of IT in acquisitions have speculated that firms with a properly architected IT platform might be more prepared for future acquisitions (Ross et al. 2006, Yetton et al. 2013), but what specific characteristics of digital resources prepare a firm for acquisitions remains unclear. In this study, we investigate two architectural features that prepare a firm for parenting new acquisitions. In particular, we propose that an acquirer has greater potential to redeploy its digital resources when it is more advanced in two dimensions of its digital resources: IT extensiveness and IT standardization.

2.3.1. IT Extensiveness of Acquirers0 We define the IT extensiveness of a firm as the functional and organizational scope of IT tools and systems used in a firm to support its business activities. A firm with higher IT extensiveness has implemented a larger variety of IT tools and systems to inform, automate, and transform its business activities, and also uses each IT tool or system across a wider spectrum of organizational units (Broadbent et al. 1999, Keen 1991). Thus, the functional scope of IT systems in use and the organizational scope of their usage jointly depict the degree to which a firm leverages IT in support of its business operations. In our research context, a multihospital system with higher IT extensiveness has implemented a larger variety of health IT systems across a larger portion of its member hospitals.

When a firm with higher IT extensiveness becomes an acquirer, first, we argue that it is likely to find more opportunities to redeploy its digital resources to improve its targets’ operations. The more IT-extensive an acquirer is, the more opportunities it has to identify resource gaps in a specific target and fill the gaps by selectively redeploying its existing digital resources.

In addition, a firm with greater IT extensiveness has accumulated more experience in implementing various IT systems in different contexts, so it should have had more opportunities to build generic IT capabilities (Bharadwaj 2000). The tasks of implementing various IT systems in different organizational contexts share common routines such as discovering business requirements, codifying processes, managing change, training end users, and many others. Hence, implementing one IT system in one organizational unit often serves as a learning opportunity for implementing another system in the same unit or the same system in another unit later. With extensive IT systems deployed, a firm will have accumulated generic skills and will be able to envision and implement digital solutions across myriad digitization scenarios, which should reduce the challenge of redeploying digital resources to a target. Henningsson (2015) provided evidence of this learning spillover effect in a case study, which shows that acquirers can learn from IT challenges during regular operation periods and exploit the learning to address postacquisition IT challenges.

In our research context, a multihospital system with more extensive IT systems in use is more likely to find that some of its IT systems are needed by its newly acquired hospitals, either because the acquired hospital still uses manual processes or its existing IT systems are inadequate or ineffective. In addition, a multihospital system that has experienced a large variety of IT implementation projects in its different hospitals will have gradually accumulated capabilities for managing the same or similar IT projects, which eases the challenge of redeploying digital resources to its newly acquired hospitals. Because an acquirer with greater IT extensiveness has more digital resources available for redeployment and also is more capable of managing the redeployment projects, we hypothesize the following relationship:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2A).</sup> The acquirer’s IT extensiveness before an acquisition is positively associated with digital resource redeployment after the acquisition.

2.3.2. IT Standardization of Acquirers. We define IT standardization as the extent to which common IT tools and systems are implemented in different business units of a firm to support common organizational activities. For organizations with multiple business units, this construct reflects the commonality of IT resources across business units. We apply this construct to multihospital systems and consider each member hospital in a system as one business unit. IT standardization in a multihospital system thus reflects the extent to which different member hospitals use standard IT tools and systems in support of their common administrative or clinical activities.

When a firm with greater IT standardization becomes an acquirer, we argue that it has stronger incentives to redeploy its digital resources to its targets to achieve both economies of scale and cross-unit collaboration. IT standardization is a corporate-level strategy that aims to encourage different business units to work as a unified whole and to unlock the synergies among them (Ross et al. 2006). It can reduce costs through pooled purchasing power, simplified maintenance work, and reduced complexity of operations (Tanriverdi 2006). It can improve operations by enabling the sharing of best practices in common tasks and by enabling the coordination of interdependent tasks (Ravichandran et al. 2009, Tanriverdi 2006). We expect that a firm that has successfully implemented such a strategy in its existing business units will be prone to implement it in the newly acquired units to achieve the same benefits.

In addition, a firm with greater IT standardization has more standardizing experience and thus is more capable of seeing how the same standard IT tools and systems can benefit a newly acquired unit. When different organizational units accept standardized IT resources, they often have to sacrifice some local benefits in exchange for global optimization (Ross et al. 2006). Managing this local-global trade-off is a well-known managerial challenge. In acquisitions, conforming to the acquirer’s standard IT environment instead of investing in IT at its own pace may be particularly challenging for a target (Johnston and Yetton 1996, Mehta and Hirschheim 2007). However, as an organization gradually implements common IT standards across its organizational units, it accumulates the capabilities to prepare local units and facilitate their transition to global standards (Venkatesh et al. 2007). An acquirer with greater IT standardization will have more cross-unit IT standardization experiences to bring to bear, so that postacquisition deployment will be less disruptive (Tanriverdi and Du 2011, Tanriverdi and Uysal 2011).

In our research context, a multihospital system that has accomplished greater IT standardization across its member hospitals is more motivated to redeploy its standard digital resources to its newly acquired hospitals, and it is also more capable of doing so, because of its previous standardization experiences. In summary, we hypothesize the following relationship:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2B).</sup> The acquirer’s IT standardization before an acquisition is positively associated with digital resource redeployment after the acquisition.

## 2.4. The Acquirer-Target IT Gap

Different targets vary in the strength of their resource stocks before the acquisitions, which might shape acquirers’ postacquisition resource redeployment decisions (Capron et al. 1998). In this study, we examine the moderating role of resource asymmetry (between the acquirer and the target) on digital resource redeployment activities. First, we introduce the acquirer-target IT gap as the relative strength of an acquirer’s digital resources over the target’s, prior to the acquisition transaction.

2.4.1. Acquirers’ IT Extensiveness, Acquirer-Target IT Gap, and Digital Resource Redeployment. As we have argued, the magnitude of postacquisition digital resource redeployment will increase when an acquirer’s IT extensiveness increases. However, with a lower IT gap between an acquirer and its target, the target’s need for the acquirer’s digital resources will be lower, the target’s resistance to the acquirer’s resource redeployment is stronger, and the cost and risk of replacing the target’s existing digital resources could be higher. For such a target, there are fewer digital resource gaps to fill, and the acquirer’s experience in deploying digital resources is less valuable. Thus, a low acquirer-target IT gap might reduce the influence that the acquirer’s IT extensiveness has on its resource redeployment decision. By contrast, when the IT gap is high, the acquirer will find more opportunities to improve the target by addressing the target’s resource and capability deficiencies. Moreover, the acquirer and target often have different internal governance and control mechanisms that mitigate various operational, regulatory, and IT risks. After the target becomes a unit of the acquirer, to comply with the acquirer’s internal governance and control rules, it may need to implement certain software or adopt certain software configurations and data definitions (Tanriverdi and Uysal 2011). For example, a target that previously used manual processes for procurement may have to use automated IT systems after the acquisition, because its acquirer has more stringent rules on procurement fraud control. Thus, with a higher IT gap, an acquirer will face greater pressure to redeploy its digital resources to the target, not only for harvesting the benefits of improving the target’s operations but also to mitigate operational, regulatory, or IT risks to which the target may expose the combined entity. We hypothesize the following positive moderating effect of the acquirer-target IT gap:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3A).</sup> The acquirer-target IT gap enhances the influence of the acquirer’s IT extensiveness on digital resource redeployment from the acquirer to the target after an acquisition.

2.4.2. Acquirers’ IT Standardization, Acquirer-Target IT Gap, and Digital Resource Redeployment. As we have argued, the magnitude of postacquisition digital resource redeployment will increase when an acquirer’s IT standardization increases. However, with a higher IT gap between an acquirer and its target, the standardization experiences of an acquirer become less valuable. A high IT gap suggests that the target may be operating mainly with manual systems. Because the target has less IT systems to be standardized, the acquirer’s standardization experiences become less applicable to digital resource redeployment. With a high IT gap, redeploying digital resources to the target entails IT projects in which IT systems are built from scratch or rolled out to a new location. As argued above, the experiences of implementing new IT systems in new locations are accumulated as an acquirer’s IT extensiveness increases. Thus, for a given level of IT extensiveness, acquirers with either high or low IT standardization are likely to be capable of redeploying a similar magnitude of digital resources when the acquirer-target IT gap is high.

By contrast, an acquirer’s level of IT standardization matters greatly when the acquirer-target IT gap is low, which implies that the target was already using IT systems extensively before the acquisition. In this scenario, an acquirer with low IT standardization would undoubtedly leave the target’s IT systems as they are, whereas an acquirer with high IT standardization might actively pursue standardization with the target. Studies on the evolution of enterprise IT architecture offer relevant insights (Ross 2003, Ross et al. 2006). According to these studies, when IT standardization is low, an acquirer is operating in a siloed IT architecture, in which each business unit chooses its own IT systems. In this case, acquiring another business unit with its own IT systems in place just adds another silo of IT systems to the overall IT landscape. Such an acquirer is less likely to replace the target’s IT systems, as doing so would yield few benefits. When IT standardization is high, the acquirer has evolved from the siloed architecture to a standardized architecture (Ross et al. 2006). Such an acquirer will have more incentives and capabilities to maintain its architecture by also standardizing the digital resources of its targets. Overall, because the influence of IT standardization increases as the acquirertarget IT gap decreases, we hypothesize the following negative moderating relationship:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3B).</sup> The acquirer-target IT gap attenuates the influence of the acquirer’s IT standardization on digital resource redeployment from the acquirer to the target after an acquisition.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Sample and Data Sources

The study sampling frame consists of all of the hospital acquisitions in the United States from 2006 to 2008. We collected IT and performance information in a timeframe from one year prior to the transaction to three years after the transaction, making the data collection period 2005 to 2011.

The study data set was built by using five archival data sources. First, hospital-level IT application data were obtained from the HIMSS Analytics (HA) database.<sup>4</sup> Second, hospital acquisition data were primarily based on the entity history records in the HA database, supplemented by Standard and Poor’s Capital IQ database. Third, financial and operational data of hospitals were extracted from the Healthcare Cost Reporting Information System (HCRIS) database. Fourth, quality data for hospitals were extracted from the Hospital Compare database. Fifth, we collected data on hospitals’ case-mix index scores from the Hospital Inpatient Prospective Payment System (IPPS) database.

The unit of analysis in this study is a hospital acquired between 2006 and 2008. Each hospital acquired during this timeframe generates one observation in the data.<sup>5</sup> Our initial sample included 291 hospitals that had been acquired in 199 transactions. We then dropped the observations generated by specialty hospitals and by hospitals that were acquired multiple times in the study timeframe. We further dropped 52 observations because of missing IT data, 60 because of missing quality data, and 25 because of missing financial and other data. The final sample size for analysis was 108.

We measured the quality of care delivery process using 20 process-of-care quality indicators reported in the Hospital Compare database.<sup>8</sup> For example, one of these indicators is the percentage of patients with acute myocardial infarction who are given aspirin at arrival. For each quality indicator, Hospital Compare reports both the numerical score of the indicator for a specific hospital and also the number of patients sampled to calculate this score. We calculated a composite quality measure (QUL) for a given hospital year as the average of the quality scores across all 20 indicators, weighted by the number of patients used to calculate each quality indicator. Then, we calculated the quality improvement of a target as $T g t \_ Q U L I m p r v _ { t + 3 } = \dot { Q } U L _ { t + 3 } - \dot { Q } U L _ { t - 1 } . ^ { 9 }$

For both efficiency and quality measures, we further adjusted an acquired hospital’s performance improvement based on the average performance change in a control group of reference hospitals, as illustrated in Figure 2. We followed the logic of a difference-in-differences (DID) research design, subtracting the average efficiency or quality improvement of hospitals in the control group from the improvement of a focal acquired hospital. We built control groups in two different ways—using either all of the U.S. hospitals that were not acquired or only the nonacquired hospitals that resided in the same state as the focal acquired hospital. Thus, the performance improvement measure was adjusted according to either national or state trends. Our results are robust using unadjusted, national-trend-adjusted, or statetrend-adjusted measures. In §4, we report the results based on state-trend-adjusted measures.

## 3.2.2. Primary Explanatory Variables.

IT Extensiveness of Acquirer Acq\_EXT We measured the IT extensiveness of one hospital by counting the number of different health IT applications that were installed in this hospital. To ensure comparability, we counted only applications on a predetermined list of 71 health IT applications that are commonly needed by nonspecialty hospitals.<sup>10</sup> Then, because all acquirers were multihospital systems, we measured the IT extensiveness of an acquirer by calculating the average of the IT application counts of all of its member hospitals, weighted by the bed counts of these hospitals.

IT Standardization of Acquirer 4Acq\_STD50 We used an entropy measure to capture the degree of IT standardization across the member hospitals of an acquiring multihospital system. Different member hospitals of a multihospital system often install different brands of software products for the same type of IT application, and our measure intends to capture the diversity of the software products in use. Specifically, assume that (1) the total bed count of an acquirer in year t is $H _ { t } ;$ (2) for a specific type of IT application i (for example, an EMR system), this acquirer has implemented totally $M _ { i t }$ different brands of software products in its different member hospitals (for example, EPIC in some hospitals and Cerner in some others); and (3) the $j \mathrm { t h }$ software product (for example, EPIC) is implemented in hospitals whose total bed count is $B _ { j i t } ;$ then the entropy of this $i _ { t h }$ type of IT application in this acquirer is given by

$$
A c q _ {-} S T D _ {i t} = - \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {M _ {i t}} \biggl (\frac {B _ {j i t}}{H _ {t}} \ln \frac {H _ {t}}{B _ {j i t}} \biggr).
$$

The negative sign of the formula ensures that a larger value represents a higher level of standardization. Then, we took the average of the IT standardization levels across all of the IT applications to get an organizationlevel measure of standardization for an acquirer.

Digital Resource Redeployment 4DRR50 We measured digital resource redeployment by comparing a target’s IT application portfolio at year t + 2 and at year t − 1. Specifically, we counted the number of newly installed software products in the target hospital within two years after the acquisition, if and only if such software satisfied the following two criteria: (1) the installation had not been scheduled and was not in progress prior to the acquisition; and (2) the newly installed software product had been used by the acquirer before the transaction. The first criterion assures that the installation is attributable to the acquisition, and the second one assures that it is attributable to the acquirer’s resource redeployment.

Acquirer-target IT Gap 4ITGap50 We measured the IT gap between an acquirer and a target by comparing their IT extensiveness at year t − 1. We used only IT extensiveness as the proxy of the strength of an organization’s digital resources, because standardization does not apply to a target as a single unit. We then calculated the acquirer-target IT gap as the IT extensiveness of an acquirer minus the same IT extensiveness measure applied to the target.

3.2.3. Control Variables.<sup>11</sup> We controlled for a variety of characteristics of the acquirers, the targets, their commonalities or differences, and the transactions themselves. First, we controlled for four factors related to the similarities of the two parties prior to the transaction, including their for-profit status difference, the relative bed count of the target to the acquirer, their geographic proximity (measured as whether they were in the same state), and their organizational similarity (measured as whether the acquirer had at least one member hospital that resembles the target in terms of bed count, net income, and teaching status). Second, we controlled for four factors related to the characteristics of the target prior to the transaction, including its bed count, age, financial slack (measured by whether it was profitable), and its status as a teaching or nonteaching hospital. We further controlled for two more factors related to other changes in the target after the acquisition, including its bed count change within two years after the transaction, and the IT investments it made independent of the acquirer. Third, to account for the effect of learning from past acquisition experiences, we controlled for the acquisition experience of the acquirer in the three years prior to the focal acquisition. Fourth, we controlled for the IT governance mode of the acquirer as a binary variable, with 0 indicating that member hospitals of an acquirer could make software purchase decisions independently and 1 otherwise. Fifth, we controlled for the type of transaction to capture the different origins of acquired hospitals, because hospitals could be acquired as standalone entities or divestitures from another multihospital system, or they could be acquired when one multihospital system acquired another multihospital system in its entirety. Finally, we included a set of binary variables to represent different transaction years. Table 1 presents descriptive statistics and correlations among the study variables.

compared the average performance improvement of a group of acquired hospitals with the average of a control group of reference hospitals that were not acquired in the same period. The DID results are consistent with previous findings in hospital acquisitions: on average, being acquired did not significantly improve either the efficiency or the quality of the acquired hospitals.<sup>12</sup> However, the variance of acquisition effects is also large. We thus further investigate whether the variance of acquisition effects on targets can be explained by acquirers’ IT characteristics and digital resource redeployment activities, as we hypothesized.

We used three-stage least squares (3SLS) models to test our hypotheses, for two reasons. First, we need to account for the endogeneity of digital resource redeployment, because redeployment is a management decision that could be made based on factors that also influence acquisition performance. Second, the efficiency and the quality improvement of an acquired hospital can be simultaneously influenced by some unknown common factors. Three-stage least squares models can address both issues (Greene 2002).

In specifying our 3SLS models, we treated the level of digital resource redeployment (DRR) as the endogenous variable and then used three instrumental variables for identification, including the acquirer’s IT extensiveness (Acq\_EXT), its IT standardization $( A c q \_ S T D )$ and its IT governance mode $( A c q \_ I T G O V ) . ^ { \mathrm { i } 3 }$ The first two instrumental variables have already been justified in $\ S 2 ,$ in which we explained how an acquirer’s preacquisition IT characteristics can influence the resource redeployment decision. We reason that the state of the acquirer’s IT resources (before the transaction) on the target’s performance (after the transaction) should not be directly related but instead be mainly connected through digital resource redeployment. In addition, we include the third instrumental variable, the acquirer’s IT governance mode (whether the acquirer allows its member hospitals to make independent software purchase decisions). We reason that the acquirer’s IT governance mode could influence the decision to redeploy digital resources to a newly acquired target, but it cannot influence the target’s performance in significant ways other than through the redeployment decision.

Finally, we controlled for the moderating variable, the acquirer-target IT gap (ITGap), in all three equations and included its interaction items with Acq\_EXT and Acq\_STD in the DRR equation to test our hypotheses. We standardized Acq\_EXT, Acq\_STD, and ITGap before creating their interaction items to simplify the interpretation of their regression coefficients. Our 3SLS model is specified as follows:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} T g t \_ E F C I m p r v _ {t + 3} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} D R R _ {t + 2} + C o n t r o l s + \varepsilon_ {1}, \\ T g t \_ Q U L I m p r v _ {t + 3} = \gamma_ {0} + \gamma_ {1} D R R _ {t + 2} + C o n t r o l s + \varepsilon_ {2}, \\ D R R _ {t + 2} = \delta_ {0} + \delta_ {1} A c q \_ E X T _ {t - 1} + \delta_ {2} A c q \_ S T D _ {t - 1} \\ \qquad + \delta_ {4} I T G a p _ {t - 1} \times A c q \_ E X T _ {t - 1} \\ \qquad + \delta_ {5} I T G a p _ {t - 1} \times A c q \_ S T D _ {t - 1} \\ \qquad + \delta_ {6} A c q \_ I T G O V _ {t - 1} + C o n t r o l s + \varepsilon_ {3}, \end{array} \right.
$$

where $\beta , \gamma ,$ and $\delta ,$ are regression coefficients, $\varepsilon _ { 1 } , \varepsilon _ { 2 } ,$ and $\varepsilon _ { 3 }$ are error terms of equations, and t is the acquisition year. As introduced in §3.2, Tgt\_ $E F C I m p r v _ { t + 3 }$ and $T g t \_ Q U L I m p r v _ { t + 3 }$ are the efficiency and the quality improvement of a target, adjusted by state trends. The other notations can be found in §3.2 and also in Table $^ { 2 , }$ which also presents our results.

In Table $^ { 2 , }$ we include two panels of equations, with three equations estimated simultaneously as one 3SLS model in each panel. In Panel 1, we present the results without interaction items, and in Panel $^ { 2 , }$ we present the full model results. To examine the validity of including interaction items in the model, we compared the AIC (Akaike Information Criterion) and BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion) scores of Panels 1 and 2 and performed the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test for nested models based on their log-likelihood values. All of these tests suggest that the model in Panel 2 is a better fit with our data, thus supporting the inclusion of interaction items in the model.

In Equations 2 and 3 in both panels, the coefficients of DRR are significant and positive, providing strong supportive evidence for Hypothesis 1 that the digital resource redeployment from the acquirer to the target improves the target’s operational performance in terms of both the efficiency and quality of its care delivery $( \beta = 0 . 1 3$ and 0.19, respectively, both p-values < 0005, Panel 2). In Equation 1 in both panels, the coefficients of Acq\_EXT and Acq\_STD are significant and positive $( \beta = 1 0 . 1 3$ and 6.63, respectively, both p-values $< 0 . 0 1$ , Panel 2), thus providing evidence in support of Hypotheses H2A and H2B that the acquirer’s IT extensiveness and standardization lead to digital resource redeployment activities.

For Hypotheses H3A and H3B, we found mixed results. In Equation 1 of Panel 2, the interaction between the acquirer-target IT gap and the acquirer’s IT standardization is significant and negative $( \beta = - 4 . 8 9 $ p-value < 0001, Panel 2), thus supporting Hypothesis H3B that the acquirer-target IT gap attenuates the influence of the acquirer’s IT standardization. However, we do not find empirical support for Hypothesis H3A $( \beta = - 0 . 6 0$ , not significant, Panel 2), in which we had hypothesized that the IT gap enhanced the influence of the acquirer’s IT extensiveness.

We speculated that there could be both conceptual and empirical explanations for the lack of support for Hypothesis H3A. Conceptually, previous studies suggest that postacquisition IT decisions are not always rational. In many cases, an “aura of conquer” dominates the combined entity after an acquisition (Allred et al. 2005), and postacquisition IT decisions thus may simply reflect the “acquirer’s way” (Mehta and Hirschheim 2007). An irrational acquirer might impose its IT resources and standards on the target regardless of the target’s needs. Empirically, the operationalization of an IT gap as the difference in IT extensiveness of the acquirer and target is coarse-grained and may not fully capture the relative strength of the digital resources in the two parties. Moreover, this operationalization causes relatively high correlations among the moderating and independent variables, consequently increasing the variance of the estimated regression parameters and making it harder to conclude the significance of statistical relationships.<sup>14</sup>

## 5. Discussion, Contributions, and Limitations

## 5.1. Discussion of Results

The first major finding of this study is the performance impact of digital resource redeployment. Strategy scholars have reported that acquirers often redeploy R&D, manufacturing, marketing, managerial, and financial resources to targets (Capron et al. 1998), but only the redeployment of R&D and managerial resources actually create value for acquirers (Capron and Pistre 2002). Our study extends the strategy literature by confirming the positive performance effect of redeploying digital resources. However, our findings do not simply extend the literature by adding another category of resources that, when redeployed, can create value. We offer two new insights. First, the general argument has been that only redeploying strategically valuable resources can create value, because redeploying commodity resources is no different from the target’s purchasing the same resources in the market (Barney 1988). If this is true, then our study provides indirect evidence in support of one of the most important claims in the information systems (IS) field: that digital resources have strategic value. Second, Capron and Pistre (2002) found that redeploying certain context-dependent resources such as marketingrelated ones actually destroyed value. Although the portability of digital resources has not been conclusively studied in the IS literature, our findings suggest that digital resources appear at least portable enough to redeploy in horizontal acquisitions.

The second major finding of this study has to do with the determinants of digital resource redeployment during acquisitions: the IT extensiveness and standardization of an acquirer. These two determinants reflect two different rationales underlying resource redeployment. When an acquirer has extensive IT in use, it wants to reuse its digital resources to fill in the resource gap of the target. When an acquirer has a standard IT environment, it wants to apply the same standard to the target, so that the target better collaborates and integrates with the rest of the firm. By identifying IT extensiveness and standardization as two enabling factors for digital resource redeployment, this study extends the acquisition literature by shedding light on how to assess firms’ acquisition readiness.

Third, this study reveals new insights about the resource gap between acquirers and targets. Acquisition studies in the management literature have argued that filling resource gaps is one of the most common motivations for acquisitions (Eschen and Bresser 2005) and that acquirers always tend to redeploy resources from their strong position to the weak position (Capron et al. 1998). However, in the IS field, Tanriverdi and Uysal (2015) refuted this argument by showing that sometimes redeploying IT resources and capabilities from their stronger position to the weaker one destroys rather than creates value. With finer grained measures, our study reveals that the influence of the acquirer-target resource gap might not be direct but rather contingent on the rationale behind resource redeployment. With a larger gap, an acquirer redeploys its resources to its targets, regardless of whether the acquirer is pursuing standardization or not, because value-creating opportunities arise from filling the target’s resource gap. With a smaller gap, only an acquirer with high IT standardization has incentives to redeploy its standard resources, because that is when value-creating opportunities arise from migrating the target onto the common digital resource base of the acquirer.

## 5.2. Contributions and Implications

This study contributes to the acquisition literature with a parenting logic for acquisition value creation. With proper “parenting,” an acquirer can benefit from purchasing underperforming targets and nurturing them through resource redeployment. Although acquiring and then developing previously underperforming businesses has long been suggested as one type of acquisition strategy (Chatterjee 1992), the challenge is to identify and seize appropriate parenting opportunities. Acquirers need to have unique resources or capabilities that can be used to develop their targets, or else their acquisitions are more likely to fail (Barney 1988). In this study, we show how acquirers’ accumulated IT resources and experiences could make them a better parent organization. Thus, the study reveals a new IT-enabled acquisition strategy—seek underperforming firms and reinvigorate them by serving the acquirer’s digital resources.

The parenting logic also extends the acquisition literature by putting more research attention on the acquired businesses. We know that an acquisition often disrupts rather than develops the acquired business (e.g., Datta 1991, Hitt et al. 1991, Paruchuri et al. 2006). Acquisition practitioners have also noted that postacquisition integration often entails a rather destructive mindset, diminishing acquirers’ chances to benefit from their acquisitions (Kale et al. 2009). However, probably because of data limitations, in previous studies the fate of the acquired business has often been neglected once it became part of its acquirer. By highlighting parenting instead of integration, this study extends the acquisition literature by revealing in detail how acquired businesses can benefit from being acquired.

Second, this study also contributes to the emerging research on how IT resources and capabilities can increase firms’ acquisition readiness. The IT-acquisition literature has intensively documented the challenges of managing IT activities in the implementation phase of an acquisition (e.g., Mehta and Hirschheim 2007). Researchers have also suggested that the role of IT executives in acquisitions could be more proactive (Henningsson and Carlsson 2011, Merali and McKiernan 1993). However, acquisitions after all are relatively rare events, and the deals are often kept confidential before their public announcements; it is not surprising that IT executives find it difficult to prepare for acquisitions that are unknown to them. This study substantiates the idea that, instead of preparing for a particular future transaction, IT executives could focus on increasing a firm’s acquisition readiness (Yetton et al. 2013). A firm can become more prepared for future acquisitions if it achieves higher IT extensiveness and standardization during its regular operations. The findings also support claims that a standard IT platform will have value in acquisitions (Jain and Ramesh 2015, Ross et al. 2006).

Third, this paper also contributes to studies of postacquisition IT integration. Despite the consensus that IT integration approaches should be aligned to business integration objectives (Henningsson and Carlsson 2011, Wijnhoven et al. 2006), several studies have shown that acquirers sometimes made suboptimal IT integration decisions, because of the unanticipated risks of their desired IT integration approach (e.g., Johnston and Yetton 1996, Mehta and Hirschheim 2007). By identifying acquirers’ IT extensiveness and standardization as two enabling factors and the acquirer-target IT gap as a contingent factor, our study provides comprehensive guidance on how to assess the feasibility of digital resource deployment.

Finally, this study has practical implications regarding the debate over hospital acquisitions. Some healthcare researchers have provided evidence that hospital acquisition creates monopolies and consequently increases care price (e.g., Melnick and Keeler 2007), and others have argued that hospital acquisitions are necessary for scale-related benefits (e.g., Beteze 2010). Our study provides deeper insights. It shows that, although acquisitions are not universally effective in solving the cost and quality problems of healthcare, they can be effective if acquiring multihospital systems have extensive and standard IT resources and subsequently redeploy these resources to their acquired hospitals.

## 5.3. Limitations and Future Studies

One limitation of the study is that our data does not allow us to take into account other organizational and technological integration activities carried out as part of acquisition integration, such as personnel rotation, knowledge sharing, and process reengineering in both business and IT functions. Another empirical limitation of the study is the coarse-grained measurement of our IT constructs, which is based on the presence of various IT systems rather than the effectiveness of those systems in use. In addition, we only theorized and measured IT standardization across business units, but we ignored possible cross-unit integration or other functional interdependencies among IT systems in different units. Last, we did not consider possible outsourcing arrangements of these IT systems. In fact, our knowledge about the influence of outsourcing on acquisition performance is scarce and certainly warrants future study.

A few boundary conditions of our findings need to be noted. First, our theory assumes that targets keep continuous and separate operations after they are acquired; it does not apply where acquirers dissolve targets substantially and only selectively retain some acquired resources such as intellectual property or customer relations. Second, our theory assumes the value-creation objective of acquisitions, and thus it may not apply where executives make acquisition decisions for other reasons, such as management hubris or regulatory pressure. Third, we developed our theory to explain only horizontal acquisitions. We expect future studies to deepen our understanding of parenting activities in other types of acquisitions.

Last, although our study is based on the U.S. hospital industry, we believe that the findings can be generalized to horizontal acquisitions in other industries, as long as acquirers’ digital resources are applicable to, and needed by, their targets. In horizontal acquisitions, acquirers and targets often share similar business processes and serve common customers, which makes it both possible and desirable that acquirers and targets operate on a common digital resource base. We observed some anecdotal evidence in support of our findings from the acquisition practice of CEMEX, a Mexican cement manufacturer (Ross et al. 2006) and Procter and Gamble, a multinational consumer products manufacturer, (Fulton 2011), but large-scale empirical validation of our findings in other industry contexts and other parts of the world are certainly still needed and may generate more insights.

## 6. Conclusion

Both practitioners and IS scholars have discussed the strategic role of IT in acquisitions, however, large-scale empirical studies that link IT resources to acquisition performance remain scarce. Our research developed and tested a model encompassing characteristics of the acquirer’s and the target’s IT resources prior to an acquisition, the resource redeployment activities after the acquisition, and the consequent performance effect on the target after the redeployment. It thus provides a comprehensive view of one IT-enabled value creation mechanism in horizontal acquisitions.

As an important economic activity, acquisitions have attracted great attention from academia. Both strategy and finance scholars have conducted a large volume of acquisition studies (Haleblian et al. 2009). By contrast, there are relatively few acquisition studies from the IS perspective. We end this paper by calling for more research attention to the role of IT in acquisitions.
