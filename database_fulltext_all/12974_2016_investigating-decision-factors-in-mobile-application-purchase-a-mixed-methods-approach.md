# Investigating Decision Factors in Mobile Application Purchase: A Mixed Methods Approach

## Abstract

The growth of the smart devices market and the development of mobile applications (Apps) for them have given rise to an App economy. Sales of mobile applications are a key revenue source in this economy, with the worldwide marke xpected to grow to US\$75 billion by 2017. Despite the trend, many mobile Apps fail to attract customers, yet there has been a lack of research and understanding of the factors that influence the decisions to buy them. Motivated thus, this study examines what factors people consider in deciding to buy mobile Apps for their smartphones. This mixed-methods investigation first adopts an exploratory and qualitative approach to identify the purchase decision factors based on interviews with consumers. It then undertakes a quantitative, confirmatory study using a survey to test the model derived using mental accounting theory and the findings of the exploratory study. The results show the direct and indirect effects of five factors, i.e. word of mouth about App, App usefulness, monetary value of App, App trialability, and App enjoyment, on the intention to purchase an App. In this manner, the study advances our understanding of the decision factors leading to the purchase of mobile Apps. It also informs developers and marketers on how to promote the sales of Apps for revenue generation.

Keywords: Smartphone, mobile App, purchase intention, mental accounting theory, mixed methods

## 1. Introduction

With the rapid growth of the smart device (e.g., smartphone and tablet PC) market and the development of mobile applications (Apps) for these devices, a new economic landscape called the App economy<sup>2</sup> has emerged. As of August 2015, the two largest App markets, App Store<sup>3</sup> and Google Play<sup>4</sup>, offered about 1,700,000 Apps each. Of these about half of the Apps are paid Apps (average price estimated at US\$1.24<sup>3</sup>), with 50 percent of iPhone users having downloaded at least 1 paid App a month [57]. In 2012, App Store’s daily revenues from App sales amounted to over US\$15 million, while Google’s corresponding figures totaled US\$3.5 million<sup>5</sup>. As the number of global smartphone users increases with over 2 billion users expected by 2015<sup>6</sup>, the worldwide smartphone App market is also forecast to grow to US\$75 billion by 2017<sup>7</sup>.

In the App economy, while companies can generate revenue from other sources, such as advertisements within Apps, and selling digital items for Apps [43], App sales constitute the largest component. Indeed, consumers’ purchase and use of paid Apps is essential for the business success of developers and service providers. As a result, App developers have been exploring ways to increase their sales revenues, and App markets (e.g., Google Play and App Store) are adopting marketing practices such as online feedback mechanisms (e.g., customer reviews), ranking Apps in terms of popularity and sales, and allowing trial versions of Apps. However, a large fraction of Apps do not break even - 17% of developers generate no revenue and 52% of developers make less than US\$100 per month<sup>8</sup>. With relatively high development costs and low prices of Apps, sales must be stimulated significantly to recover costs and generate profits. Encouraging sales requires understanding customers’ decision factors for purchasing Apps.

Despite the need to understand what consumers are looking for in order to enhance App sales, little research exists on how and why people decide to buy them. Research in this area has mainly addressed issues of App market analysis [e.g., 21], App design and development [e.g., 42], App adoption [e.g., 3], and App usage [e.g., 80], as seen in our review in section 2. Here, we would like to clarify that App adoption differs to some degree from App purchase. Specifically, purchasers of paid Apps that are the focus of this study, play dual roles of technology buyers and adopters, i.e., they must buy the paid Apps before adopting and using them. Indeed, most people purchase Apps for personal use, with the cost of such voluntary purchase being borne by the individual. This differs from people’s adoption of free apps or of IS that have been provided by the organization. In this respect, we see a limitation of technology acceptance models in explaining App purchase decision making, which we overcome by drawing from theory on consumer decision making. Overall, missing from previous research is an explanation of individuals’ App purchase decision factors that can lead to revenue generation for developers and marketers. This gap in the literature and the practical problem it represents, provide the motivation for this study.

This study, thus, aims to investigate the key factors underlying consumers’ decision to buy Apps for their smartphones as its research question. For this purpose, we adopt a mixed methods approach of an exploratory, qualitative study followed by a confirmatory, quantitative (survey) study [53]. A mixed methods approach is potentially superior to a single method design because it can provide rich insights into phenomena and develop novel theoretical explanations [72]. Of the 7 different purposes of mixed methods research suggested in the prior literature [72], we subscribe to the ‘developmental’ purpose in which a qualitative study is used to identify or develop constructs and hypotheses, after which a quantitative study is conducted to test the hypotheses. Thus, in our research, the exploratory study identifies potential purchase decision factors based on interviews with App buyers. Because an individual’s decision to buy an App involves framing and evaluation of the purchase transaction, we interpret and interrelate these decision factors based on mental accounting theory [67] to develop a model which explains consumer decision making for App purchase. The model of the decision factors influencing App purchase intention was then tested through a survey of 411 respondents and largely

supported.

This work contributes to information systems (IS) research by adding to the mobile Apps and online purchase literatures and extending the use of mental accounting theory. Particularly, it advances the understanding of mobile App purchase decision factors and their interrelationships using a mixed methods approach. The study can also inform developers and marketers on how to promote the sales of Apps for revenue generation. The paper starts by reviewing the previous literature on online purchase and mobile Apps adoption to position our study and identify the gaps that it seeks to address. This is followed by a description of our exploratory study and its findings. The main, confirmatory study section begins with a discussion of the framework of mental accounting theory and follows with our model development. After presenting the empirical results of the model test using a survey, we discuss the limitations, theoretical and practical implications, and conclude with a summary of the study.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Online Purchase Literature

We classified the findings from the online purchase literature as per four essential elements (vendor, transaction channel, customer, and product) of an online transaction between a vendor and customer. Based on our review (see Table 1), prior research on consumer decision making in e-commerce suggested several purchase decision factors related to the vendor e.g., trust and risk [23, 25, 37, 61, 62], transaction channel e.g., website design and quality [23, 28, 39, 74, 75], customer e.g., web skills, attitude, and satisfaction [39, 61, 77], and product e.g., product quality, product value, price and product review [10, 25, 36, 51, 75, 84]. Much of the prior work [9, 21, 23, 26, 33, 35, 53] concerned book purchase at an online store. However, consumers may utilize different decision factors depending on the product (in this case, paid App) and purchase context (in this case, App Store). Thus, the prior findings on online purchase may not be completely relevant to explain the purchase of mobile Apps at the App Store.

Specifically, decision making for mobile App purchase could exhibit several differences and unique aspects. First, vendor issues are less likely to be of concern to customers here because the primary

App markets are provided and managed by trustworthy vendors (e.g., Apple and Google)<sup>9</sup>. This differs from previous findings where the lack of trust in an online vendor has been identified as a salient barrier inhibiting Internet transactions [23, 25, 37, 61]. Second, compared to prior findings on online book purchase, transaction channel-related factors (e.g., website quality of a vendor) may be less salient for purchase decision making in the App market because the two major channels (the App Store and Google Play) are typically easy to use and organized to help consumers search for, review, and purchase Apps.

Third, customer-related decision factors, e.g., attitude towards the purchase, could be influential as per the theory of planned behavior [1]. Also, if a buyer was satisfied with his or her previous purchase at a store, he or she may return to the store and consider repurchase there [77]. Such a belief, however, has been challenged by counter arguments that higher customer satisfaction does not necessarily lead to higher repurchase [78]. In fact, Anderson and Srinivasan [6] found that the effect of satisfaction is contingent on various other factors (e.g., inertia, convenience motivation, and purchase size). Thus, when a consumer considers buying a mobile App from an App Store, he or she may pay more attention to the characteristics of the App rather than his or her previous satisfaction at the store.

Fourth, product-related factors are likely to be of concern in mobile App purchase as in other online purchase contexts [36, 51] because consumers’ purchase decision could hinge on whether the App meets their needs. Thus, we expect that App utility would affect consumers’ purchase decisions of mobile Apps. In addition, reviews such as online word of mouth about Apps could affect their purchase decisions as in prior e-commerce studies [84]. However, here too, there is a difference from previous research on online book purchase [10, 23, 25, 28, 37, 39, 61]. Specifically, Apps are software products while books are physical products (except digital books). Thus, software trials may be relevant for software products, which have not been identified as a decision factor in previous research on online book purchase. In sum, we posit that purchase decision making in the App market could be more influenced by the product itself, and less by vendor and channel issues.

## 2.2. Mobile App Literature

Considering the differences mentioned above, it is important to separately examine what drives consumers in making their purchase decisions about mobile Apps [34]. However, despite the growing interest in mobile Apps, there is a lack of research on mobile App purchase decision factors. This was evident from our review of mobile App literature published in the last 10 years in IS and marketing journals. Essentially, prior research on mobile Apps has mainly focused on App market analysis [21, 27, 33], App design and development [30, 42, 48, 68], App adoption [3, 4, 6, 11, 31, 58, 73], and App usage [12, 50, 63, 80, 83].

Regarding App market analysis, prior studies have proposed a taxonomy for mobile commerce innovations [33] and discussed the mobile App market from a developer’s perspective [27]. Previous research has also examined the relationship between the App rank and its demand in the market [21]. With respect to mobile App design and development, prior studies have proposed several design approaches [30], frameworks for testing and development of Apps [48, 68, 82], and a mobile platform [42]. With respect to App usage, prior research examined the motivators affecting the disclosure of personal information in the use of location-based mobile services [83], the consequences of mobile App usage [12, 50, 80], and user satisfaction [63].

Prior studies also examined the adoption of other types of mobile Apps, such as Apps for note-taking [58], mobile learning [52], location-based services [31], and general Apps for Internet access, mapping services, and games [73]. However, these studies focused on free Apps and made use of adoption theories such as the technology acceptance model, theory of planned behavior, and the diffusion of innovations [4, 11, 31, 73]. Thus, we see that there has been a lack of research on the adoption of paid mobile Apps, which require purchase prior to adoption and use. As mentioned earlier, technology adoption theories used in these studies may not be able to fully explain the purchase of mobile Apps, for which consumer decision making theories should be relevant.

In sum, our literature review and analysis indicate that: (1) there is a paucity of research examining mobile App purchase; (2) prior research on online purchase decision making may not be differs from mobile App purchase; and (4) background theories used to explain mobile App adoption may not be fully relevant for mobile App purchase. Thus, although previous studies have contributed substantially to our understanding of mobile App related phenomena, our research investigates the hitherto unexplored question of identifying the decision factors behind the purchase of paid mobile Apps. We now describe the method and findings of our exploratory study.

## 3. Exploratory Study

## 3.1. Research Method

Following the guidelines for developmental mixed methods studies [72], we adopted a qualitative research method first to explore the potential purchase decision factors for mobile Apps by interviewing mobile App buyers. An interview approach has several strengths such as the capability to focus directly on the research topic and to deliver perceived causal inferences [79]. We conducted interviews with 30 randomly selected buyers who had purchased Apps at least once for their smartphones in the last 1 month <sup>10</sup> . The majority of the interviewees were Apple iPhone users. The sample consisted of 10 undergraduate students, 9 graduate students, and 11 professionals, with an average age of 27.3 years. Eighteen of them were males (60%), and 12 were females (40%). Their average length of smartphone use was 11.39 months. The interviews were conducted one-on-one in an informal environment and lasted from 10 to 20 minutes. The interviewees were asked a series of questions, i.e., what mobile Apps they bought, the factors that affected their purchase of each mobile App, and further questions to clarify the factors. As a token incentive, we offered a gift voucher worth US\$5 to each interviewee.

The interviews were recorded and transcribed, after which the transcripts were analyzed using open and axial coding following principles from Corbin and Strauss [13]. The coding was performed by three researchers, with one coder uninvolved in the data collection to avoid potential bias. During open coding, each coder examined the interview transcripts line by line to find concepts within the textual data that could explain interviewees’ mobile App purchase. The coders then discussed each concept identified and named it after arriving at a consensus. We then grouped these concepts into broader categories (i.e., higher level concepts) that reflected commonalities among the codes, which helped reduce the number of concepts and evolve them into theoretical constructs. Open coding was thus used to delineate concepts and group them into meaningful categories. During axial coding, we related the categories to develop causal relationships that can tentatively explain mobile App purchase, i.e., our core category. Subsequently, the categories (constructs) were interpreted and inter-related using a framework based on mental accounting theory, thus developing our research model (as described in sections 4.1 and 4.2).

## 3.2. Data Analysis and Results

Overall, 21 concepts were identified from the open coding of the interview transcripts regarding the decision factors for mobile App purchase. After initial coding, similar concepts were grouped into broader, higher-order categories, which were intended to evolve into theoretical constructs. As a result, 6 major categories were obtained, i.e., Word-of-Mouth (WOM) about an App, App usefulness, App trialability, monetary value of App, and App enjoyment, and one unspecified (‘Others’) category (see

Table 3 for details). The inter-rater agreement scores averaged 0.83, while Cohen’s Kappa scores averaged 0.82. The core category here is mobile App purchase which is our outcome of interest.

In the context of our study, WOM about App refers to the positive evaluation from other users about the App of interest. For this category, interviewees mentioned that they decided to purchase their paid Apps because the Apps are “recommended by friends”; the Apps are “highly rated in the App Store”; the Apps are “popular”; and by considering “customer reviews,” “feedback from other users,” and “App rating information in the App Store.” App trialability indicates the extent that the trial version allowed the individual to adequately try out the target App before purchase. With respect to this category, interviewees mentioned that they decided to purchase their paid Apps because they “liked the App after using the trial version”; they are “satisfied with the trial version”; they could “test the App” before they made a purchase.” App usefulness implies the instrumental utility derived from the target App. For this category, interviewees mentioned that they decided to purchase their paid Apps because the Apps have “convenient functions”; and the Apps are “useful,” and “necessary for work,” “information collection,” “learning,” and “communication.” App enjoyment refers to the degree of pleasure or joy from the use of the target App. With respect to this category, interviewees mentioned that they decided to purchase their paid Apps because the Apps are “playful” and just for “fun” and “pleasure”; they were “curious” about the Apps; and they wanted to “enjoy free time” by using the Apps. Monetary value of App indicates the economic utility derived from the target App in comparison to its price. Regarding this category, interviewees mentioned that they decided to purchase their paid Apps because the Apps are “cheap”; the Apps are “worth its price”; and the Apps have “value for the price.”

As expected, decision factors related to the vendor and transaction-channel did not surface in the interviews. <sup>11</sup> Rather, the identified factors represent App-related issues (App usefulness and App enjoyment), bargain (Monetary value of App), and marketing practices (WOM about App and App trialability). Monetary value of App as a customer’s App evaluation also represents customer-related factors like attitude [61] because attitude stems from a summary evaluation of a psychological object [2]. There were “other” concepts, such as ease of use, compatibility, and interest, that were not included in the model as they did not garner much mention in the interviews (covered only 3.28% of the codes).

We then related the identified categories through axial coding. Designating mobile App purchase as the core category (dependent variable), we were able to relate this category with the other 5 categories representing the purchase decision factors as its antecedents. In the main, confirmatory study, we then interpreted the 5 decision factors from the exploratory study based on mental accounting theory [67] as will be described next. As mental accounting theory is comprehensive in accounting for all the decision factors from our exploratory study, we used it to derive the interrelationships among the factors and with the dependent variable resulting in the theoretical framework and research model described in the following sections.

## 4. Confirmatory Study

## 4.1. Theoretical Framework

Mental accounting theory explains human decision making under conditions of risk and uncertainty from a value (i.e., utility<sup>12</sup>) maximization perspective [67]. In purchasing a new mobile App, there could be a risk that the App does not meet the buyer’s needs [47]. There could also be uncertainty in the buyer’s mind about the App quality [47]. Thus, people often make decisions about purchasing Apps under conditions of risk and uncertainty. At the same time, customers are making purchase decisions to maximize their total utility. Thus, we consider mental accounting theory relevant to understand the decision making for mobile App purchase.

According to the theory, there are two types of utility i.e., acquisition utility and transaction utility, that affect consumer choice (see Figure 1). Acquisition utility refers to the value of the goods received compared to the payment (e.g., the worth of a bottle of wine in comparison with the price). It is a function of the equivalent value of the product and its objective price [67]. In the context of our study, acquisition utility would be perceived based on a comparison between the utility (i.e., benefit) of the mobile App and its objective price (i.e., purchase price). Thus, acquisition utility would equate with the monetary value of the App, i.e., the App benefits with respect to its price. The benefits could be functional and/or hedonic [60, 70]. In our study, App usefulness represents the functional benefit of the App, while App enjoyment denotes the hedonic benefit.

Figure 1. Conceptual Framework of Mental Accounting Theory

The second type of utility, i.e., transaction utility, refers to “the perceived merits of a transaction or deal [67, p. 19]” (e.g., reducing uncertainty and risks of product quality by purchasing a bottle of wellrated wine). Thaler [67] further noted that transaction utility can be assessed by the discrepant price between actual price and reference price. However, such transaction utility measurement assumes that reference prices are known with certainty [69]. When the reference price is uncertain, the measurement of transaction utility based on the discrepant price does not work well [69]. This is often the case in mobile App purchase where individual buyer’s reference prices could be uncertain. Thus, we do not measure transaction utility in terms of the discrepant price between the actual price and reference price. Rather, we consider transaction utility as the perceived merits of a deal (i.e., App purchase in this case) following its conceptualization in [24] and [67].

#

There can be both financial and non-financial merits of a deal (i.e., purchasing a paid App). While the financial merits are apparent, examples of non-financial merits include time and effort savings and certainty in the deal, especially in a transaction context which is characterized by risks and uncertainty. For instance, uncertainty about the functions and performance of the App being purchased is an important nonfinancial aspect of price, which then reduces the merits of the deal. Uncertainty gives rise to a perception of risk based on the probability of future loss, which has the effect of reducing the value of a product at the time of purchase [65]. In the context of App purchase, buyers can perceive merits in the deal when they are able to reduce uncertainty by checking other’s opinion about the App (i.e., WOM about App) or trying out the App by themselves (i.e., App trialability). Further, uncertainty aversion i.e., an attitude or preference of known risks over unknown risks, is common in consumer choice [49]. When consumers are uncertainty averse, they can perceive merits in the deal when uncertainty is reduced through WOM and the product trial. Similarly, prior research [37] explained the effect of trust in Internet shopping in terms of transaction utility as trust can increase the perception of the merits of a deal by reducing uncertainty and risk in online transactions. For this reason, WOM about App and App trialability correspond to the merits of the deal in our theoretical framework (see Figure 2).

As per the discussion above, the five mobile App purchase decision factors identified in our exploratory study can be related to the two types of utility as per mental accounting theory [67] as shown in Figure 2. App usefulness and App enjoyment respectively represent the functional and hedonic values associated with a particular App. These factors determine the acquisition utility, in terms of the benefits of the App.

Monetary value of App corresponds to the acquisition utility (the value of the App compared to the payment). The remaining two decision factors (WOM about App and App trialability) can be mapped to transaction utility in terms of merits of the deal as we discussed above. Hence, we adopted the theory to explain and inter-relate the purchase decision factors in our study due to its coverage of the key factors.

Figure 2. Theoretical Mapping of the Explored Factors

Although their previous conceptualization as per mental accouting theory [67] seems to suggest that acquisition utility and transaction utility are independent each other, there could be a relationship between them. Buyer’s perception of transaction utility is situation specific while the assessment of acquisition utility is a more holistic evaluation of the deal [24]. Situation-specific merits of a deal (i.e., transaction utility) could influence buyer’s holistic evaluation of the deal (i.e., acquisition utility). We therefore propose a relationship between them in our theoretical framework (see Figure 2).

## 4.2. Research Model and Hypotheses

Based on our exploratory findings and the theoretical framework of Figure 2, we propose the research model shown in Figure 3. Although the five factors identified earlier could directly affect the intention to purchase an App, we also expect inter-relationships among them according to our framework. Because App functional and hedonic utilities are key determinants of acquisition utility as per mental accounting theory, we propose App usefulness and App enjoyment as antecedents of the Monetary value of the App. The other two factors, WOM about App and App trialability, correspond to the merits of the deal. We therefore propose WOM about App and App trialability as additional antecedents of the Monetary value of the App as per our framework of Figure 1. We now discuss and justify each hypothesis in the model.

App usefulness is defined in this study as the perception that the use of the target App would enhance an individual’s performance in doing what he or she wants to do, adapting from the definition of perceived usefulness in prior research [14]. Perceived usefulness refers to the functional value of a good, which is the utility derived from the perceived quality and expected performance of the good [64]. Functional value has been reported as a key determinant of how customers make purchase decisions [44, 60]. An individual who is considering the purchase of an App is likely to assess whether it is useful for doing what he or she wants to do (e.g., mobile information or map search). Thus, an App’s perceived usefulness should directly increase the intention to purchase it.

## H1: App usefulness has a positive effect on the intention to purchase the App

This study defines App enjoyment as the pleasure or joy felt from the use of the target App based on the definition of enjoyment from prior research [45]. In other words, enjoyment refers to the hedonic value of a good, which is the utility derived from feelings or affective states that the good generates [64]. Consumers often purchase products based on feelings of pleasure, especially hedonic products [70]. Accordingly, hedonic value has been observed as a key determinant of how consumers make their purchase decisions [18, 59]. Someone who is going to buy an App may evaluate whether it is fun to use and able to generate pleasure (e.g., a mobile game). Hence, perceived enjoyment of an App should directly increase the intention to purchase it.

## H2: App enjoyment has a positive effect on the intention to purchase the App

Monetary value, also sometimes called customer return on investment, corresponds to acquisition utility in mental accounting theory and is defined as the trade-off between App utility and App price adapting from prior work [64]. Customers perceive value based on their perceptions of what is received (i.e., customer return) in exchange for what is given (i.e., customer investment) [81]. As customer return increases in comparison to customer investment, monetary value will increase. Monetary value has been found as a key determinant of purchase decisions in different settings [44, 65]. A person considering buying an App is likely to assess its monetary value and purchase it if the value is high. Consequently, the monetary value of an App should directly increase a person’s intention to purchase it.

## H3: Monetary value of App has a positive effect on the intention to purchase the App

WOM refers to the communication concerning the evaluation of the product or service of interest [7]. This conceptualization has been adopted by a number of studies [10, 15, 84] that explained WOM as a key factor affecting trust and sales. As one of the most influential sources of marketplace information for customers [15], WOM is often created and spread through online platforms such as product review websites, retailers and brand websites, personal blogs, message boards, and social networking sites [41]. Furthermore, WOM can be positive, neutral, or negative about the product or service. By considering the positive valence for the purpose of hypotheses formulation, this study conceptualizes WOM about App as the communication of positive evaluation from other users about the target App. Most WOM content is contributed by real users or customers [15]. WOM about App thus reduces uncertainty about the App and the attendant risks in purchasing and using the App. This form of reassurance could lead to development of customer trust in the target App. Thus, WOM about App can affect the reference price in purchasing it and increase the merit of the deal, i.e., the transaction utility, as per mental accounting theory [67]. Perceiving the merit of the deal, customers are likely to proceed to purchase the target App. This agrees with previous research [10] where WOM was found to influence online sales. Hence, WOM about App should increase customers’ intentions to purchase the target App.

## H4: WOM about App has a positive effect on intention to purchase the App

In the context of this study, we define App trialability as the degree to which a potential customer can adequately try out the target App before purchase. Trialability has been considered as an important determinant of acceptance of innovations [56]. A new idea or technology that individuals can use on a trial basis will reduce uncertainty in the decision-making process of potential adopters. Similar to the effect of WOM, App trialability can alleviate uncertainty about the App that may affect its non-financial reference price and the merit of the deal, especially in the context of information asymmetry between buyers and sellers. As people place greater weight on outcomes that are considered certain than outcomes that are merely probable [29], trialability, by increasing the certainty of outcomes from the App adoption, should affect customers’ intentions to purchase the target App.

## H5: App trialability has a positive effect on intention to purchase the App

Corresponding to the concept of acquisition utility in the mental accounting theory, monetary value is assessed based on a comparison between product utility and purchase price [67]. App usefulness represents functional utility, while App enjoyment represents hedonic utility. As the functional and hedonic utilities of the App increase, monetary value of the App should also increase. Thus, App usefulness and App enjoyment are expected to enhance the monetary value of the App.

H6: App usefulness has a positive effect on the monetary value of the App H7: App enjoyment has a positive effect on the monetary value of the App

WOM, i.e., the communication of the positive aspects of the target App by others, serves as a form of informational social influence [8] to a potential buyer. Here, social influence implies the acceptance of information from others as evidence about reality [32]. Social influence occurs through the process of internalization when the individual accepts others’ views because of the similarity of their goals and values [32]. Thus, potential App buyers may adjust their perception of the value of the target App based on others’ feedback by internalizing such informational social influence in the form of WOM about the App. For instance, Venkatesh and Davis [71] reported that social influence increased individuals’ perception of the utility of a target technology. Monetary value of App as acquisition utility refers to the value of the App compared to the payment [66]. WOM about App should thus increase the monetary value of App by increasing the value perception of the App.

H8: WOM about App has a positive effect on the monetary value of the App.

App trialability allows potential buyers an opportunity to test a target App and assess its demonstrability of a new technology in the IS literature [71]. Venkatesh and Davis [71] found result demonstrability to be an instrumental determinant of the perception of the utility, i.e., perceived usefulness, of a target technology. Similarly, App trialability allows potential buyers to judge the value of the App by cognitively comparing what it is capable of doing with what they want or need it to do. App trialability also reduces uncertainty about the functions and performance of the App being purchased. Uncertainty gives rise to a perception of risk based on the probability of future loss [65]. By trying out a target App, potential customers can reduce uncertainty and develop their own perceptions of the App, which may then enhance their evaluation of its utility. As a result, App trialability should increase the monetary value of App by increasing the value perception of the App.

H9: App trialability has a positive effect on the monetary value of the App.

## 4.3. Research Method

We made use of a field survey to empirically validate the model hypotheses. We developed the survey instrument for the research model by adapting existing validated scales wherever possible. The dependent variable was the intention to purchase an App, for which the respondents were asked to think of a particular App they had in mind after browsing an App market at the start of survey. Intention was chosen as the dependent variable since actual purchase behavior information was not available and prior studies have highlighted behavioral intention as the most influential predictor of behavior [51].

The measurement items for App purchase intention were adapted from Dodds et al. [16] and Kim et al. [36] by considering our study context, i.e., App purchase. As per these previous studies, we adopted the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) scale development guidelines for intention [19] and ensured that the questions were specific and consistent with respect to the action (purchase), target (mobile App), context (individual purpose), and time-frame (within the next 3 months) of action. Although Dodds et al. [16] proposed 5 measurement items, other studies [36] used 3 of these items for purchase intention. Among the 5 measurement items of Dodds et al. [16], the same 3 items were used here, i.e., “consider buying”; “willing to buy”; and “likelihood of my purchasing”. The remaining 2 items were not used in our study because they highlight price in the wording: “I would consider buying this model at the price shown,” and “At the price shown, I would consider buying the product,” which is different from the other 3 measures. The use of 3 measurement items for reflective constructs (in our case purchase intention) is considered acceptable.

## 4.4. Data Analysis and Results

We first tested the convergent and discriminant validity of the survey instrument before structural model testing. To assess the convergent validity of the instrument, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) through principal components analysis with varimax rotation [26] using SPSS. We identified a total of six factors with eigenvalue greater than 1.0. When compared across factors, the items loaded highest on their own factors. The six factors explained 79.24 percent of the total variance. We then conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using LISREL following suggested guidelines [22]. We performed covariance analysis using LISREL because this is more rigorous than the partial least squared analysis [22]. The standard path loadings for the CFA were all significant (t-value > 1.96) and greater than 0.7, except the second item of App enjoyment $( \mathrm { E N J } 2 = 0 . 6 9 )$ that was close to the threshold (see Table 6). The average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct exceeded 0.5. The composite reliability (CR) and Cronbach’s α of each construct were greater than 0.7. The convergent validity of the instrument was thus supported [20].

Next, we assessed the discriminant validity of the instrument by comparing the square root of the AVE for each construct with the correlations between this and other constructs. Since the square root of the AVE was greater than the correlations with other constructs (see Table 7), discriminant validity was indicated [22]. The discriminant validity of model constructs is also supported by the results of EFA. Multicollinearity should also not be a problem since none of the correlations exceeded 0.6 [26].

The structural model satisfied the thresholds for all indices except the GFI (0.87) and the normed Chi-square (3.03), which were close to the thresholds. Overall, the structural model appeared to adequately fit the data. The standardized path coefficients were then used for hypothesis testing.

The results shown in Figure 4 indicate that App usefulness (H1), App enjoyment (H2), monetary value of App (H3), and App trialability (H5) had significant effects on the App purchase intention. The four constructs explain 41 percent of the variance in App purchase intention. App enjoyment (H7), WOM about App (H8), and App trialability (H9) significantly affected the monetary value of the App, explaining 44 percent of its variance. However, we did not find an effect of WOM about App (H4) on the App purchase intention. We also did not find and effect of App usefulness (H6) on the monetary value of the App. Overall, 7 out of 9 model hypotheses were supported. Control variables of age, gender, profession, and smartphone usage experience were included in the model because online shopping can be influenced by age, gender, experience, and income [35].<sup>13</sup> However, no significant effects were found for the controls on the dependent variable, App purchase intention.

We conducted an additional test to determine if monetary value of App mediates the effects of three decision factors (App enjoyment, WOM about App, and App trialability) on App purchase intention. We used a bootstrapping procedure following the guidelines from Preacher and Hayes [55] with a 95% confidence interval and 5,000 bootstrap resamples in SPSS version 21. The tests showed: 1) a significant indirect effect of App enjoyment on App purchase intention through monetary value of App (indirect effect = 0.3196, standard error = 0.0535, lower confidence interval = 0.2203, upper confidence interval = 0.4343); 2) a significant indirect effect of WOM about App on App purchase intention through monetary value of App (indirect effect = 0.2358, standard error = 0.0413, lower confidence interval = 0.1587, upper confidence interval = 0.3288); and 3) a significant indirect effect of App trialability on App purchase intention through monetary value of App (indirect effect = 0.1622, strandard error= 0.0270, lower confidence interval = 0.1122, upper confidence interval = 0.2206). The mediation analysis results demonstrate that monetary value of App mediates the effects of three decision factors (App usefulness, App enjoyment, and App trialability) on App purchase intention.

## 4.5. Post-hoc Analysis

Prior research [70] proposed two main factors (i.e., usefulness and enjoyment) in user decision making for IS. Accordingly, we were interested to observe the differences between the effects of App usefulness and App enjoyment, i.e., functional vs. hedonic utility, in our model. Regarding the relative impacts of App usefulness and App enjoyment on monetary value of App, the structural model test results (Figure 4) show that App enjoyment (path coefficient = 0.44, p < 0.001) has a higher path coefficient than App usefulness (path coefficient = 0.03, p > 0.05). To statistically compare the relative impact of the two factors, we employed the two-step constraint test as suggested by Byrne [9]. In the first step, the base model, which is the original model, is fitted. In the second step, the structural paths of the two antecedents (App enjoyment and App usefulness) to monetary value of App are set as equal. If the constrained model produces significantly lower fit than the base model, we can assume the two antecedents are of unequal effect on monetary value of App. The two-step constraint test results show that the change in model fit was significant $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 6 . 8 8$ , ∆df = 1, p < 0.001). We can therefore confirm that the coefficient of the path from App enjoyment to monetary value of App is significantly higher than the coefficient of the path from App usefulness to monetary value of App.

Regarding the relative impacts of App usefulness and App enjoyment on App purchase intention, the structural model test results show that App enjoyment (path coefficient = 0.28, p < 0.001) has a higher path coefficient than App usefulness (path coefficient = 0.11, $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ . For the confirmatory test, we again employed the two-step constraint test. However, our research model proposes that both App enjoyment and App usefulness affect App purchase intention directly and indirectly through monetary value of App. The test results (section 4.4) also indicate that monetary value of App mediates the effect of App enjoyment on App purchase intention. For the confirmatory test of relative impacts of App usefulness and App enjoyment on App purchase intention, we first have to remove the mediating effect. For this reason, the base model is fitted after removing monetary value of App from the model in the first step. The base model test results show that App enjoyment (path coefficient = 0.48, p < 0.001) has a higher path coefficient than App usefulness (path coefficient = 0.16, p < 0.01). In the second step, the structural paths of the two antecedents (App enjoyment and App usefulness) to App purchase intention are set as equal. The two-step constraint test results show that the change in model fit was significant $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } = 8 . 8 1 , \Delta \mathrm { d f } = 1 $ $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 \mathrm { \Omega }$ . We can therefore confirm that the coefficient of the path from App enjoyment to App purchase intention is significantly higher than the coefficient of the path from App usefulness to App purchase intention. These two results indicate the salience of App enjoyment as compared to App usefulness in customer purchase, i.e., in assessing the monetary value of App and in determining App purchase intention.

## 5. Discussion and Implications

## 5.1. Discussion of Findings

This research, consisting of an exploratory, qualitative study followed by a confirmatory, quantitative study, provides several important findings. Foremost, the results inform about four decision factors (monetary value of App, App enjoyment, App usefulness, and App trialability) that have direct effects on mobile App purchase intention. The influence of the monetary value of App, representing acquisition utility in our theoretical framework, indicates its importance in decision making about the purchase of mobile Apps. The impacts of App enjoyment and App usefulness, representing hedonic and functional utility respectively in our framework, show their significance in mobile App purchase. Further, the theoretical framework in terms of transaction utility. Potential buyers perceive lesser uncertainty and risk in buying an App after having tried it out, which increases the merit of the deal (i.e., transaction utility) for the buyer. Additionally, our study found that App enjoyment, WOM about App, and App trialability have significant effects on the monetary value of App (i.e., acquisition utility), which validates our theoretical framework. Especially, the significance of the two factors (WOM about App and App trialability) corresponding to the merits of the deal supports the relationship between acquisition and transaction utility in our theoretical framework (see Figure 2) although previous conceptualization [67] seems to suggest that acquisition utility and transaction utility are independent each other.

Another interesting finding from our study is the salience of App enjoyment compared to App usefulness in customer purchase, especially in assessing the monetary value of App and in determining App purchase intention. Further, this study did not find an effect of App usefulness on the monetary value of App (H6 was not supported). The salience of App enjoyment and the non-support of H6 could result from the characteristics of mobile Apps and smartphones. Because of the small screen size of a smartphone and the difficulty in data input (e.g., typing), mobile Apps for the use of smartphones have limitations as utilitarian tools in comparison to other commonly used devices, e.g. laptop and desktop computers [38]. In contrast, a smartphone with mobile Apps is characterized by its mobility and personal use. Smartphones with Apps, therefore, have several strengths as hedonic tools, e.g., for entertainment when on the move. This could be a reason why potential buyers value the hedonic utility of an App i.e., App enjoyment, more than its usefulness in making decisions about its purchase.

This study did not find an effect of WOM on App purchase intention (H4 was not supported), which conflicts with the results of the exploratory study. This also contradicts previous research where a direct effect of WOM, i.e., online customer reviews, on sales was reported [10, 40, 84]. This finding could be because though both App trialability and WOM about App can reduce uncertainty, trialability depends on direct experience while WOM depends on indirect experience or others’ information. It has been suggested that buyers rely more strongly on their direct experience as compared to second-hand information [17]. Our study shows that the direct experience of App trial has a stronger effect on the monetary value of App and App purchase intention than the second-hand information of WOM. Further, previous research suggested that acquisition utility (monetary value of App in our study) plays a more important role than transaction utility (WOM about App and App trialability in our study) in decision making when the level of uncertainty is high [69]. Accordingly, our results show that monetary value of App has a stronger effect on App purchase intention than the other two factors corresponding to transaction utility. Another potential reason for the insignificance of H4 is the mediation of acquisition utility. The model results (see Figure 4 and the additional test) indicate that the effect of WOM on App purchase intention is mediated by acquisition utility i.e., monetary value of App. Hence, a direct effect was not observed in our model.

## 5.2. Limitations and Future Research

The results of this study should be interpreted in light of its limitations. First, this research explored the potential App purchase decision factors through an exploratory study based on interviews and then tested

#

them through a main study using a survey. The purchase decision factors identified in the exploratory study were inter-related to develop the model in the main study. For this reason, the study could not include and directly measure total utility and transaction utility in our research model. Future research can attempt to directly assess total utility, acquisition utility, and transaction utility in the context of mobile App purchase. Another potential lens for modeling factors in mobile App purchase decisions is customer value theory [44, 60, 64, 81]. This may be useful for exploring other sub-types of customer value, such as conditional value, epistemic value, and social value [60].

Another possible limitation is that we could not check each respondent’s browsing and search behavior at the App Store. It is possible that respondents could select an App from the first page of App Store because of the order effect in customer information search [76]. Future research can attempt to collect clickstream data to alleviate this issue. Also, this study examined the factors affecting the purchase of paid Apps by considering smartphone users. With their increasing popularity, future research may also need to consider the purchase of mobile Apps for tablet devices e.g., iPad. Tablet devices can be more useful as utilitarian tools as compared to smartphones. Last, future research may want to explore differences between inexperienced (i.e., those who have never purchased) and experienced customers (i.e., those who have purchased) in buying a mobile App because the two groups may perceive different levels of uncertainty and risk in conducting a transaction in the App market. Future research can also examine customer-related decision factors such as customer computer self-efficacy, involvement, and innovativeness in IT adoption and usage.

First, this study serves as an initial attempt that we know of to model and test the decision factors of mobile App purchase for paid Apps and their interrelationships. While previous mobile App research has addressed issues related to App market analysis [27], App design and development [44], App adoption [3], and App usage [80], there is a lack of studies examining mobile App purchase decision factors. Even prior mobile App adoption research has by and large focused on explaining the adoption of free Apps for different purposes e.g., [31, 73]. Thus, this study adds to the existing mobile App literature by explaining and testing the decision factors of mobile App purchase for paid Apps that must be purchased prior to their adoption and use.

Second, our study adopted a mixed methods approach with developmental purpose to examine mobile App purchase decision making. While quantitative and qualitative research are valuable in their own right, a mixed methods approach is potentially superior to a single method design because it can provide rich insights into the target phenomena and develop novel theoretical models [72]. In our research, the qualitative study contributed by identifying the salient purchase decision factors specific to the context of mobile App purchase, from a plethora of possible factors examined in prior online purchase literature e.g., [23, 39, 74]. Among the identified App purchase decision factors, App trialability has not been examined in the previous online purchase literature. Further, monetary value of App as an antecedent is not considered in the commonly used technology adoption and IS theories. Particularly, there is a lack of research examining the main effect of monetary value of App and its relationships with other App purchase decision factors (App usefulness, App enjoyment, WOM about App, and App trialability). In this manner, this study adds to the existing online purchase literature that has focused on other products such as books e.g. [10, 36]. Next, our quantitative study contributed by conceptualizing the relationships between the identified factors from the qualitative study, developing the research model based on mental accounting theory, and validating the model across a large sample of App customers.

Third, this study has implications in terms of extending how mental accounting theory [67] has been applied. It contributes to the theory by additionally proposing and testing the relationship between transaction utility, i.e., merits of a deal (corresponding to WOM about App and App trialability) and acquisition utility i.e., value compared to the payment (corresponding to monetary value of App) while the original conceptualization [67] seems to suggest that acquisition utility and transaction utility are unrelated. The application of mental accounting theory also contributes to our understanding of the mobile App purchase phenomenon by explaining how the decision factors identified from our qualitative study inter-relate to influence the intent to purchase a mobile App.

Specifically, our study based on utility concepts from mental accounting theory shows that all five decision factors do not impact App purchase intention in the same way and that there are interrelationships among the factors. Among them, App enjoyment and App trialability influence purchase intention both directly and mediated by monetary value of App. On the other hand, App usefulness and monetary value of App have only direct effects on the App purchase intention while WOM about App has only an indirect effect (mediated by monetary value of App). Thus, monetary value of App plays a key role in mediating the effects of three of the other decision factors on the App purchase intention. Additionally, these findings add to the previous literature [10, 40, 84] where only a direct effect of WOM on online sales has been examined. However, using mental accounting theory, we see that WOM about App affects purchase intention indirectly, i.e., mediated by monetary value of App. Further, as mentioned before, the effect of trialability was not studied in the previous online purchase literature. Here, App trialability is seen to be an important decision factor that affects App purchase intention directly and indirectly through the monetary value of App as explained through our theoretical framework. Further, this study adds to the literature by highlighting the relative importance of hedonic utility (App enjoyment) over functional utility (App usefulness) in assessing the monetary value of an App and deciding on its purchase in general.

## 5.4. Implications for Practice

This study offers several practical implications. First, it suggests that App developers and marketers should consider the five factors (monetary value of App, App enjoyment, App usefulness, WOM about App, and App trialability) identified as App purchase decision factors in promoting their sales. The monetary value decision factor serves to inform them of the importance of App pricing relative to the target App’s utility. Consumers perceive monetary value based on the trade-off between price and utility. App developers need to reflect in their pricing how potential buyers perceive the utility of the App. They can attempt to increase the monetary value of the App by enhancing its utility or lowering its price. The utility of the App can be enhanced by improving its utilitarian (i.e., usefulness) and hedonic features (i.e., enjoyment). The findings also indicate the salience of App enjoyment and App usefulness in directly affecting App purchase intention.

Additionally, the results highlight the direct and indirect effects of two marketing practices (i.e., WOM and App trialability) in promoting the purchase of mobile Apps. WOM about the App represents an online feedback mechanism for customers. The findings of this study show that WOM affects the buyers’ purchase intention through their perception of monetary value of the App. Thus, marketers need to work on promoting Apps in channels such as blogs, social network sites (e.g., Facebook), as well as in the App market by focusing on the App benefits with respect to its price, i.e., monetary value of App. The App market (e.g., the App Store and Google Play) can also provide online feedback mechanisms to support WOM communication among buyers and publish lists of App ranks in terms of popularity and sales volume.<sup>14</sup> This study also suggests that free trial versions should be an effective marketing tool in promoting App purchase, both directly and indirectly through increasing the perception of monetary value of the App. The significance of App trialability suggests that developers and marketers should consider distributing free trial versions of paid Apps, as is already done by some.<sup>15</sup> The trial version can operate on a time-basis (i.e., use of the trial version for a limited time period) and/or a feature basis (i.e., the trial version has limited features). After experimenting with the trial version, users may perceive the merits and the monetary value of the App and then decide to purchase it.

Further, this study suggests that App developers and marketers should place different weights on the decision factors for promoting purchase and increasing the perceived monetary value of the App. The customers’ monetary value assessment of the App. The findings from the post-hoc analysis also show the greater impact of App enjoyment than App usefulness on the overall App purchase intention. These results suggest that developers and marketers should consider enhancing and emphasizing hedonic features. They also need to highlight hedonic features in their trial versions, which should increase the App’s monetary value and purchase intention.

## 6. Conclusion

Sales of mobile Apps are a key revenue source in the App economy. Nevertheless, little research has been conducted to understand the individual purchase decisions that fuel this economy, with many Apps being unsuccessful [34]. Motivated thus and following the guidelines for mixed methods research [72], we explored the decision factors in the purchase of mobile Apps through a qualitative exploratory study and then tested the significance of those decision factors through a quantitative main study. There are four main contributions of this study: (1) contributing to research by being among the first to investigate what influences people to purchase mobile Apps comprehensively and inter-relating the purchase decision factors, (2) extending how mental accounting theory has been applied in order to explain decision making in mobile App purchase, (3) adding to the literature by explaining the direct and indirect roles of two marketing practices (i.e., WOM and App trialability) in the mobile App market, and (4) contributing to our understanding of how the effects of the decision factors differ in App purchase decision making. The findings also inform App developers and marketers on how to promote the purchase of Apps and thereby leverage this source of revenue. As the App economy grows across the globe, studies such as ours can help to throw light on this important phenomenon.
