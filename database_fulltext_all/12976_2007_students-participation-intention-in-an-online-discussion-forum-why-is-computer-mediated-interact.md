# Students’ participation intention in an online discussion forum: Why is computer-mediated interaction attractive?

Xue Yang <sup>a</sup>, Yan Li <sup>b</sup>, Chuan-Hoo Tan <sup>a,\*</sup>, Hock-Hai Teo <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Systems, National University of Singapore, Singapore <sup>b</sup> ESSEC Business School, Paris, France

Received 25 October 2006; received in revised form 15 January 2007; accepted 20 April 2007 Available online 29 June 2007

## Abstract

Anecdotal evidence indicates that an online discussion forum may not be utilized to its full potential in enhancing the effectiveness and efficiency of teaching due to a lower than expected student participation rate. This paper seeks to identify the motivational behavioral factors influencing students’ intention to participate in an online discussion forums (ODF). Drawing on the literature on social psychology and applying the theory of reasoned action, we develop a conceptual model of intention to participate in an online discussion forum and empirically test the hypotheses in a cross-sectional quantitative survey. The findings indicate that expectancy on hedonic outcome and utilitarian outcome and peer pressure positively influence the participation intention of students. Also, the perceived importance of learning positively moderates the relationship between utilitarian outcome expectancy and participation intention. Theoretical and practical implications of the findings are discussed. <sup>#</sup> 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Online discussion forum; Collaborative learning; Theory of reasoned action

## 1. Introduction

Online discussion forums (ODF) have been deployed to complement traditional techniques (lectures and tutorials) in the educational arena [16,31]. An ODF is a text-based web application that facilitates constructive discussion between instructors and students as well as among students [6] without temporal and geographical constraints [22]. It harmonizes with the educational philosophy that considers communication a necessary and fundamental mechanism for effective learning [37]. Moreover, Zhang et al. [40] found that learners’ interactions with both human and inanimate objects were essential for gradual development of personal understanding and a quality learning experience. By supporting extensive online interactions to complement conventional classroom teaching, ODF can effectively enrich the process of acquiring, sharing and exchanging knowledge among participants, thereby improving learning performance and outcomes [27].

Furthermore, the positive effect of technology on students’ performance has been shown to depend on the degree of the students’ participation [24]. Thus, ODF can be successful in enhancing collaborative learning only when it manages to attract students to participate enthusiastically in its interactions [21,32,34]. However, this may not occur and the vision of an exciting online discussion seldom happens (e.g., ref. [23]). On the one hand, taking into account the high perceived usefulness of an ODF in facilitating collaborative learning, a low participation rate is counter intuitive. On the other hand, the functions of a text-based ODF are relatively straightforward and easy to use, and this means that technology-related factors are understood by most users and are unlikely to be major negative factors in influencing the participation intention. In our context, we therefore define the intention to participate in an ODF as the individual’s propensity to engage actively with peers or lecturers in a forum discussion. Thus, understanding the key motivational factors that influence the students’ intention to participate rather than the features of the application becomes an overriding concern for today’s educators. However, most prior studies of an ODF have focused on examining its impact on learning performance and effectiveness [18] rather than the students’ intention to participate. We therefore sought to fill this gap by adopting a mixed-method approach through a two-phase research process, thereby answering two research questions:

(1) How do the major beliefs about learning outcomes influence the students’ ODF participation intention?

(2) How do the major external pressure-related factors (subjective norms) influence the students’ ODF participation intention?

The rest of this paper will be organized as follows. First, we propose a theoretical model based on extensive qualitative exploration of student feedbacks on the online discussion forum adopted on campus by grounding on the theory of reasoned action as the theoretical backbone. Second, we test and validate the research model by applying quantitative measures with survey data. Subsequently, data analysis results and discussion are presented. We conclude the paper with limitations and implications.

## 2. ODF participation and the theory of reasoned action

The term ODF stems from literature on internet technologies developed to facilitate learning and to augment traditional classroom education [9,28]. Much research has focused on understanding the influence of different feature- or individual-centric factors on students’ learning performance in an ODF context. To this end, some researchers have reported significant positive influence of ODF but others have not (e.g., ref. [9]). Reconciling the contradictory empirical evidence may involve understanding the way that motivation affects learning. Indeed, Martins and Kellermanns [28] found that change-enabling factors (such as technical support) and change-motivating factors (such as perceived incentive to use) could influence students acceptance of a web-based course management system (such as a chat room). However, most studies did not systematically examine the individual-oriented motivational factors of ODF.

Our study built on the literature of individual technology adoption and usage that centered on devising measurements of perception of the technological, individual and environmental characteristics, as well as that investigating their correlation with attitudes and behavioral intentions toward use and actual usage [5]. Specifically, we adopted the theory of reasoned action (TRA) [12] as the theoretical backbone: it has been widely accepted in examining the determinants of consciously intended behaviors [39]. With TRA, the behavioral intention is considered as a function of beliefs related to attitude, an individual’s feelings toward performing a behavior and subjective norms, the social and environmental pressures to perform an action.

In the ODF context, since mandatory requirements may not motivate the students to achieve high quality learning [26], we hypothesized that social and personal factors were crucial in shaping individual’s beliefs and perceptions toward ODF participation, which in turn would determine the success of collaborative learning using ODF. Therefore, we adopted a mixed-method approach to our investigation, starting with an exploratory qualitative survey to develop the theoretical model from an individual’s (student’s) perspective.

## 3. Stage 1—developing the conceptual model

We first developed and used a qualitative survey to help in understanding the important behavioral and motivational determinants of students’ intention toward ODF participation [11]. The observations derived from the qualitative data were then used to support the decomposition of the fundamental constructs in TRA. Specifically, a university-wide qualitative survey was conducted by the instructional technology centre of our university in March, 2004 to gather students’ feedback on the various virtual learning environment services deployed there. Open-ended questions (providing feedback such as dislikes, suggestions for improvement and other comments) were asked.

In total, 1503 responses on use of the ODF were collected from different faculties. Comments vary widely. For example, many mentioned the usefulness of ODF and their enjoyment when using it (e.g., ‘‘I think the forum is a great place to communicate and interact with our fellow school mates and lecturers, very useful for enhancing the study’’). Concern for the unsatisfactory utilization rate was also raised (e.g., ‘‘The forum is very useful, but it is underutilized. The lack of awareness and participation from students and lecturers makes the forum rather dry.’’) To establish an independent assessment of the feedback received, four ‘‘blind’’ coders (IS post-graduate students) were recruited to screen, tabulate and categorize the comments based on their own understanding. Each coder examined every comment carefully and put them into categories, which they also labeled. Subsequently, all results were consolidated and the coders met to discuss their categorizations. A consensus was reached and an overall categorization was derived.

During this process, we observed that abstract labels could be formed by combining similar and more specific categories. For instance, the usefulness of the ODF in helping students obtain higher grade and the usefulness of the ODF in helping students gain more course-related knowledge were regrouped into the utilitarian outcome expectancy. Through this process, the comments were categorized into seven major motivational factors:

(1) Social outcome expectancy;

(2) Hedonic outcome expectancy;

(3) Utilitarian outcome expectancy;

(4) Perceived importance of learning;

(5) Peer pressure;

(6) Superior pressure;

(7) Conformity motivation [15].

The first three of these factors correspond to TRA’s outcome expectancy, reflecting the beliefs of consequences resulted from participating in the ODF, while the last three relate to subjective norms, concerning the influence from other people in the ODF.

## 3.1. Intention to participate

The students’ intention to participate in the ODF is the dependent variable in our study. The intention to perform a behavior is assumed to capture the motivational factors that influence a behavior and further lead to performance of the target behavior [20]. It highlights the importance of examining the behavioral intention of the ODF participation. For instance, a student said:

‘‘I am willing and actually like to participate in this online discussion forum as it is a great place to spontaneously communicate and interact with fellow school mates and lecturers.’’

The construct of attitude toward participation was not included in our conceptual model. Instead, we posited that outcome expectancy, the motivation of the students, would directly influence students’ intention to participate in the ODF.

## 3.2. Outcome expectancy

Outcome expectancy is the internal force of the individual that initiates ODF usage behavior [4]. It represents a student’s belief that participating in the ODF will lead to a desirable learning outcome. The outcome and feelings of achievement should motivate an individual to use the technology and result in favorable consequences. Furthermore, the expectancy toward an outcome, which is decomposed into social, hedonic and utilitarian outcomes, can jointly influence the decision to participate in the ODF. The influence of outcome expectancy can be further moderated by a students’ valuation of the outcome.

## 3.2.1. Social outcome expectancy

The emphasis on a person’s perceived influence on others [35] may lead to a desire for social outcomes. In our context, a student may achieve such an outcome by behaving effectively in the ODF [13]. For instance, a student noted:

‘‘I enjoyed joining in the discussion forum since it actually acts as a tool for me to be present and in contact with the learning communities.’’

Throughout the modern education literature, social interdependence in the learning process is encouraged. Effective learning should go beyond mere access to information but also require engagement with others, thereby establishing a role in the learning community [25]. Since the ODF provides an additional interaction channel for students to interact, their performance will be recognized by fellow classmates and lecturers. Furthermore, the asynchronous nature of the ODF affords less opportunity for anyone to dominate the discussion or develop leadership; hence students can achieve more equitable opportunities than in traditional classroom discussion [33]. Participants are also able to project themselves socially and emotionally, creating feelings of presence. To this end, students could expect to be remembered by fellow classmates and lecturers for contributing in discussions. The perceived power of the ODF can thus motivate students to participate. Hence, we posited that:

H1a. The higher the social outcome expectancy, the more likely students will form positive intention toward participating in the ODF.

## 3.2.2. Hedonic outcome expectancy

Hedonic outcome expectancy refers to the pleasure derived from the use of the system [3]. The ODF empowers students to conduct reflective and rigorous learning through dynamic forms of interaction. Thus, the learning process becomes more collaborative. Since online learning allows voluntary participation, students should enjoy the experience more than when the process is mandatory [7]. Additionally, we believe that students anticipate a well-designed and attractive forum interface, availability of customization and added functionalities (e.g., multimedia contents). For example, one opinion was:

‘‘Forums are great to facilitate active off-class discussions. Furthermore, I intend to see the forum being made more attractive and ‘‘funky’’, i.e. give the user the ability to customize the preferences of the background design, facilitate different forms of contents like video and so on.’’

Overall, the perceived ability of the ODF to fulfill students’ hedonic outcome expectancy should motivate students to participate in the ODF. Hence, we posited that:

H1b. The higher the hedonic outcome expectancy, the more likely students will form positive intention toward participating in the ODF.

## 3.2.3. Utilitarian outcome expectancy

Utilitarian outcome expectancy is the tangible result of technology-enabled collaborative learning. It is the extent to which participating in the ODF enhances the quality and effectiveness of learning [36]. Through interactions, students develop advanced critical thinking and absorb contributions from others [30]. Empirically, the quality and effectiveness of collaborative learning can be enhanced by informal spontaneous discussions in the ODF [1,2]. Holding the belief that participating in the ODF can help achieve the anticipated utilitarian outcome, students will be highly motivated to participate. For example, some students said:

‘‘The forum is a very useful and highly interactive means of communication among us fellow classmates plus between the lecturers and students to learn from FAQ and speed up our learning curve. It’s a very good platform for one-stop academic access.’’

Thus, we hypothesized:

H1c. The higher the utilitarian outcome expectancy, the more likely students will form positive intention toward participating in the ODF.

## 3.2.4. Perceived importance of learning

Learner differences have been shown to influence a student’s motivation to participate [10]. Serious intention to learn can intrinsically motivate students to make effort and deploy strategies to enhance learning [17]. Thus, while students regard learning as important, the expectancy to obtain utilitarian learning outcomes will probably motivate them to participate in the ODF. Since social outcome and hedonic outcome are not directly related to utilitarian learning outcomes, we posited the absence of a moderation effect due to the perceived importance of learning on these two outcome expectancies. Hence, we hypothesized:

H1d. The higher the perceived importance of learning, the more likely the utilitarian outcome expectancy will lead to students’ positive intention toward participating in the ODF.

## 3.3. Subjective norms

TRA has considered subjective norm to be determined by the set of beliefs about the initiators or proponents of the process. As a form of social behavior, online collaborative learning based on computermediated technology involves interactions among participants, creating new social relationships. The interpersonal relationships establish social pressures which are the subjective norms of TRA.

In ODF, the perceived social pressure could be a result of peer pressure from friends or classmates (with much the same social status) and also from the superior pressure of instructors (lecturers and tutors) having formal authority. Such pressures are weighted by the participant’s motivation to comply with these people.

## 3.3.1. Peer pressure

Peer pressure is formed when a student believes that his or her peers put more effort into the ODF than he or she does. The communication of ideas raises interest and motivates the student to catch up. One student observed:

‘‘Certain functions of the forum are hardly utilized by my friends. I think most students are not aware and will not bother these uses, as they may have other alternatives.’’

Individuals who are originally reluctant to participate in the discussion may, however, still modify their actions. In our study, students were encouraged to participate once they saw that there would be active responses from peers. Hence, we argued that:

H2a. The greater the peer pressure, the more likely students will form positive intention toward participating in the ODF.

## 3.3.2. Superior pressure

As the principle actor and being perceived as the formal authority, the instructor can influence student performance. Evidently, the instructor’s proactive (or passive) involvement can attract (or discourage) students. Students may be eager to get approval or feel frustrated by the lecturer. Some students elaborated on this:

‘‘A forum without the lecturer’s frequent comments is nothing better than no forum at all because students may tend to mislead one another as a result of lecturers’ ignorance toward postings.’

‘‘The usage of the forum largely depends on the lecturer encouragement. Lecturers should be aware of the forum’s importance for students and engage us in the discussion; otherwise not much information can be found there.’’

There is normally intense desire for guidance in learning. To this extent, lecturers’ active engagement can increase students’ intent to participate in the ODF. Hence, we posited that:

H2b. The greater the superior pressure, the more likely students will form positive intention toward participating in the ODF.

## 3.3.3. Conformity motivation

Extant studies have shown that the strength of pressure depends on a person’s willingness to comply. Stronger conformity motivation will enhance the effect of the peer/superior pressure on the intention to participate, especially when it is encouraged by the lecturer. In contrast, the dislike over complying with external pressure can impair the effect of the forces:

‘‘Although this online discussion forum is very popular among students, I don’t feel I should participate just because others are doing so.’’

The degree of conformity motivation will increase or reduce individuals’ perceived level of pressure as motivation to participate in the ODF. Hence, we hypothesized:

## 4. Stage 2—validation of the model

We constructed a questionnaire using only items validated in prior studies. The card sorting procedure of Moore and Benbasat [29] was considered in the pre-test step; it involved the help of 25 IS postgraduate students. As a result, the questionnaire was revised to incorporate their feedback and that of three information system faculty members. Through a face validity test, the content validity was ascertained; four items which were not appropriate for the context of the study were removed or rephrased. At the end of the pre-test, we had 8 constructs with 25 items in the survey. Respondents were asked to give answers on a Likert scale from 1 to 7 stating their agreement or disagreement with the statements about the ODF.

At the end of March in 2004, we disseminated the questionnaire to the students who were mostly full-time third year undergraduate computing school students enrolled in an information systems strategy course. These were especially suitable subjects as they were familiar with the operation of the ODF; thus technological factors could be excluded as affecting the outcome. Students who had responded in the prior qualitative survey were not used in this part of the study. All course members were given the questionnaire, thereby reducing selection bias. The responses reported on experience with the ODF which is part of an award-wining virtual learning system in a local public university. A small incentive was offered: US\$ 20 winnings to five respondents. In total, 254 sets of questionnaires were distributed, and 206 responses (81%) were received. We could use only 183 entries because 23 questionnaires were incomplete.

## 4.1. Data analysis and results

We used partial least squares (PLS), as implemented in PLS Graph Version 2.91.03.04, for hypothesis testing, conducting all tests at 5% level of significance.

The general demographic information shows the respondents’ profile. Among 183 respondents, there were 50.27% males and the mean age was 22. Most respondents were Singaporean (75.4%) and had taken 5 course modules (55.2%) with an average number of 4.5 modules. 63.9% of the respondents anticipated an average grade of B or better while of 24.1% expected an A or better.

The strength of the measurement model was demonstrated through convergent and discriminant validity [19]. To determine item-construct loadings, a factor analysis (Table 1) was conducted using SPSS. The resulting loadings were used to compute the internal consistency statistics and a single, overall measure of each of the sub-constructs.

Table 2 shows the indicator loadings, Cronbach alpha, composite reliability and average variance were computed as the exploratory analysis. All the multiple-item constructs demonstrated strong convergent validity. For item reliability, all indicators had loadings greater than 0.7, suggesting that they explained more than 50% of the variance in the construct. The composite reliability and the Cronbach alpha of all the constructs were well above the recommended score of 0.7, indicating that they had adequate reliability. Every construct also had an average extracted variance exceeding 0.5 [14], suggesting that the amount of variance in the items was less than that attributable to the construct.

According to the correlation matrix of the studied variables and the average variances extracted along the diagonals, the square root of the average variance extracted for all constructs was larger than its correlations with other constructs [38]. In addition, the results of factor analysis showed that the loadings of items on their corresponding factors were much higher than the cross-loadings on other factors. Hence, all the constructs demonstrated adequate discriminant validity.

The Bootstrap resembling procedure was applied to test the significance of the path coefficients. In formulating and testing the effects of moderating variables, we applied the procedure of Chin et al. [8]. First, we standardize all indicators reflecting the predictor and moderator constructs to a mean of zero and variance of one. Then, using the standardized indicators of the predictor and moderator variables, product indicators were generated to reflect the latent interaction variables. The PLS procedure was then applied to estimate the latent variable. Moreover, we tested both the full model, including the two main categories of outcome expectancy and subjective norms, and the base model which excluded the moderating relationships.

The empirical results supported the hypothesized influence of outcome evaluation on intention formation with three of the four hypotheses supported (see Table 3). H1a was not supported. The moderator construct of perceived importance of learning significantly moderated the relationship between utilitarian outcome expectancy and the intention toward participation at a 0.05 level and a path coefficient of 0.188. At the same time the possible significant relationship between the perceived importance of learning and intention was not found. Hence, H1d was supported.

Subsequently, through PLS results, the positive relationship between peer pressure and intention was significant at the 0.05 level, with path coefficient of 0.136. Hence, H2a was supported. However, the conformity motivation was found to negatively moderate the peer pressure effect with the path coefficient of -0.062. Hence, H2c was not supported. The effect of superior pressure on the intention was not significant with a coefficient of 0.060 and the conformity motivation did not significantly moderate this relationship. Hence, H2b and H2d were not supported. The overall PLS results are shown in Table 3. The estimated path coefficient (standardized) and its associated significance level were shown for each link. ## 5. Discussion

Our research investigated a student’s willingness to participate in ODF in terms of its ability to support a learner’s online interactions. Our results provided empirical support for the hypothesized relationships between outcome expectancy and participation intention and between subjective norms and participation intention.

## 6. Limitation

Several limitations of this study must be addressed. First, the model explored the main motivating factors of students’ intention to participate in ODF rather than their actual participation behavior. We believed there was value in investigating whether the intention to participate would lead to the behavior and its reasons. The determinants of actual participation rate thus possess the potential to enhance researchers’ and practitioners’ understanding toward students’ ODF participation motivation. Besides, given the current focus on motivational factors, the influences of technological factors were not examined.

Second, simply investigating participation was not comprehensive enough to show the results of attending ODF.

Third, to ensure the content validity of the survey instrument, some constructs (e.g., utilitarian outcome expectancy) contained only two items after deleting those inappropriate items in the face validity test.

## 7. Conclusions and implications

The online discussion forum (ODF) has increasingly been recognized as a viable education alternative. We focused on the question of students’ participation intention and built on extensive qualitative feedbacks and the TRA model to investigate the influence of outcome expectancy and subjective norms on ODF participation intention. The empirical results indicated that the expectancy toward the hedonic and utilitarian outcomes were important by forming the intention to participate. It provided empirical support for our conjecture that students are driven by desire for better collaborative learning outcomes and enjoyable experience. However, it does not suggest that social outcome expectancy could motivate students to use the ODF.

For the two decomposed constructs of the subjective norms, peer pressure positively influenced the dependent variable but superior pressure did not. Thus, we found that, within a common learning community, peer pressure possesses greater powers in inducing people to follow others than superior pressure from higher authorities. However, it is likely that they are more inclined to make decisions based on their personal needs.

Methodologically, our study possessed certain novelties. First, it adopted a mixed-method, rigorous approach in its empirical examination of the participation intention; this is unique in the area. The rich qualitative information effectively enhanced our understanding of the phenomenon and supported the construction of the theoretical development.

Practically, two issues are worth mentioning. First, to motivate students to participate in ODF, the outcome expectancy and the perceived behavioral norms of the students must be taken into account. Second, from extensive student feedback, the instructor’s influence may produce mixed effects on participation intention. Practitioners have to be careful when exploiting the instructor’s role.

In conclusion, with a better understanding of the factors leading to participation intention, ODF could be more readily accepted by students and thus provide a better collaborative learning environment.
