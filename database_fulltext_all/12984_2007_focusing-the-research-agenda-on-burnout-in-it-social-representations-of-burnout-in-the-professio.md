# Focusing the research agenda on burnout in IT: social representations of burnout in the profession

Suzanne D. Pawlowski<sup>1</sup>, Evgeny A. Kaganer<sup>2</sup> and John J. Cater III<sup>3</sup>

## Abstract

Despite the pervasiveness and high costs of burnout in IT, our understanding of this problem remains limited. Review of IS research on job stress/burnout reveals that this body of work has relied heavily on general OB theory (parsimonious models generalizable across a variety of occupations and social contexts). More recently, scholars have argued for the addition of an occupation-specific approach to complement general OB theory, incorporating factors and concerns particular to a profession/work environment to increase explanatory power and relevance. The purpose of the exploratory study in this paper is to provide a foundation for such an occupation-specific research agenda on burnout in the IT profession. As an initial step, we employ social representations theory and methods to understand how IT professionals today make sense of and assign meaning to burnout in the context of their work. Transcripts from in-depth interviews of 20 IT professionals were contentanalyzed and 22 key topics (concepts) were identified. Quantitative methods, including analysis of similarity, were used to create a social representations ‘map’ reflecting these professionals’ views of burnout. The study contributes to the development of a renewed research agenda by pinpointing highly salient issues and specific work contexts warranting priority in future investigations. The experiences of a software engineer captured in Tracy Kidder’s classic account of the project to create the first 32-bit super-minicomputer remind us that burnout is not a new phenomenon in the IT profession. There are, however, indications that burnout has become increasingly pervasive as IT organizations face an accelerated pace of technology change, increased demands, and tight resource constraints. A study by Meta Group reports that 71% of IT managers surveyed believe that burnout is a serious problem for their company (Hayes, 2003) and burnout in IT has become a common topic of professional books and magazines (e.g., McGee et $a l . ,$ 2000; Kaluzniacky, 2004).

Recognition of the individual and organizational costs of this severe form of job strain, including increased turnover, absenteeism, and reduced productivity (Cordes & Dougherty, 1993), has resulted in a substantial body of research. An estimated 2500 papers investigating burnout in a variety of professional and organizational settings have been published over one 20-year period (Schulz et al., 1995). IS researchers have also been actively engaged in this effort, with approximately 31 studies since 1983 aimed at understanding burnout in the IT profession. Consistent with the broader literature on occupational stress, burnout research in general and IS studies on the subject, in particular, traditionally rely on parsimonious models explicating antecedents, symptoms, and consequences of the work-strain relationship that are generalizable across a variety of occupations and social contexts (Bacharach & Bamberger, 1992; Fletcher & Jones, 1993). This ‘global’ approach also usually entails the use of nomothetic designs and quantitative methodologies. While this research tradition has made significant progress in furthering our understanding of the occupational stress and burnout phenomena, an increasing number of scholars in the fields of organizational behavior, occupational psychology, and sociology point out important limitations associated with this approach. Accordingly, they argue that the mainstream literature needs to be complemented by studies employing ideographic techniques and incorporating factors and concerns specific to a particular work environment. Three inter related arguments have been offered in support of this view.

First, proponents of the occupation-specific approach argue that stress and burnout are predicated on the subjective meanings that individuals attribute to their work-related experiences (Handy, 1988; Perrewe & Zellars, 1999). Because these attributions do not arise in a vacuum but rather are shaped by professional and organizational institutional environments in which people interact, the meanings of stress and burnout will vary across different occupational communities (Barley & Knight, 1992; Meyerson, 1994). This, in turn, translates into diversity of specific sources and manifestations of job-related strain experienced by individuals in different contexts. A number of empirical studies have corroborated this view, showing, for instance, that the same job characteristics may have a different impact on members of different professions with respect to their physical and mental ill-health (Sparks & Cooper, 1999).

Second, insofar as the global models of burnout and stress encompass only those aspects of the work-strain relationship that are common across a wide spectrum of professional and organizational environments, it has been suggested that these models are inherently limited with regard to their analytical scope (Sparks & Cooper, 1999). Moreover, in combination with nomothetic research designs, exclusive reliance on such models tends to narrow the range of variables accounted for in the mainstream literature, which results in diminished explanatory power of this body of research. For example, several empirical investigations have demonstrated that broader models incorporating not only generic but also job-specific factors and conditions explain more of the variance in strain outcomes than the pure global models and, hence, are more plausible (Bacharach & Bamberger, 1992; Fletcher & Jones, 1993).

The final argument put forth in support of the occupation-specific approach concerns its implications for devising stress management interventions. Researchers have argued that intervention strategies based on idiosyncratic job and organizational characteristics are likely to be more effective and less expensive than those derived solely from global and, therefore, more simplistic frameworks (Hemingway & Smith, 1999; Sparks & Cooper, 1999; Beehr et al., 2000). The greater effectiveness, in this case, stems from the fact that contextspecific factors and concerns are more salient to employees in a particular job and, consequently, have the greater impact on individual strains and performance (Beehr et al., 2000). Furthermore, managers responsible for conceiving and carrying out the intervention strategies can better relate to concepts grounded in their everyday activities and will be quicker to act upon them.

As pointed out above, most of the IS research on burnout follows the global approach and seeks to explore generic stressors, symptoms, and outcomes of burnout in the IT context. While we were able to identify some studies addressing occupation-specific aspects of jobrelated strain among IT professionals (Fujigaki, 1990, 1996; Lim & Teo, 1996; Moore, 2000; Sethi et al., 2004), these investigations are rather sparse and limited in a number of respects. First, the age of the papers poses a concern. Out of the five studies employing either the occupation-specific or mixed approach to studying burnout, three were conducted over a decade ago. Considering the fast-changing nature of the IT environment, it is reasonable to assume that the key sources and the overall severity of burnout experienced by IT professionals today are quite different from those experienced 10 years ago (consider concerns over IT offshore outsourcing, for instance). Hence, we argue that a more up-to-date view of the specific manifestations of burnout in the IT profession is warranted.

In addition, the two more recent studies with the occupation-specific emphasis (Moore, 2000; Sethi et al., 2004) limit the scope of their analysis to stressors. While stressors are the most obvious and most explored context-specific element of the burnout phenomenon (e.g., Hemingway & Smith, 1999; Sparks & Cooper, 1999; Beehr et al., 2000; Dick, 2000), previous research has shown that other aspects, such as, for example, coping strategies (Narayanan et al., 1999), may also vary across different occupational settings. Accordingly, in order to gain a deeper understanding of not only why but also how burnout occurs in the IT profession, researchers need to extend their occupation-specific analyses to include all pertinent aspects of the work–strain relationship. Also importantly, to be able to prioritize future research efforts and help practitioners to effectively manage stress in the workplace, we need to be able to assess the relative salience of individual burnout elements in the IT context.

Finally, continuing this line of argument, we posit that occupations may vary not only with respect to the presence or absence of individual burnout elements, but also in terms of the nature of inter-relationships among them. For example, certain job characteristics may interact with conditions in the organizational and/or business environments to produce complex constellations of factors leading to severe emotional strain among IT employees. Likewise, different stressors may be more likely to entail particular types of coping strategies and burnout outcomes. Similar to evaluating salience of individual burnout elements, explicating inter-relationships among particular stressors, strains, and outcomes is important for designing effective stress prevention and management strategies. The existing occupation-specific literature, nonetheless, does not address this issue, which can be partly attributed to its methodological limitations.

In light of the preceding discussion, the goal of this paper is to further the occupation-specific view of burnout in the IT profession by addressing the three limitations of the existing literature outlined above. To achieve this objective, we adopted an ideographic research design and conducted an exploratory study aimed at gaining an understanding of the meanings through which today’s IT professionals make sense of and experience job-related burnout. To this end, we employed a social cognition approach based on social representations theory (Moscovici, 1981) to capture and analyze the perspectives of 20 IT professionals on burnout. Social representations have been defined as the shared images and meanings through which people within a particular social group organize the world around them (Parker, 1987). This conceptualization is consistent with the social-constructionist view of burnout (Handy, 1988; Barley & Knight, 1992; Meyerson, 1994) underpinning the occupation-specific approach. Data were collected via in-depth interviews and content analyzed to generate a social representation ‘map’ reflecting the participants’ views on a full range of concepts constituting the burnout phenomenon. Analysis of the map structure also allowed us to evaluate the nature of inter-relationships among the burnout elements, as well as to prioritize the elements with respect to their salience. The results of the study contribute to a renewal and focusing of a future research agenda on job stress and burnout in IT as well as preliminary implications for IT practitioners.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we provide a brief literature review addressing key elements of general burnout theory and showing that the IS literature on the subject is based largely in models/constructs drawn exclusively from this body of theory. We then introduce social representations theory and justify its use to capture and analyze the sensemaking of IT professionals on burnout in IT work. Next, we present the study methods and findings, followed by a discussion aimed at comparing the general theory of burnout against the social representation map generated in this study, including the identification of aspects particularly germane to IT work. We conclude the paper by suggesting directions for a more focused research agenda on burnout in IT based on an occupation-specific approach.

## Job-related burnout

Within general OB theory, burnout is viewed as an extreme and specific manifestation of job-related strain and is a long-term consequence of stress in the workplace (Burke et al., 1996; Cooper et al., 2001). The general model of stress and its relationship to burnout is shown in Figure 1. This model is based on the transactional view of stress as a dynamic cognitive state resulting from individuals interacting with the environment and appraising and coping with the demands that arise (Lazarus, 1991). Stress occurs when the individual perceives that the demands of the environment exceed or tax the resources available, threatening their well-being. Strain is defined as the individual’s psychological, physical, and behavioral responses to stressors. Burnout is severe strain – ‘an extreme state of psychological strain and depletion of energy resources arising from prolonged exposure to stressors that exceed the person’s resources to cope’ (Cooper et al., 2001, p. 84).

The most widely accepted model of burnout is the three-component conceptualization developed and elaborated by Maslach and colleagues (Maslach, 1982, 1998; Maslach et al., 1996). Early work by Maslach (1982) focused on occupations where workers have extensive interaction with other people, defining burnout as ‘a syndromeythat can occur among people who do ‘‘people work’’ of some kind’ (p. 1). Subsequently, a more general model was developed, intended for applicability to a broader range of occupations (Maslach et al., 1996). The components in this model are: (1) exhaustion, (2) cynicism (a distant attitude toward the job), and (3) reduced professional efficacy. An alternative perspective, one that is gaining support in the research community, views emotional exhaustion as the core element of burnout and suggests that the other two dimensions are separate, but related variables (see, e.g., Koeske & Koeske, 1993; Lee & Ashforth, 1996). In practice, however, it is still common for instruments based on the threecomponent view (the MBI (Maslach Burnout Inventory) and MBI-GS (General Survey) (Maslach et al., 1996)) to be used in burnout research. We will return to the discussion of alternative conceptualizations later in the paper, in light of the findings of the current study.

Our literature review identified 31 studies specifically on the topic of burnout in IT. We classified each study according to the research approach – ‘universal’ (using general models and constructs), ‘IT-specific’ (identifying/ incorporating occupation-specific factors), or ‘mixed’ (employing elements of both strategies). As noted earlier, the dominant approach used in this research was a universal, or non-specific, approach (26 studies). One example is the study by King & Sethi (1997) to examine the moderating role of organization commitment on the relationship between role stressors (role ambiguity and role conflict) and burnout in IS professionals. All of the concepts used in the study were pre-defined and drawn from general OB theory. Moreover, for the four studies classified as using a mixed research approach, universal models/constructs represented the core of the research strategy, with the IT-specific component playing a smaller, supplemental role. The concepts in the research model in Moore’s (2000) study of work exhaustion in IT, for example, were fixed (perceived workload, role ambiguity, role conflict, autonomy, fairness of rewards, work exhaustion, and turnover intention) and measured using established scales. In addition, however, Moore also used a qualitative approach to gain insights into the primary causes of exhaustion, content-analyzing responses to an open-ended question. The single study exclusively employing an occupation-specific approach to the research was an investigation by Fujigaki & Mori (1997) into the relationship between work events and symptoms of stress (physiological and subjective). Work content and stress reactions of 10 software engineers were surveyed every

1–2 weeks for 7 months. Data on work content were collected using a diary method and semi-structured interview; urine and salivary samples were collected daily and analyzed for biochemical measures of stress (e.g., adrenaline, cortisol); and self-reports of states of feeling (depression, anxiety, pain arousal, drowsiness, and degree of busyness) were also recorded daily. The situated nature of this research facilitated the emergence of concepts as the researchers observed and interacted with the subjects over time. Based on our review of the literature, then, we were concerned with the prevalence of theories and concepts stemming from the ‘global’ approach to studying burnout. We also felt that, as discussed earlier, the IS studies pursuing the occupation-specific or mixed approaches suffered from three limitations, viz., (1) the age of studies, (2) the exclusive focus on stressors and insufficient emphasis on assessing salience of individual burnout elements, and (3) the lack of attention to occupation-specific aspects of the inter-relationships among the individual elements. These concerns led us to the use of social representations theory and methods in the present study.

## Social representations theory

Social representations theory provided the basis to capture and analyze the perspectives of IT professionals on job-related burnout. Social representations theory was formulated by French social psychologist Serge Moscovici and associates (Moscovici 1961/1976, 1981; Farr & Moscovici, 1984). The theory has roots in Durkheim’s (1912/1995) distinction between collective and individual representation, and links macro-level social discourse with individual social behavior, cognition, affect, and symbolic understanding (Wagner et al., 1996). Social representations can be defined as commonsense knowledge about general topics (in this case, burnout) that are the focus of everyday conversation (Lorenzi-Cioldi & Clemence, 2001). Formed through discursive practices, social representations are the shared images and concepts through which we organize our world (Parker, 1987; Wagner et al., 1996).

A fundamental hypothesis of social representations theory relates to the structure of representations, which are seen as consisting of a central core and peripheral elements (Abric, 1976). The central core, or attitudinal component, is the most resistant to change – ‘It constitutes the most stable element of the representation, the one that ensures the perennial nature of the representation in moving and evolving contexts’ (Abric, 2001, p. 44). The core provides a ‘generating function’ through which the other elements of the representation acquire meaning and value (Abric, 2001). Peripheral elements are organized around the central core and are the area of adaptation based on new information or transformation of the environment. One of the functions of peripheral elements is to act as a defense system, or ‘shock absorber,’ because they may change without disturbing the central core (Flament, 1994). Effective transformation of a representation, then, is only possible if the central core itself is questioned (Moliner, 1992).

While social representations theory has been used to understand commonsense knowledge in the larger society about topics such as intelligence, mental illness and gender, the theory and related methods can also be used as a tool for the analysis of organizational cognition (see, e.g., Nicolini, 1999). In this study, our goal was to capture the social representations of burnout of the members of an occupational group – IT professionals. An advantage of this approach is that it allowed us to address the limitations of the existing IS literature on burnout discussed above. First, by eliciting a cognitive representation reflecting how burnout is currently understood within the IT profession we avoided biasing the exploration towards prior theoretical conceptualizations and obtained a situated up-to-date view of the burnout phenomenon. Second, the holistic nature of the social representation concept enabled us to capture and evaluate not only stressors, but also all burnout elements salient to members of the IT profession. Finally, by employing a structural approach to analyzing social representations, we were able to gain additional insights into the relative salience of individual burnout elements as well as to assess the nature of their inter-relationships. We discuss the methodological approach next.

## Method

## Eliciting social representations

Farr & Moscovici (1984) recommend the analysis of interview transcripts as one data collection method to elicit social representations. The goal in this study was to understand the social representations of burnout of IT professionals from diverse work settings, types of IT jobs, and levels of responsibility. Study participants were solicited from IT professional organizations/user groups (e.g., ACM and IEEE SIGs, AITP, Linux, and Oracle user groups). Leaders of local chapters were contacted and asked to forward an e-mail to their membership requesting volunteers to participate in the study. One of those contacts resulted in the publication of the request in the online newsletter of Women in Technology International (WITI) (http://www.witi.com/). The request gave a brief description of the topic and purpose of the research and that we wanted to interview IT professionals who had either: (1) experienced work-related burnout or (2) had observed situations where other IT professionals had experienced burnout. To avoid biasing the input, the solicitation request did not explain what we meant by the term burnout, nor were any examples given. Solicitation of participants continued until: (1) information saturation was reached (i.e., when the incremental information provided by additional interviewing was judged to be insignificant), and (2) sufficient diversity in terms of job types and work settings was achieved.

Twenty IT professionals participated in the study. Participants first completed a web-based form with questions about their IT work experience and demographic information (see Table 1). Data were collected via telephone interviews (30 min on average; tape recorded and transcribed). The interview format was semi-structured, following an interview guide. Subjects were asked to: describe, in detail, a specific case of burnout they had experienced or observed; to define burnout; to identify causes of burnout in IT; and to recommend ways to prevent burnout.

## Mapping social representations

Coding The first step in the analysis process was detailed coding of the interview data. Atlas.ti software was used to manage and organize the results of the coding. Transcripts were coded by one of the researchers using an open coding process (i.e., codes were not pre-determined, but rather emerged from the data). For example, five codes were assigned to the following transcript segment:

Acquisition (of another company) is very stressful because you really don’t get additional resources to handle it. You have to just fit it into your regular day-to-day work. So, not having all the proper skillsy We hadn’t really had a large acquisition. That was our first time. We didn’t know how to do it. So a lot of it was trial and error. And then there was a lot of stuff just dumped on us, tooy unrealistic due datesy Just to make it happen regardless of what. And as an IT professional you want to do things the right way. (Subject # 3 – Manager of Information Services)

Assigned codes were: SI-2, company growing fast/ acquisition of new company; SW-19, extra work in addition to ‘regular work’; SW-10, no prior experience with this type of project/doing by ‘trial and error’; SW-7, unrealistic deadlines; SP-7, want to do things ‘the right way.’ One hundred and eight detailed codes were identified during the coding process. A second coder, an MBA student, received training in the coding process and then independently re-coded the interview transcripts using this set of codes. Inter-coder agreement, based on presence/absence of codes in each of the interviews was 77.8%. The second step in the coding process was to group related codes. Output of this roll-up step was the set of 22 topics shown in Table 2.

In step 1 of the analysis of similarity, the source-byattribute data matrix obtained from the content analysis was transformed into the inter-attribute similarity (IAS) matrix. Each cell of the IAS matrix contains a Jaccard’s similarity coefficient, indicating a degree of co-occurrence (proximity) for a given pair of attributes (Hammond, 1993). In step 2 of the procedure, significant relationships among the elements of the representation were identified by constructing the ‘maximum tree’ of the system based on the pair-wise similarity indexes from the IAS matrix. Flament’s (1986) notion of the maximum tree in this case is equivalent to the minimum spanning tree concept originating in graph theory (Doise et al., 1993, p. 33). Minimum spanning trees search for the shortest path to connect all nodes within a graph in such a way that there is only one link between any two nodes. In the context of social representations theory, Flament’s maximum tree seeks to single out those relationships among the elements that maximize the overall similarity within the representation. In order to construct the maximum tree the nearest neighbor algorithm was run on the IAS matrix; absolute frequencies of themes’ appearance were used to break ties (Kruskal, 1956). The resulting social representation map is presented in Figure 2. It is important to point out that the map does not reflect the ‘actual’ location of the elements in the representation, but serves as an illustration of the pattern of significant relationships among the major themes.

In the final stage of data analysis, we explored the core/ periphery structure of the social representation of burnout. Abric (2001) identified three fundamental criteria that characterize elements belonging to the central core, namely symbolic value, expressive value, and associative value. Symbolic value stems from the generating function of the core and is based on the concept that central elements cannot be questioned without affecting the signification of the entire representation. A number of experimental and empirical methods have been developed that utilize this principle to discover elements that form the central nucleus of the representation (Flament et al., 1998). Expressive value assumes that, due to their place in the representation, central elements will be more frequently present in the discourse concerning the object of the representation than their peripheral counterparts.

The final criterion, associative value, is operationalized as the degree of connectivity of an element within a representation. Because central elements are responsible for giving meaning to the representation, they must be associated with a larger number of representation constituents than any of the peripheral elements are.

Among the three aforementioned criteria only the first one, symbolic value, is deemed sufficient to establish the core status of an element. The other two are necessary but not sufficient conditions. Since the objective of this study was to make a first approach to understanding the structure of the representation, we focused our attention primarily on evaluating the two latter criteria of centrality. Symbolic value of the items was not assessed as it would have required conducting additional experiments and/or administering more surveys. While our approach does not allow for unambiguous definition of the core elements, it provides enough information to outline which components of the map presented in Figure 2 might form the central nucleus of the representation.

In this study, expressive value was measured by computing frequencies of appearance of individual elements in the source data (i.e., the number of interviews where that topic was cited). This parameter, termed salience, is widely used in social representation analysis (Nicolini, 1999; Abric, 2001). Associative value was assessed by means of two indexes, sum similarity and coreness. Sum similarity is conceptually analogous to the notion of closeness of a graph (Sabidussi, 1966), and was calculated as a sum of the similarities of an element to all other elements in the IAS matrix. An item with high sum similarity is ‘close’ to a large number of other components of the representation, and thus has high associative value. Finally, a continuous model of core/periphery structure was fit to the IAS data matrix to estimate coreness (i.e., closeness to the core) of each element. This method was developed by Borgatti & Everett (1999) to discover core/periphery structures in network data consisting of continuous values representing strengths of relationships. ## Discussion

The findings of the empirical investigation described above provide a detailed and comprehensive view of job stress and burnout situated specifically in the social context of the IT profession today. In this section, we report on the implications of those understandings for research and practice. An important advantage of the structural approach to social representations is that it enables the analysis of both the parts and the whole (i.e., the individual concepts and their relationships) of the understandings of the social reality within the collectivity of the IT profession.

In the discussion that follows, we first focus on individual elements of the representation elicited from IT professionals and compare those to the elements reflected in the global burnout models. In doing so we seek to accomplish two goals: (1) to complement the global conceptualization of the burnout elements with a description of their specific manifestations in the IT context, and (2) to identify burnout elements that are not reflected in the global models. Our analysis is not limited to stressors but covers a full range of aspects of burnout, including strains, employee traits, and outcomes. We also evaluate salience of the individual map elements and the extent to which they are addressed in the extant IS literature on burnout. This allows us to identify priority areas for future research on burnout in IT. Second, we interpret the structure of the social representation in order to glean additional insights regarding potential inter-relationships among the burnout elements. A deeper understanding of the relations among various stressors as well as among stressors, burnout symptoms, and burnout outcomes is critical to enable better management of burnout in IT.

Extended hours/workload (T1) was the most commonly mentioned stressor intrinsic to the job, particularly in a project context. This finding is consistent with both the global burnout models and the existing IS studies of IT professionals. Quantitative workload (the amount of work required and the timeframe in which it must be completed) and the number of hours a person works have been found to be significant stressors for workers (e.g., Narayanan et al., 1999). Moore’s (2000) study of work exhaustion in technology professionals, for example, found that perceived workload was the strongest contributor to work exhaustion. Work overload was the foremost project issue (T3) described, a common problem for members of IT project teams working under the pressures of tight deadline conditions.

Four other intrinsic job characteristics were also identified as contributors to burnout in IT – multiples/ multi-tasking (T15), skills issues (T19), missing job satisfactions (T22), and boring work (T20). Of these, only skills-related issues (e.g., fear of obsolescence, keeping up with technological change) have received much attention in prior research. Our results highlight the additional stresses that arise when new skills need to be developed in conjunction with the accomplishment of an assignment. Our review of the literature did not identify any studies of the relationship between multi-tasking and job stress, although role overload (Cooper, 1987) may be closely related. This is a topic deserving of further study, because it is an area where supervisors may have some discretion in terms of framing assignments and responsibilities.

Similarly, the observation that IT work can be stressful because it does not provide some of the ‘satisfactions’ found in other types of work brings up the question of whether some work elements can be redesigned in ways to provide these positive aspects. Lastly, boring work identified as a stressor is a reminder that too little stimulation can be a problem for IT workers. This stands in contrast to the general viewpoint that that understimulation/boredom is a source of stress primarily in blue collar and clerical settings (‘blue collar blues’), whereas overstimulation/elevated responsibility is primarily a source of stress in professional and managerial settings (‘white collar woes’) (Xie & Johns, 1995).

Consistent with the original research on burnout, relationships with others in the workplace (T8) was identified as a major source of stress in IT, particularly interactions with users. The findings of prior studies involving people-oriented professions may be most applicable to this aspect of some IT jobs. Several IS studies of burnout have examined stresses from interpersonal relationships, studying, for example, the quality of social interaction within teams, and friction between IS and users.

The three stressors identified in the organizational environment paint a vivid picture of the leadership and organizational issues that can negatively affect IT workers. First, an overwhelming majority of the study participants saw ineffective IT management and supervision (T4) as a root cause of burnout. They reported a range of deficiencies, including the inability to manage internal politics effectively, poor project planning/management skills, lack of communication, and lack of supervisor support. Studies have shown that supervision style (e.g., rigid performance monitoring) and inconsiderate behavior from a supervisor can contribute significantly to feelings of job pressure and stress (McLean, 1979; O’Driscoll & Beehr, 1994). An interesting question is whether IT supervisors/project leaders are less effective in preventing and addressing burnout in their employees than their counterparts in other occupations. Perhaps one of the most telling comments from participants was that IT managers and supervisors should be chosen for their leadership and people skills, not for their technical skills. While poor leadership/supervision was seen as a long-standing problem, participants also noted two areas of change in their organizational environments that can negatively impact IT workers and produce elevated levels of stress. One trend was increasing dependence on IT (T21) and the high business impact of IT-related problems. The second change noted was a deterioration of corporate ethics and values, seen largely as a result of increased competitive pressures. Changing environmental conditions such as these may overtax the coping abilities of some IT workers, pushing them beyond their personal ‘tipping point’ into burnout.

Looking to the larger business environment, sources of stress for IT workers included increased business competitiveness/company survival (T5) and downturns in the IT job market creating feelings of job insecurity (T14). Given the continued expansion of IT offshore outsourcing and globalization of the IT job market, it is somewhat surprising that job insecurity did not emerge as one of the primary stressors in this study, but as a secondary concern. This finding is also consistent with a recent study of IT professionals by Sethi et al. (2004). The final job-related stressor identified was the home/work interface (T7). Some aspects of IT work, such as being on-call, the ability to work at home, the inability to ‘shut off’ mental work after leaving the office, and the long work hours can negatively impact an individual’s family, social life, relationships with significant others, and so on. Work–life balance has been a heavily studied topic in OB and also has received attention in IS research.

In summary, a wide range of stressors were identified in the study. While some had been previously addressed in the IS literature, others, such as missing job satisfaction and increased IT dependence, had not (see Table 4). Furthermore, the methodological approach employed to analyze the social representation of burnout allowed us to gain a greater understanding of the salience of individual stressors in the IT profession, as reflected in the core– periphery structure of the map.

Employee traits Wanting to do things ‘right’ (T17) emerged as the single individual characteristic seen as contributing to burnout. This situated concept corresponds to the global construct of self-critical perfectionism that has been shown to be strongly related to stress (e.g., Dunkley et al., 2003). A question that arises, however, is whether individuals drawn to the IT profession are more likely to exhibit this trait than members of other professions.

Outcomes of Burnout Three outcomes of burnout were identified in the study – reduced job performance (T16), leaving the job/profession (T2), and reduced physical well-being (T9). All three elements belong to the core of the social representation, which points to their importance in giving meaning to IT professionals’ understanding of burnout. Each of these outcomes is also prominent in the literature drawing upon the global burnout models. Empirical studies have demonstrated the negative correlation between burnout and job performance (Burke & Richardsen, 1993; Wright & Bonett, 1997). Linkages between burnout and organizational and professional commitment have also been explored, although these relationships need further study (Leiter & Maslach, 1988; Lee & Ashforth, 1996). Healthrelated consequences of burnout can include cardiovascular and coronary heart disease, drug and alcohol abuse, and accidents (Hobfoll & Shirom, 1993).

## Key insights based on map structure

The social representation map in Figure 2 provides an initial view of inter-relationships among the concepts through which IT professionals may organize their understandings of burnout in the profession. Analysis of these structural aspects of the representation is useful in helping us uncover insights that may not be evident from the examination of individual elements of the map. In this regard, we offer the following preliminary observations relevant to future research on burnout in IT:

\- Three concepts appear central to IT professionals’ understanding of burnout: (T1) Working long hours over an extended period of time/heavy workload, (T10) emotional strain/negative emotions, and (T16) negative impacts on job performance.

The three aforementioned concepts exhibit the highest scores on all three criteria used in this study to establish core status of the elements. The core, as discussed earlier, is the most stable part that provides meaning to the rest of the social representation. Furthermore, in the representation map, which was designed to explicate significant relationships within the representation, the three elements, T1, T10, and T16, appear as adjacent nodes connected by links with the highest degree of similarity (i.e., link strength). While the methodology employed in this study by no means allows establishing causal relationships between elements, these observations seem to suggest that IT professionals’ understandings of burnout are organized around the following conceptual schema: long work hours (emerged as the primary stressor) is related to the development of emotional strain and exhaustion, which in turn is related to negative impacts on job performance. This conceptualization is consistent with the global burnout models and, hence, provides additional and independent evidence validating the use of these models in the context of the IT profession. Furthermore, the grouping of the three concepts reiterates the importance of long working hours and work overload as a key factor among IT professionals leading to the development of severe emotional strain, a core component of the job-related burnout.

\- Project issues (T3) emerged as an organizing concept, relating IT management/supervision issues (T4) and interpersonal issues (T8) with the primary stressor – working long hours/heavy workload (T1).

One interpretation of this concept cluster is that projects were seen as the main work environment where pressures for long work hours and heavy workload are likely to occur. Participants relayed a complex set of issues and concerns related to project selection, prioritization, planning, management, deadlines, unexpected requirements/demands and other problems often seen as outside of the control of project leaders and team members. Perhaps most revealing is the strong association between project issues and perceived shortcomings of IT management/supervision. Besides poor management, dissension in the workplace, including conflicts with users and colleagues, was cited as both a cause of strain and an outcome of job pressures. The dominance of projects, as a way of organizing work, is perhaps one aspect that sets apart the IT profession from the generic professional context that underpins global burnout models. Consequently, while none of the individual factors discussed above is new to the burnout literature, we argue that the role of the project environment in bringing together and facilitating effects of other stressors on IT professionals warrants further attention on the part of both IS researchers and practitioners. For researchers, incorporating project-related variables into the research models will likely enhance the models’ analytical potential and improve their explanatory power. Similarly for practitioners, being cognizant of various aspects of the project environment that foster the development of jobrelated stress may help to design more effective and efficient prevention strategies.

\- Stresses of the current business environment are reflected in peripheral concepts in the map – job market/job insecurity (T14); competitive business environment (T4) and corporate culture/values/ethics (T6).

These peripheral elements highlight concerns stemming from current circumstances in the larger business environment. This finding is consistent with the assump tions of the structural view of social representations, where the periphery is seen as the area of adaptation based on new information or transformation of the environment. In general, we would suggest that researchers focus their efforts on studying stressors comprising the core of the representation (e.g., extended work hours, project issues), inasmuch as these issues are seen as more central and long-standing in IT work. Nevertheless, a close monitoring of the peripheral elements is also recommended, as over time these concerns may rise in salience and drift into the core of the representation. Furthermore, we posit that the peripheral elements also need to be attended to by managers who should seek means to alleviate the compounding effect of these factors on the core stressors.

## Limitations of the study

There are, of course, limitations to the study that need to be acknowledged. First, our data were drawn from a small number of IT professionals. While we took steps to achieve diversity in terms of job types and job settings, generalizability to the entire profession cannot be assumed. Second, as noted previously, the method used for this study, analysis of similarity, can only provide preliminary evidence of centrality of elements in the social representation. Confirmation of the core elements would require additional steps such as surveys or experiments.

## Conclusion

We began by advocating an occupation-specific approach to research on job stress and burnout in IT wherein generic variables and concepts drawn from global models of burnout are integrated with factors and concerns specific to the IT profession. We argued that the advantages of an occupation-specific approach include not only the potential to improve the explanatory power of our models but also the ability to develop prevention and intervention strategies targeted at issues that are highly salient and germane to the IT work context. To that end, we conducted an exploratory study to gain an understanding of the meanings through which today’s IT professionals make sense of job-related burnout. The results of the study contribute to the development of a research agenda that balances parsimony and completeness and seeks to achieve higher relevance for the IT profession. In this conclusion, we summarize the key implications of our findings for research and practice.

The key contribution of the empirical exploration reported in this paper is that it helps to refine directions for future research on burnout in the IT profession, which in turn enables a more targeted vs a shotgun research approach. The paper accomplishes this in several important ways. First, the results of the core/periphery analysis of the social representation map point to the specific topics from the global research on job stress and burnout that are likely to be most relevant in the IT context (e.g., work overload, home/work interface, supervisor support). Furthermore, by integrating the core/ periphery analysis with the examination of burnout constructs reflected in the IS literature, we identified areas that warrant further investigation by IS researchers in the near-term. These priority areas encompass elements that are highly salient to IT professionals (i.e., core elements) yet underrepresented in the existing literature. In particular, issues revolving around the effectiveness of

IT management/supervision and the notion of IT dependence emerged as the two priority areas for future studies. Second, the evaluation of structural aspects of the social representation revealed an issue that, in our opinion, previously had not been explicitly addressed in the literature. The issue concerns the special role of the project work environment in facilitating effects of burnout stressors on IT professionals. Due to the prominence of projects in IT work, we argue that IS researchers need to afford special attention to exploring this matter in more depth.

Another contribution of this paper is that it provides a foundation for capturing changes over time in how the burnout phenomenon is understood and experienced by IT professionals. Attending to such temporal shifts in meaning is critical considering the fast-paced nature of the IT environment. To accomplish this, we could use the social representation map constructed in this study as a ‘baseline’ for a longitudinal view of job stress and burnout in IT. In the future, similar studies will need to be conducted to indentify shifts in the cognitive elements constituting burnout, as well as changes in the elements’ relationships and centrality.

The findings of the study also enable us to shift attention towards aspects of the job that can mitigate the negative effects of these particular job stressors. The job demands–resources (JD-R) model (Demerouti et al., 2001; Bakker et al., 2003), for example, offers a theoretical framework that can be used to develop a model with job stressors and job resources relevant to a particular occupation or work context (e.g., IT projects). Using this approach, particular demands can be matched with particular resources in the workplace (De Jonge & Dormann, 2003). A recent study, for example, found that social support from colleagues, a high-quality relationship with the supervisor and performance feedback were capable of buffering the impact of workload on exhaustion (Bakker et al., 2005). These findings resonate with key elements and relationships in the social representations map. Finally, this paper offers valuable insights to IT managers who need guidance for strategies to prevent burnout in their employees. In the absence of this guidance, attempts to deal with the problem may do little to address the root causes of stress in these jobs. At a general level, while our findings are largely consistent with the OB conceptualization of burnout, in many cases we provide a more nuanced view of burnout concepts – a view grounded in the terms and events specific to IT work. Consequently, IT managers will find it easier to relate to the ideas discussed in the paper and to recognize their ramifications in dealing with burnout in the workplace. We also identify specific areas wherein efforts to prevent burnout may yield greater returns. A survey of CIOs, for example, found that the actions most commonly taken to address burnout in their organizations were offering skills-development programs, beefing up retention programs, raising salaries, offering cash incentives, and occasionally moving employees to new locales in an attempt to improve morale (Hayes, 2003). The findings of the present study, however, suggest that directing resources towards training and skills development in the areas of project management and supervision, including the recognition of the symptoms of burnout and interventions, and may prove to be a more effective strategy. Supervisors, for example, can play a critical role in buffering the strains of organizational politics through open communication relationships with employees and participatory decision-making (Harris & Kacmar, 2005). Training and mentoring to develop these kinds of skills, not just technical skills, should be given high priority.

In conclusion, we reiterate our call for a more balanced research agenda on burnout in the IT profession, wherein research based on global models is complemented and enhanced with insights generated through occupationspecific investigations. We suggest that future studies within this agenda initially focus on the research directions outlined in this study. This represents a departure from previous work on burnout in IT, which has traditionally taken a more ad hoc approach. We believe that directing future efforts in this way will leverage limited research resources in ways that are most likely to benefit IT professionals, helping to prevent situations such as those experienced by the software engineer whose story was told by Kidder (1981).
