# THE VALUE OF CENTRALIZED IT IN BUILDING RESILIENCE DURING CRISES: EVIDENCE FROM U.S. HIGHER EDUCATION’S TRANSITION TO EMERGENCY REMOTE TEACHING<sup>1</sup>

Jiyong Park Bryan School of Business and Economics, University of North Carolina at Greensboro Greensboro, NC, U.S.A. {jiyong.park@uncg.edu}

Yoonseock Son and Corey M. Angst Mendoza College of Business, University of Notre Dame Notre Dame, IN, U.S.A. {yson@nd.edu} {cangst@nd.edu}

The COVID-19 pandemic forced organizations, including higher education institutions, to rapidly adjust their operations. In the face of the pandemic, most higher education institutions shut down their campuses and transitioned to emergency remote teaching mode. This study examines digital resilience in higher education institutions through the conceptual lens of disaster response management, by assessing the role played by the centralized governance of information technology (IT) investments. We posit that centralized IT helps organizations maintain customer satisfaction with services during a crisis (e.g., student satisfaction with classes during COVID-19) by facilitating the organization-wide transition to an emergency operational mode and supporting its service operations. Consolidating data on IT investment, governance, and course evaluations from 463 U.S. higher education institutions from 2017-2020, we show that centralized IT helped organizations adapt better to the pandemic in terms of maintaining student satisfaction. Moreover, we found that centralized IT investments geared toward facilitating organizational coordination and providing instructional and technical support played a pivotal role in enabling ERT and improving student ratings during the crisis. These results are corroborated by interviews with CIOs of U.S. higher education institutions. Additional analyses also suggest that the effectiveness of centralized IT governance is contingent upon organizational size, dissimilarity of local units, and the strategic role of the CIO. We also discuss theoretical extensions toward digital resilience as well as practical implications.

Keywords: Digital resilience, business value of IT, IT governance, higher education, disaster management, emergency management, emergency remote teaching, COVID-19

## Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has taken a massive toll on the global economy and created tremendous worldwide turmoil in virtually every industry. In particular, the education system is one of the sectors that was severely affected by the pandemic shock (Benzell et al., 2020). Similar to other sectors, university faculty, staff, and students were advised and/or mandated to work from home in compliance with COVID-19 protocols (Aucejo et al., 2020). In order to continue instruction during the crisis, most higher education institutions (hereafter, HEIs) suspended all in-person classes and transitioned to online delivery, leveraging information technology (IT)—referred to as emergency remote teaching (ERT; Hodges et al., 2020). ERT is one of the key enabling factors that allowed HEIs to continue to operate during the crisis. It is differentiated from typical online teaching in that it entails an unplanned, temporary shift of instructional delivery to the remote (online)

mode, without other alternative options, and does not allow adequate time for a careful instructional redesign, planning, or development that would typically be required for adapting an in-person course to an online environment (Hodges et al., 2020; Iglesias-Pradas et al., 2021). This rapid transition to ERT is analogous to crisis-driven agility in transitioning to remote work and virtual contactless services in other industry sectors (Rai, 2020). As COVID-19 has elevated the importance of organizational IT capabilities in managing such unplanned transitions and continuing service operations during crises, this study aims to answer the following research question in the context of higher education: How does IT investment help HEIs build resilience to crises?

Building on the definition provided by Boh et al. (2023), we define digital resilience, particularly in the service sector, as the investment, usage, and governance of IT in a way that affords organizations the capabilities to offer high-quality services and maintain customer satisfaction, similar to what they offer under normal conditions, during a crisis. Given the nature of emergency operational modes (e.g., ERT and remote work) that enable organizations to continue business and operations during crises, the findings from extant literature on the business value of IT might not be applicable in a crisis situation, which calls for new research integrating the information systems (IS) perspective with disaster management perspectives (Abbasi et al., 2021). While organizational crisis adaptation strategies have received attention in the literature (Carugati et al., 2020), empirical evidence on the effectiveness of IT-driven organizational transitions during crises has been elusive. Thus, we examine digital resilience in HEIs through the conceptual lens of disaster response management, by assessing how IT investment plays a role in affording HEIs the ability to transition to ERT more effectively, which relies on digital environments to maintain operational continuity during a crisis.

As HEIs needed to make a rapid transition to ERT since resources and time were constrained during the pandemic, it was critical for them to coordinate institutional priorities and deploy the limited resources across the organization in keeping with institutional goals. Although it has been suggested that IT governance (i.e., centralization to decentralization mix) plays a role in weathering environmental uncertainties (Xue et al., 2011), to the best of our knowledge, no study has scrutinized the role that the governance of IT investments plays during a crisis where organizations need to rapidly respond to a new environment with limited resources. Drawing upon the disaster response management literature (Harrald, 2006), we develop our theoretical arguments on the effectiveness of centralized IT investments in building organizational digital resilience. Specifically, we posit that centralized IT helps organizations maintain customer satisfaction with services during a crisis (e.g., student satisfaction with classes during COVID-19) by (1) enhancing the organizational coordination of information and resources and thus streamlining the transition to an emergency operational mode (e.g., an HEI’s ERT), and (2) helping to prioritize resources across the institution to provide the technical support needed for service operations (e.g., instructional and technical support to both students and instructors in HEIs).<sup>2</sup>

For the empirical analysis, we proxy an HEI’s student satisfaction with classes using average student ratings of courses offered by professors teaching at the institution. This information was collected from the leading, non-HEI-specific, online platform for higher education review, RateMyProfessors.com (RMP), from the first quarter of 2017 to the third quarter of 2020. We combined student ratings with data on annual IT spending for 463 U.S. HEIs obtained from the EDUCAUSE annual survey, which allowed us to measure the extent to which IT expenditures are controlled and governed by the highest-ranking IT officer (e.g., the chief information officer; CIO)—which we define as centralized IT. Employing a difference-in-differences (DID) model, we compared how the impact of the pandemic shock on student ratings differed across two groups: HEIs with higher and lower centralized IT investments.

Our findings suggest that HEIs that invested more in centralized IT adapted better to the transition to ERT in terms of maintaining student satisfaction during the pandemic. Intriguingly, this finding contradicts those from prior studies showing that decentralized IT governance is more beneficial under high environmental uncertainty (within the range of business-as-usual scenarios), suggesting that extremely uncertain environments that force abrupt responses, like that brought on by COVID-19, do require different responses. We also found that the driving forces affording a higher level of resilience are not IT applications that are specific to unique needs in the education sector (e.g., educational technology and research computing) but are instead non-industry-specific IT and IS (e.g., administration/management of IT, enterprise IS/infrastructure, communications infrastructure, and IT support services) that help to facilitate the processes of making organizational changes and coordinate and support internal operations across an organization. This finding is corroborated by interviews with numerous CIOs of U.S. HEIs. Finally, we found the effectiveness of centralized IT governance to be contingent upon organizational size, dissimilarity of local units, and the strategic role of the CIO in aligning IT with institutional goals.

Our study provides the first empirical evidence supporting the role of centralized governance of IT in building resilience, which can support organizations in effectively managing a rapid transition to the emergency mode during a crisis. This research extends strands of IS literature on the business value of IT and IT governance by demonstrating that IT-enabled digital resilience can assist with maintaining business continuity and customer satisfaction during a crisis. While our research context is specific to higher education, we believe our findings are generalizable to other service sectors confronted by turbulent environments with limited resources. In particular, whenever a disruptive event forces a shift to a remote work environment that requires leveraging digital platforms, we expect the centralized governance of IT investment to be the key driver of business continuity and performance.

## Theoretical Background and Conceptual Framework

## IT Investment and Governance

In the IS literature, a scholarly consensus has been formed around the positive contribution of IT investment to organizational performance (Melville et al., 2004), including but not limited to customer service (Ray et al., 2005) and customer satisfaction (Mithas et al., 2016), both of which are central to our study. IT allows organizations to more effectively redesign existing processes and optimize internal operational processes, fostering organizational agility (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). As organizations need to sense and respond to threats in an agile manner during turbulent environments to maintain performance (Tallon & Pinnoseault, 2011), the importance of IT capabilities is likely to be further amplified during an extreme crisis, such as a global pandemic, because organizations are forced to make rapid online transitions to maintain business continuity.

Research has suggested that IT governance is critical in linking IT capabilities and organizational performance, depending on environmental uncertainty (Melville et al., 2004; Xue et al., 2011). IT governance refers to the allocation of decision authority and control of IT investments in an organizational structure (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999; Xue et al., 2008). It is primarily categorized into centralized and decentralized structures, depending on how IT investments are organized in decision-making, resource management, and usage. As discussed below, the two governance structures have distinct comparative advantages in that centralized and decentralized governance are uniquely positioned to enhance coordination efficiency and flexibility/responsiveness, respectively (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999).

In the HEI context, centralized IT refers to the proportion of IT budgets and resources that the highest-ranking IT officer, typically the CIO, has control over and for which they make requests for campus-wide IT services, such as enterprise applications and software licenses that are applied institution-wide. IT centralization can confer benefits to HEIs by collecting and coordinating information and resources, spearheading and guiding campus-wide initiatives, and monitoring them across the institution so that they can achieve institutional goals while providing sufficient configurability to serve each departmental need (Perkins, 2021). Alternatively, decentralized IT refers to the degree of IT budgets and resources that each academic unit (e.g., department, school) requests and spends independently, beyond the control of the CIO, to provide customized technology services (Bates, 2017).

It is noteworthy that given the trade-off between the coordination efficiency of centralization and the responsiveness of decentralization, prior studies have suggested that the decentralized governance of IT is preferable for better responsiveness and reactivity to market changes under more dynamic and uncertain environments (Xue et al., 2011). However, disasters or crises impose unprecedented levels of uncertainty on organizations, which could reshape the dynamics of environmental uncertainty and rebalance the trade-off between decentralized responsiveness and centralized coordination. Moreover, crises force organizations into a rapid emergency response, typically with limited resources, which heightens the importance of coordination, interoperability, and integration for disaster/emergency management (Guo et al., 2021).

## Emergency Response and Emergency Remote Teaching

Emergency response is “the process of gathering resources and acting upon problems immediately after an incident happens” (Shen & Shaw, 2004, p. 2210). Prior studies in the IS literature have examined the role of IT and IS in emergency management (van de Walle et al., 2014). Chen et al. (2010) suggested that IS for managing emergency incidents improves the performance of first responders. Based on a case study of three European HEIs, Carugati et al. (2020) illustrated how IT tools and practices have been exploited to deal with emergencies and how they help institutionalized crisis practices during the pandemic. However, we still have a limited understanding of the role that IT investment and governance play in emergency response when a rapid transition of operations to digital environments is required for business continuity.

ERT is one of the most notable corrective measures that HEIs took for emergency response during the COVID-19 pandemic (Hodges et al., 2020). ERT, which is distinct from typical online teaching, is usually undertaken with limited resources and insufficient time for training and preparation. Its primary objective is not to provide high-quality curricula and a robust educational ecosystem but instead to provide reliable, temporary, and quick access to instruction and its affordances by quickly establishing readily available educational resources (Karalis, 2020). Given the circumstances of a transition to ERT, there are two challenges for HEIs. First, faculty and students are likely to be neither trained nor prepared for the sudden transition and both may lack experience in online instruction. Second, existing IT resources are likely to be neither sufficient nor allocated appropriately to support an institution-wide transition to ERT. While the resources to support faculty and students were available before the emergency, the scope and scale of resources required for the transition may be beyond the current IT capacity (Johnson et al., 2020). Furthermore, the shortage of resources accentuates the importance of effective resource coordination and allocation during the transition to ERT.

## Role of IT in HEIs’ Transition to ERT

Given the nature of ERT, we draw upon the process of disaster response proposed by Harrald (2006), integrated with a framework of absorb-adapt-transform for digital resilience (Boh et al., 2023), to explain how IT can play a role in allowing HEIs to cope with the transition to ERT. First, organizations initially react to a disaster using existing resources while mobilizing external resources to the extent that they are able (Harrald, 2006). Once there is some degree of situational awareness, it is important for senior leaders to identify needs and response strategies across the organization. Second, resources should be coordinated and integrated across functional units. Specifically, available resources are restructured and reallocated by priority into functional units capable of identifying the needs and providing services (Harrald, 2006). Third, with resources being coordinated and reallocated across the organization, production (corresponding to service operations in service sectors) can be continued and important services can be delivered, with priority given to mission-critical service operations during and in the aftermath of a disaster.

Against this backdrop, HEIs were able to identify ERT as the most viable and realistic means to adapt to COVID-19, and we contend that IT facilitates the organizational integration and production phases of ERT as a disaster response strategy. Thus, in the following section, we elaborate on how IT plays significant roles in (1) organizational coordination and integration and (2) providing the means for instructional support applications (e.g., collaborative software, video conferencing) and technical support for service operations for ERT in HEIs.

## Organizational Coordination and Integration

Making effective use of the limited resources available during the abrupt transition caused by the COVID-19 pandemic was critical to responding to the uncertainty of the new environment (Bojovic et al., 2020). For HEIs, the key objective was to identify institutional priorities and coordinate information and resources across the organization to achieve student success during the pandemic (Grajek, 2020b). For the transition to ERT, HEIs needed to align technology choices with institutional strategic goals and planning. With the primary mission of HEIs being educating students, it was critical for them to be able to continue delivering high-quality instruction. To do this, it was necessary to scale up IT capacity to a level not seen during normal times. During this phase, the extensive use of cloud-based services and applications (e.g., Zoom, WebEx, Microsoft Teams) was critical to remote delivery (Grajek, 2020b). However, each department within an HEI could potentially use different on-premises systems and services, often creating information and resource silos (Lee, 2021). Hence, HEIs needed to decide which technology would be best integrated with the existing IT infrastructures across departments to align with the HEI’s mission (Bojovic et al., 2020). Furthermore, consideration needed to be given to software licensing for both students and faculty. For instance, in some cases, licensed software was not allowed to be used off-campus and/or installed on students’ personal devices. Whereas students would have usually accessed the software in libraries and computer labs, with the campus being closed, they had no choice but to access it remotely via virtual desktops. For scaling up the capacity of IT services related to facilitating ERT, it has been suggested that cloud services and virtual desktops are among the most influential technologies for ERT. These types of IT systems are typically under the direction of the CIO, making them centralized IT.<sup>3</sup>

With the constrained budgets and limited resources brought on by the pandemic, HEIs needed effective IT governance to collaboratively prioritize IT investments and initiatives across the institution and decide how and when to pool the restricted resources and where to spend on technologies (Reinitz et al., 2020). In many cases, however, the ways each department uses and governs IT are not transparent, and this may cause a source of duplication when measuring IT costs (Grajek, 2020a). Therefore, the drive to transition to ERT required centralized IT authority (e.g., CIO) to streamline and support internal processes and operations at the institutional level, which aligned the restructuring of resources and technology plans with the goals of the organization (Grajek, 2020b; Reinitz et al., 2020). When responding to disasters, centralized authority is considered by many to be a necessity because there is no time for negotiation within the organization and rapid decisions need to be made by eliminating layers of bureaucracy (Janssen et al., 2010). Thus, we expect that centralized IT for organizational coordination and integration can help facilitate HEIs’ transition to ERT.

## Instructional Support Applications and Technical Support

As the transition to ERT needed to happen abruptly without proper planning or training for either faculty or students, IT and administrative support services were critical in maintaining educational continuity during crises (Hodges et al., 2020). Accordingly, studies show that the important determinants of student satisfaction during online learning amid the pandemic were instructional support (e.g., instructors being readily available to students) and technical support (e.g., students knowing where to ask for help when they had technical issues, students getting help when needed) (Foerderer et al., 2021). Similarly, service quality (e.g., administrative, technical, and learning assistance) and overall system quality (e.g., recordings of tutorials, seminars, and practical classes) were also found to be among the most critical factors affecting student satisfaction and the quality of e-learning with the new learning environment (Kerzic et al., 2021).

Although technical support to students was undoubtedly a key factor in student satisfaction, providing faculty with applications that support ERT and technical assistance was also critical in indirectly affecting students’ satisfaction (Hodges et al., 2020). With respect to resource constraints, the abrupt transition to ERT requires faculty to prepare and/or redesign their course content to be compatible with online delivery—typically in 1-2 weeks (Adedoyin & Soykan, 2020). Thus, we contend that for an effective online transition, instructional support applications such as collaboration software should be readily available and integrated with other technologies for seamless operation. Moreover, whereas faculty can normally be deliberate in their evaluations of the functionalities and usability of technologies most suited to their courses, in a crisis situation, they may have no choice but to use the resources and technologies available for ERT (Dill et al., 2020). The burden of using such technology may also lead to technostress (Zeeshan et al., 2020). Faculty struggling with technology use may cause frustration and even humiliation, which is likely to result in dissatisfied students. Thus, readily available support for inexperienced faculty is critical in facilitating students’ learning experience.

Notably, the need for instructional support applications and technical support is typically common across departments, which can be effectively managed by centralized IT. For instance, institution-wide workshops and training are often provided by centralized IT for faculty and students to become familiar with technologies (Al-Sholi et al., 2021). Thus, we expect that centralized IT offering instructional support applications and technical support to faculty and students can facilitate HEIs’ transition to ERT.

## Data and Variables

We consolidated an institution-quarter level panel dataset of 463 U.S. HEIs from the first quarter of 2017 to the third quarter of 2020, which we obtained from three primary sources: (1) RMP for professor and course evaluation across U.S. HEIs, (2) the EDUCAUSE’s annual survey on HEIs’ IT investment, and (3) Integrated Postsecondary Education Data System (IPEDS) for HEIs’ characteristics. We used one-year lagged variables of institutional characteristics obtained from the second and third sources. Table 1 presents the descriptions and summary statistics of the variables used in the analysis. Table A1 in Appendix A reports the correlation matrix.

Student ratings as a proxy of student satisfaction: As the main outcome of interest, we proxied an HEI’s student satisfaction with classes using student ratings for courses offered by professors teaching at the institution. Our data collected from RMP has notable advantages in the context of our study. First, the online review platform allowed us to collect data related to students’ satisfaction with courses offered by hundreds of U.S. HEIs over the years in a consistent manner. Although prior work has used a single HEI’s end-ofcourse evaluations to document the relationship between student satisfaction and course modality (face-to-face vs. online) (e.g., Lowenthal et al., 2015), data from one or few HEIs are limited in terms of examining the role of organization-level factors, such as IT investment and governance. Even if we could collect the internal evaluation records of multiple HEIs, the problem of compatibility and consistency of evaluation systems would arise because evaluation criteria differ across HEIs. In contrast, consistent evaluation criteria are used on RMP for all HEIs, allowing for an appropriate comparison.

Second, the change in students’ evaluation of professors and courses in an HEI can be traced over time in RMP reviews. Such time-series cross-sectional data allowed us to employ a DID model that compares how the impact of the COVID-19 outbreak on student ratings differed across the two groups of HEIs with higher and lower centralized IT investments.

On the RMP platform, users write reviews for a specific course code taught by a professor, rate the course and its level of difficulty on a 5-point scale, and report whether they would be willing to take the course again. We measured student satisfaction using student ratings on RMP, which is a common approach in the literature (e.g., Ackerman & Chung, 2018; Cavanaugh et al., 2022), and used the percentage of students who reported they would take the course again as a robustness check. In addition, RMP users self-report additional course information, such as letter grades and whether the course was taken for credit, required a textbook, or mandated attendance. We developed a web scraper to acquire data on the reviews from RMP and collected all RMP reviews from January 2017 to September 2020, coinciding with data availability from other sources. To better reflect the satisfaction level of students who enrolled in the courses they reviewed, we only considered RMP reviews that reported grades and were marked as “for credit,” resulting in approximately 1.5 million reviews for 833 U.S. HEIs. We then averaged the course ratings of RMP reviews at the institution level per quarter.

IT investment and governance: We obtained data on IT investment and IT governance from the EDUCAUSE annual survey of its member HEIs on IT expenditures and IT-related practices and policies. This dataset has been used to examine IT usage in HEIs in prior IS literature (Liu et al., 2020). There are 1,017 U.S.-based institutions (809, 4-year HEIs) that participated in EDUCAUSE’s annual surveys at least once during 2016-2019. We measured the degree of IT investment as annual IT spending in U.S. dollars. Moreover, the survey separates IT expenditures controlled by the highest-ranking IT officer (e.g., CIO) and central IT teams from total IT spending, which we define as centralized IT spending, the main treatment of interest.<sup>4</sup>

Higher education institutional characteristics: We obtained the characteristics of U.S. HEIs from the IPEDS of the U.S. National Center for Education Statistics. <sup>5</sup> Specifically, we considered HEIs’ characteristics to control for factors that could affect an institutional response to the pandemic shock, including number of students, percentage of students enrolled exclusively in online education courses, admission rate, percentage of undergraduates awarded financial aid, value of endowment assets, and expenses in instruction-related functions (instruction, academic support, and student service).

Data consolidation procedure: In this study, we focused on degree-granting, public, or private nonprofit four-year HEIs in the U.S. We first matched the EDUCAUSE and IPEDS data by institutional names and locations, which were subsequently matched to the data collected from RMP. We manually examined the unmatched names to resolve any discrepancy among the three databases. As a result, 802 institutions were matched and available across all sources during the sample period. To ensure that student ratings on RMP were sufficient to proxy the institution-wide periodic student satisfaction with classes, we excluded HEIs with less than 35 reviews per quarter on average, resulting in 463 U.S. HEIs—the mean and median values of the quarterly number of reviews for our sample HEIs were 207 and 103, respectively. The HEIs in our sample accounted for approximately 60% of total full-time equivalent enrollments in all U.S. HEIs with more than 1,000 students in the fall of 2019. Our final dataset included 6,945 institution-quarter-level observations for 463 HEIs from the first quarter of 2017 to the third quarter of 2020.

## Methodology

## Difference-in-Differences Framework

We employed a DID modeling framework to use the COVID-19 pandemic as an exogenous shock to HEIs. Conceptually, an ideal natural experiment would quantify the average treatment effect as the difference in an outcome of interest after the treatment is applied between the treated and untreated groups. Unfortunately, our research setting (i.e., global pandemic) did not allow us to distinguish between treatment and control groups, as no institutions or organizations avoided the reach of the pandemic shock and virtually every U.S. HEI had transitioned to the ERT mode in March-April 2020. In that sense, recent studies that examine the impact of COVID-19 on organizational performance have adapted the typical DID model by creating treatment and control groups based on entities’ pre-pandemic characteristics of interest and comparing the changes in performance metrics before and after the COVID-19 outbreak between the two groups (e.g., Albuquerque et al., 2020; Bai et al., 2021).

To estimate the impact of centralized IT investment on student satisfaction during the pandemic, we thus designed the DID model in a way that compares how the impact of the pandemic shock differs across two groups, split by the treatment of interest in the pre-COVID period. Consistent with prior studies (Albuquerque et al., 2020; Bai et al., 2021), we define the treatment group as HEIs falling into the top quartile of average centralized IT spending per student during 2016-2019 and the control group as others—hereafter termed high and low centralized IT groups. Thus, our DID model assists in netting out unobserved time-varying factors that may have led to different levels of student ratings, irrespective of the pandemic shock, by subtracting the difference between the two groups in the before-COVID period from their difference in the after-COVID period. In this regard, our DID model relies on an identification assumption that high and low centralized IT groups are comparable in terms of student ratings in the pre-pandemic period, after controlling for observed characteristics. The parallel trends assumption was validated using matching and a relative time model.

## Empirical Model

To examine the effect of centralized IT investment on student ratings during COVID-19 for an institution i at year-quarter $t ,$ we employed the following DID specification:

$$

$$

where $y _ { i t }$ is the outcome of interest (i.e., student ratings); ??????????19<sub>??</sub> is a dichotomous variable indicating the after-COVID period subject to the transitioning to the ERT mode (after-COVID indicator, in short); ??????ℎ ?????????????????????? ???? ??????????<sub>??</sub> is the treatment indicator for centralized IT investment; $Z _ { i t }$ is a set of control variables listed in Table 1; and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ represents a random error not explained by the observables. All continuous variables, except percentage variables, are log-transformed to correct for skewness. For yearly variables, we assigned the same value to four quarters within each year. To account for serial correlations within an HEI, we used clustered standard errors by HEI in all DID estimates.

??????????19 is coded as 1 for the period after the second quarter of 2020 and 0 otherwise. <sup>6</sup> Thus, the main DID estimate of interest $( \alpha _ { 3 } )$ captures the extent to which the high centralized IT group outperforms the low centralized IT group during this period, compared to the pre-COVID period, in terms of student ratings. This modeling approach is similar to Lins et al. (2017), who interacted the corporate social responsibility (CSR) measure in 2006 with the crisis indicator (the fourth quarter of 2008 and the first quarter of 2009) and the post-crisis indicator (after the second quarter of 2009) to examine the value of CSR during the 2008 financial crisis.

In the DID model, the remaining sources of endogeneity stem from unobservables that are correlated with the centralized IT investment and student ratings during the pandemic. Thus, we also augmented our model specifications with fixed effects and notable time-varying control variables. First, we included institution-level fixed effects $( \mu _ { i } )$ and year-quarter fixed effects $( \theta _ { t } )$ to account for time-invariant factors (e.g., institutional types, locations) and nationwide trends in outcomes (e.g., national emergency declaration for COVID-19), respectively. Note that while our goal is to examine the extent to which centralized IT investment helped HEIs relieve the negative impact of the pandemic shock on students’ ratings, the main term, ??????????19<sub>??</sub>, is absorbed by the yearquarter fixed effects. Thus, we did not include time dummies for the second and third quarters of 2020 to explicitly estimate the after-COVID indicator. Second, given that most HEIs operate on the basis of their own academic calendars, we additionally controlled for the interactions between institution-fixed effects and four quarters $( \mu _ { i } \times Q u a r t e r _ { t } )$ to account for HEI-specific quarterly patterns.

In addition to the fixed effects, there could also be timevarying factors that relate to both centralized IT investment and student ratings. For example, heterogeneous course structures and pedagogical practices that influence student ratings might require different levels and types of IT investment. Institutional characteristics reflecting different capabilities of HEIs for ERT may be correlated with centralized IT investment and student ratings, including college sizes, selectivity, preparedness for online education prior to the pandemic shock, and institutional financial and slack resources. To account for these important time-varying factors, we controlled for an extensive array of both course characteristics and institutional characteristics. First, we included course-related characteristics measured at the institution-quarter level using RMP data, including average level of difficulty, percentages of self-reported grades on RMP reviews, and percentages of RMP reviews that reported the courses required textbook and mandated attendance. Second, we controlled for notable institutional factors, including (1) full-time equivalent enrollment for assessing institution size, (2) percentage of students enrolled exclusively online for assessing the preparedness for online education, (3) admission rate for assessing selectivity, (4) percentage of financial aid recipients as a proxy for students’ financial status, (5) value of endowment assets per student for the financial capability for extra expenditure in case of crisis, and (6) expenses per student in instruction, academic support, and student service, related to providing education services. ## Qualitative Evidence from Interviews with CIOs of HEIs

For a more contextualized and firsthand understanding of how the different domains of centralized IT impacted the online transition of HEIs in the face of COVID-19, we conducted interviews with 10 present and former CIOs or IT executives who have worked for 14 different HEIs during their careers. Each interviewee was provided with a brief description of our study in advance and asked an open question about how IT affected the transition to ERT. All interviews were conducted virtually via Zoom and each interview lasted about 30 minutes (see Tables A3 and A4 in Appendix A for sample quotes from the interviewees and the information about interviewees, respectively).

Although the CIOs worked for different types and sizes of HEIs, their views as CIOs on the roles of IT in the transition converged to a great extent. In the interest of illuminating the mechanisms, we organized the interviewees’ responses into three categories in accordance with theoretical arguments: (1) role of IT for coordination, (2) role of IT for offering instructional support applications, and (2) role of IT for providing technical support.

First, there was a general consensus on the importance of organizational coordination in the process of transitioning to ERT. The interviewees were convinced by our main results and informed us that it was highly important for the HEIs to establish institution-wide standards and monitor what was happening across campus. Most HEIs did not receive additional funding, with some even experiencing a cut in ITrelated funding immediately following the onset of the pandemic. Hence, identifying priorities, removing barriers between organizational units, and reallocating resources to required areas were of great importance. Furthermore, decisions about changing policies around technological issues such as technology standards and computer requirements needed to be made quickly, and HEIs had to restructure how different departments operated to adapt to the changing policies. Specifically, it was critical for the HEIs to restructure and reallocate both IT and human resources so that the issues raised during the transition could be addressed efficiently and effectively. In doing so, it was also important to reach agreements between the decision makers on what was needed and technologically possible. These responses align with our theoretical arguments regarding the importance of resource coordination and organizational integration in enabling ERT and indirectly affecting student satisfaction. With respect to the different domains, we were informed that the budgets allocated to the administration and management of IT (e.g., administration of the central IT organization, institutional IT planning, and IT human resources management) were closest to what was used for coordinating resources and achieving organization integration in the institution.

Next, it was also critical for the HEIs to scale up IT capacity to enable online teaching at an institution-wide scale. Cloudbased platforms such as Zoom and WebEx as well as virtual desktops played a critical role in scaling up and enabling IT services across the campus. Furthermore, the importance of centralized decision-making heightened as HEIs had to decide on the services that would fit with the existing IT structure; it was reportedly more efficient for HEIs to standardize to a single IT service across the institution. These technologies were the necessary prerequisite for online teaching and learning, and it was important that these services were provided seamlessly to enable a rapid transition to ERT. The interviewees also pointed out that not only the IT services that directly affect student learning but also service-oriented technologies such as course registrations, portals, and webpage access during the online transition may have indirectly affected general student satisfaction across all courses. Interviewees considered the budget allocated to implementing such IT services to be centralized and categorized it as enterprise IS/infrastructure or communications infrastructure as they are required by all units and departments across the institution for communication and collaboration and are not constrained solely to educational purposes.

Lastly, providing readily available support to both faculty and students was also very important in preparing everyone for a sudden switch to online courses. HEIs held workshops for faculty training and the interviewees noted that the more successful the IT support service was in supporting and training faculty in knowing how to adopt the technology, the more effective IT was in supporting the students by allowing faculty to be more available to the students. Furthermore, the importance of IT support services was more prominent for less tech-savvy faculty or those who had never offered online courses. The interviewees noted that a considerable portion of the faculty was not apt to provide online courses and required a significant amount of help in preparing for the online courses. Among the domains, budgets allocated to IT support services were identified as most closely related to providing technical support to faculty and students. In sum, the interviews with CIOs provided qualitative evidence supporting our theoretical arguments on the role of centralized IT in facilitating the organization-wide transition to ERT.

## Empirical Extensions

## Contingent Effects of IT on Organizational Size and Dissimilarity of Local Units

To offer deeper insights and practical implications on the optimal choice of IT governance in building resilience, we examined whether and how the effectiveness of IT centralization in adapting to a crisis differs according to organizational structure, which research suggests is an essential factor affecting the resilient capacity of organizations (Sabatino, 2016).

Organizations of different sizes are known to incur different levels of coordination costs and benefits from local information processing and tailoring. Larger organizations tend to have lower interdepartmental connectedness (Jaworski & Kohli, 1993) and more complex decision-making processes (Kelly & Amburgey, 1991). Previous studies have found that larger firms seem to be less agile in leveraging IT (Yousif et al., 2017) and less responsive to external demands (Roberts & Grover, 2012). The emergency management literature has also suggested that there is a trade-off between command-andcontrol requirements necessary for managing a large organization and the need to ensure broad coordination (Harrald, 2006). Although centralized governance of IT is still in play for swiftly changing business processes for adaptation to a crisis, the benefits of decentralized governance may be more evident as organizational size increases. This is primarily because larger organizations tend to be less agile and need to contemplate the benefits of centralized IT for crisis adaptation against the benefits of distributed decision-making and local information processing (Ford & Slocum, 1976; Lee & Xia, 2006). Hence, we conjecture that the benefits of IT centralization during a crisis are greater for smaller HEIs.

The (dis)similarity of local units is also one of the key dimensions that need to be considered to determine optimal IT governance (Velu et al., 2013). For HEIs, the pandemic has forced them to seek effective and efficient ways to enable the reconfiguration of different IT resources across the institution. However, it is expected to be more challenging to lead institution-wide cross-department initiatives if the demands from individual departments are more divergent and heterogeneous. This is where we expect centralized IT to play a more salient role in reconciling and coordinating divergent demands across the institution. In a similar vein, Velu et al. (2013) suggested that centralization of IT is preferable for firms with more dissimilar business units in the presence of high uncertainty. Thus, we conjecture that HEIs with more dissimilar local units (e.g., more diverse academic departments and instructional programs) can reap more benefits from IT centralization during a crisis because of the benefits it affords related to integration, coordination, and the reallocation of resources across the institution.

Table 5 reports the results for the subsample analyses by median-splitting the HEIs by the average number of fulltime equivalent students as a proxy of organizational size and diversity of instructional programs as a proxy of dissimilarity of local units.<sup>9</sup> The findings suggest that while centralized IT spending helps a broad range of HEIs irrespective of organizational size and dissimilarity in mitigating the negative impact of the pandemic shock to a certain extent, its impact is estimated to be greater in magnitude in smaller HEIs and in HEIs with more diverse programs. Considering the large difference in effect sizes between the subsamples, the results imply that the dissimilarity of local units plays a more critical role in determining the effectiveness of centralized IT in coping with the crisis.

## Role of CIO in Leveraging Centralized IT

In the extant literature, board-level IT governance mechanisms (e.g., board-level committees, the role of CIOs or IT executives) have been found to play an important role in appropriating the value of IT—for example, by enhancing IS strategic alignment (Wu et al., 2015) and reducing operational IT failures (Benaroch & Chernobai, 2017). In addition, an EDUCAUSE’s survey (Brooks, 2020) as well as our interviews with CIOs of HEIs highlight the role of the CIO and senior IT leadership in institution-level decisionmaking, such as decisions that were required for the transition to ERT and the IT support required by faculty and students during COVID-19. Therefore, we conducted an additional analysis by considering the CIO’s role in leveraging centralized IT in building resilience by strategically aligning IT with institutional goals and missions—termed the strategic role of CIO.

First, EDUCAUSE surveys in 2018 and 2019 asked HEIs about the priorities of centralized IT spending on major issues (including the priority of “repositioning or reinforcing the role of IT leadership as an integral strategic partner of institutional leadership in supporting institutional missions”). To assess the effect of centralized IT depending on the extent to which HEIs prioritize the CIO’s role in making IT-related decisions in ways that align with the institutional mission, we split HEIs into two subsamples based on the priority of the strategic role of the CIO (split by the rank 5 out of 10 IT priorities). Second, we tested how the impact of centralized IT varies depending on the CIO’s administrative position in institutional leadership; that is, whether the CIO is the highest-ranking IT officer and a member of the president’s cabinet.

In Table 6, Columns 1 and 2 show that centralized IT was more positive and statistically significant for coping with COVID-19 in HEIs with a higher priority placed on the strategic role of the CIO. Columns 3 and 4 also reveal that centralized IT had a more profound effect on maintaining student ratings during COVID-19 when CIOs were the highest-ranking IT officer and in the president’s cabinet.

## Discussion and Conclusion

## Summary of Findings

Using the COVID-19 pandemic as an exogenous shock, we assess the role of centralized governance of IT investment in building HEIs’ ability to facilitate an emergency operations mode, sustain their service operations in digital environments, and maintain student satisfaction during a crisis—what we view as digital resilience for HEIs. Our findings demonstrate that HEIs with a larger amount of centralized IT investment per student are more successful in maintaining their student ratings. From an additional analysis and the interviews with CIOs of HEIs, we also found that centralized IT investments for facilitating organizational coordination and providing instructional support applications and technical support—such as administration/management of IT, enterprise IS, communications infrastructures, and IT support services—are enablers of ERT that can help adapt HEIs to the crisis. Furthermore, we found that centralized IT confers benefits to a varying extent, according to organizational size and the dissimilarity of local units. Lastly, we present suggestive evidence that CIOs can play a pivotal role in leveraging centralized IT in building resilience when they are in a position of authority to strategically align IT with institutional goals.

## Theoretical Contributions

This study makes several scholarly contributions. First, our study contributes to the IS literature on digital resilience by synthesizing the literature on IT value and IT governance with the literature on disaster/emergency management. Whereas a few studies in the IS literature have examined the role of IT in how individuals, such as first responders (Chen et al., 2010), physicians (Liu et al., 2023), and open-source software developers (Malgonde et al., 2023), respond to major shocks, less attention has been paid to how organizations survive in the face of crises through the use and governance of digital technologies. Although some work pioneered the presentation of evidence of the U-shaped relationship between the level of uncertainty and a firm’s choice of IT centralization (Xue et al., 2011), environmental uncertainty and sales volatility under business-as-usual scenarios may not be comparable to an unexpected and unprecedented crisis that compelled all types of organizations to respond in an urgent manner. However, we still have a limited understanding of how IT investment and governance contribute to digital resilience under extreme levels of turbulence and uncertainty to which organizations need to adapt rapidly with scarce resources, such as during pandemics and natural disasters. Our study can advance the body of knowledge in this stream of literature by illuminating the role of centralized IT investment in facilitating a rapid transition to an emergency operations mode and supporting operations during a crisis, which is contradictory to conventional beliefs that decentralized IT is more favorable in the presence of high uncertainty. This study calls for extending the boundary of the business value of IT by exploring it through the lens of emergency response and digital resilience, especially under conditions of crisis.

Second, we present suggestive evidence of enabling and moderating factors in realizing the effects of centralized IT in dealing with emergencies and achieving business continuity under extreme uncertainty and scarce resources, which can offer theoretical insights and managerial implications regarding centralized IT-enabled digital resilience. Our findings support the notion that centralized IT investment, especially for organizational coordination and operations support, is a key driving force of digital resilience when local units are more dissimilar. However, the role of centralized IT has a more limited role in larger organizations. In addition, our findings imply that CIOs or IT leadership matter to digital resilience, shedding new light on the literature regarding IT/IS-business alignment that has received much attention in the IS literature.

Third, we contribute to the IS literature on the public sector (Pang et al., 2014; Pang et al., 2016; Dawson et al., 2016), in which education is one of the essential public services that need to be delivered stably, even during crises. Although HEIs had been investing in IT resources to meet their students’ and faculty’s instructional and operational needs, the pandemic has brought renewed attention to IT as a strategic asset. In line with Tremblay et al. (2023), who shed new light on the government’s digital capability to ensure health, safety, and the continuity of services, our study extends this nascent literature by highlighting the strategic role of IT in the public sector for robust service deliveries during crises.

## Practical Implications

Our findings have practical implications for organizations in building resilience with IT and IS to counter unexpected exogenous shocks and crises that are occurring more and more frequently.

First, our study shows that centralized IT governance should be considered a key precursor of digital resilience that allows organizations to adapt to rapidly changing environments and continue to deliver high-quality services. Moreover, our findings emphasize the importance of the strategic role of the CIO in leveraging centralized IT for digital resilience and disaster management. Thus, organizations might consider granting more authority to CIOs and senior IT leaders by strategically positioning them in the organization so that they can align IT activities with organizational priorities, allowing the organization to establish adaptation strategies to respond to a crisis. As central authorities are legitimized during a disaster (Hamblin, 1958), a centralized task force where CIOs can temporarily centralize IT resources across the organization during a crisis can also be a viable option for organizations that do not have centralized IT governance during normal conditions. As this is beyond the scope of this study, we recommend that future research delve deeper into the investigation of the role of board-level IT governance and an alternative form of centralized IT governance (e.g., task force) for digital resilience.

While our research context is specific to higher education, we believe that our findings are generalizable to other organizations in service sectors that face rapidly changing environments. Indeed, our analysis of the heterogeneous impacts of different domains of IT spending reveals that the driving forces leading to a higher level of student satisfaction in HEIs amidst extreme turbulence are not IT applications that are specific to the education sector, but instead are general IT management, and institution-wide IS and infrastructure for supporting and coordinating the processes of systematic changes and internal operations. These types of IT and IS are also key components of any organization in the service sector. Therefore, when any disruptive event interrupts physical operations and forces organizations to shift work from in-person to remote, we expect IT investment and governance—particularly general IT management and institution-wide IS/infrastructure—to be key drivers of performance enabling business continuity.

Second, our findings demonstrate the differential effectiveness of centralized IT when organizational size varies and there is dissimilarity in local units, calling attention to the need to balance centralized and decentralized IT investments, depending on organizational characteristics. Hence, decision makers should attentively identify where their organizations stand and make an optimal choice regarding how the investment should be governed to build digital resilience for future crises. Our findings related to the relatively small effect of centralized IT in larger HEIs might be because IT centralization may come at the expense of efficiency and effectiveness in normal times as organizational size increases (Huber et al., 1990). Thus, managers need to determine an optimal level of IT centralization and identify ways to reconcile the benefits of centralized and decentralized IT to facilitate organizationwide changes for adaptation while still being flexible and effective in managing a large organization. On the other hand, small organizations reap benefits from centralized IT governance, enabling them to be more resilient to future crises. In addition, the centralized governance of IT investment can be more beneficial to organizations with more dissimilar business units by effectively coordinating the divergent needs of local units, allowing for a rapid organization-wide transition in response to a crisis.

## Limitations and Future Research

Although our study provides important scholarly and practical implications, it also has some limitations that provide avenues for future research. First, our empirical measure of IT centralization, as proxied by IT spending controlled and governed by CIOs and central IT teams, should be considered with caution when applied to other contexts. In HEIs, the need for rapid transitions during the pandemic prioritized IT spending for new IT applications and IT support required for ERT, and the most critical decision-making in IT was about how to spend and coordinate IT expenditures across the institution. Thus, during a crisis situation, we contend that the extent to which CIOs and central IT teams appropriately govern IT expenditures generally reflects the degree of IT centralization in terms of both budget/spending and decision authority on IT applications and systems. However, the locus of IT-related budget/spending and decision authority may not necessarily be the same in other contexts, and future research may need to distinguish them in examining digital resilience.

Second, due to data availability, this study did not disentangle different domains of decentralized IT investment. For instance, while centralized IT investment in educational technology was not found to be more effective during the pandemic, this does not diminish the importance of decentralized educational technology in building resilience during crises. Future research could examine the roles of various decentralized software and services, such as specialized lab simulation software purchased by each school. Third, while our primary focus is the relative effectiveness of IT centralization vis-à-vis IT decentralization in building resilience, our findings do not speak to the federal mode of IT governance where central IT and local units assume different levels of authority for different types of IT decisions (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999). In addition, our findings are silent about disparity or inequality in resource allocation among business units or employees because of IT centralization (Boh et al., 2023). Future research could extend our research into other types of IT governance mechanisms and other aspects of outcomes, including digital divides and inequalities. Lastly, while we tried our best to rule out alternative explanations with a series of robustness checks and interviews with experienced subject matter experts, other unobserved factors might be in play during the pandemic, as we could not directly observe how each HEI invested and allocated their IT resources within the fiscal year of 2020. Despite limited access to longitudinal monthly budget information, such data would provide an additional opportunity to zoom into how IT shapes digital resilience and how its role changes over time as the crisis situation evolves.

## Concluding Remarks

This study speaks to an important message that IT is no longer an afterthought but is the key to building resilience for business continuity and sustainable growth. IT investments can build digital resilience in a way that affords organizations the capabilities to enable organization-wide transition to adapt and support service operations in the emergency mode, thereby maintaining customer satisfaction during crises. Regarding disaster/emergency management, the conventional norms established during normal conditions might not hold during a period of pandemonium. Our findings contest the traditional view by demonstrating that centralized IT can help HEIs become more resilient by enabling and supporting organizational transitions to ERT and maintaining better education services during crises. Given the potential for future crises, serious consideration should be devoted to revisiting how long-established IS viewpoints can be extended and adapted to explain new environments and unprecedented phenomena such as the one brought on by the COVID-19 pandemic. We believe that our theoretical perspective using the conceptual lens of disaster response management and empirical evidence from the global pandemic can provide guidance to IT managers and policy makers regarding how to devise IT strategies to make organizations more robust to external environments and turbulence, and can inform future research on the role of IT and IS in digital resilience and disaster/emergency management.

## Robustness Checks

Matching and the parallel trends assumption: We complemented the DID model with propensity score matching (PSM) to further reduce the imbalance between the high and low centralized IT groups. PSM matches the sample based on the similarity in their propensity scores for being treated (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983). In this study, the propensity score for being included in the high centralized IT group is predicted by all institutional characteristics in the pre-pandemic years using logistic regression. We consider not only institutional control variables, but also institution types that could influence the HEI’s centralized IT investment, including institutional control (private vs. public), Carnegie Classification (doctora and master’s vs. associate’s and baccalaureate degrees), and having a medical school that may have a large discrete IT budget. The samples from the two groups are matched based on the three-nearest-neighbor algorithm within a caliper size of 0.01. In Table B1, institutional characteristics in the pre-pandemic period are not significantly different between the two groups after matching is applied, indicating that PSM ensures a statistical balance.

Alternative identification strategy—synthetic difference-in-differences: While we leveraged the DID model in tandem with the relative time model and matching to identify the causal effect of centralized IT investment after COVID-19, we present additional causal evidence based on an alternative identification strategy using a synthetic control approach. The synthetic control method developed by Abadie and his colleagues (Abadi & Gardeazabal, 2003; Abadie et al., 2010) has been increasingly adopted for causal inference in the IS literature (e.g., Krijestorac et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021), as it offers a systematic way of constructing a pseudo-counterfactual that resembles the treatment group (so-called synthetic control) with the minimal involvement of researchers. Recently, Arkhangelsky et al. (2021) combined the synthetic control method with a DID model—namely, the synthetic DID, which accounts for not only weights of covariates and control units but also weights of time periods in constructing the synthetic control. Using the synthetic DID method developed by Arkhangelsky et al. (2021), we construct the synthetic control using pre-COVID student ratings for the High centralized IT group. For comparison, we repeat the analysis using the High decentralized IT group.

Figure B1 shows the trends of the high (de)centralized IT group and the synthetic control group in student ratings. The left figure demonstrates that student ratings of the high centralized IT group and its synthetic control follow the parallel trends in the pre-pandemic period, but they took divergent paths after COVID-19. In contrast, this divergence is not observed between the high decentralized IT group and its synthetic control in the right figure. These findings are consistent with the evidence from the main DID estimations, supporting the causal effect of centralized IT on maintaining student ratings during the crisis.

Ruling out alternative explanations—curriculum changes: In response to the pandemic shock, HEIs may have changed and updated their curriculums, possibly due to the changes in course demand and staffing. If the curriculum changes are correlated with the level of IT spending per student (or its confounders) and student satisfaction, it could lead to a spurious positive correlation between IT and student ratings during the COVID-19 pandemic. Thus, we attempt to rule out this alternative explanation by accounting for the number of unique course codes and professors reviewed on RMP per quarter in each HEI.<sup>11</sup>

In Columns 1 and 2, Table B4, we additionally interact the number of unique course codes and professors reviewed per student with the after-COVID indicator to account for the HEI’s capabilities to cope with the pandemic shock by taking advantage of sufficient resources. The results show that larger numbers of course offerings and professors per student are positively associated with student ratings during the crisis, implying that HEIs that are affordable to offer a variety of courses and maintain instructors and lecturers amid the pandemic shock dealt with COVID-19 in terms of student satisfaction with classes. More importantly, the positive contribution of centralized IT investment to maintaining student ratings in the after-COVID period remains significant even after controlling for the interactions between the after-COVID indicator and the number of courses and professors per student, highlighting that our main findings are not likely to be driven by curriculum changes due to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Ruling out alternative explanations—Financial status and slack resources: Institutional financial status and slack resources may vary by HEI. In general, pre-crisis slack resources are useful for organizations to adapt to turbulent environments and crises, such as an economic downturn and the COVID-19 pandemic (Jiang et al., 2019). A case in point is the university’s endowment, which is one of the main slack resources for HEIs. HEIs could potentially receive special appropriations or emergency draws from the endowment in emergency cases.<sup>12</sup> In addition, the value of endowment assets may be indicative of HEIs’ capability to solicit special donations during the COVID-19 pandemic. To rule out this important alternative explanation based on financial status and slack resources, we additionally accounted for the interactions between the after-COVID indicator and institutional indicators for financial status and slack resources, including the value of endowment assets per student and expenses in instruction, academic support, and student services, which pertain to student experiences. In Columns 3 to 6 of Table B4, while the endowment assets and institutional expenses for instruction, academic support, and student services—indicative of financial and slack resources of HEIs—were found to significantly contribute to improving student ratings during COVID-19 as anticipated, the coefficient of centralized IT investment in the after-COVID period remains positive and significant, even after controlling for their effects. Taken together, the analyses from Table B4 illuminate the unique role of centralized IT spending distinct from the HEI’s financial status and slack resources in building resilience.

Immediate Adjustment of IT Governance: While the role of centralized IT investment remains significant after accounting for the HEI’s slack resources as discussed above, one might be concerned with the HEI’s time-varying investments within the first half of 2020 and immediate adjustment of IT budgets and governance in response to the pandemic, which is not captured in our measure of IT investment. To the best of our knowledge, there was no data to capture HEIs’ monthly investments within a fiscal year or internal adjustments at a granular level. While it is challenging to directly observe where and how HEIs invested and adjusted their IT budgets and resources during the crisis, the interviews with former or current CIOs of HEIs (see Table A6) inform us that the alternative explanation based on time-varying investments and intermediate budget adjustments in response to COVID-19 are unlikely to alter our results because of the budgeting process in the institution. Specifically, IT budgets for a CIO (centralized IT spending in our definition) are determined and approved in the prior academic year, and budget adjustments would not be procedurally feasible in March when nine months of the year had already transpired, as the vast majority of U.S. universities operate on academic years that run from July to June. From a practical standpoint, one of the interviewees also noted that much of the budget is typically spent by that time of year.

In contrast, while IT budgets remain largely stable in the short run, there was a large change in IT staffing in response to the pandemic (e.g., role/authority change, redeployment, or layoff). This fact favors our approach to defining the degree of IT centralization based on IT expenditures spent by the CIO and the central IT team, rather than IT staff in the central IT team or their authorities in IT services. It is noteworthy that the interviewees noted that the restructuring and reallocation of IT resources required to facilitate the HEI’s transition to ERT had been done within the limit of affordable resources to the CIO and central IT team, as it was difficult to solicit extra resources and budgets outside in the face of the pandemic shock. Thus, the transient centralization of IT, if any, should be a function of the prior degree of IT centralization, as it is reasonable to assume that the extent to which HEIs can reap benefits from transient centralization of IT in response to COVID-19 would be proportional to their prior experiences and proficiencies in centralized IT governance before its outbreak.
