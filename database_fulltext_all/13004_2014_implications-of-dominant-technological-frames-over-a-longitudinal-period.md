# Implications of dominant technological frames over a longitudinal periodi

Karin Olesen

Department of Accounting, Business School, AUT University, Private Bag 92006, Auckland, New Zealand, email: karin.olesen@aut.ac.nz

Abstract. Successful adoption and use of information systems is an area of continued research in the field of information systems. This prior research has shown that how we adopt and use an information system depends on how we make sense of it. The sense-making activity is carried out through our cognitive structures of knowledge that relate to technology (our technological frames). The sense-making activity changes over time, as use and exposure to differing technologies occur. Most research into information systems in organisations has focused on a specific information system; this preoccupation with studying discrete projects at one point in time may be limiting. In an attempt to fill this research gap, we use the socio-cognitive perspective of Orlikowski & Gash to analyse technological frames in one organisation over a longitudinal period to evaluate sensemaking in relation to multiple systems. The interpretive case study looks at the technological frames of senior management, faculty teaching staff, information technology (IT) mediating staff and IT groups over a 10-year period in a university and finds that there were incongruent frames between senior management and other groups within the organisation with senior management holding a dominant frame. The consequences of these frames were demonstrated when they were linked to the use of the four major information systems in the organisation, showing repeated historical patterns of use that caused inefficiencies due to the incongruent frames of the various groups. The unchanging dominant technological frame contributed to this pattern.

Keywords: technological frames, longitudinal research, social construction, information systems use

## INTRODUCTION

To adopt and use technology, people first draw on their cognitive structures to make sense of the technology (Scott & Barrett, 2005). The cognitive structures or frames around technology use are the technological frames that are the assumptions, knowledge and expectations an individual holds in regard to technology, or put simply, how individuals construct meaning in relation to information systems (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). The concept of technological frames has been used in prior studies to examine the adoption of a specific information system within a specific organisation over a discrete period of time (Acha, 2004; Bjørn et al., 2006; Puri, 2006; Davis & Hufnagel, 2007; Hsu, 2007; Jensen & Aanested, 2007 Karsten & Laine, 2007; Wang et al., 2007; Sandford & Bhattacherjee, 2008; Sobreperez, 2008; Chang, 2008; Mengesha, 2008; Mishra & Agarwal, 2010. However, the concentration of such studies on a discrete period of time has led to the criticism of technological frames research as being temporally bound (Gal & Berente, 2008) when indeed technological frames may change over time (the exception to this is the work of Davidson, (1996; 1997; 2002)). The study described in this paper has been designed in such a way as to address this criticism by applying the lens of technological frames within a longitudinal approach in which frames are examined at different points in time. Although longitudinal studies have been called for in technological frames research (Lin & Cornford, 2000; Lin & Silva, 2005; Karsten & Laine, 2007; Mishra & Agarwal, 2010, the risk and time investment involved in conducting them is given as reason for the lack of them (Davidson & Pai, 2004; Davidson, 2006). Therefore, there is value in examining the use of all information systems in an organisation over an extended period of time, something that this study does (Williams & Pollock, 2009). Further, this study examines multiple systems rather than a single system to enable a more holistic view of information systems adoption and use.

The empirical case study was carried out within a faculty of a university (originally a polytechnic) with data collected ethnographically over a 10-year period. The study focuses on the interpretive process and adopts technological frames analysis as its theoretical lens. The objective is to identify the perceptions, assumptions and knowledge (i.e. technological frames) of different social groups in the organisation and examine the organisational consequences that resulted from frame incongruence over a 10-year period. The data have been analysed to construct the technological frames of the main social groups involved and to link these to practices around use of the main information systems within the organisation. The findings of this investigation have both theoretical and practical relevance. From a theoretical perspective the findings contribute to our understanding of technological frames and their effects on information systems adoption and use in an organisation over an extended period of time and the role that frames can play in repeated patterns of use of technology within an organisation. The practical significance of this research is to show that senior management within an organisation can have a strong impact on the use of technology through the dominance of their frames within an organisational setting. The incongruence between the frames of management and those of other social groups had a strong impact on technology use over the entire 10-yea period. From a practical perspective, because such dominant frames and their institutionalised use can be very difficult to change, strategies to intervene and align frames within an organi sational setting may not be effective.

Specifically, the research questions that this study aims to address are:

1 What are the technological frames of the main social groups in the organisation? and 2 How do these technological frames influence the patterns of use of major information technologies (ITs) in the organisation?

The next section discusses the theoretical background to technological frames, including the history of the concept of frames, frame congruence and dominant frames. This is followed by a description of the data collection and analysis. A background to the case study organisation is then given. The findings and discussion presents the frame changes that occurred within the case study organisation over the 10-year period, together with contextual information to make sense of each social group’s frames and any frame incongruencies that occurred. These frames are then linked to technology use within the organisation. Finally, the conclusions are presented, including the contribution and limitations of the study.

## TECHNOLOGICAL FRAMES

This section reviews the concept of technological frames from their inception in the social psychology discipline to their introduction into the information systems field by Orlikowski & Gash (1994), who called for socio-cognitive research in the field in order to understand how users make sense of information systems. Drawing on prior studies, the domains of the technological frame are explained, as are frame congruence and dominant frames.

The field of socio-cognitive research began much earlier in social psychology, founded on the information processing by individuals. To achieve information processing, individuals require schemas, or cognitive structures. Such schemas have been referred to as frames (Davis, 1975), scripts (Gioia, 1986; Gioia et al., 1989), mental models (Stata, 1989; Senge, 1990; Schein, 1992) or interpretive schemes (Giddens, 1984) by various researchers. A schema (or a frame or script) is defined as ‘a mental representation of sequences of events that guides our own behaviour and our interpretation of others behaviours in similar situations (Gioia et al., 1989, p. 507). Schemas provide us with cognitive structures that enable us to make sense of our world (Bandura, 1986; Walsh, 1995). Schemas enable us to reduce ambiguity when a problem arises that is similar to one that we have already experienced by giving us a template for problem solving. Unfortunately, schemas also constrain our information processing when new or inconsistent information presents itself. If these frames become institutionalised and embedded in the organisational processes, they may become resistant to change, a process that is called cognitive inertia (Orlikowski, 1993; 2000). The inability to change the institutionalised status quo is used by Orlikowski & Gash (1994) to discuss how frames can enable action or constrain it.

Within social psychology research, schemas are examined at the individual level. Organisational researchers such as Weick (1995) and others have examined schemas at the group level. For example, Gioia (1986), Gioia et al., 1989), Fiol (1994) and others have looked at group-level decision-making. People share frames or cognitive structures with those whom they work with, through shared experiences within their organisation or socialisation via industry and professional training.

## Definition of a frame

The concept of technological frames was introduced to the information systems field by Orlikowski & Gash (1994), who define technological frames as ‘that subset of members organizational frames that concern the assumptions, expectations, and knowledge they use to understand technology in organizations’ (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994, p. 178). These authors view technological frames as comprising three domains: technology-in-use, which encompasses how individuals within an organisation currently use a particular technology; technology strategy, which relates to individuals’ beliefs about future uses of technology; and the nature of technology, which refers to how individuals construct meaning in relation to technology.

The empirical study on which the current paper is based was initially examined in terms of Orlikowski & Gash’s (1994) three technological frame domains utilising content analysis to determine the issues that various groups had in relation to technology. The initial analysis done using this provided insights into how technological frames are formed and how subsequent use of IT can be affected by initial perceptions. However, although this framework was found to be adequate in that all of the empirical data fitted into the frames domains defined by Orlikowski & Gash (1994), a small variation was adopted in the subsequent data analysis based on the fact that the technology-in-use and technology-strategy frames define the third frame – the nature of technology frame. Therefore, this research uses the technology-in-use and technology-strategy frames to inform the nature of technology frame. Removing the nature of technology frame from the initial categories of the data analysis, as was done in this research, is not seen as a departure from Orlikowski & Gash (1994), who suggest that these technological frames overlap. In reviewing and recoding the empirical data in the subsequent analysis of data from the case study, the nature of technology domain was found to be not relevant. This is probably because the social groups involved were acting in relation to all of the information systems in the organisation, not just one new system. Therefore, we can have confidence in using the domains technology-strategy and technology-in-use frames and removing the nature of technology from the analysis and presentation of the findings of this case study.

Since the original article by Orlikowski & Gash (1994) (based on earlier working papers in 1993 and 1992), the concept of technological frames has been used in a range of studies to examine frames, actions and outcomes in the development, adoption and use of IT systems. These studies are presented in Table 1, which distinguishes between studies identified in Davidson & Pai’s (2004) review of the field and those conducted more recently. Many of the studies listed in Table 1 perform frame analysis using Orlikowski & Gash’s (1994) original frame domains as a framework. In particular, most of the studies conducted since 2004 have done so and found that these are adequate to fit all of their empirical data. It should be noted, however, that these studies are characterised by a snapshot in time, based on one system only, and generally focus on adoption rather than long-term use.

## Dominant technological frames

A dominant frame is one that is able to dominate the frames of other social groups, swaying those groups into accepting (or at least not resisting) the dominant viewpoint. Dominant technological frames have been observed in a number of studies (Davidson, 1996; 1997; 2002; 2006: Barrett. 1999: Lin & Cornford. 2000: McLoughlin et al., 2000: McGovern & Hicks. 2004: Lin & Silva, 2005). In contrast, Bjørn et al. (2006) found no evidence of a dominant frame, although their study examined the implementation of groupware in a university course for adults – a quite different setting to workplaces where dominant frames can result as part of the organisational politics that occur within the hierarchical employment relationship in most organisations (Rose & Kræmmergaard, 2002).

The notion of dominant frames is important to the current study, particularly in terms of the organisational group (or individual) whose frame is dominant. In Davidson (1996; 1997; 2002; 2006) and McGovern & Hicks (2004), the dominant frame holder was a senior manager. In both studies, the senior manager’s frame was not influenced by those of others, but was able to change the frames of another group. Interestingly, the occurrence or dominating effect of a dominant frame can be situation specific. In Davidson (2002), the executive manager held the dominant frame but only for as long as he was the executive manager. When he left the organisation, the effect of his dominant frame on the frames of the IT group ceased and their frames moved back towards what they had been prior to his appointment as executive manager. The IT group’s frame was influenced heavily by a prior IT implementation disaster.

In other studies, different groups held the dominant frame. In the study by Barrett (1999), the dominant frames were held by the users of a new electronic data interchange system for the London Insurance Market, who refused to use the new system so that implementation efforts failed. Senior managers tried to influence the use of the system because they considered that transactions were standard and of low risk so that using the system should be feasible in most cases. However, the users could not be influenced. Lin & Cornford (2000) and Lin & Silva (2005) discuss a study in which the IT group held the dominant frame. When the email package chosen by the user group was different from the one preferred by the IT group, The IT group staff used their expert knowledge to dominate the frames of users and management, who subsequently accepted the email package preferred by the IT group. McLoughlin et al. (2000) examined three research sites in which they tried to introduce team-based cellular manufacturing to replace assembly line manufacturing techniques. Dif ferent individuals held the dominant frame at different points of time, depending on who exerted most influence at a given point in time. Progression of team-based cellular manufacturing by the researchers stalled at all sites

In each of these studies, the dominant technological frames held by a specific group in an organisation were mobilised so as to exert influence over other groups in the organisation and influence their actions and resulting outcomes in ways that the dominant group wanted. However, some of the observed outcomes and consequences were unintended by the domi nant frame holders. Although technological frames have been used in these studies to examine frames, actions and outcomes in the development, adoption and use of IT systems, dominant frames are examined at one point in time. This current study is interested in examining how much variation frames exhibit over time, particularly given the important implications for technology adoption and use that arise from who holds the dominant frame and for how long.

Other criticisms of technological frame studies relate to their being technologically centred, lacking contextual information and failing to monitor changes over extensive time periods. Ir the study presented in this paper, these criticisms are addressed through the research design – the study is a longitudinal (spanning a 10-year period), and presents contextual information relevant to the ‘stories’ of each of the social groups involved (Gal & Berente, 2008). A further criticism of frame studies relates to data gathered via interviews that do not capture frame change through the interaction of participating individuals or groups (Gal & Berente, 2008). Given that many frame studies use multiple data collection techniques, this is not a valid criticism. In the current study, interviews are just one of a range of data collection techniques utilised. To complement the interviews, the ethnographic element allows for observation of technology use in situ, enabling interpersonal interactions to be captured. As suggested previously, the study presented in this paper attempts to address the issue of being temporally bound by examining the changes in technological frames that occur over an extended period of time in relation to multiple systems and for multiple social groups. The study concentrates on long-term use of an information system rather than implementation of a discrete project as prior research does. The effects of frame incongruence on the use of technology are examined. We suggest that where a dominant frame exists in an organisation, congruence may not necessarily be achieved by negotiation and surfacing of frames, due to cognitive inertia (Ovaska et al., 2005; Bjørn et al., 2006).

## DATA COLLECTION AND ANALYSIS

The study was undertaken between 1993 and 2003 in a relatively large faculty (with 350 staff members) of an Australasian university (with approximately 1000 staff members). The organisation was considered suitable in that it was committed to research and gaining knowledge; it also considered itself to be a learning organisation as defined by Senge (1990) and to be a leading edge in its acquisition and use of technology. At the time of the study, all staff had access to a computer, a Microsoft Office desktop, an organisational-wide intranet and a shared directory structure for various joint developments. The email tool had group messages (in the later years from 2001) and could send and receive attachments. There were various specialised organisational information systems such as for payroll, purchasing, human resources and billing. An organisation-wide IT group was responsible for the delivery of IT service delivery within the organisation.

Given the research objectives and approach, a knowledge-intensive organisation was important so its use of technology as such would be of interest. The organisation’s willingness to accept the researcher on site for an extended period of time was a favourable factor as were the ability to access confidential data and discuss it with senior management of the faculty.

This research uses an interpretive case study methodology following the principles of Klein & Myers (1999). Data were collected 2 days a week over a 10-year period through participant observation, formal and informal interviews, from strategic and normal memorandum, intranet, advertising material, documents published and internal newsletters, as well as the researcher’s knowledge of the organisation from archival data and informal social contact (see Appendix A). Data were collected via different means and from different sources to enable the researcher to develop a full account of any incident. Semi-structured interviews were used to clarify any data that were unclear.

The number of unstructured interviews and informal discussions conducted over the course of the 10-year study was approximately 50 per year (consisting mainly of informal discussions), (see Appendix A, second table). The first sets of interviews were exploratory and related to how the individuals viewed IT within the organisation and the relationships between them and other groups in relation to IT. All senior managers in the faculty (eight) were interviewed every second year, while selected individuals from the other social groups identified in the study (faculty teaching staff, IT mediating staff and staff in the organisation-wide IT group) were also interviewed periodically. (The four social groupings are discussed in the next section.) Each interview lasted 30–60 minutes, and while interviews were not audio-recorded, extensive notes were made. As noted earlier, interviews were not the most important source of data, as they do not capture frame change through the interaction of participating individuals or groups (Ga & Berente, 2008).

Data from various documentary sources (such as reports, minutes of meeting, etc.) were categorised into different social groups (senior management, faculty teaching staff, IT medi ating staff and IT group) and by technological frames (technology in use and technology strategy) across the 10-year period.

## Social groupings

Wilson & Howcroft (2005) apply a notion of relevant social groups to identify the social groups in an organisation who share the same set of meanings attached to an artefact. The concept of relevant social groups comes from the social construction of technology work of Pinch & Bijker (1987). In the current study, four social groups were identified by the researcher: senior management, faculty teaching staff, IT groups and IT mediating staff. These groups were used as categories for subsequent analysis of the empirical data.

The choice of the social groups in this study was done after a period of observation to ensure that examination was possible within each of the social groups, as each group had a different culture and we were interested in patterns of use within each culture (Bjørn & Ngwenyama, 2007). Initially, three social groups were identified: the faculty’s senior management group, staff within the faculty, and the organisation-wide IT group. However, it soon became obvious that a fourth group existed: the IT mediating staff group. Originally categorised as faculty teaching staff, this group was different from other staff in the faculty in that they were very computer literate and able to influence senior management to a degree. Because the empirical data that had been collected were stored electronically in folders in a flexible way (by year, date, social group, person name and topic), separating out individuals into these new social groups was a relatively simple matter of reorganising folders.

The empirical data were then analysed in order to identify the technological frames of each of the social aroups that existed over the 10-year study period. These technological frames showed how the social groups in the organisation constructed meaning in relation to technology. In determining the technological frames of each of the social groups, the data for each social group were examined for themes suggested by the data and then split into the technological frames of Orlikowski & Gash (1994) – technology in use and technology strategy. Categorising the data by year was convenient in the initial analysis. Because each item of data was identified by its date of occurrence, an alternative way of ordering the data by significant periods or episodes was subsequently undertaken to reveal patterns in the data.

Interactions between the technological frames across the 10 years were then analysed, together with changes in frame congruence between social groups. For illustrative purposes, sample data relating to the technological frames of the faculty teaching staff group in 1995 is presented in Table 2. The technology-in-use frame illustrates some of the problems faculty teaching staff experienced in getting adequate hardware, maintenance of hardware, software and networks, systems efficiency and lack of training, while the technology-strategy frame discusses ways of fixing these problems. We can see that in 1995, the faculty teaching staff group technology-in-use frame is congruent with their technology-strategy frame.

## Use of stories

One of the criticisms of frames research was its lack of contextual focus (Gal & Berente, 2008) and failure to take into account the broader social and organisational processes. In order to address this criticism, in the current study the contextual and institutional factors that affected the technological frames across the longitudinal study were considered throughout the data analysis. The context in which each social group was situated was used to construct a story of that group’s experiences in using the information systems within the institutional context. From these stories, it was possible to see how the technological frames guided the interpretations and actions of the social group to which they belonged. This approach is consistent with the social cognition literature (Bandura, 1986).

## Practices of IT use

Because IT use is influenced by technological frames, the practices of use of IT within the organisation were examined in relation to the identified technological frames. The empirica data were also analysed in terms of the various information systems within the organisation in order to establish a chronology of information systems use across the 10-year time frame of the study.

From initial analysis, there were approximately 20 information systems in use. However, because of similarities, many of these systems were combined together into functional groups such as email/groupware/communication systems. As systems evolved, the initial systems in this study were superseded by newer systems that also incorporated the functions of other systems. Studying systems as a group enabled such patterns of evolution to be analysed. In this way, the analysis of technological frames, which includes the context in which the cultura patterns of use of information systems occurred, were established.

## ORGANISATION BACKGROUND

This section considers the stories of each of the four social groups identified as part of the data analysis over the 10-year period and their different stories on how they saw the world. These stories help to explain the reasons behind any shifts in groups’ technological frames. A summary of the stories is described in the following for each of the social groups – senior management, faculty teaching staff, IT mediating staff and the IT group. Such stories enable a large amount of data to be summarised and presented more easily in order to give a contextual focus for each group over a study period.

The senior management group have a very high level official story of the organisation. The organisation was granted the right to offer degrees at the start of the 10-year period, and the changes in organisational structure that were needed to enable this occurred. Large amounts of program development and research capability development occurred over the 10-year period. This included one-third of faculty academic staff undertaking higher education to obtain advanced qualifications. Rapid growth in the organisation was present throughout the 10 years, which helped to explain the low priority placed on IT resources.

The faculty teaching staff group had a much lower level story of the organisation. They used IT equipment in their work. Initially, a lot of the IT was not replaced and old machines using a disk operating system called DOS software were common in the organisation. Because the IT infrastructure (i.e. the networks, hardware and software) was unreliable, lower level faculty teaching staff invested considerable time trying to get their jobs done, which left them frustrated. Many faculty teaching staff utilised manual procedures and localised information databases so that they could have information on hand when they needed it. As machines slowly started to be replaced during the first 5 years, faculty teaching staff did not have the time available to undertake training on the new technology. They were busy dealing with the rapid expansion in student numbers, trying to gain advanced qualifications and producing research outputs to fulfil accreditation requirements of the university. This pressure continued through out the remainder of the study period and explained the low level of faculty teaching staff training on IT and the slow improvement in their ability to use their systems.

The IT mediating staff group comprised staff who were very IT literate and spoke to senior management regarding the types of technology that should be present in the organisation. They also liaised with the IT group regarding the types and levels of service that they should be providing. The story of the IT mediating group remained the same across the years, although each year they focused on the development, maintenance and support of a different IT system. For example, on 1 year they focused on the email system, and on the next year on the enrolment system.

The organisation-wide IT group began the 10-year period with many student and staff clients but without a fully formed view on customer service. Years of dissatisfaction with the IT systems ensued, given the IT group’s problems with maintaining the IT resources. Six years into the study, the IT group moved to a cost accounting model for IT service delivery to the various faculties and groups across the organisation. Instead of paying a lump sum service charge to the IT group, charges to faculties or groups were now based on the various IT services they used (e.g. a standard amount was charged for a standardised PC with software and network configuration). The prior indirect charging method had meant that faculties contributing the most to the IT budget might have had little of that budget spent on their faculty. The new cost accounting model had unanticipated effects on the experimentation and use of various technologies by staff. It curtailed these experimental efforts due to the fact the IT staff were unable to spend time on projects unless there were specific funds allocated towards them.

The technological frames of each of the social groups (senior management, faculty teaching staff, IT mediating staff and IT group) were analysed over the 10-year period. The evolution of information systems over this period was analysed and then compared with these frames in order to establish frame congruency within the social groups themselves and between them.

## Changes in technological frames

The frames for most of the main social groups changed in the early 2000s as a result of actions that had occurred previously.

## Senior Management

Senior management’s technological frames were under pressure to change for the entire period of the study as a result of a number of events that occurred within the organisation, one of which (the Lotus Notes trial) is discussed in the following. Across the 10-year period, there were episodes in which each of the social groups tried to influence the technological frame of the senior management. The frames of staff and IT mediating staff groups appear to have had little weight in being able to influence a change of the frames of the senior management group. Instead, the frames of the senior management group appear to have dominated over the other social groups within the faculty, in much the same way as was observed in prior studies of dominant frames (Davidson, 1996; Barrett, 1999; Lin & Cornford, 2000; McLoughlin et al., 2000; McGovern & Hicks, 2004; Lin & Silva, 2005).

An example of a social group trying to influence the frames of senior management was the Lotus Notes study in which faculty senior management and their personal assistants used Lotus Notes™ version 4.5 connecting to a Lotus Domino™ 4.5 server (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA). The senior management and their personal assistants formed a working group that was required to communicate, coordinate and collaborate with each other and others within the organisation to perform the administrative functions of the organisation. A study of this project is presented in Olesen (1998). The personal assistants used the diary functionality of Lotus Notes to enforce their ability to control their senior manager’s diary, which they did by removing the options of viewing each other’s calendars. In the process, the staff (personal assistants) endeavoured to impose their personal productivity frame – an individual productivity frame – on their senior managers. The individual productivity frame relates to individuals such as senior managers working alone, as opposed to a group work productivity frame in which senior managers work together by communicating, coordinating and collabo rating. However, trying to change the frames of senior manager in this way proved to be somewhat difficult. In this phase, the capabilities of the product were discussed with senior management. When the trial ended, however, the senior managers decided not to fund a continued use of the product or roll it out to others in the organisation. It appears that they did not see any significant benefits from the product, even though their staff (personal assistants) had become used to the product and wanted to continue using it. Had they believed that the product would have assisted them, perhaps senior management would have funded it or required the IT group to support it. Overall, the senior management felt that the groupware product was primarily a tool to help their staff (personal assistants) and not themselves. Thus, the dominant technological frame of senior management did not change.

The senior management’s frame shifted in 2002, as a result of a core management systems upgrade proposed by the university. In the process, the technological frames of the wider university management appeared to dominate the frames of senior management of the faculty.

## IT mediating staff group

The technological frame of the IT mediating group stayed the same across the 10-year period. As discussed in their story previously, they focused on a different system each year and tried to influence the senior management and the IT groups to get problems with the different systems fixed.

## IT group

As shown in Figure 1, the first social group to change frames was the IT group in 1999. This involved a complete reframing of the way in which IT services were to be provided and accounted for, and had lasting implications on the IT group’s frame. The way that the IT group serviced departments completely changed once the costing charging model for IT services began, with new processes and accountabilities being implemented.

## Faculty teaching staff group

The next frame shift that occurred was for the faculty teaching staff group in 2001, involving a gradual change in the group’s technological frame as their technology-in-use frame gradually improved over time. The gradual improvement that led to the frame change in 2001 is shown by looking at faculty teaching staff members’ perceptions relating to technology-in-use, particularly in relation to having adequate hardware or software to do their jobs. In 1995, there were many comments relating to a perception of inadequate hardware or software. The content of the frame remained the same up until 1999, although the number of comments observed gradually reduced. By 2001, this frame did not appear. These perceptions probably changed as a result of the IT group adopting a charging model and IT resources improving. The faculty teaching staff group was the only group that showed this gradual change in their frame structure. ## Incongruence within a social group

Frames within a social group may be incongruent. In the case study, the senior management group, e.g. exhibited incongruent frames. In the earlier years of the study, particularly from 1993 to 1995, senior management’s technology-strategy frame consisted of strategies that originated from the management of the university, and comprised strategies such as providing full-motion video through computers, multimedia and video conferencing. However, the actual technology-in-use frame for senior management and shared across all groups during these years shows that the IT network was at the point of collapse in 1995, to the extent that it would have been unable to cope with the bandwidth required by these technologies. Moreover, most machines in the organisation were not video capable. Thus, within itself, senior management’s technological frame was incongruent. Other social groups did not have incongruent frames.

## Incongruence between social groups

Senior management’s technological frame was also incongruent with the frames of other social groups, in particular the faculty teaching staff group. The faculty teaching staff group used the hardware, software and network in order to perform their jobs.

In 1995, the faculty teaching staff group’s technology-in-use frame was congruent with their technology-strategy frame (see Table 2). However, their technology-strategy frame, which focused on problems with the current systems and processes and the improvements or new systems needed to fix these problems, was incongruent with the senior management group’s technology-strategy frame, which was concerned with the use of new technologies such as multimedia and virtual reality. The faculty teaching staff group did not come up with new technologies as a strategy as senior management had, but looked instead at technology that could help them deal with their current issues, such as performing their jobs in enrolling students.

From 1993 to 2003, the IT mediating staff group’s technology-strategy frame was also incongruent with that of the senior management group. IT mediating staff had extensive discussions with senior management about the need for improved technology within the faculty, arguing that technology upgrades and improvements were required to enable faculty teaching staff to work efficiently. However, the large funds needed for such a technology upgrade were not allocated towards solving this problem.

Overall, key individuals and groups within the faculty were able to influence other groups through their actions and interactions. The main relationship between the social groups was hierarchical, with the senior management having some control over the IT mediating staff and the faculty teaching staff groups. Senior management had limited influence over the IT group, which was controlled by the university management rather faculty management. However, management of the faculty could complain to the management of the university regarding the level of service provided by the IT group.

## Implications of dominant and incongruence frames

The dominant technological frame of senior managers was a contributing factor to the incongruent frames between senior management and other groups. This was despite frequent periods of change and communication. Senior management’s technological frame dominated the frames of the other social groups, despite attempts by these groups to communicate their points of view and change senior management’s technological frame. The IT mediating group, in particular, tried to communicate with senior management as their frames held some weight with senior management. Challenges to senior management’s technological frame were also observed at different times. For example, the implementation of Lotus Notes, a new technology for the organisation, provided an opportunity for the senior management group to change their frame. While the senior management group participated in discussions concerning the assumptions, knowledge and perceptions related to Lotus Notes, they did not reframe or change their technological frames as a result of its implementation.

## Linking frames to technology use

One of the most unexpected findings of this study was that the dominant technological frame also caused repeated patterns of use of technology. This was evidenced by the various technology systems that were implemented over the 10-year period. These information systems were grouped into four different IT system categories, each of which had a different start date: network/hardware and software, email systems, internet/intranet and online systems. The network/hardware and software and email systems were in use at the start of the study, the internet/intranet was first used in 1995, and online systems were first used in 1997.

Figure 2 plots the progression observed in the use of each of these systems across the 10-year study period. A similar pattern of use was observed for each system showed, which usually started off as technically unreliable before moving into a more technically robust phase. Thus, when a system was first implemented, it suffered a long period of technical unreliability and a lack of skills among users (highlighted in light grey). Over time, a higher skill level and technical robustness emerged for each system (shown without any shading), with the excep tion of the online course system.

The interesting thing is the online systems had an initial period of instability but a technically robust period does not appear before the study finished. It is extremely likely the online system then entered a period of more technically robust use in 2005. This would mirror the evolution of the other systems in the organisation. This was verified after the study was completed.

Because the faculty teaching staff group were given technically unreliable systems to start with and they had poor technical skills, they continued to use these systems as best they could. They were unable to change the systems and the patterns of technological use shown in Figure 2 constrained the development of future information systems in the organisation. In this way, these patterns of development and the ways in which faculty teaching staff dealt with them continued. The other groups (faculty teaching staff, IT mediating staff) could not chal lenge the senior management’s technological frame – so they did the best they could within their existing systems.

This finding highlights the value of the longitudinal nature of the study. The perspective of a 10-year period enabled a different view to be taken of the organisation. This helps explain why the actual use of IT in an organisation frequently does not live up to the claims that are promised. We can see how senior management largely ignored the problems with IT. This may have been due to the efforts being expended with growth of the organisation and requirements of the changing environment, resulting in IT being given a lower priority. The lack of accountability of the IT group also contributed to this situation. Changes to practices could contribute to an organisation’s efficiency and effectiveness. However, the dominant social group of senior management prevailed, as well as the repeated patterns of technological use by the faculty teaching staff group in using systems so these changes to practices may not have been effective.

Orlikowski & Gash (1994) focused on congruency of frames: if frames are incongruent, difficulties can be experienced in implementing and using IT. In particular, the author describes dominant frames and cognitive inertia, both of which played a larger role in this research than originally anticipated. As the findings show, the technological frames of the main social groups were incongruent, and the senior management group’s frame was dominant. Cognitive inertia was observed in the practices of IT use, when the frames of senior management were challenged but remained unchanged. In the current study, frame incongruity and cognitive inertia were observed over a long period of time, something that prior studies that have found dominant frames have not shown. The incongruity of frames between the main social groups continued across time, and practices of use of information systems repeated themselves across multiple systems across time. This study provides an extended view from that of Orlikowski & Gash who examined a single project over a brief 5-month period. Looking at these patterns across time is a contribution of this research.

## CONCLUSIONS

This study contributes to the literature by identifying the perceptions, assumptions and knowl edge about technology (i.e. the technological frames) of different social groups in an organisation, and by examining the organisational consequences resulting from frame incongruence over a 10-year period. The data have been analysed to construct the technological frames of the main social groups and these have been linked to the practices of use of the information systems in order to answer the research questions posed at the beginning of the paper.

The first research question was addressed by examining the technological frames of senior management, faculty teaching staff, IT mediating staff and IT groups over a 10-year period in a single organisation. These frames included elements of a technology-in-use frame and a technology-strategy frame (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Davidson, 1996). This then enabled us to answer the second research question by examining how a particular social group constructed meaning from their frame over time, which was reflected in their use of an information system.

Theoretical contributions of the findings of dominant technological frames reinforce earlier studies in the area such as those by Davidson (1996; 2000), Barrett (1999), Lin & Cornford (2000), Lin & Silva (2005) and McGovern & Hicks (2004). However, this study goes one step further in that it looks at the dominant technological frame over a much longer period of time (10 years) as applied to the use of multiple systems. By taking an extended view, we have demonstrated how these dominant technological frames can cause repeated practices of use of IT within an organisation. Earlier studies have been unable to look at this effect on use over such a long period. This situation continued in the organisation over the 10-year period because the frames incorporate deeply embedded issues related to pas technology use.

Practitioners need to be aware that there may be deeply embedded issues in the use of technology in an organisation that are not readily addressed. A dominant social group’s frame can have significant influence on other social groups’ frames, and it may not be possible to change a dominant frame through change programs and communication. As was demonstrated in this study, the senior manager’s technological frame was extremely resistant to change and this dominant frame persisted for a 10-year period. A limitation of this study is that fieldwork was conducted in an educational organisation. Additional longitudinal studies are required in different organisational settings to build on this knowledge. Such studies could add to what is known the dominance of frames, their stability and susceptibility to changes over time. Such effects could also be linked to practices of IT use. In addition, different lens such as theories of power and politics could be utilised to provide additional insights into the rich data. One caution is that longitudinal research requires long periods in the field to collect data which must then be analysed; thus, there is the risk that the object of interest may not occur in the data set.

Conducting longitudinal research, rather than examining discrete IT projects, provides a unique angle for understanding IT development and use in organisations. Looking at the technological frames of different social groups in a single organisation and relating these frames to the use of technology over an extended period of time has provided useful new insights.
