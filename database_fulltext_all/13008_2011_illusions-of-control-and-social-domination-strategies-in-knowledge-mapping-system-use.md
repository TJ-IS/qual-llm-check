RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Illusions of control and social domination strategies in knowledge mapping system use

Aure´lie Dudezert<sup>1</sup> and Dorothy E. Leidner<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Knowledge Management Research Group, Laboratoire Ge´nie Industriel Ecole Centrale, Paris; <sup>2</sup>Baylor University, Hankamer School of Business, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Aure´lie Dudezert, Knowledge Management Research Group, Laboratoire Ge´nie Industriel Ecole Centrale, Paris, Grande Voie des Vignes, 92295 Chaˆtenay-Malabry cedex, France E-mail: aurelie.dudezert@ecp.fr

## Abstract

In recent years, the overload of information has driven companies to develop visualization technologies as a way to graphically represent knowledge for decision making. On the basis of visualization technologies and on knowledge modeling techniques, knowledge maps (Kmaps) help represent the knowledge assets of a company as graphical discrete objects. Yet such systems, because of their visual representation of knowledge, are prone to creating illusions. Using a case study of three firms’ use of Kmaps in their Human Resource departments and drawing upon Bourdieu’s social theory, illusion of control theory, and IS as signal and symbol theory, our study provides a critical perspective on the use of Kmaps by groups of actors to reinforce their social domination in business organizations. We find that Kmap use can lead to illusions of knowledge control and illusions of executive control and that in response to the potential for knowledge illusions, three social domination strategies – a signal and symbol strategy, an avoidance strategy and an evidence strategy – are employed. Implications of these results on Knowledge Management and Information Systems Management future research are discussed. ## Introduction

Since the early 1990s organizations have been using various knowledge management (KM) tools, such as Knowledge Bases, Weblogs, Wikipedia, and Groupware. In recent years, the overload of information has driven companies to develop visualization technologies as a way to graphically represent knowledge for decision making (Forrester, 2006). On the basis of visualization technologies and on knowledge modeling techniques, Knowledge Maps (Kmaps) are a class of Knowledge Management Systems (KMS) that represent the knowledge assets of a company as graphical discrete objects and identify the knowledge areas the company must focus on in order to develop a knowledge intensive culture and infrastructure (Eppler, 2001). Because of the difficulty in assessing through informal communications the various knowledge proficiencies that exist within companies, Kmaps can be helpful tools for companies to capture and represent knowledge (Eppler, 2001). Kmaps purport to allow companies to improve KM by monitoring, driving, and managing knowledge as easily as they would tangible assets (Aubertin et al., 2003). They are meant to support top managers by helping them assign employees to the appropriate projects or business units and align organizational knowledge with the company’s business plan (Aubertin, 2003).

The use of Kmaps has grown in organizations as the maturation of graphics technologies and data mining tools enable the easy design and creation of Kmaps. Knowledge mapping is particularly valuable in fields such as engineering and science where knowledge is generated at a fast pace (Dang et al., 2011). For example, graphics technologies, such as Autonomy’s Knowledge Visualizer, Microsoft’s Visio, Mindjet’s Mindmanager, and Trivium’s SEE-K facilitate the creation of Kmaps in order to make knowledge visible for companies. However, even as these new technologies have facilitated the presentation of large amounts of information into an easy to represent form (e.g., the maps of organizational knowledge), new challenges have arisen. The ultimate users of the Kmap may, or may not, interpret the knowledge as visualized in the Kmap in the same way as the design team intended. This then raises the possibility that users will make decisions based upon the knowledge represented in the Kmap even though their interpretation of the knowledge deviates from that of the design team, resulting in the potential for illusions – or the ‘seeing’ of something in the Kmap that is not an accurate reflection of reality. Such illusions could militate against the effective use of Kmaps for managing employee knowledge.

Nevertheless, in the knowledge economy where knowledge is considered a core competitive resource (Grant, 1996; Spender, 1996), managers seek to develop managerial practices and systems that provide evidence of their control over this intangible resource. The actual outcomes of the managerial practices and systems may be less important than the symbolic value that they provide for the managers in legitimizing their hierarchical position and responsibility. As symbolic objects, Kmaps may provide the illusion to organizational stakeholders that the organization’s knowledge assets are being effectively managed. Yet little is known about the nature of the potential illusions nor the outcomes of them. Using a critical perspective inspired by Pierre Bourdieu’s social theory (1974, 1979, 1994), by illusion of control theory (Langer, 1975; Langer & Roth, 1975), and by IS symbol and signal theory (Feldman & March, 1981), we seek to explore how top managers in an HR management context employ Kmaps as symbolic objects to help legitimize their authority as managers of the organization’s human capital and the outcomes of the illusions embedded in the Kmap implementations.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. First, we present background information on Kmaps and the theoretical foundation for the study of the use of illusion of control. Second, we describe our method, a case study of three firms’ use of Kmaps based on interviews with Kmap users. We then present our findings, including within case analyses of the three firms and a cross-case analysis. We conclude with our interpretation of the findings and suggestions for future research.

## Background literature: Kmaps

Knowledge, a specific action-oriented resource linked to a person (Yates-Mercer, 2002), is created through a dynamic process of combination, internalization, socialization, and externalization that transforms knowledge between tacit and explicit modes (Nonaka, 1994). The effectiveness of knowledge transfer across individuals or organizational units depends upon many factors, including the extent of shared experiences, language, and culture between the involved parties as well as the characteristics of the knowledge to be transferred. The tacit and ‘sticky’ nature of some knowledge renders it particularly difficult to transfer (Von Hippel, 1994). Consequently, organizations have often struggled both to know what they know and to manage what they know. Many organizations, particularly large-global concerns, attempted to address these knowledge struggles by developing global KMS that provided a central source of information on the organization’s knowledge assets (Pan and Leidner, 2003). Yet for many organizations, these large KM systems quickly became enormous repositories whose use was hindered by the sheer volume of data and the associated difficulties of keeping the knowledge accurate and up-to-date (Alavi et al., 2005–2006). Part of the problem with such large repositories is that they can be difficult to search: searching through a large knowledge repository resembles a search of the internet, with little holistic view of the overall knowledge assets of the organization emerging. In response to this problem, organizations have implemented Kmaps.

Kmaps are a class of KMS focused on helping organizations, and the HR unit in particular, with competence management programs. Kmaps depict competencies graphically as discrete objects. A competence is an organizational attribute resulting from processes that bring together the individual skills and knowledge of the members of the organization (Caldeira & Ward, 2003). Competence management involves the specification of an organization’s competence needs, the identification of competence gaps, competence sourcing, competence development through training and coaching, and the staffing of projects (Lindgren et al., 2004). Therefore competence management has to reconcile two perspectives on competence: an organizational perspective that focuses on competence at an organizational level and considers competence as a basis for organizational competitive advantage (Prahalad & Hamel, 1990; Lado & Wilson, 1994) and an individual perspective that views competence as an underlying characteristic of an individual based on the individual’s emotional, social, and cognitive intelligence (Boyaztis, 1982, 2008; Von Krogh & Roos, 1995). Individual competence is the enactment of individual knowledge (Muffato, 1998) and Kmaps by representing individual knowledge allow organizations to represent individual competencies and to build a collective and organizational view of competence in an organization. As such, Kmaps can help bridge the organization’s macro level competence management with skill-based approaches to HR management that focus on individual competencies. While graphical visualization (pictures, signs, maps, etc.) can often enable the sharing of information on competencies more efficiently than language can, research on visual perceptions (Von Helmholtz, 1866; Gregory, 1997), semiology (Barthes, 1957), and graphical semiology (Bertin, 1999; Beguin & Pumain, 2003) underline the ambiguous nature of visual representations. Research suggests that visual representations may create cognitive illusions for the users of these representations if the brain employs misapplied knowledge to interpret the representations (Gregory, 1997). Visual representations trigger unconscious inferences from sensory data and knowledge derived from a person’s past experiences (Von Helmholtz, 1866). Individuals’ knowledge based on past experiences influences the interpretation of visual representations that can cause different individuals to build different perceptions of the same visual representation. This can lead to illusions of understanding if the individual believes he or she understands the meaning of the visual representation when in reality he or she does not have the proper understanding to interpret it. For example, maps, a common form of visual representation, are recognized as having a strong impact on the cognition process of users (Bartels and van Beurden, 1998). Rekacewicz (2006) emphasizes that map designers take responsibility when choosing colors for signs because people’s cultural backgrounds condition them to interpret colors in maps differently. Decisions based on misinterpreting visual representations can be detrimental to outcomes. Moreover, whereas Kmaps represent knowledge with rational tools, they show a subjective representation of what is knowledge in a company. Indeed, knowledge mapping process is inspired by a cognitive mapping process described by Cossette & Audet (1994) in which designers and consultants (or internal company sponsors) decide upon how to extract, code knowledge, and how to display the knowledge. This can lead to illusions of understanding if the individual believes knowledge representation in Kmaps is rational and objective when in reality it represents the point-of-views of specific groups of actors (i.e. designers and consultants) on knowledge. The problem of the ambiguous nature of maps is only beginning to be explored in the research literature on Kmaps (Eppler & Burkhard, 2004, Dudezert, 2007).

Not only do potential misunderstandings arise from the ambiguity of knowledge representation (e.g., the way the knowledge is visualized), but the knowledge content itself, absent important contextualization, can also create misinterpretations. Kmap users may be making decisions based solely on the Kmap representation without taking into account the whole context of the knowledge. For example, if a Kmap describes knowledge as a graphical element that is easy to move around on the map, the decision maker may base decisions on a perception that knowledge can be easily and immediately moved within the company. To the extent that the process of knowledge transfer is in reality quite complex, the perception of knowledge mobility is an illusion created by the graphical depiction of the knowledge elements. As such, the visualization techniques of Kmaps can potentially lead to illusions, defined as subjective perceptions of stimulus that do not match the reality of the stimulus (Macknich et al., 2009). While it is recognized that information and information systems are often used intentionally as symbols or signals to project an image (Feldman & March, 1981), there has been little work that examines the outcomes of unintended illusions that systems may create. Our work seeks to uncover the outcomes of illusions resulting from the implementation of Kmaps in organizations. To this end, we draw upon Pierre Bourdieu’s social theory (1974, 1979, 1994), illusion of control theory (Langer, 1975; Langer & Roth, 1975), and IS symbol and signal theory (Feldman & March, 1981). These theories are described next.

## Theoretical foundation

Bourdieu (1974, 1979, 1994) focuses on the power of symbolic systems and their domination over the construction of reality. His Theory of Practice proposes that individuals exist in fields defined by a shared way of thinking, acting, and behaving. Within a given field, individuals possess economic, social, and intellectual/ cultural capital, which they use to reinforce their status relative to others in the field. Economic capital represents an individual’s access to monetary resources. Social capital refers to an individual’s access to a social network. Intellectual capital refers to an individual’s education, knowledge, and professional expertise. The volume of capital an individual possesses as well as the overall distribution of capital in the field determines the individual’s repertoire of behavior and influences the individual’s perception of reality. A fourth form of capital – symbolic capital – is quite different from the other three. Symbolic capital represents the power to dictate within a field the value attributed to the other forms of capital. Individuals who have symbolic capital are the leaders and trend-setters within their field. In an organizational setting, they are often those with hierarchical authority over others. Unlike the other resources, symbolic capital cannot be acquired through the efforts of the individual; rather, it is assigned or ascribed to the individual by other individuals in the same field. Once such symbolic capital is ascribed to an individual, the individual maintains power by reinforcing his legitimate possession of the symbolic capital.

Symbolic capital is based on the taboo of explicitation (1994, p. 179): people in a social group accept the magic strength (1994, p. 187) of the symbolic capital (i.e. people believe in the authority of the symbolic capital) because of an individual and collective self-deception (i.e. illusion) (1994, p. 174) based on structural mental schemes shared in the group. An illusion is ‘a mental conception which may be a misinterpretation of a real appearance or may be something imagined. It may be pleasing, harmless or even useful’ (Stein, 1982, p. 662). At the individual level, illusion can have positive impacts on mental health and productive work. Research on positive illusions (Taylor & Brown, 1988; 1994) shows that an unrealistically positive view of the self contributes to making an individual’s world a warmer and more beneficent place in which to live. Individuals therefore tend to develop these positive illusions and construct a cognitive system able to maintain them.

In business organizations, KMS as a class of Information Systems can contribute to the maintenance of illusion.

More specifically, research has shown that IS can contribute to maintain the illusion of control. Illusion of control theory (Langer, 1975; Langer & Roth, 1975) espouses that individuals tend to believe they have control over personal success outcomes even when in reality they do not. As such, when people are placed in manipulative skill situations such as competition or in a cognitive skill situation such as making a difficult decision, they treat the situation outcome as if it were determined by their skill rather than by chance. IS contributes to this illusion of control because they are symbol and signal of rational organizational choice (Feldman & March, 1981): in modern western civilization where the bureaucratic organizations are edifices built on ideas of rationality and control (Weberian’s myth), using information, asking for information, and justifying decisions in terms of information symbolize that the decision process is under control and legitimate. By rationally organizing such information processes, IS contributes to this illusion of control. Using KMS thereby reinforces decision makers’ own illusion of control by providing them a sense of assurance that they have considered the most relevant information on knowledge assets.

Moreover, KMS can help establish and maintain an illusion of executive control. According to Schwenk (1986), in any organization, individuals will be asked to contribute to multiple courses of action, some of which may conflict with each other. Therefore an executive has to convince others to contribute their resources (money, effort, etc.) to courses of action in which the executive believes. In order to increase contributors’ commitment, executives can provide statistical and anecdotal information to prove their control over outcomes. Thereby they construct an ‘illusion of executive control’ (Schwenk, 1986, p. 304). Illusion of executive control differs from illusion of control in that the former is a reflection of others’ perception of the individual whereas the latter is a reflection of the individual’s perception of him/herself. Specifically, KMS may be perceived by contributors as a means for others to control their knowledge. In this sense, it is not the contributor gaining control by managing his/her knowledge assets, but it is the executive in charge of the system that gains control over the collective knowledge assets of the organization. Contributors must have confidence in the intentions and ability of the executive controlling the knowledge assets before they will be comfortable providing accurate updates on their own knowledge. By using KMS themselves, executives can gain credibility with contributors.

Because rationality and control are important symbolic capital for managers in business organizations, managers are subject to using KMS both to legitimate their hierarchical authority and to provide evidence of their effectiveness. Managing knowledge workers and their knowledge, indeed, proves challenging because the sticky, tacit, and emotional nature of knowledge calls into question the rationality and control symbolic capital, requiring that top managers and executives have to prove they are able to control this specific asset in order to maintain the managerial legitimacy. In addition, the intangible nature of much knowledge makes it difficult to convince knowledge workers to contribute to its management. Using Kmaps to manage knowledge can contribute to an illusion of control useful for top managers and executives to reassure themselves that they are doing everything possible to control the knowledge resource in their organization and to an illusion of executive control useful for top managers to convince others that they are able to employ the most advanced technological tools available to control the organization’s knowledge resources.

Taken together, Bourdieu’s social theory, illusion of control theory, and IS as symbol and signal theory are useful in understanding the ways that individuals manipulate capital in order to influence perceptions in such a way as to maintain or increase their status (Table 1). With these theories as our interpretive lens, we will analyze three cases of Kmap implementations in the HR departments of organizations in three different industries. Our analysis will seek to understand the ways that organizational actors use Kmap systems as symbolic capital to reinforce their power and perpetuate illusions of rationality and control. We next present our method followed by our case descriptions.

## Method

In this study, we focus on Kmaps designed with Trivium’s SEE-K methodology. SEE-K maps, illustrated in Figure 2 (adapted from http://www.trivium.fr), consist of a database, a statistical module, and a visualization tool that produces knowledge trees. In this graphical representation, color schemes and branches are used to represent three categories: the knowledge capital, the skills, and the areas of specialization.

We employ a multiple case study methodology in order to investigate a contemporary phenomenon within a real-life context (Benbasat et al., 1987; Yin, 2002). The selection of the cases was guided by the principle of theoretical replication (Yin, 1994). Our goal was to expand and generalize theories (analytical generalization) and not statistical generalization. Data were collected between 2001 and 2004 from human resource managers in three French companies in the areas of Consulting to the Energy and Defense industry, Banking & Insurance, and IT Consulting to the Banking and Insurance industry. We chose these three companies because they had implemented Kmaps and had given interviews to business journals about their use of Kmaps. While all of these cases have similar characteristics to some extent in that they had similar systems implemented during the same general time period and are all service-oriented, we sought variation in other characteristics to be able to tease out theoretical differences. For example, the approaches to designing the Kmaps varied across the three companies, as did the role of the HR department executives as well as the role of the Kmap users. We knew in advance of this variation based upon a content analysis of published interviews with HR directors in 10 companies, of which these were three (see Dudezert, 2007).

A primary point of data collection was open-ended discussions with SEE-K users during a Workshop in 2001 in which the users spoke candidly with each other and one of the researchers about their experiences with Kmaps. This workshop was organized by Les Echos journal in Paris and deals with the impact of KM and KMS on Competence Management. Presentations were made by HR practitioners on Kmaps use in companies followed by a debate with attendees. The goal of the workshop data was to gather spontaneous discourses about the HR managers’ Kmap use.

We also gathered data via interviews of human resource managers in professional journals. These professional journals aim to communicate human resource management best practices and explain how Kmaps can help

HR managers make accurate decisions. All of the HR managers interviewed had been involved in the Kmap initiatives in their companies. In addition to the interviews, feedback comments from these SEE-K users on Trivium’s website were gathered and compiled. Feedback comments consist of interviews of customers conducted by the Communication Department of Trivium. These interviews focus on the users’ understanding of Kmaps’ design processes and how the Kmap is used in the companies. The goal of the interview data was to assess the HR managers’ public, formally stated perspective. Sometimes, formal statements may differ from the less formal statements (e.g., statements given during a workshop break-out discussion). Formal statements help assess the signal that the HR directors seek to give to stakeholders.

Thus we have collected both spontaneous (workshop) and controlled discourses on Kmaps design process and use by HR managers in three French companies. Additional sources of data for this study include the companies’ websites and annual reports. This supplemental data provided information about the companies’ business models and strategies. The intent of gathering this information was to help us understand the organizational context surrounding each company’s use of Kmaps.

To write the cases, we relied upon an analysis of the discussions of the HR managers attending the workshop in Paris concerning their Kmap uses as well as an analysis of the company websites, feedbacks, and published interviews with individuals in the organizations. We conducted within-case analyses to uncover the specific form and nature of illusion in each company and then undertook a cross-case analyses to determine the similarities and differences across the three firms and to build theoretical explanations on the use of illusions created by Kmap in HR management context. A description of the research method is provided in Appendix 1.

Given the variety of case study approaches available to IS researchers (Myers, 2009), it is important to specify the criteria by which such a research project should be evaluated. On the basis of Yin’s (2002) work and on Myers’ (2009) analysis about the evaluation of case study research the following criteria can be used: the respect of the principle of originality, the respect of the principle of evidence, and the respect of the principle of alternative perspectives. For critical research, a specific criterion is that the research seeks to be emancipator, that is it should help to eliminate the cause of alienation and domination. As explained by Schultze & Leidner (2002, p. 217): ‘ The critical discourse aims to create the conditions in which the conflicts between different groups can be surfaced, discussed openly and resolved fairly’.

To assess the quality of our study, we compare our case study method to these principles (see Appendix 2). We next present the case descriptions and within case analysis for each firm followed by the cross-case analysis.

## Data analysis: case studies of the Kmap experiences in three firms

## Company 1: Protiware

Case description Protiware is a subsidiary of a world leader in nuclear power and connectors and provides its clients consulting and information technology (IT) services in energy, defense, and other industries. It employs 1800 people and has sales of 210 million euros. In spite of the economic downturn between 2001 and 2003, Protiware was able to maintain profit stability due to sales from its parent company. Although its employee turnover rate was half that of its competitors, Protiware nevertheless decided to develop specific competence management techniques in order to retain the individual competencies of its employees. One technique was the implementation and use of a Kmap.

Protiware’s Kmap was a KM tool used to track the knowledge of its employees. The HR managers explained that the Kmap was primarily used by the company’s employees and their operational managers (i.e. supervisors). Employees and their supervisors were trained to use the Kmap and employees were asked to regularly update their knowledge in the Kmap database. Each time an employee accomplished a mission in which new knowledge were acquired, the employee was expected to update his or her expertise description in the Kmap database. Supervisors would then check their employees entries during annual performance evaluations. One HR manager suggested that the Kmap permitted an employee to locate his or her own position in the company. Stated the HR manager, The Kmap allows an employee to understand whether he is on a branch that the firm will cut in the future, or on the contrary, whether he is a very tiny leaf on the tree, but essential to the strategic development of the firm. The Kmap allows employees to situate their own knowledge in relation to others in the company. In short, the Kmap of Protiware simplifies the tracking of the progress of knowledge acquired by employees throughout the year as well as facilitates the ability to categorize employee competencies.

Within-case analysis: Protiware employees and operational managers use Kmaps to enter, update, and track individual knowledge. That Protiware allows individuals and operational managers to enter this information without any formal system of information quality control indicates an assumption that employees are capable of accurately updating the company’s knowledge base. Such an assumption ignores the possibility that employees may have biases or lack the ability to independently assess their own level of expertise: one employee may consistently underestimate his/her own expertise because he/she is shy or humble whereas another may overestimate his/her expertise because he/she is especially confident. Moreover, it is possible that individuals may intentionally under- or overestimate their ability in a particular area and enter this in the database inaccurately. For example, an IT employee with an expertise in Windows NT but a budding interest in Linux might intentionally understate his NT expertise and overstate his Linux expertise in hopes of being assigned to Linux projects. Thus, accepting at face value the knowledge representation displayed by Protiware’s Kmap that is based upon individuals’ assessments of their own knowledge may foster an illusion of control of organizational knowledge moreso than an accurate perception of Protiware’s knowledge assets.

HR managers at Protiware seem aware of these potential assumptions and illusion: ironically, while they describe the Kmaps as quite easy to use and laud the Kmap’s provision of lists of relevant people, according to the skills the operational managers are looking for, the HR managers do not actually use the Kmap themselves. This suggests that they are aware of the potential for employ ees to enter inaccurate accounts of their competence. Nevertheless they have not initiated any means of addressing this issue for the operational managers who use the Kmaps. Granted, managers may review the entries during year-end performance evaluations and correct any glaring misrepresentations. Yet the supervisors, divorced in time and process from the acquisition of the employees’ competence, may fail to notice any but the most jarring of inaccuracies. Perhaps not surprisingly, Kmap use by HR managers at Protiware remains limited to tracking knowledge assets in a superficial manner.

## Company 2: Teamama

Case description Teamama is an insurance and banking company. The particular branch office we investigated has 239,000 customers in different geographic regions in northeastern France. In addition, the branch has 87 commercial agencies, 1060 employees, and 47 million euros in sales. After a merger with another insurance company, Teamama began a transformation of its HR management, focusing on the implementation of an employee competence management program with the goal of increasing agility and flexibility in a global economic environment. This competence management program resulted in the development of a Kmap in the regional branch office as a pilot experiment before implementation throughout the entire company.

Teamama’s Kmap is used by HR managers as well as by operational managers and employees. Employees are able to evaluate their own knowledge and visualize the pathways for their potential promotions within the company. Employees can identify their own chances for promotion based on comparing their current knowledge to the knowledge required for their desired positions. Identifying their own promotion opportunities allows employees to also identify the additional knowledge they would need to acquire in order to reach their desired positions. In essence, the Kmap provides employees the ability to envision their potential movements within the company.

One HR manager described the Kmap as a useful tool for KM in the firm: not only were employees able to manage their own skills development and potential movements, but also their supervisors were able to access the information in the Kmap in order to assess the knowledge progress of their subordinates and make promotion decisions during annual performance evaluations. Thus, at Teamama, the Kmap is used not only to track the knowledge assets of the company, but also to manage these assets with a view towards enabling employees to be in charge of their own knowledge.

Within-case analysis Knowledge, in the form of levels of expertise, is entered into the Teamama Kmap by employee supervisors using a three-phase process. During the first phase, operational managers involve employees in the annual evaluation process and require them to describe their competence in a pre-defined database. In the second phase during the annual evaluation meeting, operational managers check the description of the competence with the employee and confirm this description in the database. In the third phase, employees access the Kmap in order to see their own knowledge, their position in the company, and their potential for promotion. The fact that supervisors rather than the employees confirm the knowledge in the system reflects an awareness of the potential biases that can be introduced when employees enter their own knowledge and increases the degree of objectiveness in the knowledge represented, thereby reducing the potential for knowledge illusions.

Although the HR managers are aware of the potential for individuals to enter inaccurate assessments of their knowledge and employ measures to address this problem (e.g., having the supervisors enter the information), they do not develop corresponding measures to ensure that the operational managers enter competence consistently and maintain regular updates. In fact, they assume that operational managers will be motivated to maintain the accuracy of the knowledge database. Furthermore, HR managers claim that operational managers have easier access to the information about their people even though it is the operational managers that entered the information into the system, suggesting at the least that they were a priori fairly knowledgeable of their team members skills. Apparently recognizing this potential contradiction, the HR managers describe the effectiveness of the Kmap as dependent upon the motivation of operational managers to keep abreast of their employees’ knowledge development, and attribute any poor data quality in the Kmap to lack of motivation on the part of the operational managers. Thus, HR managers are aware of the potential problems associated with the knowledge representation yet downplay these problems, relying instead on the assumption that individuals will keep their operational managers accurately informed of their knowledge development and that operational managers will regularly modify the Kmap as employees.

The primary use of Teamama’s Kmap is for employee KM. No longer required to seek assistance from human resource managers for professional growth advice, employees can plan their own career path by using the system to identify needed knowledge and access online training. In this sense, the HR department has shed some of its responsibilities to operational managers and employees. And while the HR managers report using the system to track knowledge assets, they do not use the system to manage their own competence. Essentially, the primary aim of the Kmap system from the HR perspective is not necessarily to manage knowledge efficiently, but to shift responsibility for career development from HR to operational units. Whether the resulting procedures improve the KM of employees or not appears irrelevant to the HR managers. Indeed, an employee with an operational manager who did not enter accurate and up-to-date knowledge on the employee would be at a disadvantage in trying to use the Kmap to manage his/her knowledge. Thus, while the HR managers at Teamama initiated procedures to avoid the potential for inaccurate knowledge assets to be entered into the system, they did not go so far as to initiate procedures to ensure that the KM aspect of the Kmap system was effective. Consequently, the potential for an illusion of knowledge control is relatively low at Teamama, but the potential for illusion of control in the management of that knowledge remains high.

## Company 3: Melifrance

Case description Melifrance was established in France in 1986 with the objective of providing consulting services to banks and insurance companies. Melifrance grew rapidly: by 1993, it had developed a strong legacy modernization expertise based on software solutions to industrialize transformation projects. Moreover, with the success of the Y2K and euro transformation projects, Melifrance extended its scope to include redevelopment and architecture transformation projects. The company became public in 1999 and by 2002, Melifrance employed 1000 people in 19 geographic zones in France and Benelux. In 2002, the company was chosen to provide services for a large insurance company.

In order to develop the capacity to offer consulting services for the large insurance company, Melifrance needed to form a team consisting of 200 of its employees. To form such a team with the appropriate skill set, Melifrance implemented a Kmap as a way to assess the knowledge of its employees. Using the Kmap, employees accessed e-learning sessions that taught them how to update the Kmap database from their workstations. They were able to enter their knowledge sets into the database and express their job preferences, such as desired job positions, availability for relocation, and availability for workgroup repositioning. In addition, employees were able to request their supervisor to attach their annual evaluation reports to the Kmap database so that the HR department could better reposition them to new jobs. Furthermore, the Kmap was linked to a training catalog system. This integration allowed further exploitation of the Kmap such that when the appropriate employee was identified from the Kmap database for staffing a new position, the Kmap would display the training sessions available to help prepare the employee for the new position. HR managers describe the Kmap as a tool that creates a standardized HR management workflow in the following activities: knowledge description by employees in a pre-formatted database, validation by operation managers, identification of relevant people for a specific mission, evaluation of the training costs, training of relevant people, and updating employee skill descriptions. As explained by one manager, Each year, operational managers evaluate the skills their teammembers have developed and if employees agree, their annual evaluation report is attached to their knowledge description in the competence management tool.

The objectives of the Kmap implementation were to better supervise the development of employee skills and to reinforce the HR re-staffing strategy the company wished to employ. HR managers believe that the Kmap is achieving these objectives. They credit the Kmap as giving employees the ability to manage their own careers and providing the Melifrance with a showcase of the knowhow of the company’s employees.

Within-case analysis At Melifrance, the knowledge in the Kmap was entered by the individual employees. However, the HR managers involved in the Kmap development did not rest on the assumption that individuals would willingly enter and regularly update their knowledge assets; rather, the HR group mandated Kmap usage by relinquishing personnel services to those who did not regularly update their knowledge profiles. Even while they mandated use, the HR managers also recognized that not all entries into the system would be accurate. Said one HR manager, Some people don’t want to present their skills, more often because they don’t want to change their jobs. He continued, In such cases, nobody can blame the firm if these people look unemployable [due to their apparent lack of skills as portrayed in the Kmap]. The HR manager indicated that responsibility for lack of internal job movement and promotion potential rested on those employees who did not update their knowledge accurately because the company always favors internal appointments before considering external recruitment. Hence, the HR managers do not assume that all the knowledge in the system is accurate, but do assume that for those using the system properly, internal job promotions should follow. Even while acknowledging the potential for inaccuracies in the system data, the HR managers continue to espouse the viewpoint that the system provides excellent visibility to individuals and reinforces the HR strategy the firm wants to deploy.

The HR managers demonstrate selective attention to the potential problems involved in coding employee knowledge. If employees do not enter their knowledge accurately and objectively, the Kmap may provide a firmwide misrepresentation of employees’ knowledge. In thi case, the Kmap would not accurately reflect the com pany’s knowledge capital, but merely provide an illusion of the knowledge. Yet, the HR strategy to mandate use only compensates for the problems of having less than full participation of all employees; it does not address the problem of intentional, or unintentional, knowledge misrepresentation. Thus, while aware of the potentia for the system to provide an incomplete or inaccurate representation of knowledge (e.g., a knowledge illusion), the HR managers choose to address the problem superficially. In fact, because the HR self-interests are served when operational managers use the Kmap – it increases the visibility of the HR initiative to serve a more strategic role in the organization – the HR managers are implicitly involved in creating the illusion of knowledge control. Furthermore, since each employee is likely to evaluate himself or herself differently based on different standards, this self-assessment of skills can potentially mislead the company’s re-staffing decisions. Inaccurate knowledge in the Kmap database could have detrimenta effects on the company’s internal re-staffing efforts if the Kmap is the only source by which the company bases re-staffing decisions. For instance, if an employee boasts of his or her expertise in the Kmap database in an area in which he or she has little knowledge, an operational manager may accidentally re-staff him or her in a vital position that depends on the expertise that had been falsely reported. Assuming that the operational managers are acutely aware of their own employees’ true knowledge assets, this is unlikely to occur. But then one must question the real effectiveness of the system, if it is merely providing an illusion of competence management and staffing decisions rather than actually informing either. Ultimately, the real value of the Melifrance Kmap appears to be that its use creates a visible symbol of HR effectiveness in sponsoring a ‘successful’ (in the sense of widely used) system.

## Cross-case analysis

In all three cases, the HR managers were involved in the design of a Kmap system and considered that the Kmaps were effective tools for KM. In each case, the Kmaps were referred to as tools, implying that one could use the tool without actually knowing how the tool was constructed or how it operated, much as one uses modern day electronic equipment without knowledge of the underlying technologies that make the equipment operate. In one company, the Kmap was compared to a hammer that one uses to strike a nail with complete disregard for how the hammer was constructed. In another, the Kmap was viewed as a robot that one could use again without knowing the intricate details. And in another, simply as ‘a tool’. These views of the Kmaps suggest that even while the HR managers are involved in designing the Kmaps, they are all too ready to ignore the potentials for inaccuracies in the knowledge profiles entered into the system. Unlike actual tools, one does need to know how the knowledge that forms a Kmap was entered, by whom, and under what circumstances. Otherwise, one is susceptible to basing HR decision (staffing, promotions) on knowledge illusions rather than reality. In fact, in none of the firms did the HR managers take steps to ensure that the Kmap was truly efficacious in managing competence and helping employees identify and acquire needed knowledge or in helping employees achieve promotions. This suggests that the Kmaps served as a symbol of rationality and control in competence management even though the real effects of the Kmaps were not known.

While there were similarities in the perception of the Kmaps across the HR managers in the three cases, there were differences across the cases both in terms of how the Kmaps were used as well as in how the assumptions behind Kmaps, and the resulting illusions, were addressed. Although each of the three companies uses its Kmap to store its employees’ knowledge, the goals and level of usage of the Kmap differs across the three companies. For example, Protiware uses its Kmap primarily to track its employees’ knowledge. The Kmap was promoted as an easier way for employees to track their competency development throughout the year, which proved especially useful for supervisors during the employees’ annual performance evaluations. Teamama uses its Kmap to manage its employees’ knowledge. Whereas Protiware’s employees entered their own knowledge information into the Kmap database and their supervisors read the entries, Teamama’s supervisors are more involved in the employees’ knowledge description because they have to confirm this information in the database. Thus, the supervisors at Teamama were more involved with the Kmap and with the progress of their employees.

In addition, users at Teamama were more involved in their use of the Kmap than users at Protiware. Employees at Teamama were asked to manage their own developments by using the Kmap to identify their promotion opportunities and the additional knowledge they would need to acquire to reach their desired positions. In contrast, Kmap use at Protiware was primarily useful during annual performance evaluations to track individual competence developments. Finally, Melifrance applied its Kmap to achieve organizational goals. Melifrance’s HR department used its Kmap to assist in internal job placements and re-staffing efforts. Melifrance also used its Kmap to form temporary project teams as required, such as developing the 200-member team needed to offer consulting services for the large insurance company. Furthermore, Melifrance’s Kmap was integrated with a training catalog system, which allowed the Kmap to be further exploited to support the personnel re-staffing efforts of the company. Unlike the voluntary Kmap use at Protiware and Teamama, Kmap use at Melifrance was mandated by the company.

These differences in goals and levels of use suggest that there are variations in the progressiveness with which a Kmap is used: Teamama appears to be using its Kmap more progressively than Protiware and Melifrance appears to be using its Kmap more progressively than either Teamama or Protiware. Therefore, we see a threestage usage progression emerging from the case descriptions: the first stage involves using Kmap for tracking the knowledge of the organization; the second, for tracking organizational knowledge assets and for managing individual knowledge; and the third, for tracking individual knowledge assets, managing individual knowledge, and informing organizational staffing decisions.

However, the three stages of use are associated with different assumptions concerning illusions of control created by Kmap use. Using Kmaps to track knowledge rests on the assumption that the knowledge entered into the system is accurate. One of the three firms – Protiware – recognized that not all knowledge entered might be accurate but ignored this problem. The resulting Kmap reflects an illusion of knowledge control moreso than a depiction of the reality of organizational knowledge assets. Given the engineering and technical orientation of Protiware, the illusion of control may be of particularly high importance: in order to legitimate their role vis-a\`-vis employees and to prove that knowledge and competence can be rationally and efficiently controlled, the company HR managers use the illusion of knowledge control created by Kmap use as a symbol of their active involvement in the competence management of employees. Thus the Kmap is used to send a signal to the employees and give them the illusion HR managers have the ability to control rationally and efficiently such an individual and action-oriented resource.

Two of the three firms – Teamama and Melifrance – recognized the knowledge accuracy problem and attempted to manage around it, by either having supervisors enter the knowledge (Teamama) or providing training to employees on how to assess their knowledge and enter it (Melifrance). Yet both companies are still subject to the illusion of knowledge control since there is no reason to assume that operational managers will consistently assess employee skill (e.g., one operational manager might consider 2 months expertise with technology ‘high’ whereas another views this as ‘low’) nor that online training guides and standardized HR processes will reduce individual proclivities to exaggerate, or understate, skill. But because the HR managers in these two firms took some measures that at the least project an image of high knowledge quality in the Kmap, they were then able to convince others to use the system for competency management.

Therefore in both cases, HR managers use Kmap to create an illusion of executive control: they provide information that the executive is in control of the outcomes of knowledge and competence management. In the case of Teamama, HR managers chose to use the Kmap to create an illusion of operational managers control on knowledge and competence rather than to create an illusion of HR managers’ control. In fact, they claim that the Kmap provides enough information to operational managers to manage knowledge and competence and they describe the effectiveness of the Kmap as dependent upon the motivation of the operational managers. In such an insurance and banking company, employees may be aware of the difficulty of controlling intangible knowledge assets. In this context, if the HR managers tried to control or take credit for the employee competence, they would risk losing credibility (e.g., the employees would not accept it). Thus, the HR managers chose to transfer the responsibility of competence management to the operational managers. Ironically by relinquishing HR control of competencies the HR managers reinforce their hierarchical positions and involve operational managers in the competence management program.

On the contrary, in the case of Melifrance, HR managers use Kmap to create an illusion of HR effectiveness. In fact with the use of the Kmap, Melifrance’s HR managers have the opportunity to prove to senior management that they can develop a KMS able to contribute efficiently to provide services for its main client. Thus in order to be credible in KM, HR managers use the Kmap and combine it with new specific practices and processes that control and monitor individual actions and involvements in the company. Moreover, they transfer responsibility for unsuccessful KM to employees by imparting blame on employees if the employees are unable to successfully develop their knowledge through the Kmap. Thus, by mandating use and inculpating employees (rather than incorrect knowledge representation or use) if they were unsuccessful, the Melifrance HR managers were able to transfer the responsibility of competency management to individuals all while creating an image of HR effectiveness with senior management. In this sense, a specific illusion was present in the case of Melifrance: the illusion of HR effectiveness, that is the illusion of HR executives control over competence management such than any failures in competence management are not their fault. This illusion of HR effectiveness was achieved by making explicit the symbolic organizational capital (i.e. rationality and control embedded in the Kmap) that regulates social hierarchy in the organization. ## Discussion and implications

Our study has several theoretical implications relevant to the domain of KM and the study of IS. First, our study raises questions about the concept of ‘objective’ knowledge representation. In the same way that much KM research views knowledge as an object (Schultze & Leidner, 2002), organizations too are prone to view knowledge as a readily manageable object. Yet, a Kmap is influenced by its designers and the many (employees and supervisors in our case) who contribute to its creation. Rather than striving for knowledge objectivity, which can never be fully achieved, perhaps organizations should focus more on the attainment of consistency in knowledge representation. Future research could examine the enabling conditions of attaining consistent knowledge representation (e.g., ensuring that experience and competence are assessed similarly across individuals, departments, and business units) as well as the consequences of inconsistent knowledge assessment.

Second, our study suggests the need for more critical analysis of KM. As underscored by Schultze & Leidner (2002), very few KM research programs have adopted a critical perspective with a view towards highlighting how different stakeholder interests, social practices, and structures interact to create power differences. KM is often portrayed as a win-win situation: individuals benefit from ready access to expertise as well as gains in the visibility of their own expertise and organizations benefit from faster problem resolutions, reduced costs associated with ‘reinventing the wheel’, and improved knowledge transfer across business units (Alavi & Leidner, 1999). However, the cases presented in this paper suggest that in reality, the outcomes of KM are more nuanced. A specific group of individuals in an organization may use KM and KMS to achieve its own interests while neither the organization nor individuals experience major benefits from the KM implementation. In this sense, the benefits of the KM and KMS may be confiscated by a specific group in order to legitimate its role in the company, much as the HR group at Melifrance benefited from the Kmap without actually using the Kmap or ensuring that it was used effectively by individuals. Such phenomena question the win-win perspective to study KM in companies and raise the need for more critical studies to uncover the political motivations underpinning Kmap and KMS implementation and use.

Third, our study offers insights into the study of symbolic capital. Our findings suggest that Kmaps are symbolic resources useful for groups of actors to maintain social domination in business organizations and to involve operational managers and senior management in knowledge and competence management projects. This suggests that KMS can be used to maintain and/or develop social domination. In this paper we identify three strategies of Kmap use to maintain HR hierarchical positions in a company – signal and symbol strategy, avoidance strategy, and evidence strategy. Future research can examine these, and potentially other, strategies to determine how they are used to reinforce other forms of social domination. Future research can also study how the technical evolutions of Kmapping software impact the strategy of Kmap use in business organizations. In fact Kmapping software has evolved since 2004. New functionalities may allow the development of new strategies of use. Moreover, future research is needed to examine the power of KMS-based symbolic capital in different contexts. For example, in a virtual team context, it is conceivable that the individual most adept with coordinating via the available technology tools will emerge as the de facto leader even if this individual is not the most qualified topic expert on the team. In such a case, the technology skill may serve as symbolic capital that accrues power to the holder, even power in an area where the individual has less legitimate claim than another. Future research may also consider why employees accept such social domination built by KMS use – is it more prevalent in cultures where technology itself is a highly valued resource and is it more prevalent with younger generations? More generally, Bourdieu’s theory considered social, intellectual, and financial sources of capital. One might argue that technology is itself becoming an important source of capital in today’s global world, that it is not merely symbolic capital, but a fundamental form of capital that imparts advantages to those that are able to acquire it.

Lastly, our study offers insights to the study of illusion by users of an information system. An important implication of illusion of control (Langer, 1975) is that when individuals perceive themselves as largely responsible for their own success, they are likely to make decisions based on their level of confidence and selfperceptions and ignore contravening information. This behavior can be illusory because the perception that most people hold is not well balanced and most people see themselves in terms of their strengths vs their weaknesses (Taylor & Brown, 1988). Our study suggests that the use of Kmaps can produce illusions: individuals using the Kmaps for their own competency development believe that if they update their skill set according to the suggestions of the Kmap, then their likelihood of promotion should increase. In reality, promotion decisions are very complex and hinge on many factors, of which skill is only one. However, firms might be making themselves subject to lawsuits by employees if employees are led to believe that by engaging in the competence management program and following the Kmap’s recommendations for competency development, they will be promoted but are not. Future research can delve deeper into the consequences, good and bad, of illusions from the use of Kmaps and other IS tools that aid in competence management.

In fact, illusions may have positive benefits. For instance, the illusion of control has been associated with greater persistence, greater feelings of happiness, increase of social bonding, and consequently, greater success (Taylor & Brown, 1988). Interestingly, individuals who distort their reality to enhance their ability to foster a sense of well being are more likely to diminish and put less emphasis on the negative aspects of their lives. Thus, even if a Kmap (or more generally, an KMS) is not having a direct impact on employee competency development, if the employee maintains the illusion that the system provides control over his/her career, then the employee may have a more positive attitude about work, may work harder, and may become more productive as a result of the illusion that the system use creates. Future research may contrast the impact of system use vs the impact of the illusion of the system use In addition, future research might consider the impacts that system illusions have in relation to building and enhancing employee well being and the impacts on IT departments and organizations.

## Limitations and conclusions

Before concluding this paper, some limitations should be noted. First, the study is based on a small sample size of French-based companies in the service sector using Kmap technology available in 2004. As such, the findings may not generalize to companies based in other countries or in other industries. Moreover, the features of Kmap technology might have changed since 2004. Our study did not attempt to directly link behaviors with system features, but future work might consider how the features of the system influence the domination strategies employed. Second, the study was conducted in an HR KM context, which may not be generalizable to the management of other knowledge in other contexts. For instance, people tend to be more sensitive and have more motivation to be biased when evaluating and representing their own competencies; however, when they evaluate and represent knowledge that is not about them, such as website content, inventory, store sales, etc., they may exhibit less bias. Third, our study of illusion depended upon our interpretation of the HR managers’ statements and behaviors to identify instances of illusion. As one of the first studies of illusion in the context of IS, we have relied upon our analysis of multiple sources of data to attempt to identify the illusions. ## Description of our case study research

Phase 1: Data collection Spontaneous and controlled discourses of HR managers in the workshop in Paris. Feedbacks and published interviews of HR managers. Companies’ websites.

Phase 2: Within-case analysis Data analysis: The data collected in the first phase were discussed and analyzed using the IS as signal and symbol theory and the illusion of control theory.

Research question: Are Kmaps used in companies to create illusion of control and illusion of executive control? Results: We have identified three different Kmap uses to create illusion of control and illusion of executive control.

Phase 3: Cross-case analysis Data analysis: Within-case analysis material was discussed and analyzed using Bourdieu’s Social Domination and Theory of practices theories.

Research question: Are illusions of control created by Kmap use used by HR managers to reinforce their social domination in their company?

Results: The analysis generated three different strategies of domination associated with Kmap use.

Phase 4: Building theoretical contributions In this phase we analyzed the material generated by within-case analysis and cross-case analysis to build theoretical implications of the results.

4. The principle of emancipation

a. Does the research work help to eliminate the cause of alienation and domination?

From an academic point of view, stakeholders interests in KMS use is an important and under-researched aspect of KM.

This research work uses spontaneous and controlled discourses. Theoretical arguments are built by confronting verbatim and discourse analysis, secondary data and our theoretical background.

We consider Pierre Bourdieu’s social domination theory, illusion of control theory and IS symbol and signal theory in order to build a theoretical framework able to understand stakeholders interests in KMS use.

Our research helps individuals and organizations by identifying the potential for KMS to be used by specific groups of actors to maintain and comfort social domination rather than to manage knowledge efficiently.
