# Internet of Things and Competitive Advantage: A Dynamic Capabilities Perspective

Yulia Sullivan,<sup>1</sup> Samuel Fosso Wamba,<sup>2</sup> Mary Dunaway<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Baylor University, USA, yulia\_sullivan@baylor.edu <sup>2</sup>TBS Business School, France, s.fosso-wamba@tbs-education.fr <sup>3</sup>Morgan State University, USA, mary.dunaway@morgan.edu

## Abstract

Internet of Things (IoT) is one of the enabling technologies of Industry 4.0. Although IoT has shown great promise for organizations, its practical use in generating value alone or in combination with existing IT capabilities remains unclear. Drawing upon the literature on dynamic capabilities and innovation capability, we propose IoT-enabled innovation capability (IoT-IC), consisting of three dimensions (IoT use for sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring) to explain competitive advantage. We examine three ordinary IT capabilities (flexible IT infrastructure, IT business experience, and relationship infrastructure) as the predictors of IoT-IC. We also test the moderating effect of competitive aggressiveness in influencing the relationship between IoT-IC and competitive advantage. Analysis of survey data from 175 U.S. companies provides empirical support for our research model. The results suggest that flexible IT infrastructure, IT business experience, and relationship infrastructure are positively associated with IoT-IC. Furthermore, the effect of IoT-IC on competitive advantage is positively moderated by competitive aggressiveness. The results point to the important role of existing IT capabilities in shaping IoT-IC and expand our understanding of the relationship between IoT-IC and competitive advantage at different levels of competitive aggressiveness.

Keywords: Internet of Things, IoT-Enabled Innovation Capability, Competitive Advantage, Dynamic Capabilities, Competitive Aggressiveness

## 1 Introduction

Internet of Things (IoT), a system characterized by the interconnection of everyday objects, is considered one of the pillars of Industry 4.0 (Ancarani et al., 2019; Bai et al., 2020). In fact, the IoT industry is expected to be worth US\$3 trillion by 2025, with over 27 billion heterogeneous things connected to the internet (Hoffman & Novak, 2018). This projection indicates that IoT is one of the biggest information technology (IT) transformation initiatives of all time (Adamopoulos et al., 2021). The benefits of IoT include enabling innovation (Frank et al., 2019) and facilitating connectivity among various value chain activities (Ancarani et al., 2019). As a result, organizations continue to invest in IoT to improve their existing operations and enable transformative changes in their businesses (Gartner, 2018; Koh et al., 2019).

Although a recent industry survey indicates that the adoption of industrial IoT has tripled in the last two years (Morton et al., 2020), reports estimate that nearly 75% of IoT projects fail (The IoT Magazine, 2020). Unlike other types of systems that have been adopted in organizations, IoT imposes its own unique challenges. From a technical perspective, IoT consists of more than a single system. Many devices and resources in IoT are highly distributed. Whereas legacy systems (e.g., the internet and the web) have been designed primarily for specific purposes with limited flexibility (Barnaghi et al., 2012), IoT requires a new platform and the integration of several subsystems to facilitate the addition of various utilities to the system, such as sensing, identification, communication, and management (Čolaković & Hadžialić, 2018). Moreover, IoT is considered a complex system (Benbya et al., 2020; Tarafdar & Tanriverdi, 2018) that requires the integration of physical (e.g., devices, sensors) and digital objects (e.g., software, protocol, data) (Baiyere et al., 2020; Faulkner & Runde 2019). IoT introduces multiple types of heterogeneity into the system by bringing together a variety of devices as well as various stakeholders (Baiyere et al., 2020; Whitmore et al., 2015). Thus, creating value from IoT is a huge challenge (Saarikko et al., 2020); if it is not conducted strategically, a total failure of the entire business model will occur. This challenge has yet to be encountered in traditional IT systems (e.g., De Cremer et al., 2017; Porter & Heppelmann, 2014).

Further, meeting all the technical requirements needed to develop IoT is not sufficient to create business value from IoT (Baiyere et al., 2020; Barnaghi et al., 2012). Anecdotal evidence shows that many organizations have failed to comprehend the potential gains from IoT despite meeting technical requirements (Adamopoulos et al., 2021; Ancarani et al., 2019; Saarikko et al., 2020). Although IoT supports digitalization, the digitalized processes empowered by IoT exhibit continuous changes and adaptation, which, if not managed properly, can lead to unexpected outcomes (Benbya et al., 2020; Pentland et al., 2020). To avoid such outcomes, organizations need to understand how to use IoT to enable dynamic capabilities. However, despite the need for IS research to examine how organizations can gain value from IoT, current research does not answer how IoT will generate value alone or in combination with existing IT capabilities. Although organizations generally agree on the positive relationship between IoT use and performance outcomes (e.g., Côrte-Real et al., 2020; Lee and Lee, 2015), insufficient knowledge persists concerning how organizations can strategically leverage IoT for competitive advantage. Therefore, the underlying mechanisms that lead to organizations’ IoT capability building, as well as organizational outcomes of such capability building, deserve close investigation.

The main objective of the current study is to examine how organizations can leverage IoT to generate value.

According to the IT management literature, one way that emerging technology such as IoT can create value is to enable innovation<sup>1</sup> (Akhtar et al., 2018; Mamonov & Peterson, 2021; Saldanha et al., 2020; Yoo, 2010). According to the dynamic capabilities perspective, effective pursuit of innovation depends, in particular, on the firm’s ability to sense opportunities (e.g., identifying customer needs and technological possibilities), seize them (e.g., selecting and responding to those opportunities), and reconfigure its resources (e.g., recombining and reallocating resources as the firm grows) (Guidici et al., 2018; Rothaermel & Hess, 2007; Teece et al., 1997; Teece, 2012). Organizations possessing these three capacities can integrate their key resources and stimulate innovation (Lawson & Samson, 2001). Drawing upon dynamic capabilities theory, our study proposes IoT-enabled innovation capability (IoT-IC), which refers to a firm’s ability to use and leverage IoT to sense opportunities for innovation, select and seize innovative ideas for further detailing, and modify/coordinate resource allocation activities to successfully stimulate innovation. We assert that developing IoT-IC can help firms create competitive advantage through the identification, data capture, and processing abilities of IoT.

In addition to examining the effect of IoT-IC on competitive advantage, firms must understand what types of existing IT capabilities contribute to the formation of IoT-IC. As markets emerge, existing resources or capabilities can decay. One way to cope with the potential of losing value from existing resources is to leverage ordinary IT resources and capabilities to build new, higher-order capabilities (i.e., IoT-IC) (Karadag & Poppo, in press). In this study, we focus on the role of three ordinary IT capabilities (flexible IT infrastructure, IT business experience, and relationship infrastructure) in influencing IoT-IC. We assert that IoT-IC is a higherorder capability—i.e., it is more than the sum of individual IT systems and is directed toward strategic change—that can be established by leveraging existing IT capabilities.

Further, with rapid technological changes and increases in rivalry, competitive advantage is temporary (Chen et al., 2021; Sirmon et al., 2010). With IoT emerging as a strategic differentiator, organizations need to understand how the interplay between firm competitiveness and IoT-IC can help them achieve competitive advantage. Given that competitive advantage is temporary, it demands action aggressiveness if a firm is to succeed (Carnes et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2010). We demonstrate that organizations need competitive aggressiveness as a strategic choice to secure competitive advantage through the use of IoT. Therefore, we also examine the effect of IoT-IC on competitive advantage, considering the moderating effect of competitive aggressiveness. To this end, we test our research model using survey data provided by business leaders from 175 US-based companies that have adopted IoT solutions in their businesses.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 Internet of Things: Overview

IoT consists of a variety of things/objects that can interact and cooperate with each other through wireless and wired connections to create new applications/services and reach common goals (Vermesan et al., 2017). In general, IoT consists of four main components (objects, network, data/information, and user), and these components typically define IoT. As an object, IoT is a technology that enables physical and digital objects to interact with one another (Al-Fuqaha et al., 2015; Baiyere et al., 2020). As a network, it is viewed as a “technology infrastructure” in which users are not seen as key participants in an integrated system (Baxter & Sommerville, 2011). As data and information, it includes data analytics tools and data processing solutions that can be shared in a ubiquitous network (e.g., Chin et al., 2019; Sousa & Rocha, 2019; Yang et al., 2017). Lastly, the user component of IoT places human users at the center of the system, where they perform active roles in their operations (Conti et al., 2017; Yoo, 2010).

Combining these four elements into a conceptualization of IoT leads us to the sociotechnical perspective of IoT. This perspective focuses on the relationship between users and the system and how this relationship can bring value to organizations (Baiyere et al., 2020; Faulkner & Runde, 2019). Consistent with the sociotechnical perspective, we define IoT as a system that contains ubiquitous everyday objects (i.e., physical and digital) designed to deliver high-level functionalities within an organization. Such functionalities are delivered through deep analysis and data analytics where objects embedded in the system can both sense the environment and communicate to each other with or without human intervention. In an IoT environment, some parts of the system are intelligent and agentive since they can observe and interpret stimuli from the environment and also have connections and mutual dependencies with other parts. These interactions promote continuous change and adaptation (Benbya et al., 2020), making IoT a complex, sociotechnical system encompassing an irreducible type of uncertainty (Tarafdar & Tanriverdi, 2018).

## 2.2 Organizational Capabilities

An organizational capability is “a set of current or potential activities that utilize the firm’s productive resources to make and/or deliver products and services” (Teece, 2014, p. 328). Two important categories of capability are ordinary and dynamic. Ordinary capabilities enable a firm to perform an activity on an ongoing basis using relatively similar techniques (Danneels, 2016; Helfat & Winter, 2011; Mikalef & Pateli, 2017). Ordinary capabilities include achieving technical efficiency and “doing things right” in core business functions (Teece, 2014, p. 331). In contrast, dynamic capabilities involve high-level activities that enable organizations to direct their ordinary activities toward high-payoff endeavors (Teece, 2007, 2014).

Teece et al. (1997) introduced the idea of dynamic capabilities as a framework to explain firms’ innovation capability and how this approach is made into a cornerstone of a firm’s long-term competitive advantage (Guidici et al., 2018). Dynamic capabilities enable firms to not only invent but also innovate profitably (Teece, 2007). The following three capacities underlie the effective pursuit of entrepreneurial innovation: (1) identification and assessment of opportunities (sensing); (2) response to such opportunities (seizing); and (3) recombination, coordination, and reallocation of assets in relation to the pursued identified opportunities (reconfiguring). Given that these three capabilities are interrelated, an organization will need to develop and apply them simultaneously to build competitive advantage (Teece, 2007).

In addition to revealing those three capacities, prior research has also identified specific types of firms dynamic capabilities. These capabilities include acquisition (e.g., Heimeriks et al., 2012), alliance (e.g., Kale & Singh, 2007; Schreiner et al., 2009), new product development (e.g., Pavlou & El-Sawy, 2010), organizational speed (Dykes et al., 2019), capital allocation competency (e.g., Arrfelt et al., 2015), information-gathering and information-processing capability (e.g., Sleptsov & Anand, 2008), knowledge creation (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000), resource allocation/integration capability (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000; Lovallo et al., 2020), and strategic decisionmaking (e.g., Eisenhardt, 1989). Although these specific dynamic capabilities are usually studied independently and in isolation, many of them are related to sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring capacities (Steininger et al., 2022). For example, information-gathering and information-processing capabilities are related to sensing (Sleptsov & Anand, 2008), and alliance and acquisition cover the gain and release of resources (i.e., seizing and reconfiguring) (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000).

## 2.3 IoT-Enabled Innovation Capability

IS research has drawn upon the stream of dynamic capabilities to explain how organizations can create value from IT (e.g., Anand et al., 2020; Castillo et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2021; Kathuria et al., 2018; Khuntia et al., 2021; Kulkarni et al., 2017; Mikalef et al., 2021; Park & Mithas, 2020). Although prior IS research has operationalized the relationship between IT and dynamic capabilities in various ways (e.g., IT as a tool, IT as an antecedent to dynamic capabilities), scholars need to conceptualize IT as embedded in dynamic capabilities (Steininger et al., 2022). Such conceptualization can better demonstrate that IT strategies are a part of businesses’ core competencies (Steininger et al., 2022), an idea consistent with the common view that the use of IT is considered a means to achieve one or more organizational capabilities (Castillo et al., 2021; Mamonov & Peterson, 2021; Wang et al., 2012). Further, according to Strininger et al. (2022), the conceptualization of IT-embedded dynamic capabilities must include any of the three capabilities (sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring). Following Strininger et al.’s recommendation, we conceptualize IoT as embedded in the three main dynamic capabilities mechanisms— sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring—to enable the firm’s innovation capability.

Innovation capability<sup>2</sup> is particularly relevant in the IoT context. IoT offers a series of new opportunities for innovation and provides decision makers with information access that they can use to respond quickly to environmental changes (Faulkner & Runde, 2019; Saarikko et al., 2020). Further, unlike other Industry 4.0 technologies, IoT is a new type of IS that can connect physical and digital objects to achieve a specific business goal (Shi et al., 2021). It streamlines operations in a new way, leading to a new approach to innovation (Tarafdar & Tanriverdi, 2018). IoT is also considered an adaptive system (Pentland et al., 2020). Adaptive systems are more prone to transformative changes than systems with deterministic programming. Although changes do not always lead to innovation, innovation is “a means of changing an organization,” whether as a response to changes in its environment or as a preemptive action taken to influence the environment (Damanpour, 1991, p. 556). Thus, viewing IoT from the innovation system perspective can provide organizations with opportunities to learn, adapt, and leverage digitalization.

To gain a better understanding of what constitutes innovation capability, we reviewed previous studies on innovation capability from various perspectives (see Appendix A). Our review was not meant to be exhaustive or systematic but rather reflective of previous work that highlights characteristics of innovation capability. In addition to adopting the dynamic capabilities perspective, we found that prior literature has primarily used one of the following theoretical lenses: (1) strategic management view, (2) resource-based view (RBV), and (3) knowledge-based view. The strategic management view of innovation capability explores “a firm’s ability” to introduce new products, services, or processes (e.g., Adler & Sherban, 1990; Akman & Yilmaz, 2008; Miller & Friesen, 1983). Innovation capability is viewed as an important factor that can facilitate a firm’s success. RBV defines innovation capability more specifically than the strategic management view. According to RBV, innovation capability depends on a firm’s resources and competencies (e.g., Yam et al., 2004, 2011). Similarly, some reviewed papers were derived from the knowledgebased view, where knowledge is considered the core source of innovation capability (e.g., Kogut & Zander, 1992). The development of RBV has greatly influenced scholars in studying innovation capability from the dynamic capabilities perspective. Although these three theoretical lenses seem different, they share some commonalities with innovation characteristics specified by dynamic capabilities. In particular, the dynamic capabilities perspective explicitly regards the importance of resources for innovation and innovation capability as a mechanism to achieve a desired future for the organization (Lawson & Samson, 2001). Thus, we argue that the dynamic capabilities perspective offers the best approach to study IoT-IC.

Innovation capability has long been associated with dynamic capabilities (e.g., Swink & Hegarty, 1998). Although dynamic capabilities involve more than just innovation capability, we argue that innovation capability is one of the central entities of dynamic capabilities (Lawson & Samson, 2001; Strønen et al., 2017). According to the dynamic capabilities perspective, entrepreneurial innovation is about “sensing and understanding opportunities, getting things started, and finding new and better ways of putting things together. It is about creatively coordinating the assembly of disparate and usually cospecialized elements, getting ‘approvals for nonroutine activities, and sensing business opportunities” (Teece, 2007, p. 1346). By viewing innovation capability as a dynamic capability, we emphasize the importance of mechanisms in achieving innovation or “an ability to act” through purposeful processes, actions, and resource-related capabilities (Daronco et al., 2023).

Specifically, we conceptualize IoT-enabled innovation capability as a firm’s ability to use and leverage IoT to sense opportunities for innovation, select and seize innovative ideas for further detailing, and modify/coordinate (i.e., reconfigure) resource allocation activities to successfully stimulate innovative actions. Our conceptualization of IoT-IC addresses the interdependence nature of these three capabilities, underscoring the importance of “bundling” activities or “combinative” capabilities to gain competitive advantage (Karadag & Poppo, in press; Kogut & Zander, 1992). Table 1 summarizes our conceptualization of IoT use for sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring, along with the relevant references from innovation research that links each dimension to innovation (see Appendix A for details).

First, IoT-IC concerns the use of IoT for sensing— capabilities to detect opportunities that may lead to innovations (Helfat & Raubitschek, 2018). The importance of sensing for innovation capability has been established in prior studies (e.g., Adler & Shenbar, 1990; Akman & Yilmaz, 2008; Den Hertog et al., 2010). Innovation capability involves the capacity to identify problems (Swink & Hagerty, 1998), capture needs (Strønen et al., 2017), monitor environments (Shi et al., 2021), and apply the ideation process (Daronco et al., 2023). Accordingly, the sensing function of IoT comprehends efforts toward the identification of innovation opportunities through its ability to detect changes and collect real-time data using smart sensors (Perera et al., 2014). The interconnectivity of IoT is essential to the identification and evaluation of innovation opportunities since it can establish a new mechanism for organizational intelligence, allowing firms to search for novel solutions to business problems. As Teece (2007) suggested, such a mechanism involves the ability to collect, filter, and evaluate technology and market information so that firms can extract useful meaning from the data.

Second, IoT-IC concerns the use of IoT for seizing— capabilities that respond to problems and support decisions and business changes necessary for innovations (Schilke et al., 2018). Seizing refers to the activities that focus on implementing opportunities identified through sensing. According to the innovation literature, these activities include responding to problems or threats, changing or modifying business practices as an action to influence an environment (Damanpour, 1991), evaluating and selecting new ideas, and applying new methods to solve problems (Swink & Hagerty, 1998). The synergy of the access and potential data exchange opens huge new possibilities for IoT applications (Vermesan et al., 2017). With such applications, organizations can create new business processes or make existing ones more highly efficient and profitable. Further, with IoT data captured and analyzed in real time, firms can improve their decision-making speed and responsiveness to events inside as well as outside of organizations (Krotov, 2017).

Third, IoT-IC concerns the use of IoT for reconfiguring—capacities used to support coordination activities directed toward resource modification and reallocation (Helfat & Raubitschek, 2018). Numerous studies have underscored the importance of the reconfiguring mechanism for innovation (e.g., Lawson & Samson, 2001; Rothaermel & Hess, 2007; Strønen et al., 2017). For example, Lawson and Samson (2001) noted that innovation capability relates to a firm’s ability to integrate key resources. Yam et al. (2004) specified resource allocation capability (i.e., a firm’s ability to acquire and allocate appropriate resources) as one of the key dimensions of technological innovation capabilities. Rothaermel and Hess (2007) also suggested that a firm’s ability to integrate, build, and reconfigure its competencies lies at the center of its capability to innovate. IoT changes the way organizations reconfigure their resources. It can even self-reconfigure various resources connected to the network (Adamopoulos et al., 2021), enabling organizations to reallocate resources and generate new and synergistic resource combinations among business functions.

## 3 Research Model and Development of Hypotheses

This section explains the research model (see Figure 1) and hypotheses. In this model, we identify three ordinary IT capabilities (flexible IT infrastructure, IT business experience, and relationship infrastructure) as the factors influencing IoT-IC and then examine the moderating effect of competitive aggressiveness on the relationship between IoT-IC and competitive advantage. Recent work in the stream of strategic management has demonstrated that achieving sustained competitive advantage is rare, if even possible, and often operates only for short periods of time (e.g., Dagnino et al., 2021; Sirmon et al., 2010). Thus, we view competitive advantage as a temporary competitive advantage, defined as a firm’s attainment of its objectives in comparison with its competitors for a relatively short period of time—at least until rivals react and attempt to mount their own competitive responses (Ferrier, 2001; Ferrier et al., 1999).

We conceptualize IoT-IC as a second-order formative construct formed by the use of IoT for sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring routines based on two reasons: First, prior studies have underscored the importance of understanding innovation capability as a higher-order integration capability (Lawson & Samson, 2001). The dynamic capabilities perspective itself acknowledges that although each capability is valuable in its own right, having a strong capability in all areas will likely lead an organization to gain competitive advantage (Teece, 2007). Sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring are interrelated but not interchangeable (Fainshmidt et al., 2019). Second, according to the theory of supermodularity (Milgrom & Roberts, 1990), “a combination of activities or resources of firms might be more valuable than in isolation” (Schweiger et al., 2019, p. 1827). Therefore, we argue that the synergism among IoT use for sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring causes organizations to form IoT-IC.

## 3.1 Flexible IT Infrastructure, IT-Business Experience, and Relationship Infrastructure as the Antecedents to IoT-IC

As mentioned previously, the literature has suggested that dynamic capabilities operate in a hierarchical structure in which existing, ordinary capabilities lead to higher-order capabilities (Irfan et al., 2019; Mikalef & Pateli, 2017; Svahn et al., 2017; Winter, 2003, 2008). In this study, we conceptualize IoT-IC as a higher-order capability in which its construction is determined by three IT ordinary capabilities: (1) (flexible) IT infrastructure, (2) IT business experience, and (3) relational infrastructure (e.g., Wang et al., 2012). These three capabilities are ordinary because they can span across organizational boundaries (Mandrella et al., 2020) but cannot create value by themselves (Kathuria et al., 2018). For example, human resources, such as IT teams with management skills and relationship infrastructure, are needed to develop a system. Still, they do not directly alter the way a firm currently conducts business. IT infrastructure is also an ordinary capability because it performs basic functional activities (e.g., communication network, shared databases). Flexible IT infrastructure serves to build distinctive IT capabilities within an organization (Aral & Weil, 2007; Irfan et al., 2019; Sabherwal & Jayaraj, 2015). As such, the term is not sufficient to explain firm success (Irfan et al., 2019; Kathuria et al., 2018). On the contrary, IoT-IC focuses on the mechanisms by which firms use IoT to explore and address opportunities for innovation, making it a dynamic capability.

Further, according to the strategic resource decay (SRD) perspective, existing capabilities can degrade and require constant maintenance (Karadag & Poppo, in press). For example, IT infrastructure can degrade as technology changes, IT-business experience can decay when individual employees leave the company or when a new business skill is required to perform a specific process, and relationship infrastructure can lose its value when more tasks are automated. Firms can minimize the threat posed by SRD by leveraging the existing capabilities to create a new, higher-order capability (Karadag & Poppo, in press). In our study, we argue that IT infrastructure, IT-business experience, and relationship infrastructure can be used to support IoT-IC. Next, we justify the relevance of each capability in supporting IoT-IC.

## 3.1.1 Flexible IT Infrastructure and IoT-IC

Organizations need a flexible IT infrastructure to establish IoT-IC. We define a flexible IT infrastructure as the extent to which “the IT infrastructure is scalable, compatible, modular, and can handle multiple business applications” (Bhatt et al., 2010, p. 342). According to Kim et al. (2012), a flexible IT infrastructure enables IT teams to develop necessary applications, create effective information- and data-sharing channels across business units, and launch systems that reinforce functional integration.

Without a flexible IT infrastructure already in place, establishing IoT-IC would be difficult, if even possible. The characteristics of flexible IT infrastructure (e.g., IT compatibility, connectivity, and modularity) allow firms to share any type of information across any IT components through the firm, enable IT components to communicate with any other IT components, and reconfigure or reuse any IT components with ease and without any major overall effects (Benitez et al., 2018). IoT use for sensing requires a convergence of multiple technology devices to create an open, global network connecting people, data, and things (Vermesan et al., 2017). This convergence leverages a flexible IT infrastructure (e.g., IT connectivity to connect intelligent things that sense and transmit a broad array of data), allowing firms to scan and monitor their environment. For example, a flexible wireless connection connecting both mobile and fixed devices to the network is required to build Intelligent Products, such as smart packaging (Porter & Heppelmann, 2014). A smart package in the food industry can be equipped with a label that offers visual indications of temperature history during distribution and storage. It can also incorporate biosensors that detect, record, and transmit information about biochemical reactions in order to ensure food safety. The data access and interconnections of objects in the network allow rapid response and real-time analytics usage for new idea identification (Caputo et al., 2016).

IoT use for seizing also requires a flexible IT infrastructure. An IT infrastructure that is compatible and can handle multiple applications provides a solid foundation on which to execute various IoT functions (e.g., systems that enable things to acquire contextual information and process contextual information), providing decision-makers with the ability to turn realtime data into the actionable insights they need to select the best innovative ideas. A flexible IT infrastructure also allows firms to reallocate and coordinate their resources (i.e., reconfiguring) (Bi et al., 2016). With a high level of IT infrastructure connectivity, the deployed objects and integration of new devices in an IoT platform can discover resources automatically with the least amount of human intervention (Li & Xu, 2021). For example, firms can utilize an optimization selection mechanism based on real-time IoT data to determine the most profitable business models (Li & Xu, 2021). With a modular IT infrastructure (i.e., reusing and recombining IT components), firms can then accommodate various devices and services that may enter or exit the system (Fernandez-Gago et al., 2017) and coordinate the compatibility between tasks, people, and technology objects in an effective and simple way (Ghanbari et al., 2019). Thus, a flexible IT infrastructure is positively associated with IoT-IC.

H1: Flexible IT infrastructure is positively associated with IoT-IC.

## 3.1.2 IT-Business Experience and IoT-IC

IT-business experience can positively influence IoT-IC. We define IT business experience as the extent to which IT teams understand business, such as having the ability to interpret business problems and develop appropriate IT solutions (Bhatt & Grover, 2005). IT teams utilize their technological skills to develop software systems, and business-domain skills enable them to modify systems and tailor them to fit a business environment (Chatterjee, 2017). Prior to IoT, the scope of an IT unit’s involvement in innovation processes was typically limited to support roles (e.g., providing business units with computing and collaboration infrastructure) (Tarafdar & Tanriverdi, 2018). This scope expands as firms bring IoT to their business operations. Unlike other types of IT implementations that existed primarily at the individual firm level, IoT systems span across IT and core business functions (Kranz, 2017; Shi et al., 2021). As firms utilize IoT, their IT teams begin to cover the early stages of innovation activities (e.g., initiating changes, matching ideas to potential business opportunities). Thus, organizations must increasingly depend on their IT teams to optimize the use of IoT for sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring activities.

IT-business experience can enhance the use of IoT for sensing through appropriate designs and development of sensing applications (Saarikko et al., 2017). Firms employing IT groups with business experience understand how to simplify IoT configurations to accommodate user needs. For instance, assume that an IT team works on smart cars that are equipped with sensors to capture information about the cars themselves. An IT team with business experience can optimize the sensing capability by expanding a range of IoT sensors to gather data about road and traffic conditions, knowing that this data may lead to a new business opportunity. In short, IT teams who are knowledgeable about business opportunities can quickly spot relevant opportunities from the vast data produced by IoT (Gilchrist, 2016; Marr, 2017).

The use of IoT for seizing also requires IT-business experience. Although IoT practices can broaden the number of opportunities, organizations need to ensure that their IT teams can accommodate and respond to such opportunities. Developing innovation assets requires technical and managerial skills to improve existing technology or to create new technology (Romijn & Albaladejo, 2022). For example, IT teams with high business skills (e.g., understanding what causes a firm to grow, whether IoT practices are heading in the right direction, whether installing a sensor to gather specific data is permissible according to business policies) can develop core metrics to be used in selecting the best innovative ideas (Marr, 2017).

Lastly, IT-business experience can also facilitate the right flows of action to organizations, enabling them to mobilize resources and continually combine resources in an entrepreneurial fashion (Kranz, 2017). Although IoT generates output in terms of knowledge that is later presented to users, a flexible flow of information and knowledge depends on the ability of IT teams to communicate such information in a business context. Without proper support from an experienced IT team, companies may overspend on or underinvest in the wrong project because they lack strategic design thinking (Matthyssens, 2019). Thus, we hypothesize that IT-business experience is positively associated with IoT-IC.

H2: IT-business experience is positively associated with IoT-IC.

## 3.1.3 Relationship Infrastructure and IoT-IC

Relationship infrastructure can also positively affect IoT-IC. We define relationship infrastructure as the extent to which “there are positive relationships between IT and management teams” (Bhatt & Grover, 2005, p. 260). A strong relationship infrastructure (e.g., between change agents, such as senior managers and data scientists) could help cue the firm to potential environmental shifts and direct it toward promising innovative ideas. A strong relationship between IT and business teams can create a new routine of searching for new IT and business opportunities that can be stored and shared in the IoT network. By combining and leveraging cross-functional team knowledge, firms can engage in broader sensing activities that are harder to handle using stand-alone IoT devices only (Hamrouni et al., 2021).

Relationship infrastructure also guides the use of IoT for seizing. Rapid detection of changes enabled by IoT requires firms to accelerate innovation (Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2012). The IT alignment literature supports the idea that a flexible and trustworthy interaction between IT and business teams can accommodate such rapid innovation cycles. According to this literature, a strong relationship infrastructure can help firms more easily communicate business changes and quickly respond to those changes (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). In this way, the alignment between business and IT can enable increased adaptiveness and innovation. To the same extent, the collaboration between IT and business teams enhances the firm’s value, capturing skills consisting of unpredicted combinations of products, objects, systems, services, and information (Matthyssens, 2019). Further, research on the microfoundations of innovation (Salvato & Vassolo, 2018) reveals that focusing on “embedded relationships” among individuals in organizations can ease resource coordination, which is an essential component of IoT use for reconfiguring. Therefore, we hypothesize that relationship infrastructure is positively associated with IoT-IC.

H3: Relationship infrastructure is positively associated with IoT-IC.

## 3.2 IoT-Enabled Innovation Capability and Competitive Advantage

IoT-IC influences competitive advantage in multiple ways. First, it enables organizations to scan their environments for competitive ideas (i.e., to use IoT for sensing). In light of uncertainty, organizations need to simultaneously innovate in order to combat environmental threats and take advantage of emerging opportunities (Helfat & Raubitschek, 2018; Teece, 2007, 2018; Teece et al., 1997). IoT use for sensing allows organizations to monitor the behaviors of persons, objects, and data in the network, enabling them to derive insights about changes in the environment (Krotov, 2017; Sousa & Rocha, 2019). Whereas individual decision makers are bound to cognitive limitations (Sleptsov & Anand, 2008), IoT can help firms overcome information overload by uncovering patterns from large volumes of data that help organizations identify threats in the market and generate innovative business ideas (Côrte-Real et al. 2020; Whitmore et al., 2015).

Second, IoT-IC enhances competitive advantage by aiding decision-making processes (i.e., IoT use for seizing) (Sestino et al., 2020). To gain competitive advantage, firms need to bring new products or services to the market and engage in new practices (Schweiger et al., 2019). By leveraging IoT, organizations can improve the understanding of business opportunities, reducing the uncertainty associated with them. The ability to link humans, objects, and data in a network is a new complementary strength with improved problem-solving capabilities. Such capabilities transform the old ways of doing business, ensuring continuous growth and competitive advantage (Sestino et al., 2020). For example, the business models of water pump manufacturers formerly revolved around selling pumps. Now, using IoT, firms can also monitor the health conditions of pumps. A new business model focusing on asset monitoring solutions instead of hard sales is possible only because of IoT.

The use of IoT for seizing also allows organizations to plan more effective strategies (Porter & Heppelmann, 2014). IoT systems are knowledge-intensive systems that require a collection of appropriate data analysis and reporting (Brous et al., 2019). By leveraging the predictive analytics of big data brought by IoT, decision makers can select the best innovative ideas at the right time. Firms that utilize such knowledgeintensive systems can better grasp the problems to which the new knowledge can be applied to improve their business processes (Trantopoulos et al., 2017). IoT also provides access to multiple viewpoints and brings diverse perspectives to the decision-making process, enabling a better evaluation and choice of ideas that can better position firms to enhance their competitive advantage.

Lastly, IoT-IC can lead to improved competitive advantage by enabling firms to coordinate their resources and capabilities. The integration of technology devices in IoT can automatically discover resources with minimal human intervention as the enterprise grows (Li & Xu, 2021). With the help of IoT, data and information can be processed immediately from a single digital ecosystem (Nagy et al., 2018). Thus, managers can coordinate various activities involving people, machines, and data as efficiently as possible (Nagy et al., 2018; Mithas et al., 2017). Knowing how to integrate different activities and capabilities allows companies to make necessary adjustments as they introduce innovations (Helfat & Raubitschek, 2018). IoT, then, provides much richer insights into the context of how individuals and organizations innovate with products and services over time, potentially helping to identify unmet needs in the market (Hagel III, 2014). In turn, it helps organizations gain competitive advantage (Côrte-Real et al., 2020). Thus, we hypothesize that IoT-IC is instrumental in deriving organizational competitive advantage.

H4: IoT-IC is positively associated with a firm’s competitive advantage.

## 3.3 The Moderating Role of Competitive Aggressiveness

As competition becomes increasingly intense and dynamic, firms become more challenged to sustain advantages (Chen et al., 2010; D’Aveni, 1994). Under a dynamic environment, firms can benefit from competitive aggressiveness (i.e., aggressively carrying out competitive actions) (Chen et al., 2010). Aggressive firms are likely to move quickly to build advantages and erode the advantage of their rivals (D’Aveni, 1994; Levine et al., 2017). Given that IoT exhibits changes and adaptation (Benbya et al., 2020; Tarafdar & Tanriverdi, 2018), aggressive firms may have better opportunities to create a string of actions that cumulatively lead to competitive advantage.

Despite disagreement among some researchers regarding the effectiveness of competitive aggressiveness as a business strategy, we posit that such a strategic choice will provide greater opportunity for firms that capitalize on IoT. Prior research has shown that competitive aggressiveness can either enhance or destroy firms’ most critical resources (Andrevski & Ferrier, 2019). We argue that the influence of IoT-IC on competitive advantage is highly likely to be amplified if firms are competitively aggressive. This argument is based on extant research suggesting that firms create, acquire, and leverage their capabilities to achieve competitive advantage. Once they have developed such capabilities, they are more likely to try to defend them (Lumpkin & Dess, 2001). We contend that the creation of capabilities represents IoT-IC, whereas the defense of existing capabilities is achieved by a competitively aggressive approach. Firms utilize IoT to sense, seize, and transform resources, and competitive aggressiveness drives them to respond to competitive demands that already exist in the marketplace. As IoT makes firms more innovative and flexible, adopting a competitive aggressive approach will help them undertake a series of actions to recreate competitive advantage.

The moderating role of competitive aggressiveness in affecting the relationship between IoT-IC and competitive advantage can also be explained from the dynamic capabilities perspective itself. Dynamic capabilities rely less on existing knowledge and considerably more on the rapid creation of new knowledge (Chen et al., 2015). IoT-IC increases organizations’ capacity to discover new knowledge and insights. Aggressive firms can quickly use such knowledge and insights and transform them into actions to improvise, probe the future, and gain hands-on knowledge (Andrevski & Ferrier, 2019). Hence, competitive aggressiveness enhances the positive relationship between IoT-DC and competitive advantage.

H5: Competitive aggressiveness positively moderates the relationship between IoT-IC and competitive advantage. Specifically, the positive relationship between IoT-IC and competitive advantage is stronger when a firm’s competitive aggressiveness is high.

## 4 Research Method

## 4.1 Measurement Development

Table 2 summarizes the operationalization of the study’s principal constructs. The exact measurement items are shown in Appendix B. All items were measured on a 7- point Likert scale, drawn from previous literature, and adapted to serve the purpose of the current study. We conceptualize IoT-IC as a formative construct, consistent with Jarvis et al.’s (2003) criteria. First, IoT use for sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring jointly shape IoT-IC. Second, they are distinct and not interchangeable. Third, they do not need to have covariation. For example, an increase in IoT use for sensing does not necessarily mean an increase in IoT use for seizing. Finally, each type of IoT use has different antecedents and consequences. For example, a direct investment in IoT research and development may influence IoT use for sensing, whereas enterprise boundaries may influence IoT use for seizing.

We controlled for various firm characteristics, including firm size, firm age, and industry type. We also controlled for the IoT use level because it could signal the organization’s readiness to build and move toward more sophisticated IoT projects (Ancarani et al., 2019). Although Teece (2017) suggests that dynamic capabilities can be built throughout a system’s lifecycle, we expect that the longer IoT is used, the higher accumulative knowledge and routines an organization has to establish its dynamic capabilities (Teece, 2012). To measure the IoT use level, we included the most common IoT systems currently used in the market and asked respondents to rate the usage level of each system in their organizations. We summarize our control variables in Appendix C.

The initial measurement items were reviewed by a group of IS scholars. Based on their comments, some adjustments were made to the questionnaire. A pilot study was conducted to evaluate the meaning, ordering, and representation of the scales. We recruited Amazon’s Mechanical Turk IT workers in the US to participate in the pilot study. Thirty-seven workers completed the study and, based on the results, several items were reviewed and modified. After the revision, all the measurement items were deemed adequate and used in the main survey.

## 4.2 Data Collection Procedures

We collected data by administering a web-based survey. To ensure data quality, we contacted a market research company (Research Now) to access its prequalified base of business panel members in the US.<sup>3</sup> Our sampling frame was the US business panel members of Research Now that have used IoT in their business operations. Since the North American region is one of the largest contributors in the IoT market (Statista, 2022), our target population is suitable for the study. As is common in organizational-level research, the survey instructions asked the IT decision-makers (e.g., CIO, IT managers, IT project leaders) to respond based on their self-judgment about IoT usage in their organizations (relative to that of their competitors). Respondents were first asked to identify the types of IoT projects that have been adopted by their organizations and the status of these projects. Those who worked in organizations with low or no levels of IoT use were eliminated from the study. Only participants who rated their firms’ IoT use as 4, 5, and 6 (on at least one IoT solution listed in the survey) on a scale of 1-6 (see Table 3) were included in the final analysis.

## 5 Data Analysis and Results

## 5.1 Assessment of Measurement Model

We tested the measurement model using the partial least squares-structural equation model (PLS-SEM). SmartPLS (Ringle et al., 2022) was used for the estimations. PLS was deemed appropriate for the study because of the existence of a second-order formative construct and moderating effects (Chin, 1998; Hair et al., 2019). A bootstrapping technique with 5000 resamplings was used to estimate the significance of the indicator loadings.

We validated the reflective constructs using guidelines suggested by Fornell and Larcker (1981) and Gefen and Straub (2005). Table 4 presents the descriptive statistics and correlations among the principal constructs. For the reflective constructs, convergence and discriminant validity were first tested. Composite reliability (CR) scores for the final reflectively measured scales ranged from 0.80 to 0.96, exceeding the 0.707 recommended by guidelines. As seen in Table 4, the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct exceeds the construct’s correlations with other constructs, demonstrating the discriminant validity of the principal constructs. The confirmatory factor analysis shows that the indicators loaded more strongly on their corresponding constructs than on other constructs in the model and were at least 0.10 less on their loadings on other constructs (Gefen & Straub, 2005) (see Appendix D). We also used the heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) ratio of the correlations (Henseler et al., 2015) to assess the discriminant validity. The HTMT refers to “the mean value of the item correlations across constructs relative to the (geometric) mean of the average correlations for the items measuring the same construct” (Hair et al., 2019, p. 9). As summarized in Table 5, all HTMT ratios of the correlations were well below a threshold value of 0.90, suggesting an adequate level of discriminant validity.

IoT-IC, which is a formative construct, required different approaches for validation because the assessment of convergent validity is meaningless to a formative construct (Petter et al., 2007). PLS analysis showed that all the indicators of IoT-IC have significant weights, providing evidence for construct validity. The signs of the item weights were consistent with the underlying theory, and the magnitudes of the item weights were greater than 0.10. The average weight for the formative items was less than the ceiling of ${ \sqrt { \frac { 1 } { N } } } ,$ with N as the number of orthogonal formative

items specified (Kathuria et al., 2018). Further, one of the concerns about formatively measured constructs is multicollinearity across the formative indicators of each construct (Hair et al., 2019). We thus tested all constructs in the model for multicollinearity. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values of the formative dimensions of IoT use ranged from 2.16 to 2.27, way below the threshold of 3.3 suggested by Petter et al. (2007). This result suggests that no serious concerns occurred with multicollinearity in the data.

Common method bias was assessed through multiple approaches. First, we performed Harman’s singlefactor test with an unrotated factor analysis (Podsakoff et al., 2003). The first factor extracted only about 14% of the variance, indicating lack of common method bias. Second, the VIF scores at the factor ranged from 1.04 to 2.76 (lower than the recommended value of 3.3), suggesting the model is free of common method bias (Kock, 2015). Third, the correlations among focal constructs ranged from 0.46 to 0.71, which do not show any exceptionally high correlated variables. Fourth, we performed a partial correlation approach by partialling out of a general factor score (Podsakoff et al., 2003; Podsakoff & Todor, 1985) and followed the statistical steps suggested by Tehseen et al. (2017). We first computed the factor scores for all respondents and added these scores as a general factor construct in PLS. Adding this factor resulted in only a small increase in $R ^ { 2 } ( \Delta { \mathrm R } ^ { 2 }$ $= . 0 1 5 )$ ), suggesting that common method bias is not a threat in the study (see Podsakoff & Todor, 1985).

## 5.2 Structural Model

To test the structural model, we also used SmartPLS. We formed the second-order construct of IoT-IC and competitive advantage by generating factor scores for each of its first-order dimensions. These scores were then used as formative or reflective measures of the second-order aggregate constructs (see Polites & Karahanna, 2012). The results of the structural model are illustrated in Figure 2. We ran the analysis in three steps (see Table 5). In the first step (Model 1), we entered the control variables. In Model 2, we included the main effects to test H1, H2, H3, and H4. All direct relationships were significant, and the effects of control variables on both IoT-IC and competitive advantage were greatly attenuated by the main independent variables. Thus, H1, H2, H3, and H4 were supported.

In Model 3, we included the interaction term. As predicted, competitive aggressiveness positively moderated the relationship between IoT-IC and competitive advantage. Thus, H5 was supported. The moderating effect size is 0.10. According to Cohen (1988), this observed moderating effect is considered small. However, Aguinis et al. (2005) showed that the average effect size in tests of moderation is only 0.009. Thus, an effect size of greater than 0.025 is considered large (Kenny, 2015). The effect sizes of other significant relationships in the structural model is presented in Appendix E. In terms of $R ^ { 2 } .$ , our model explained a high amount of variance in IoT use $( \mathbf { R } ^ { 2 } =$ 65%) and competitive advantage $( R ^ { 2 } = 7 0 \% )$ .

We also tested the significance of indirect effects of IT infrastructure, IT-business experience, and relationship infrastructure on competitive advantage through IoT-IC. The summary of the direct and indirect effects of the mediating relationships is presented in Table 6. We observed no statistically significant relationships for the direct paths between ordinary IT capabilities and competitive advantage except between relationship infrastructure and competitive advantage. These results confirm our research model, in which ordinary IT capabilities indirectly influenced competitive advantage through IoT-IC.

## 5.3 Tests of Endogeneity

We accounted for endogeneity that could arise from the omitted variables in the PLS path as well as for potential sample-induced endogeneity because IoT-IC influences whether a firm appears in the sample (Hult et al., 2018). To do so, we followed several procedures to rule out potential threats of endogeneity. First, we implemented a Gaussian copulas approach (Park & Gupta, 2012), an instrument-free method that is increasingly popular in business disciplines (e.g., Gielens et al., 2018; Vomberg et al., 2020). Because measurement error (e.g., in the form of common method bias) can also cause endogeneity, Gaussian copulas serve as an additional remedy to alleviate common method variance (CMV) (Vomberg et al., 2020).

We included $I O T I C _ { i } ^ { c } = \varnothing ^ { - 1 } [ H _ { I O T I C } ( I O T I C _ { i } ) ]$ as an additional regressor in our model with competitive advantage as the dependent variable. We also included $F I T _ { i } ^ { c } = \varnothing ^ { - 1 } [ H _ { F I T } ( F I T _ { i } ) ]$ $I T B E _ { i } ^ { c } =$ $\emptyset ^ { - 1 } [ H _ { I T B E } ( I T B E _ { i } ) ]$ , and $R I _ { i } ^ { c } = \varnothing ^ { - 1 } [ H _ { R I } ( R I _ { i } ) ]$ in our model with IoT-IC as the dependent variables, where $\varnothing ^ { - 1 }$ is the inverse of the cumulative normal distribution function and $H _ { I O T I C } , H _ { F I T } , H _ { I T B E } ,$ , and $H _ { R I }$ denote the empirical distribution functions of IoT-IC, flexible IT infrastructure, IT-business experience, and relationship infrastructure, respectively. For identification, all variables must be nonnormally distributed (Hult et al., 2018). Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests for all endogenous regressors confirmed the nonnormal distribution (IoT-IC (SIoTIC = 0.09, p < 0.01; WIoTIC = 0.96, p < 0.001); flexible IT infrastructure (SFIT = 0.16, p < 0.001; WFIT = 0.98, p < 0.001); IT-business experience (SITBE = 0.16, $p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1$ ; WIoTIC = 0.89, p < 0.001); and relationship infrastructure (SRI = 0.18, $p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 0 1$ ; $\mathsf { W I o T I C } = 0 . 8 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . To determine whether endogeneity is at a critical level, we assessed the significance of the copula coefficients using bootstrapping (Hult et al., 2018). The endogeneitycorrected results in all the models (see Appendix F, Models 2 and 4) revealed no significant copula terms. Moreover, in the IoT-IC model, the relationship infrastructure’s estimate became insignificant. In this case, adding the copula terms led only to a more conservative estimate in which the direction of the estimated relationship does not change. Thus, endogeneity is unlikely to be an issue.

Second, we tested for the potential impact of an omitted variable by calculating the impact threshold for a confounding variable (ITCV) (Busenbark et al., 2021; Frank et al., 2013) for H1, H2, H3, and H4. This method has been used effectively in recent IS and management journals (e.g., Gamache & McNamara, 2019; Venkatesh et al., 2020). ITCV addresses the likelihood that an omitted variable can bias the results by calculating how a confounding variable would have to be correlated with both the dependent and focal predictor variables to change the original inference (Hill et al., 2021). Following the procedures recommended by Busenbark et al. (2021), we compared the ITCV scores against partial correlations of control variables with the independent and dependent variables. If the absolute value of the square root of the product of the correlations between the control variables and both the independent and dependent variables is lower than the ITCV score, then an omitted variable is unlikely to invalidate the findings (Busenbark et al., 2021). The ITCV scores for H1, H2, H3, and H4 were 0.45, 0.40, 0.21, and 0.53, respectively (all at a threshold of $\mathrm { ~ \normalfont ~ { ~ a ~ } ~ } < \mathrm { ~ 0 . 1 0 ~ }$ as recommended by Busenbark et al. 2021). Upon examining the partial correlations between all of the covariates and the focal constructs, we observed that the highest partial correlation was between the IoT use level and IoT-IC $( r = 0 . 3 2 )$ , which is higher than the ITCV score of H3. However, the absolute value of the square root of the product of the partial correlation between the IoT use level and IoT-IC and the partial correlation between the IoT use level and relationship infrastructure was 0.01, which is much lower than the ITCV value of 0.21. The rest of the partial correlation scores were lower than the ITCV scores of the hypothesized relationships. This evidence strongly suggests that no omitted variable would invalidate our findings.

Further, to test whether an omitted variable can bias the result of H5, we computed the robustness of an inference to replacement (RIR) index. The ITCV is inappropriate if the parameter is an interaction term (Busenbark et al., 2021). RIR provides insights into the percentage of a parameter estimate that would need to be biased in order to invalidate a causal inference (Busenbark et al., 2021; Frank et al., 2021). The RIR value of H5 was 40.93% $( \alpha = 0 . 1 0 )$ , suggesting that 41% of the observations would have to be biased in order to invalidate H5, which is very unlikely. Taken together, the ITCV scores and RIR index indicate that our findings are unlikely to have been biased by an omitted variable.

## 5.4 Tests of Competing Models and Robustness Tests

We conducted several robustness tests to eliminate potential alternative models as recommended by recent empirical-based research (e.g., Castillo et al., 2021; Khutia et al., 2021). In the first alternative model (see Appendix G, Alternative $^ { 1 ) , }$ we tested whether competitive aggressiveness directly affects IoT-IC. The rationale for this model is that aggressive firms may establish IoT-IC to gain the advantage of intensive learning and real-time information. This first alternative model yielded results similar to those obtained in our proposed model but with a lower $R ^ { 2 }$ Although the results from this alternative model suggest that competitive aggressiveness can drive IoT-IC, our proposed model is more theoretically sound. The literature suggests that firms vary in their use of publicly available technology practices (Bromiley & Rao, 2014). Although organizations may adopt IoT to outperform their competitors, competitive aggressiveness without technology resources and capabilities will not lead to competitive advantage (Andrevski & Ferrier, 2019). Competitive aggressiveness can accelerate the effect of IoT-IC on competitive advantage because it enables firms to explore various innovation paths more quickly than their competitors can.

In the second alternative model (Appendix G, Alternative 2), we tested the direct effects of the three ordinary IT capabilities on competitive advantage. Scholars have argued that resources and operational capabilities may function not only as supportive dynamic capabilities but also as their substitutes (Rahmandad, 2012). The results of this empirical analysis show that flexible IT infrastructure and ITbusiness experience had no direct effect on competitive advantage, and the effect of relationship infrastructure on competitive advantage was only marginal $( \beta = 0 . 1 8 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . These findings suggest that ordinary IT capabilities do not function as substitutes to IoT-IC.

In the third alternative model (Appendix G, Alternative 3), we tested a model with the IoT use level as a mediator in the relationship between ordinary IT capabilities and competitive advantage. In this specification, the only positive relationship was between the flexible IT infrastructure and the IoT use level, in which IoT use level only partially mediated the relationship between IT infrastructure and competitive advantage. This model supports the notion that adopting IoT alone, without linking it to strategic organizational processes, does not lead to competitive advantage.

Further, to assess the sensitivity of our PLS results, we ran our analyses in regression instead of PLS. We also used cluster analysis to classify firms based on their IoT use level. This method has been used in prior IS research as a robustness test (e.g., Karahanna et al., 2019). To gain more interpretable results, we predetermined the number of clusters by dividing groups into three levels: high, medium, and low IoT use. We then used the k-means algorithm to generate three-cluster solutions. Analysis using the cluster membership as a control variable suggests that firms with a high level of IoT use had a higher degree of the IoT-IC and competitive advantages at all dimensions. However, no significant difference occurred between the medium and low IoT use groups in terms of competitive advantage. Overall, the results of our robustness tests stayed qualitatively the same as our main analyses in terms of significance, direction, and relative magnitude of coefficients.

## 6 Discussion

The current study proposes IoT-IC to explain how organizations can leverage IoT and, in combination with their existing IT capabilities, create business value. Our study also explains how firms can utilize competitive aggressiveness to enhance the relationship between IoT-IC and competitive advantage. Drawing upon dynamic capabilities and innovation capability literature, our integrated model provides valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms of IoT capability building. We found strong support for all of our hypotheses. These findings, then, have implications for theory and practice.

## 6.1 Theoretical Implications

The results yield several implications for theory and research. First, our conceptualization of IoT-IC enriches the literature on digitalization and dynamic capabilities. We define the role of IoT as being embedded in innovation capability (i.e., IT-embedded dynamic capabilities as suggested by Steininger et al., 2022) and position innovation capability as a dynamic capability. We explain a mechanism to establish innovation capability through the use of IoT for sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring. The key activities under the use of IoT for sensing are scanning, monitoring, and identifying ideas; the key activities under the use of IoT for seizing are investing, selecting, responding, and modifying ideas; and the main activities under the use of IoT for reconfiguring are organizing, allocating, and coordinating resources.

Second, we enhance the IT-enabled organizational capabilities literature by conceptualizing IoT-IC as a higher-order integration capability (i.e., combination of a firm’s capabilities to use IoT to sense, seize, and reconfigure its resources), consistent with Lawson and

Samson’s (2001) conceptualization of innovation capability. Our conceptualization underscores the importance of the configuration of capabilities to create a successful digital strategy (Baygi et al., 2021; Park & Mithas, 2020). The framework of building organizational capabilities enabled by IoT-IC that we propose is intended to be especially helpful for understanding the roles of IoT in shaping firm competitive advantage. Future research should explore the possibility of integrating different IT capabilities under Industry 4.0 with our IoT-IC to optimize a firm’s value creation through digitalization.

Third, our findings also contribute to the theory of dynamic capabilities by demonstrating a positive effect of competitive aggressiveness in moderating the relationship between IoT-IC and competitive advantage. In reviewing the current state of dynamic capabilities, Schilke et al. (2018) encouraged researchers to identify potential moderators of the relationship between dynamic capabilities and outcomes. We specifically observe that, relative to firms with low competitive aggressiveness, competitively aggressive firms can benefit more from IoT-IC. Directing IoT for innovation, coupled with being significantly aggressive, can help organizations gain competitive advantage.

Lastly, our findings also suggest that three existing IT capabilities (i.e., flexible IT infrastructure, IT-business experience, and relationship infrastructure) positively affect IoT-IC. In contrast to IoT-IC, these ordinary IT capabilities are directed toward maintaining firms day-to-day operations (e.g., firms’ communication networks depend on their IT infrastructure). We theoretically argue that leveraging existing IT capabilities builds new capabilities as a means of coping with resource decay (Karadag & Poppo, in press). Ordinary IT capabilities can decay as firms adopt new systems or as markets change. However, due to their relatively predictable and continuous nature (Karadag & Poppo, in press), they also provide an avenue to establishing a new dynamic capability (i.e., IoT-IC) (Svahn et al., 2017). The significant relationships between ordinary IT capabilities and IoT-IC also confirm the importance of studying dynamic capabilities from a multilevel perspective (Salvato & Vassolo, 2018). For example, a strong relationship infrastructure amplifies trust between IT and business units. This trust allows managers to quickly diagnose and solve problems and utilize IoT more effectively for sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring.

## 6.2 Practical Implications

Our study has several practical implications. For example, it presents several recommendations for IT and business leaders concerned about whether IoT can fulfill its promises to their organizations. First, our study provides new foundations for understanding how to generate value from IoT. Digitalized processes enabled by IoT can be complex, and one way to leverage IoT to create business value is to deliberately control its usage by encouraging innovation through sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring. Organization decision makers should be aware that adopting IoT itself will not necessarily lead to competitive advantage. Organizations may benefit from directing their IoT use toward establishing innovation capabilities (e.g., simultaneously monitoring a business environment through IoT use for sensing, utilizing IoT data to support a decision-making process through IoT-use for seizing, and coordinating resources through IoT use for reconfiguring).

Second, we found that the interplay between IoT-IC and competitive aggressiveness can enhance competitive advantage. These findings suggest that if organizations want to speed up or optimize the effect of IoT-IC on competitive advantage, they should consider being highly aggressive. Aggressive firms with high IoT-IC can accelerate their speed to develop actions more quickly than their rivals can, thereby improving their competitive advantage. Lastly, we found support that existing IT capabilities play a critical role in building IoT-IC. Firms can deal with the threat posed by resource decay by leveraging their existing capabilities, such as flexible IT infrastructure, IT-business experience, and relationship infrastructure, to build IoT-IC. Thus, organizational managers should be aware of the limitations of their existing IT capabilities prior to building IoT-IC.

## 6.3 Limitations

Our study’s primary limitation is the use of crosssectional data. Although our study provides a solid cross-sectional model, a longitudinal study would be needed to help us establish the causal relationship between IoT-IC and competitive advantage. Further, we only used a single key informant to evaluate IoT use and its business impact at the firm level. Although we performed various tests that demonstrated that common method bias and endogeneity were not present, a possibility remains that these biases could increase all the interrelationships among the principal constructs in the study. Future research should recruit multiple key informants in organizations across different periods of time to minimize the possibility of such biases.

We acknowledge the difficulty in correctly estimating the flexibility of IT infrastructure, especially in large organizations. Although the measurement items we used to measure flexible IT infrastructure have been well-validated and are widely used in previous studies (e.g., Bhatt and Grover, 2005; Bhatt et al., 2010), those measures provide a mere snapshot of what flexible IT infrastructure is in reality. Similarly, we only used two items to measure relationship infrastructure. Although these items were sufficient to measure trust and a positive relationship between IT and business units, trust is a broad concept that can be measured using multiple dimensions (Gefen et al., 2008). Future research should use a much broader instrument to provide a more exhaustive set of flexible IT infrastructure and relationship infrastructure measures. Additionally, a mixed-methods approach could more accurately measure these constructs.

Further, this study used the perceptions of IT decision makers to estimate competitive advantage. While our data collection precluded us from collecting objective performance data, these perceptual measures were deemed appropriate because they were reported by those likely to be knowledgeable about the actual firm’s performance and competitive advantage. Research has found that perceptual measures of firm performance correlate positively with objective measures of firm performance (e.g., Delaney & Huselid, 1996). Nonetheless, the results should be interpreted with caution, and future research should attempt to collect objective performance data at the organizational level. Lastly, our sample was drawn from the US-based companies listed as panel members of the market research company we used. The use of IoT in the US may be somewhat different from that of other countries. This possibility limits generalizability across countries.

## 6.4 Future Research Directions

Our findings suggest several questions that could be explored in future work. First, as more organizations embrace IoT, future research could explore how organizations can accommodate other adaptive technologies, such as machine learning and artificial intelligence, to build synergy and promote innovation in organizations. We encourage IS researchers to study how the integration of IoT and non-IoT systems with innovative business processes can help organizations build dynamic capabilities. We also encourage IS scholars to study the dynamic of organizational routines using digital trace data generated by IoT. Such data could provide insights into how routine dynamics can lead to superior performance outcomes.

Second, capabilities can reside at the organizational and individual levels (Helfat & Raubitschek, 2018). Key leader characteristics may play a role in creating dynamic capabilities through the use of emerging technologies. Future research should explore how the experience of leaders, as well as their entrepreneurship orientation, contributes to the development of IoT-IC. Another important question for future researchers is the role of environmental factors in explaining the effect of IoT-IC on competitive advantage. Previous studies have suggested that environmental turbulence and uncertainty can affect the relationship between ITenabled capabilities and organizational outcomes (e.g., Pavlou & El-Sawy, 2011). Future research could investigate whether environmental factors influence the relationship between IoT-IC and organizational outcomes. Lastly, Park and Mithas (2020) showed that the industry sector may impact the types of configurations needed to create high organizational performance. We call for future researchers to investigate how IoT-IC, coupled with other capabilities, behave differently in different industry sectors.

Third, although central to firms’ innovation capability, IoT is prone to process drifting—digitalized processes changing incrementally over time (Pentland et al., 2020). The idea of changes enabled by IoT can be viewed as a type of “routine dynamic” that involves innovation because organizations regularly exercise their options by using IoT to sense and seize opportunities and reallocate their assets. Future research is needed to expand our conceptualization of IoT-IC by including this potential characteristic of the “drifting” of IoT.

## 7 Conclusion

As organizations continue to invest in IoT, building IoT-enabled organizational capabilities will continue to capture the attention of practitioners and researchers from various disciplines. Our study provides insights into how organizations can create value from IoT. Drawing on the dynamic capabilities and innovation capability literature, we conceptualize IoT-IC, consisting of IoT use for sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring, as an enterprise dynamic capability. This research provides empirical evidence of the positive relationships between ordinary IT capabilities and IoT-IC and between IoT-IC and competitive advantage. The significant moderating effect of competitive aggressiveness in the current study also extends our understanding of how IoT can bring value to organizations.

## About the Authors

Yulia Sullivan is an assistant professor of information systems and business analytics at Baylor University. Her academic career includes a brief stint as an assistant professor at Binghamton University. She received her PhD in information systems from the University of North Texas. Her research interests lie in the areas of cognitive information systems, the value of IT for organizations, information systems and decision-making, and research methods. Her work has appeared in several journals, including MIS Quarterly, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Journal of Business Ethics, Annals of Operations Research, and International Journal of Information Management, among others. She is also a co-author of Conducting Mixed-Methods Research: From Classical Social Sciences to the Age of Big Data and Analytics.

Samuel Fosso Wamba is associate dean for Research at TBS Education, France, and a Distinguished Visiting Professor at The University of Johannesburg, South Africa. He earned his PhD in industrial engineering at the Polytechnic School of Montreal, Canada. His current research focuses on the business value of information technology, inter-organizational systems adoption, use and impacts, supply chain management, electronic commerce, blockchain, artificial intelligence for business, social media, business analytics, big data, and metaverse for business. Mary Dunaway is an assistant professor in information science and systems in the College of Business, at Morgan State University. She teaches courses in information systems/data analytics (undergraduate/graduate), management information systems, and IT strategy (graduate). She received a PhD in information systems from the University of Arkansas in Fayetteville.
