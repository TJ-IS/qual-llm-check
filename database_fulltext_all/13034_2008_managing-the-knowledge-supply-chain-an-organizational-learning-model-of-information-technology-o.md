## Abstract

In this paper, we present an economic learning model that helps to formalize the complex relationships among an off-shoring firm's knowledge levels, production costs, and coordination costs. Specifically, we model a domestic firm's use of a selective offshore strategy (i.e., offshoring only a portion of its information technology activities) to exploit, through IT investments or contractual provisions, the foreign vendor's large, scale-driven repository of production knowledge. We illustrate the conditions under which knowledge transfers during offshoring may reduce a domestic firm's in-house production costs, leading to total cost savings in both the short term and the long term. Alternatively, when knowledge transfers are not sufficiently large, some short-lived offshoring projects may generate substantial cost savings to the domestic firm; however, long-lived offshoring projects may cause a disruption in the knowledge supply chain, resulting in substantial losses in the later stages of the project. A firm that fails to realize the costs associated with such a disruption soon enough in the project life may find itself locked into a disadvantageous offshoring agreement without any recourse. However, a domestic firm may be able to overcome a disruption in its knowledge supply chain by exploiting the learning-by-doing production knowledge generated by the foreign vendor's economies of scale. The managerial implications derived from our learning model may help guide firms as they consider the impacts of offshore contracts and knowledge management investments on firm knowledge, production costs, and coordination costs.

Keywords: IT offshoring, offshore outsourcing, backshoring, organizational learning, learning-by-doing, knowledge management, software development

## Introduction

Information technology offshore outsourcing is an increasingly important strategic tool for firms as IT skills and communication infrastructures have become readily available at low cost overseas. Although such offshore outsourcing activities have led to significant cost savings in many cases (Aspray et al. 2006; Rottman and Lacity 2006), a critical concern is that as IT work moves offshore the learning-by-doing knowledge (or deep, experiential knowledge) that comes from designing, coding, testing, and supporting applications and solving IT problems will also move offshore (Davis et al. 2006; Dhar and Balakrishnan 2006; Earl 1996; King 1994; Lacity et al. 1995). That is, “few organizations will be able to maintain skills that are practiced only through an external vendor” (Davis et al. 2006, p. 781).

This loss of learning-by-doing knowledge, sometimes referred to as deskilling, may lead to what we term a disruption in the firm's knowledge supply chain. We define a knowledge supply chain as a sequence of related knowledge-based processes that together produce a product or service. For example, components of a software application development project form a knowledge supply chain. The components of this supply chain include not only lower-level production processes such as research, analysis, design, coding, testing, and support but also higher-level coordination (or managerial) processes such as implementation and use of the software application to solve business problems. The ability of project managers to effectively coordinate and manage the lower-level activities depends critically on a firm's experience with performing such activities (i.e., learning-by-doing knowledge). However, if a firm offshores $^{2}$ its lower-level activities, it may lose much of this experiential knowledge over time. For a specific firm, a disruption occurs in the knowledge supply chain when such a loss of learning-by-doing knowledge (both technical and business) diminishes the ability of managers to effectively coordinate and integrate the offshored activities with the firm's other internal activities.

We observe that there are two views of offshoring activities (Davis et al. 2006; Tanriverdi et al. 2007). The first view is that the domestic firm uses its internal personnel to fully specify, design, and develop quality control procedures for a product to be assembled offshore. That is, the domestic firm develops and maintains critical knowledge in-house. This view corresponds with the offshoring of many manufacturing activities (Davis et al. 2006) and highly modularized business processes (Tanriverdi et al. 2007). The second view is that the internal personnel of the domestic firm is unable to completely specify the requirements, design, and quality control procedures, leaving the foreign vendor with a more complex set of modularization tasks to complete. In fact,

leading offshore outsourcing vendors define their value proposition around modularization services.... The offshore vendors have incentives to undertake process modularization tasks of developed economy firms for market penetration, learning, and capability development purposes (Newman 2000, Uhlenbruck et al. 2003). Although the offshore vendors initially offered comparative cost advantages, over time, they have also developed world-class capabilities in executing complex tasks through repeated interactions with clients in advanced economies and gaining in-depth understanding of their business processes (Ethiraj et al. 2005). (Tanriverdi et al. 2007, pp. 5-6)

As a result, the foreign vendor is more likely to gain critical product and process knowledge (through learning-by-doing) which may not be readily available to the domestic firm (Ethiraj et al. 2005; Tanriverdi 2007 et al.). This second view corresponds more with low modularity IT activities such as software application development, which entails tightly coupled tasks such as software research, detailed design, code development, testing, documentation, and support (Tanriverdi et al. 2007). Therefore, the risk of knowledge loss and deskilling to the domestic firm may be more prominent in the context of these low modularity IT activities than high modularity IT activities and may be more likely to lead to a disruption in the knowledge supply chain.

While the IT offshore literature acknowledges the economic risk associated with deskilling and disrupting the knowledge supply chain, we are not aware of any work that models a domestic firm's use of a selective offshore strategy to exploit, through IT investments or contract provisions, the foreign vendor's large, scale-driven repository of production knowledge. In order to fill this void, we use organizational learning theory to model the potential transfer of experiential knowledge from the foreign vendor to the domestic firm and to examine the resulting impact of such knowledge transfer on total costs. Specifically, we use a learning model to evaluate the impact of several critical parameters—including (1) the rates of learning and knowledge depreciation of the domestic and foreign firms, (2) the rate of knowledge transfer from the foreign vendor to the domestic firm, and (3) the length of the offshored project—on production and coordination costs incurred by the domestic firm in both the short-run and the long-run. Based on the various instantiations of the parameters, we derive managerial implications that may help guide firms as they consider the impacts of offshore contract provisions and knowledge management investments (e.g., systems for encouraging firm learning, mitigating knowledge depreciation, and facilitating knowledge transfer from the foreign vendor) on firm knowledge, production costs, and coordination costs.

We use a learning model (based on the organizational learning literature in economics) similar to the model used by Anderson and Parker (2002) to analyze the make/buy decision in a manufacturing environment. Anderson and Parker model a firm's optimal outsource rate as a function of product modularity, discount rate, demand elasticity, and technology obsolescence. Given our focus on knowledge transfers in the IT offshoring context, we make different assumptions concerning the accumulation of production and coordination knowledge by the domestic firm. These assumptions allow us to derive a set of counterintuitive results that help explain some observations in the IT offshoring context.

Overall, our work illustrates that domestic firms may grossly underestimate their ability to exploit the learning-by-doing knowledge generated by the foreign vendor's economies of scale. Specifically, the inclusion of knowledge transfers in our model leads to the following critical insights:

\- We illustrate conditions under which knowledge transfers from a foreign vendor to a domestic firm during a selective offshore strategy may increase the amount of production knowledge accumulated by the domestic firm (despite its loss of learning-by-doing knowledge) and reduce the domestic firm's in-house production costs over time, leading to cost savings in both the short term and the long term.

\- Alternatively, when knowledge transfers are not sufficiently large, some short-lived offshoring projects may still generate substantial cost savings to the domestic firm while long-lived offshoring projects may cause a disruption in the knowledge supply chain, resulting in substantial losses in the later stages of the project.

\- Furthermore, firms that fail to realize the costs associated with such a disruption soon enough in the project life may find themselves locked into disadvantageous off-shoring agreements without any recourse. Interestingly, offshored projects that become unexpectedly delayed (i.e., the project life is extended) may be particularly problematic for firms. In this case, the domestic firm may take a wait-and-see approach, possibly delaying the backshoring decision until it becomes an economically infeasible option.

\- Finally, we illustrate conditions under which a selective offshore strategy may cause the domestic firm's in-house production costs to increase at an increasing rate, resulting in an increase in total production costs (in-house production costs + offshore production costs) over time. The result that offshoring a portion of a firm's IT activities to a more efficient vendor may lead to an increase in total production costs over time is counter-intuitive and driven by the knowledge transfer assumptions.

## Literature Review

Previous outsourcing studies have examined factors that drive a firm's decision to outsource. These factors include lower production costs of outsourcing vendors (Ang et al. 1998; Loh and Venkatraman 1992; Smith et al. 1998), a firm's need to focus on its core competences (Grover et al. 1994; Huber 1993; Lacity et al. 1995, 1996), and a firm's need to develop an adaptable management structure (Slaughter and Ang 1996). In the offshoring context, benefits are derived not only from the vendor's economies of scale that are passed on to the domestic firm through lower production costs but also from the difference in wages between the domestic and foreign countries (Gonzalez et al. 2006). For example, in India, the wages of IT workers are 20 to 70 percent lower than those in the United States (Pfannenstein and Tsai 2004).

Previous studies in the IT and economics literatures have applied many prominent and useful theories to explain various outsourcing decisions and arrangements. These theories include transaction cost theory, agency theory, resource-based theory, contract theory, innovation theory, and game theory (for an extensive survey and analysis of the various theories applied to IT outsourcing problems, see Dibbern et al. 2004). Since transaction cost theory is a common theoretical framework used to evaluate IT outsourcing decisions (Ang et al. 1998; Aubert et al. 2004; Ngwenyama and Bryson 1999) and offshoring decisions (Aron et al. 2005; Rao 2004), our work specifically leverages the transaction cost concepts and integrates them with organizational learning theory to model the risk of knowledge loss associated with IT offshoring.

According to transaction cost theory, a firm incurs two types of costs to provide any product or service: production costs and coordination costs. Production costs are the costs incurred to make the product or provide the service and include the cost of labor, material, and capital. Coordination costs are the costs incurred to monitor, control, and manage the work. Coordination costs are particularly high in the context of offshore outsourcing (Carmel and Nicholson 2005) due to

the need to know not only the providers but also the legislation, the labour culture, and, in general, the country where those providers are located, all of which implies expenses related to trips and even to lawyers and advisors, who can help to make better decisions in this field. (Gonzalez et al. 2006, p.1235)

## According to Whitten and Leidner (2006, p. 607),

While (transaction cost theory) TCT is a prominent and useful theory to explain why firms initiate an IT outsourcing arrangement....TCT alone cannot fully explain subsequent decisions to continue or discontinue an outsourcing relationship.

We would add that transaction cost theory alone cannot explain the impact of offshore decisions on the level of firm knowledge and the resulting impact of changing knowledge levels on production costs and coordination costs. That is, firms that outsource activities (especially offshore) risk losing internal capabilities such as organizational learning, innovation, and skill sets (Earl 1996). However, domestic firms may conversely gain some knowledge through knowledge transfers from the offshore firm (Das 2004). Little research has formally modeled the critical tradeoff between savings in short-run labor costs and changes in long-run firm knowledge in the offshoring context. In the following section, we model this trade-off using an economic learning model (Anderson and Parker 2002; Li and Rajagopalan 1998a, 1998b; Spence 1981).

Many empirical studies have examined organizational learning curves in a range of industries including airframe production (Wright 1936), electronics (Hayes and Clark 1986), and fast food (Yell 1979). These studies confirm that cumulative firm output is positively correlated with firm knowledge and experience levels and negatively correlated with production costs. A stream of theoretical economic literature developed to explain this correlation and to further predict dynamic market interactions in the presence of a learning curve (Fudenberg and Tirole 1983; Spence 1981).

Given the importance of IT as a knowledge asset, many empirical studies have examined learning effects in various IT environments including computerized manufacturing (Kelley 1994), CASE tools adoption (Kemerer 1992), ATM adoption (Dos Santos and Peffers 1995), packaged software installation (Saraswat and Gorgone 1990), and open source applications development (Ciborra and Audreu 2001). These empirical studies illustrate the significant impacts of a firm's knowledge of, and experience with, IT on firm costs.

## The Economic Learning Model: Knowledge and Cost

We used a modified version of Spence's learning model (Anderson and Parker 2002; Li and Rajagopalan 1998a, 1998b; Spence 1981) as the basis for our IT offshoring model. In our model, we consider conditions under which a firm's repository of knowledge may decrease over time. Following the previous organizational learning literature, the key assumptions of this model are that production costs decline as knowledge level increases and that the marginal change in knowledge level is a function of the quantity of output and the rate of knowledge depreciation. The modified learning model is presented below.

Consider a firm that has the initial marginal production cost $c_{0}$ , given the initial knowledge level $k_{0}$ . The unit production cost of this firm is

$$
c (t) = c _ {0} \theta (t)\tag{1}
$$

where

$$
\theta (t) = \left(\frac {k (t)}{k _ {0}}\right) ^ {- \beta}. \quad (\beta > 1)\tag{2}
$$

$\theta(t)$ is a decreasing function of knowledge level $k = k(t)$ at time t (i.e., $\partial\theta/\partial k < 0$ since $\beta > 1$ ), which implies that the firm's unit production costs decrease as the firm accumulates knowledge over time. $\beta$ is the innovation parameter, the size of which determines the impact of accumulated knowledge on production costs.

The marginal change of knowledge level at time t is

$$
\frac {d k (t)}{d t} = q - \delta k (t) \quad (0 \leq \delta \leq 1)\tag{3}
$$

where q is the fixed quantity of output and $\delta$ is the knowledge depreciation rate. The intuition behind Equation (3) is that a firm producing more output will accumulate more knowledge via learning-by-doing. However, some knowledge is lost over time because of technology obsolescence, imperfect knowledge management practices, employee turnover, and other factors. Solving Equation (3) yields (see Appendix A for derivation)

$$
\boldsymbol {k} (t) = \frac {\boldsymbol {q}}{\delta} + \left(\boldsymbol {k} _ {0} - \frac {\boldsymbol {q}}{\delta}\right) e ^ {- \delta t}\tag{4}
$$

Note that as time approaches infinity, the knowledge level converges to

$$
K = \lim _ {t \rightarrow \infty} k (t) = \frac {q}{\delta}\tag{5}
$$

This steady state is referred to as the plateau state (Li and Rajagopalan 1998a) and has been confirmed by empirical studies (Argote et al. 1990; Darr et al. 1995).

For convenience, we reformulate Equation (1), using Equations (2) through (4), as

$$
c (t) = c _ {0} \left(\omega + (1 - \omega) e ^ {- \delta t}\right) ^ {- \beta}\tag{6}
$$

where

$$
\omega = \frac {q}{k _ {0} \delta} = \frac {K}{k _ {0}}\tag{7}
$$

The behavior of $c(t)$ over time depends on the value of $\omega$ and the sign of the first and second order derivatives of Equation (6). In Cases I, II-1, and II-2 below we describe three possible characteristics of the behavior of $c(t)$ over time. These characteristics are illustrated in Figure 1 (see Appendix B for details).

## Case I: Knowledge Increasing ( $\omega > 1$ )

In Case I, the knowledge level increases over time (i.e., $k_0 < K$ ). As a result, production costs steadily decrease over time, which is consistent with Spence's assumption.

## Case II: Knowledge Decreasing ( $\omega < 1$ )

In Case II, the knowledge level decreases over time (i.e., $k_{0} > K$ ). As a result, production costs increase over time. This occurs when the learning rate (q) is not high enough to offset the knowledge depreciation rate ( $\delta$ ). Interestingly, there are two distinct subcases of knowledge loss, depending on the values of $\omega$ and $\beta$ as shown in Figure 2.

\- Case II-1 ( $\omega^{*} \leq \omega < 1$ ): The production costs increase at a decreasing rate over time ( $\ddot{c}(t) < 0 \quad \forall t$ ).

\- Case II-2 $(0 < \omega < \omega^{*})$ : The production costs increase at an increasing rate until it reaches the inflection point $(\ddot{c}(t) > 0 \quad \forall t < t^{*}$ , where $\ddot{c}(t^{*}) = 0)$ .

The boundary between Cases II-1 and II-2 is

$$
\omega^ {*} (\beta) = \frac {\beta}{1 + \beta}\tag{8}
$$

The value of $\omega^{*}(\beta)$ increases from 0.5 to 1 as $\beta(>1)$ increases. This implies that more innovative firms (i.e., higher $\beta$ ) are more vulnerable to the risk of rapid cost increases when knowledge levels decrease over time.

## The Offshoring Model

We now construct a model of offshoring based on the extended learning model presented above. The offshoring model is similar to the model used by Anderson and Parker (2002) to analyze the make/buy decision in a manufacturing environment. They model a firm's optimal outsource rate as a function of product modularity, discount rate, demand elasticity, and technology obsolescence. Given our focus on knowledge transfers in the IT offshoring context, we make different assumptions concerning the accumulations of production and coordination knowledge by the domestic firm.

We assume that a domestic firm produces a total quantity q of IT services and the firm may decide at time $t_{1}$ to offshore any proportion $(0 \leq \alpha \leq 1)$ of its IT services. Further, we assume that the foreign firm produces quantity D of IT services for other client firms. For country level analysis, one might interpret these two firms as aggregated entities of all the firms located in each country.

In order to produce one of the q units of IT services, the domestic firm incurs production costs and coordination costs. Production costs may be incurred in-house or contracted to the foreign firm. Coordination costs, on the other hand, are always incurred in-house but depend on the rate of offshoring.

Specifically, the unit cost incurred by the domestic firm is

$$
c (t) = c ^ {P R} (t) + c ^ {C O} (t)\tag{9}
$$

where

$$
c ^ {P R} (t) = \alpha \cdot c ^ {O S} (t) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot c ^ {I H} (t) \quad (0 \leq \alpha \leq 1)\tag{10}
$$

and $c^{OS}$ is the cost incurred by the foreign firm to produce one of the q units; $c^{IH}$ is the cost incurred by the domestic firm to produce one of the q units in-house; $c^{PR}$ is the cost incurred by the domestic firm to produce one of the q units by offshoring $\alpha$ proportion of this unit; and $c^{CO}$ is the cost incurred by the domestic firm to coordinate the production of one of the q units.

The offshoring cost incurred to the domestic firm is not fixed but dynamically changes with knowledge levels over time. This implicitly assumes that the offshoring contract between domestic firm and foreign firm is a time and materials based contract that is commonly used in IT offshoring contexts (Gopal et al. 2003; Van Mieghem 1999). In this case, the domestic firm assumes the risks of the contractual relationship (both the pain of higher costs and the joy of lower costs). This is in contrast to a fixed price contract that transfers the risks to the foreign firm. Any firm in a time and materials contract would necessarily protect itself with a termination clause (Pai and Basu 2007). In our model, we assume a termination by convenience clause that allows the domestic firm to terminate the contract without restriction. Thus, the domestic firm bears the risk of being in the contractual relationship, but has the most favorable option to leave the contractual relationship.

The functional forms of the cost components in Equations (9) and (10) are illustrated in Figure 3 and are discussed below.

## Offshore Production (OS)

The marginal change in the level of offshore production knowledge is proportional to the sum of the domestic firm's offshored output quantity ( $\alpha q$ ) and the foreign firm's output quantity (D). Specifically, the offshore vendor gains knowledge and capabilities derived not only from experiences from providing services ( $\alpha q$ ) to the domestic firm but also from repeated experience (i.e., large D) in providing similar services to other client firms (Ethiraj et al. 2005). The lower production costs derived from outsourcing vendors' economies of scale (D) has been confirmed to be an important driver of outsourcing decisions (Gonzalez et al. 2006; Loh and Venkatraman 1992; Smith et al. 1998). Consider the following example. Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) is a large IT services provider in India and is a major player in the global market for IT offshore outsourcing. TCS has developed a formal methodology to define, capture, and absorb critical knowledge required for application development and maintenance at one client project and apply that knowledge to other client projects (Oshri et al. 2007). This methodology allows TCS to codify knowledge it accumulates through project experiences (i.e., D) for reuse by other TCS teams.

The rates of production knowledge depreciation, $\delta^{PR}$ , for the foreign and the domestic firms are assumed to be the same. Similar to Equation (3), the marginal change of offshore production knowledge is

$$
\frac {d k ^ {O S} (t)}{d t} = \{\alpha q + D \} - \delta^ {P R} k ^ {O S} (t)\tag{11}
$$

The associated offshore production cost is

$$
\boldsymbol {c} ^ {O S} (t) = \boldsymbol {c} _ {0} ^ {O S} \left(\frac {\boldsymbol {k} ^ {O S} (t)}{\boldsymbol {k} _ {0} ^ {O S}}\right) ^ {- \beta^ {O S}}\tag{12}
$$

where $c_{0}^{os}$ is the initial offshore production cost, $k_{0}^{os}$ is the initial offshore production knowledge level, and $\beta^{os}$ is the offshore innovation parameter.

## In-House Production (IH)

The marginal change of in-house production knowledge for the domestic firm is

$$
\frac {d k ^ {I H} (t)}{d t} = (1 - \alpha) q + \tau (\alpha q + D) - \delta^ {P R} k ^ {I H} (t)\tag{13}
$$

where $\tau$ is the knowledge transfer rate from the foreign firm to the domestic firm. The first term $[(1-\alpha)\boldsymbol{q}]$ is the rate of knowledge accumulation derived from the domestic firm's internal production, and the second term $[\tau(\alpha\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{D})]$ is the rate of knowledge transferred to the domestic firm from the foreign firm. $^{3}$

The second term depends on two critical parameters ( $\tau$ and D). The IT literature often acknowledges a transfer of knowledge or intellectual capital from the domestic firm to the foreign firm necessary to produce the offshored services (Aron et al. 2005). However, our model focuses on the transfer of learning-by-doing knowledge from the foreign firm to the domestic firm (an issue not discussed in the IT literature but critical in the IT offshore context) after the initial transfer of intellectual property. As such, $\tau$ represents the ability of the domestic firm to capture (e.g., through contracts or knowledge management strategies) the learning-by-doing knowledge created by the foreign firm. $^{4}$ The in-house production cost associated with Equation (13) is

$$
\boldsymbol {c} ^ {I H} (t) = \boldsymbol {c} _ {0} ^ {I H} \left(\frac {\boldsymbol {k} ^ {I H} (t)}{\boldsymbol {k} _ {0} ^ {I H}}\right) ^ {- \beta^ {I H}}\tag{14}
$$

where $c_{0}^{IH}$ is the initial in-house production cost, $k_{0}^{IH}$ is the initial in-house production knowledge level, and $\beta^{IH}$ is the in-house production innovation parameter.

$$
\frac {d \boldsymbol {k} ^ {I H} (t)}{d t} = (1 - \alpha) \boldsymbol {q} + \tau (\alpha \boldsymbol {q} + \lambda D) - \delta^ {P R} \boldsymbol {k} ^ {I H} (t) \quad (0 \leq \lambda \leq 1)
$$

## Coordination (CO)

The domestic firm must coordinate both offshore and in-house production. The marginal change of coordination knowledge is

$$
\frac {d k ^ {C O} (t)}{d t} = q - \delta^ {C O} k ^ {C O} (t)\tag{15}
$$

To model the disruption in the knowledge supply chain, where the offshoring of low-level IT activities results in the reduction of upper-level expertise, we assume that the depreciation rate of coordination ( $\delta^{co}$ ) increases with the offshoring rate ( $\alpha$ ). Specifically, we set

$$
\delta^ {C O} = f (\alpha) = \alpha \overline {{\delta}} ^ {C O} + (1 - \alpha) \underline {{\delta}} ^ {C O}\tag{16}
$$

where $\underline{\delta}^{CO} \leq \overline{\delta}^{CO}$ , and $\underline{\delta}^{co}$ is the lower boundary of $\delta^{co}$ , and $\overline{\delta}^{CO}$ is the upper boundary of $\delta^{co}$ . For example, the depreciation rate of total insourcing ( $\alpha = 0$ ) is $\underline{\delta}^{co}$ while the depreciation rate of total offshoring ( $\alpha = 1$ ) is $\overline{\delta}^{CO}$ .

Equation (15) assumes that coordination knowledge is proportional to total output (whether produced in-house or offshore); that is, it acknowledges that the domestic firm gains some coordination experience (or knowledge) over time from coordinating and integrating the offshored activities with other internal processes, functions, and activities. $^{5}$ However, Equation (16) assumes a higher depreciation rate for knowledge derived from coordinating offshore activities; that is, it acknowledges that coordination costs are higher outside the firm.

The associated coordination cost is

$$
\boldsymbol {c} ^ {C O} (t) = \boldsymbol {z} \cdot \alpha + c _ {0} ^ {C O} \left(\frac {\boldsymbol {k} ^ {C O} (t)}{\boldsymbol {k} _ {0} ^ {C O}}\right) ^ {- \beta^ {C O}}\tag{17}
$$

where $c_{0}^{co}$ is the initial coordination cost, $k_{0}^{co}$ is the initial coordination knowledge level, z is the unit transaction cost, and $\beta^{co}$ is the coordination innovation parameter.

## Offshoring Scenarios

To explore our model's behavior, consider the following scenarios: Scenario I is an optimistic case that illustrates conditions favorable for offshoring in the short and long run. Scenarios II, III, and IV examine cases in which long-run increases in coordination or production costs eventually offset initial short-run production cost gains. Specifically, Scenario II examines a case in which a disruption in the knowledge supply chain generates an increase in coordination costs over time. Scenario III examines a case in which production costs increase over time due to the impacts of the knowledge transfer rate and the innovation parameter. Scenario IV examines the knowledge and cost implications of bringing offshored activities back in-house when firms face increasing coordination and/or production costs due to knowledge loss. We show that in some cases the domestic firm may find itself locked into disadvantageous offshoring agreements despite the termination by convenience clause.

Some models of outsourcing have analyzed the selection of the profit maximizing outsourcing rate (e.g., Anderson and Parker 2002; Shy and Rune 2003, 2005). Although of great interest, this is not the focus of this paper. Instead, we focus on long-run dynamic interactions of both the in-house and offshore production costs and coordination costs after the offshoring decision has been made. In the scenarios presented above we assume that the domestic firm selects an offshoring rate of 50 percent ( $\alpha = 0.5$ ) at time $t_{I}$ . This is consistent with some outsourcing research that has explored a selective outsourcing strategy, where firms keep a portion of their IT activities in-house to mitigate the possible risks of outsourcing in the future (Lacity et al. 1996, 1995; Willcocks et al. 1996). The specific values of the model parameters for all scenarios are presented in Table 1.

## Scenario I: Offshoring Is Beneficial

Scenario I illustrates a case in which the unfolding of the learning model is favorable to the domestic firm in both the short and long run perspectives. We assume that the domestic firm faces decreasing production and coordination costs prior to time $t_{1}$ based on its learning-by-doing over time. The sum of these production and coordination costs results in decreasing total costs over time as illustrated in Figure 4. Although the domestic firm's in-house production costs are decreasing, the domestic firm may be motivated to move some of its IT activities offshore to further reduce its current production costs (Loh and Venkatraman 1992; Smith et al. 1998).

In the short run, the major concern about the offshoring decision is the tradeoff between the lower production costs and higher coordination costs at time $t_{I}$ . As assumed in Equation (17), the increase in the domestic firm's coordination costs at time $t_{I}$ is limited to the transaction costs ( $\alpha z$ ). The domestic firm simply compares these costs with the decrease in production costs in the near term. In Figure 4, the total cost of offshoring (solid line) is far below that of keeping production in-house (dotted line) at time $t_{I}$ (i.e., $\Delta C(t = t_{I}) = \Delta c^{PR} + z\alpha < 0$ ). As a result, the offshoring decision at $t_{I}$ will be advantageous to the domestic firm.

Moreover, to make this scenario favorable in the long run we assume that the domestic firm is immune to the internal disruption of the knowledge supply chain after offshoring. This assumption is implemented by fixing the coordination knowledge depreciation rate before and after offshoring in Equation (16) (i.e., $\delta^{CO} = \overline{\delta}^{CO} = \underline{\delta}^{CO} = 10\%$ ).

Since the coordination costs decrease over time at the same rate as in the no offshoring case, the long-run net benefits of offshoring depend on what happens to production costs. The major driving forces include the foreign firm's specialization and implied higher learning rate (i.e., $D >> q$ ) that will impact the offshore production costs, and the portion of the foreign firm's knowledge that is transferred to the domestic firm ( $\tau$ ) that will impact the in-house production costs after offshoring. Recall that the in-house production costs depend not only on the rate of knowledge accumulation by the internal production [( $1 - \alpha$ ) $q$ ] but also on the rate of knowledge transferred from the foreign firm [ $\tau(\alpha \ q + D)$ ] as shown in Equation (13). Therefore, the domestic firm's learning rate of in-house production after offshoring will be higher than that in the no offshoring case if $(1-\alpha)q+\tau(\alpha q+D)>q$ [or $\tau>\alpha q/(\alpha q+D)=9\%$ ]. The shaded area in Figure 4 illustrates the continuous benefits accumulated by the domestic firm over time. In fact, in this case the cost benefits of offshoring will accumulate at an increasing rate.

## Scenario II: Disruption in the Knowledge Supply Chain

In Scenario II, we model the disruption in the knowledge supply chain. Specifically, we increase the upper boundary of the coordination knowledge depreciation rate $(\bar{\delta}^{co})$ from 10 percent to 25 percent, which according to Equation (16) increases the coordination knowledge depreciation rate $(\delta^{co})$ from 10 percent to 17.5 percent after time $t_{I}$ (i.e., $\delta^{co} = f(\alpha) = \alpha \bar{\delta}^{co} + (1 - \alpha) \underline{\delta}^{co} = 0.5(0.25) + 0.5(0.1) = 0.175$ ).

Figure 5 illustrates how this change affects the domestic firm's coordination knowledge over time compared to Scenario I. Although the coordination knowledge is increasing prior to time $t_I$ , it begins to decrease after time $t_I$ . All other parameters are assumed to be the same as in Scenario I.

Figure 6 illustrates how the disruption in the knowledge supply chain and associated loss in coordination knowledge affects the offshoring costs. There is no change in production costs compared to Scenario I, but the coordination costs of the domestic firm now start to rapidly increase after time $t_{1}$ . In the short run (between time $t_{1}$ and $t_{p}$ ), the total cost of offshoring is still lower than that of in-house production since the decrease in production costs outweigh the increase in coordination costs. The area ABC in Figure 6 illustrates the accumulated benefits from cost reduction during this time period.

However, after time $t_{p}$ , the increase in coordination costs offsets the decrease in production costs. The domestic firm is now accumulating losses relative to a no offshoring strategy. Further, if the domestic firm does not terminate offshoring or take actions to mitigate knowledge losses the accumulated losses will eventually outweigh the accumulated benefits (ABC), and ex post, the offshoring decision is a mistake. The costs of contract termination are addressed in Scenario IV.

In summary, Scenario II illustrates the risk of long-run cost increases from an offshoring decision when coordination knowledge is lost. One possible cause of this loss may come from the disruption in the knowledge supply chain.

## Scenario III: Production Costs Matter

In Scenario III we consider the case in which both production costs and coordination costs of the domestic firm are increasing after offshoring. In this scenario we decrease the knowledge transfer rate from the foreign firm to the domestic firm ( $\tau$ ) to 1 percent. All other parameters are assumed to be the same as in Scenario II.

Figure 7 shows the impact of reducing the knowledge transfer rate to 1 percent on the in-house production knowledge. In this scenario, the domestic firm's in-house production knowledge is decreasing over time since the learning rate becomes relatively low after offshoring (i.e., $\tau < 6\%$ ). $^{6}$

## Scenario IV: Backshoring

In Scenarios II and III we illustrated how an offshoring contract may be disadvantageous in the long run despite short-run benefits. A manager's intuitive reaction may be to backshore as soon as the losses outweigh the benefits. For example, CRM vendor Kana Software, which had more than 350 software developers based in India and China at the peak of its offshoring initiatives, is now reversing direction by bringing its software development projects back in-house (Schwartz 2006). However, as we will show, under certain conditions implementing such a backshore strategy may create more harm for the domestic firm than the alternative of remaining locked-in to an existing offshore contract.

In this scenario we specifically examine the impact of the erosion of the domestic firm's knowledge during the off-shoring period on the economics of the firm's later decision to backshore.

## An Example: The Case of FISC and Offsource

Many of the managerial insights derived from our offshore learning model are illustrated by a case study reported by Kaiser and Hawk (2004). This case describes an eight-year sourcing relationship between a U.S. financial insurance company (referred to by the pseudonym “FISC”) and an India-based offshore vendor (referred to by the pseudonym “Offsource”). This case illustrates several strategies implemented by FISC to mitigate the economic risk associated with knowledge loss and deskilling.

In 1996, FISC initiated a relationship with an offshore vendor by outsourcing, based on a time and materials contract, its Year-2000 (Y2K) compliance work to Offsource. This sourcing relationship expanded to incorporate the design, development, and support of more mission-critical software applications. While Offsource took on much of the technical work it also developed a close relationship with FISC to imbue themselves with domain knowledge of the insurance business. Although Offsource's expanded role in the sourcing relationship created short-term value, FISC's employees were becoming increasingly dependent on Offsource because Offsource's staff understood the applications better than FISC employees. In fact, FISC's employees were not given opportunities to develop certain skills and capabilities. As a result, FISC's management became concerned about its diminishing in-house IT skills and knowledge. In the context of our learning model this may be interpreted as a disruption in the knowledge supply chain at FISC.

Given the high costs of backshoring, FISC decided to maintain the offshore relationship but address the loss of in-house IT knowledge by ensuring that its employees developed good technical and business skills. FISC accomplished this by implementing two strategies: formalizing knowledge transfer from Offsource to FISC and creating a dual project management hierarchy. FISC formalized knowledge transfer and internal knowledge retention by developing, for each IT employee at FISC, an individual development plan (IDP) that formalized learning-oriented processes and structures within the firm. In these plans, FISC employees identified and listed their career goals while FISC managers identified the skills necessary to meet those goals and laid out steps for developing such skills. In addition, FISC developed a mentoring program in which FISC matched its employees with Offsource employees, who were then accountable for transferring to FISC employees the IT skills and knowledge necessary to achieve their IDP goals. By integrating the IDPs with a mentoring program FISC was able to effectively retain important IT competencies and exploit Offsource's expertise.

In addition, FISC created a dual project management hierarchy (DPMH) to help retain and grow IT competencies. The DPMH clarified the IT competencies needed by FISC managers, which was useful in creating employees' IDPs and keeping FISC from becoming dependent on Offsource. The DPMH improved FISC's organizational memory by codifying organizational knowledge and, therefore, increased knowledge transfer from Offsource to FISC and reduced the rate of internal knowledge depreciation within FISC.

In summary, FISC entered into an offshore outsourcing relationship that provided a short-term cost advantage but a long-term degradation in internal learning-by-doing knowledge (consistent with Scenarios II and III). FISC believed that this degradation in knowledge was sufficiently large to make backshoring an unattractive option economically (consistent with Scenario IV). In an effort to retain and enhance its in-house technical expertise and project management capabilities (i.e., increase the knowledge transfer rate from Offsource to FISC, consistent with Figure 9 in Scenario III and decrease the knowledge depreciation rate within FISC), during the offshore relationship FISC implemented three mechanisms: individual development plans, mentoring programs, and a dual project management hierarchy. Given the success of these mechanisms for FISC, firms trying to retain specific learning-by-doing IT knowledge during offshore relationships may want to consider an offshore vendor's predisposition toward mentoring as an important selection criteria.

## Managerial Implications

In the narrowest interpretation of the core model, the manager's role is limited to estimating the parameters and deciding whether or not to offshore or backshore. Nevertheless, the model demonstrates that, for some ranges of the parameters (that instantiate the notion of learning-by-doing, knowledge transfers, and the disruption in the knowledge supply chain), the benefits from offshoring may be very different than predicted by a simple trade-off of lower wages and higher transaction costs. Managers who ignore these impacts may do so at their own peril. Further, this narrow interpretation of the core model underestimates its power to illuminate the strategies available to the manager and the potential of extensions to the core model.

## Managerial implications of the core model are as follow:

\- Intuitively, managers may justify their offshoring decision by comparing savings in wages against increases in coordination or transactions costs. However, the core model demonstrates, in a dynamic learning model context, that this may be a very myopic heuristic. The short-run gains may be lost because of a long-run degradation of firm knowledge. On the other hand, depending on the learning parameters, the manager may grossly underestimate the benefits of offshoring by not accounting for potential transfers of knowledge from the foreign firm. The manager that ignores the learning-by-doing and knowledge supply chain impacts may be taking a “shot in the dark” when it comes to offshoring and backshoring decisions.

\- Intuitively, managers may believe that they can always back out of an offshoring decision if offshoring is not going well. However, in the context of the model, it is possible that backshoring is more costly than continuing to incur the increasing costs of offshoring. Further, project life may play a critical role in whether backshoring is viable.

A wider interpretation of the core model may consider what managerial investments (technologies, training, contractual agreements, etc.) may affect the specific values of the model parameters. Consider the following parameter-specific examples which may be incorporated as offshore contract provisions:

• Investments in monitoring applications, communication systems, group decision support systems, and negotiation systems (for offshoring contracts) may increase the rate of knowledge transfer ( $\tau$ ) from the foreign firm to the domestic firm.

\- Investments in database warehousing and data mining systems, employee training applications, e-learning applications, knowledge management systems, and other technologies that codify organizational learning may decrease the depreciation rate of learning and knowledge of the domestic and foreign firms ( $\delta^{PR}$ ).

\- Investments in contract and project management training, mentoring programs (i.e., the FISC example), and involvement with business school curriculum may reduce the impact of the disruption of the knowledge supply chain by decreasing the coordination knowledge depreciation rate ( $\overline{\delta}^{c\phi}$ ).

\- Investments in new business processes, reengineering efforts, and quality management techniques may shorten the length of the offshored project life (T).

\- Investments in careful vendor selection may result in a choice of vendor with a large potential for learning-by-doing (D).

The learning model presented in this paper enables a disciplined discussion of the economic trade-offs associated with such investments and contractual provisions in the offshore context.

## Future Research

Although the offshoring model in this paper has provided several important insights, future work should focus on both analytically extending the current model and empirically testing the relationships derived from the model.

## Model Extensions

The economic model presented in this paper has the following limitations:

\- The model focuses only on the cost side of the market for output, where output is assumed to be fixed in quantity.

\- The model assumes that output quality is fixed and is the same for the domestic and foreign firms.

• The model uses one of many reasonable cost functions.

\- The model assumes a time and materials contract with termination for convenience. Note that even with this flexible termination clause we showed conditions under which the domestic firm may still get locked-in to a bad offshoring decision. If the contract is binding or more restrictive, the resulting loss of knowledge and increase in costs may be worse for the domestic firm.

\- The model ignores strategic interactions among domestic firms and/or foreign firms. There is now a great deal of competition among foreign countries as many domestic firms consider competing offshore destinations.

\- The model focuses on learning-by-doing knowledge but does not directly address the transfer of intellectual property or acquisition of country-specific knowledge.

Extensions to the core model could incorporate the transfer of intellectual property or acquisition of country-specific knowledge as follows:

\- The model could include the notion of a one-time transfer of intellectual property at the time of the offshoring contract coupled with a “loss function” that captures the risk of making that transfer. This loss could be a one-time loss or a function of the length of the contract.

\- The model could include the transfer, management, and property rights of learning-by-doing knowledge (i.e., improvements to the intellectual property assets) that are generated after the offshore decision.

\- The model could include a learning rate for country-specific knowledge in a real options context. This model could then be used to value the offshoring for itself and its strategic value in positioning the firm for future business in the foreign country. Intuitively, managers may feel that they must offshore in order to “keep their options open” by gaining experience in dealing with the cultural, language, and legal environments of the foreign countries. However, to price these real options, the manager must be able to estimate the real costs and benefits of offshoring, including possible losses of learning-by-doing knowledge, possible lock-in to the offshore decision, and the country knowledge learning rate.

Each of these limitations represents an opportunity to extend and refine the model. We believe that exploring the strategic interactions on the vendor side and examining the trade-offs in a real options context are particularly fruitful areas of future research.

## Empirical Testing

The results derived from our model call for empirical research that examines, in the offshoring context, the acquisition and depreciation of a domestic firm's learning-by-doing knowledge and the transfer of learning-by-doing knowledge between a domestic firm and a foreign vendor. Argote et al. (1990) and Darr et al. (1995) provide a general framework to conduct empirical research concerning organizational learning among multiple organizations using a longitudinal data set; this framework could be applied to test our offshoring model. In our context, empirical models could be developed to examine the impact of knowledge flows on two dependent variables: production costs and coordination costs. Key independent variables would be the cumulative experience of the domestic firm with software application development activities and the cumulative experience of the foreign firm with software application development activities. Cumulative experience (or output) would act as a proxy variable for the knowledge acquired through learning-by-doing. Control variables would include factors such as time, contract type, firm size, firm age, firm location, firm economies of scale, and industry. The empirical models would estimate the size and significance of critical parameters, such as the internal rate of learning-by-doing, the knowledge depreciation rate, and the knowledge transfer rate, that have been analytically examined in this paper.

## Conclusions

This paper developed an economic learning model to examine the economic risk of knowledge loss and deskilling in the offshore outsourcing context. Based on the various instantiations of the parameters, we derived managerial implications that may help guide firms as they consider the impacts of offshore contract provisions and knowledge management investments (e.g., systems for encouraging firm learning, mitigating knowledge depreciation, and facilitating knowledge transfer from the foreign firm) on firm knowledge, production costs, and coordination costs.

The key finding is that although short-lived offshoring projects may generate substantial cost savings to the domestic firm, long-lived offshoring projects may cause a disruption in the knowledge supply chain, resulting in substantial losses in the later stages of the project. Furthermore, firms that fail to realize the costs associated with such a disruption soon enough in the project life may find themselves locked into disadvantageous offshoring agreements without any recourse. However, domestic firms may overcome this disruption in the knowledge supply chain by exploiting (through knowledge transfers) the learning-by-doing knowledge generated by the foreign vendor's economies of scale. In summary, the learning model presented in this paper helps to formalize the complex relationships among an offshoring firm's knowledge levels, production costs, and coordination costs and may serve as a cornerstone of a possible family of models that have critical counter-intuitive managerial implications in the offshore context.

## Derivation of Knowledge Function $k(t)$

Equation (3), $\frac{dk(t)}{dt} + \delta k(t) = q$ , is a linear first order differential equation. To solve this equation, we need to multiply both sides by the integrating factor, $e^{\delta t}$ :

$$
e ^ {\delta t} \frac {d k (t)}{d t} + \delta e ^ {\delta t} k (t) = e ^ {\delta t} q\tag{A1}
$$

The left side of Equation (A2) is the derivative of $e^{\delta t}k(t)$ , and Equation (A1) can be reformulated as

$$
\frac {d}{d t} e ^ {\delta t} k (t) = e ^ {\delta t} q
$$

(A2)

By integrating both sides of Equation (A2)

$$
e ^ {\delta t} k (t) = \int e ^ {\delta t} q d t = \frac {q}{\delta} e ^ {\delta t} + c\tag{A3}
$$

we can get

$$
\boldsymbol {k} (t) = \frac {\boldsymbol {q}}{\delta} + c \boldsymbol {e} ^ {- \delta t}\tag{A4}
$$

where $c$ is a constant.

We impose the boundary condition $\pmb{k}(0) = \pmb{k}_0$ to solve for $c$ , $\pmb{k}(0) = \frac{q}{\delta} + ce^{-\delta \cdot 0}$ , and this gives

$$
c = \frac {q}{\delta} - k (0)\tag{A5}
$$

We replace $c$ in Equation (A4) by Equation (A5) and get Equation (4): $k(t) = \frac{q}{\delta} + (k_0 - \frac{q}{\delta})e^{-\delta t}$ .
