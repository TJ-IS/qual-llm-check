# Marketspace or Marketplace? Online Information Search and Channel Outcomes in Auto Retailing

Jason Kuruzovich

he growth of the Internet has spawned an increasing number of online information sources (OISs). The Teffect of OISs on consumer information search processes has been particularly striking in sectors such as auto retailing, where the typical consumer has conventionally been confronted with an unpleasant and ineffi cient purchase process. However, the relationships between the information found in the online “marketspace,” consumer search in the offline “marketplace,” and other aspects of the multichannel shopping process are not well understood. This study examines the differential impact of price and product information found in the marketspace, relating consumers’ information needs and information retrieval from OISs to three shopping related outcomes—purchase based on online infomediary referral (i.e., referred purchase), intensity of search in the marketplace, and online search satisfaction. We draw on a large data set of more than 16,000 new vehicle purchasers who reported using the Web for search related to their new vehicle purchase. We find that OISs offer different levels of price and product information and consumers are differentiated in their ability to retrieve this information. Further, the retrieval of price versus product information online has important implications for whether consumers consummate their online search through referred purchase or extend their search into the physical marketplace. Our results suggest different business models for infomediaries providing price and prod uct information and underscore the need for designing information provisioning systems of OISs to facilitate transition between the marketspace and the marketplace.

## 1. Introduction

The growth of the Internet and other related digital capabilities have led to fundamental transformations in the way consumers seek and find information to support their purchase processes. The physical and information components of the value chain have been decoupled (Kambil and van Heck 1998), and there has been a rapid growth of online firms serving as information intermediaries (Hagel and Singer 1999). These online information sources (OISs) create value by providing price as well as product-related information (Sawhney et al. 2003) and have established themselves as pivotal players in industries as diverse as financial brokerages, banking, insurance, mortgages, travel services, and auto retailing. An important catalyst for their emergence and growth is the irreversible trend toward increasing information intensity of products, services, and value chains. In addition to these new players, incumbents within different industry sectors have also significantly enhanced the volume of price and product information they provide online. As noted by Rayport and Sviokla (1994, 1995), value creation and appropriation in the presence of multiple OISs is very different from that in the traditional offline world.

The rapid growth in the use of OISs has led to a number of studies seeking to understand the unique characteristics of online channels (e.g., Scott Morton et al. 2001, Zettelmeyer et al. 2004) as well as consumer search processes in these channels (e.g., Johnson et al. 2004, Klein and Ford 2003). Although understanding consumer behavior in the online channel is important, consumers increasingly navigate both the online “marketspace” and the physical “marketplace” to search for information and consummate their purchase process (Peterson and Merino 2003). Referring to the issues created by the growing use of multiple channels, Rayport and Sviokla (1995, pp. 75–76) observe that “Managing two interacting value-adding processes in the two mutually dependent realms poses new conceptual and tactical challenges.” To the extent that multichannel usage and linkages continue to grow in importance, there is a clear need to bring empirical evidence to bear on these issues.

In this study, we address the broad research question: How do consumers’ information needs and online information retrieval influence their shopping process? In answering this question, we draw on the needs fulfillment framework to argue that when consumers have different information needs—as distinguished by the level of need for price or product information—the fulfillment of those needs through online information retrieval yields differences in purchase behaviors and associated outcomes. We test our model in the context of the auto retailing industry, where we examine consumers’ information needs and use of OISs as part of their automobile purchase process. The traditional supply chain of manufacturers and dealers within the retail auto industry has been significantly augmented and reconfigured by the presence of online infomediaries, offering a unique and rich opportunity to study online information search and multichannel behavior. Our empirical analysis uses a secondary data set of U.S. vehicle purchasers who reported using the Internet as part of their information search process. A large sample and the extensive nature of the data collected enable us to examine detailed aspects of the information search process while controlling for other individual characteristics that may offer competing explanations for the hypothesized information-based effects.

Our research makes the following key contributions. First, using the needs fulfillment framework, we identify how consumers’ search for information online (i.e., search in the marketspace) can be appropriately characterized as a process jointly influenced by information needs and online information retrieval. In doing so, we provide a rich theoretical and empirical treatment of the drivers and intermediate outcomes of online information search. In other words, by examining the particular information needs and the extent to which those needs are fulfilled through online search, we are able to isolate the specific impact of information in the shopping process. Second, we examine how consumers’ information search in the marketspace influences their use of a referral from an online infomediary, which we term referred purchase. Because referrals are the primary source of revenue for several online infomediaries, understanding referred purchase is of significant interest to this growing market segment. Third, we examine how consumers’ online information search in the marketspace—specifically, whether they find price information or product information—influences their offline marketplace search (i.e., visits to physical retailers). With consumers increasingly using multiple channels as part of the shopping process, understanding the interdependence between online and offline search behaviors is crucial. In addition, this is among the first studies to link the type of information (i.e., price and product) found as part of online information search to other important shopping-related outcomes. Finally, we consider consumers’ satisfaction derived from online search (referred to hereafter as online search satisfaction) as an important outcome of online information search, offline marketplace search, and referred purchase. Consumers’ online search satisfaction is a significant outcome of relevance to both online infomediaries as well as firms that provide information to consumers over the Web. Our findings have important implications for the channel members in the automobile retailing sector as well as for the business models of both the online and traditional infomediaries

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides a brief description of the evolving online auto retailing landscape, and §3 provides a review of the relevant literature. The theoretical framework and research hypotheses are presented in §4 and are followed by a description of the data and research methodology in §5. Section 6 presents the findings of the study, and §7 provides a discussion of the findings, limitations, and key implications. Section 8 concludes with future research directions suggested by this study.

## 2. The Online Auto Retailing Context

The auto retailing sector accounts for over a trillion dollars in sales and services and is the biggest retailing sector in the United States. Although there are over 22,600 new car dealerships, there is no national auto retailing brand, and the 10 largest dealers enjoy less than a 6% market share, with mostly localized sales. The presence of franchise laws also helps to strengthen the position of dealers in the retail value chain. Unlike commodity items, significant differences exist in the performance and features of cars as well as the pricing structure of dealerships—making the car buying process a complex decision for most consumers. Given the strength of the dealer’s position in the value chain and that search and comparison shopping are costly for consumers, dealers typically employ nebulous pricing strategies and resort to aggressive sales tactics to maximize profits. Thus, for the typical consumer entering a dealership, the car buying process is both inefficient and unpleasant (Marti 2000).

With the advent of the Web, different types of OISs have emerged to meet the information demands of consumers. In addition to traditional sources of information such as manufacturers and newspapers moving their information content online, online infomediaries have positioned themselves as neutral and trusted sources of information. In general, the OISs supporting the automobile purchase process can be classified into four broad categories:

Independent Online Buying Services OBSs-: OBSs, considered to be the pioneers of online auto retailing, perform a variety of functions. Their primary role is that of referral intermediaries, selling vehicles to consumers through dealer referrals. Using a similar business model, OBSs such as CarsDirect.com also serve as matchmakers by offering prenegotiated prices on new vehicles to consumers. In these roles, referral intermediaries and matchmakers provide dealers with an alternate channel for customer acquisition in exchange for a referral fee. Additionally, they serve as infomediaries by providing information relating to various facets of the purchase process. In particular, some OBSs focus on providing price-related information, while others focus on providing comparative product-related information—i.e., the two basic types of information provided in the online environment.

Manufacturers’ Websites: In addition to OBSs, car manufacturers have established their own websites that provide extensive information about their brands and connect consumers to dealers. These websites serve as the most reliable source of information on vehicle features and provide services such as recall notices and scheduled maintenance reminders. Furthermore, websites such as GMBuyPower enable users to search inventory and obtain price quotes from dealers.

Dealers’ Websites: Many dealers have also developed a Web presence of their own in addition to using the services of both OBSs and manufacturers. These websites function mostly as an alternative advertising medium, often lacking the objectivity of OBSs and the loyalty-building features of the manufacturers’ websites.

Newspaper Websites: Traditional newspapers have also migrated the content of their paper editions to online sites. However, these sites tend to closely mirror the paper format and offer limited original content.

As evident from this brief discussion, manufacturers’ and dealers’ websites serve to complement and reinforce their traditional customer relationship and positioning strategies, while independent OBSs perform the key role of integrating demand generation and demand fulfillment processes. An important aspect of the informational value chain in the auto retailing context is that these different OISs offer differentiated bundles of information related to the automobile purchase process. Given the number and variety of players, the auto retailing industry provides a rich context in which to study information search in a technology-mediated environment and the resulting implications for purchase-related outcomes.

## 3. Related Research

We review three streams of literature that are directly related to our study. We begin with an overview of research examining multichannel retailing. This is followed by a brief discussion of research directly addressing the role of online information search. Finally, we provide background information about the needs fulfillment framework that forms the conceptual framework for this study.

## 3.1. Multichannel Retailing

Past research on multichannel retailing has largely focused on issues of optimal channel structure, in which channels are designed to improve the “fit” between channel characteristics, product characteristics, and consumer needs (Rangan 1987, Anderson et al. 1997, Fisher 1997). Although different channel structures provide different degrees of support for the logistical, transactional, and informational services desired by the consumer, consumers increasingly navigate multiple channels for purchasing goods and services. Three outcomes relevant to multichannel retailing include (1) channel choice, (2) the relationship between online search and offline search, and (3) online search satisfaction.

Channel choice—i.e., whether consumers purchase online, offline, or through multiple channels—is a particularly important outcome for all types of retailers. Researchers have identified the particular customer values and behaviors associated with online purchases (Keeney 1999, Moe and Fader 2004, Torkzadeh and Dhillon 2002, Venkatesh and Agarwal 2006); thereby identifying those capabilities necessary for organizations to succeed with electronic commerce. For offline purchases, researchers have investigated the issue of “free riding” or research shopping in which individuals consume information at online sites but choose to purchase offline. Lack of online channel lock-in and cross-channel synergies have been identified as drivers of this phenomena (Verhoef et al. 2007, van Baal and Dach 2005). Customers who purchase from multiple channels have been found to be the most profitable segment, with behaviors directly linked to both demographics and shopping traits (Kushwaha and Shankar 2007).

It should be noted that purchase through online infomediaries—i.e., referred purchase—does not directly fit within the categories of online, offline, or multichannel purchase. As a result, referred purchase acts as an additional purchase outcome of interest to contexts in which online infomediaries play a role. Further, although recent research acknowledges a link between online search behavior and purchase venue (Hodkinson and Kiel 2003), a limited number of studies have been able to empirically examine relationships between online search behavior and purchase outcomes (Moe 2003, Ratchford et al. 2001). With the Internet increasing in importance as an information source, understanding the link between online information search and purchase is necessary to help firms improve their ability to turn online browsers into purchasers.

Straub et al. (2002) note that there is a greater need to understand the dynamics as well as the real nature of the interdependency between online and offline search, and recent empirical studies highlight the importance of consumer search and purchase behavior in contexts in which consumers can interact with more than one channel. Thus, another outcome of interest in the context of multichannel shopping is the extent to which online information search in the marketspace influences physical search in the marketplace. For example, Ratchford et al. (2001) find that Internet users spend a lower percentage of their overall search effort at actual dealerships but that their total amount of search is higher than nonusers. Overall though, little is known about ways in which information gathered from online channels may influence consumers’ offline search behavior.

A third outcome of interest is related to the level of consumer satisfaction resulting from the use of the Web for information search. McKinney et al. (2002) identify system quality and information quality as key drivers of satisfaction. Other system-related attributes shown to influence satisfaction include the usability of the website (Agarwal and Venkatesh 2002) and dynamics of the presentation (Hong et al. 2004). Overall, these findings have focused primarily on the role of the system characteristics rather than the information characteristics. As discussed further below, there is reason to believe that the type of information provided may have an important role in outcomes such as online search satisfaction.

## 3.2. Online Information Search

To the extent that OISs play a significant role in facilitating search and reducing transaction costs and information asymmetries for consumers, a second related stream of literature is consumer information search in both traditional as well as electronic channels. Although a complete review of the search literature is outside of the scope of this work, much of the work on search has identified search effort as a key variable of interest (e.g., Ratchford and Srinivasan 1993). However, as might be expected, a core assumption underlying consumer information search behavior is that it is triggered by specific information needs. For example, in a typical car-buying process, consumers compare offerings from many dealers that match their specific needs with regard to product attributes, price, and other factors. The complex and high-involvement nature of the purchase process coupled with heterogeneity in consumers’ needs and utility for different types of information (DeSarbo et al. 2001) suggests that the information sought by consumers and provided by OISs will be multidimensional in nature.

As noted earlier, price-related and product-related information are the two basic types of online information that consumers seek in their purchase process. Studies examining consumer use of OISs report differences in outcomes resulting from the availability of price vis-à-vis product information (e.g., Bakos 1997, Lynch and Ariely 2000). For example, studies examining the impact of online price information find that providing price information leads to more competitive pricing by firms and lower average prices for consumers (Smith and Brynjolfsson 2001, Baye and Morgan 2001, Baye et al. 2003, Chen et al. 2002). In contrast, research examining the role of productrelated information finds that customers are less price sensitive when provided with product quality information (Alba et al. 1997, Lynch and Ariely 2000, Viswanathan et al. 2007). Collectively, these findings suggest that an examination of the search process must account for both the underlying needs as well as the type of information obtained during the search process.

## 3.3. The Needs Fulfillment Framework

Our study draws on the needs fulfillment framework which suggests that a “fit” between the individual’s needs and the outcomes of actions to satisfy the needs (information retrieval in this case) results in needs fulfillment (Oliver 1995). Human needs have a rich and long tradition of being used as a theoretical foundation to understanding the complexities of human motivation and action (e.g., Deci and Ryan 2000, Maslow 1954, Sheldon et al. 2001). The needs fulfillment model uses consumers’ fundamental needs as the comparison operator (Oliver 1997) with needs being present-oriented and relatively stable (Spreng et al. 1996). In the context of the consumption of goods, needs have been used as a basis for new product development (e.g., Lilien et al. 2002). In the context of information systems, needs have been used as a basis for both system design (e.g., Batiste and Jung 1984, Markus et al. 2002) and humancomputer interface design (e.g., Sawin et al. 2002). Taken together, these studies suggest that the needs fulfillment framework provides a robust theoretical foundation through which to understand online information search and the resulting shopping-related outcomes. Building on this theoretical foundation, in the next section our study uses needs fulfillment as the underlying mechanism for relating consumers’ underlying preferences and search in the online marketspace to consumer outcomes in the context of the new car shopping process.

## 4. Theoretical Model and Research Hypotheses

Figure 1 summarizes the overall theoretical model underlying this study. Information needs are defined as the extent to which a consumer expresses a need for price or product information, and online information retrieval is the extent to which a consumer obtains price or product information online. Following from the needs fulfillment framework, the fit between the level of needs and the actions of individuals to fulfill needs (i.e., information retrieval) is expected to influence three different shopping-related outcomes: (1) referred purchase, (2) offline search intensity, and (3) online search satisfaction. For each of these outcomes, we examine both the direct effect of the retrieval of price and product information as well as the moderating role of information needs. We include an extensive set of relevant controls in the model to ensure that differences in shopping outcomes are a result of information-based effects rather than effects related to selection (demographics and psychographics), search effort, or Internet expertise. Theoretical arguments for the posited effects are developed below.

4.1. Information Needs and Information Retrieval The core tenet of needs fulfillment theory is that consumers whose information needs are not met by their information retrieval are likely to suffer a loss of utility or a potential misfit cost. On the other hand, obtaining information without an identified need will also not result in outcomes such as increased satisfaction levels (Oliver 1995). This phenomenon has been characterized as “information overload”—when the information provided exceeds what the individual needs, it reduces the resulting positive impact of the information (Lurie 2004). Thus, information needs are expected to moderate the relationship between information retrieval and referred purchase, offline search intensity, and online search satisfaction for both price and product information, as shown in Figure 1. In other words, consumers’ need for information regulates the utility of the information that is obtained during the online search process.

## 4.2. Referred Purchase

A key outcome of the online consumer search process is referred purchase, in which the consumer’s purchase is consummated using a referral provided by the online infomediary. The information available to buyers in their purchase process has a direct effect on the formation of predictive expectations of perceived performance, which in turn influences key behavioral outcomes such as purchase (Oliver 1997). Simply stated, consumers who have been provided information are more likely to view the infomediary referral favorably. However, we expect differences in the extent to which price and product information prepare consumers to act on these referrals.

In general, obtaining more price information online is likely to be positively associated with referred purchase. Price information allows consumers to be able to make an informed decision with regard to the infomediary’s recommendation. This is consistent with the growing evidence from the pricing literature about the impact of reference prices on consumer perceptions (Hardesty and Suter 2005). The presence of reference prices is important to enhance the offer value of “deals” (Krishna et al. 2002) and leads to purchase (Chang and Wildt 1994). Given the ease of price comparison online, greater price information allows consumers to screen out nonviable alternatives and more effectively move toward terminating the search process. We therefore test:

Hypothesis 1A (H1A). Online price information retrieval is positively associated with referred purchase.

We further expect the relationship between online price information retrieval and referred purchase to be positively moderated by the level of need for price information. A greater need for price information implies greater consumer uncertainty about the “value” of an offer. As consumers who have greater need for price information find information that “fits” their needs, they are likely to have greater valuation for the information, will perceive themselves to be significantly better informed to terminate the search, and are therefore more liable to act on online offers. This general concept has been applied to multichannel search to explain why individuals choose one channel over another (Neslin et al. 2006). As noted by Balasubramanian et al. (2005), when a channel meets the needs of a consumer through various aspects of the shopping process, it increases the utility of purchasing through the online channel. Hence, we test:

Hypothesis 1B (H1B). The relationship between online price information retrieval and referred purchase is positively moderated by consumers’ need for price information.

In contrast to price information, we expect product information to be negatively related to referred purchase. The online channel is limited in its ability to provide adequate product-related information for consumers to be able to converge on the specific offer made by the OBS. This is particularly true for experience goods such as cars where the physical look and feel of the product cannot be fully reproduced in a digital format. As suggested by past research, the high levels of cognitive effort needed to evaluate specific brands may decrease the likelihood of a purchase decision being reached (Garbarino and Edell 1997). In general, an evaluation of the product consideration set—referring to the set of alternatives over which consumers make an explicit evaluation— is a costly and time-consuming process (Mehta et al.

2003). Thus, consumers retrieving product information online are less likely to terminate the purchase process by accepting a referral and more likely to continue their search offline to ensure that the available alternatives are appropriately evaluated. Therefore, we test:

Hypothesis 2A (H2A). Online product information retrieval is negatively associated with referred purchase.

As with price information, the relationship between online product information retrieval and referred purchase is expected to be positively moderated by the level of need for product information. We argued above that the relationship between product information retrieval and referred purchase is negative; the expectation of positive moderation implies that for a given level of information retrieval, referred purchase will be less likely when needs are high than when needs are low. Higher levels of product information need signal a greater level of uncertainty in the product consideration set, whereas lower levels of product information need signal less uncertainty. As a result, finding product information online for individuals with high levels of product information need is less likely to lead to purchase decision within the online search space (i.e., referred purchase) than if the level of product information need is low. It is also likely that consumers with greater needs for product information have a stronger desire to obtain a better product fit and seek physical validation of their product choice. Thus, the negative relationship between product information retrieval and referred purchase will be amplified in the presence of greater product information needs. We therefore test:

Hypothesis 2B (H2B). The relationship between online product information retrieval and referred purchase is positively moderated by consumers’ need for product information.

## 4.3. Offline Search Intensity

A second consequence of online information retrieval that is of interest is its impact on offline search. In general, online search provides access to greater amounts of information for consumer decision making, and the interactive nature of the Web environment further supports deep nonlinear searches initiated and controlled by customers (Kulviwat et al. 2004). Thus, it is likely that online search occurs earlier in the purchase process and shapes subsequent offline search, i.e., the retrieval of information in the marketspace influences marketplace search behaviors.

We expect the online retrieval of price information to be positively associated with marketplace search intensity. An important distinction between online and offline channels is the relative capability of the channels in providing different types of information. When searching in the online channel, consumers can quickly access relevant price information (Bakos 1997). In contrast, obtaining price information for complex products such as cars is significantly more difficult in the offline channel (Goldberg 1996). Because dealers are aware that searching in the marketplace is more costly than searching in the marketspace, they typically charge a higher price for consumers negotiating offline (Scott Morton et al. 2001). Thus, while online price retrieval increases the likelihood of a referred purchase, consumers who continue their search for price information in the marketplace would need to visit a larger number of dealers to obtain a better price than that retrieved through their online search process. As a result, we test:

Hypothesis 3A (H3A). Online price information retrieval is positively associated with marketplace search intensity.

We also expect the relationship between online price information retrieval and marketplace search to be positively moderated by the level of need for price information. Research has found that consumer characteristics that indicate a need for online services may be associated with the value obtained from online services (Scott Morton et al. 2001). When a consumer with a high need for price information continues to search offline, he/she would need to visit a larger number of physical dealers to obtain a sufficiently low-price offering than the one obtained through online search. However, when information needs are low, the information retrieved online may be sufficient for the consumer to reach a purchase decision, reducing the number of physical dealer visits necessary. Therefore, we test:

Hypothesis 3B (H3B). The relationship between online price information retrieval and marketplace search intensity is positively moderated by consumers’ need for price information.

Online product information retrieval is likely to have the opposite effect of price information, decreasing the level of marketplace search intensity. With the variety of options available, vehicle selection is perhaps the most difficult part of the shopping process. As described earlier, a great deal of product-related information including detailed reviews and thirdparty ratings can be obtained from the various OISs, helping consumers prune their consideration sets to a smaller number of alternatives. Although prices vary substantially across dealers necessitating more dealer visits for price information, product information for a vehicle in the consideration set of a consumer does not vary substantially across dealers. Thus, consumers who retrieve more product information online would need to visit fewer dealers to reach a purchase decision. Therefore, we expect:

Hypothesis 4A (H4A). Online product information retrieval is negatively associated with marketplace search intensity.

As in the case of referred purchase, we expect the relationship between online product information retrieval and marketplace search to be positively moderated by the level of need for product information. We argued above that the relationship between product information retrieval and marketplace search intensity is negative; the expectation of positive moderation implies that for a given level in information retrieval, consumers with high product information needs will search even less in the physical marketplace than consumers with low product information needs. Following a logic similar to the one described previously, the expectation for this relationship is based on the differing level of utility provided when needs are high as compared to when needs are low. When information needs are high, online information retrieval is likely to be more important in decreasing the total number of dealerships a consumer would have to visit to make a vehicle selection. When product information need is low, however, the information retrieved online will have a smaller influence on decreasing the amount of offline search because a vehicle is already likely to be selected and the consumer would have no reason to visit multiple dealerships. We therefore test:

Hypothesis 4B (H4B). The relationship between product information retrieval and marketplace search intensity is positively moderated by consumers’ need for product information.

## 4.4. Online Search Satisfaction

Online search satisfaction is formally defined as a summary affective response of varying intensity, with a time-specific point of determination, and directed toward focal aspects of the information retrieval process online (Giese and Cote 2000). In general, the retrieval of information by consumers will enable them to improve their decision making related to the vehicle and retailer selected, thus yielding greater satisfaction.<sup>1</sup>

We expect that the level of information needs will positively moderate the relationship between information retrieval and online search satisfaction for both product and price-related information. In the context of the automobile purchase process, the level or strength of needs for price or product information indicates a consumer’s desire to obtain a low price or find a product that exactly fits his/her needs, respectively. As a consequence, he/she would derive greater utility from information that satisfies specific information needs than when obtaining information which is not needed. Following this logic, we test:

Hypothesis 5A (H5A). The relationship between online price information retrieval and online search satisfaction is positively moderated by consumers’ need for price information.

Hypothesis 5B (H5B). The relationship between online product information retrieval and online search satisfaction is positively moderated by consumers’ need for product information.

In addition to the direct effect of information needs fulfillment on online search satisfaction, we suggest an additional relationship between referred purchase and online search satisfaction. Referred purchase indicates that a customer accepted an offer mediated by an infomediary, implying that the consumer evaluated this mediated offer as superior to other available choices. To the extent that the consumer would ascribe this value to the search process, and that there is inherent gratification in the search process coming to a productive end, he/she would be more satisfied than other consumers not receiving their preferred offer through online search. Hence:

Hypothesis 6A (H6A). Referred purchase is positively associated with online search satisfaction.

Finally, we expect a direct negative relationship between marketplace search intensity and online search satisfaction. Consumers seeking to purchase cars typically search online before visiting physical dealers. As a result, information obtained from offline dealer visits often supplements information not adequately found in the online channel. When offline search is extensive, outcomes of search are likely to be compared to outcomes found online. Because the offline search processes may be costly for consumers and because the extent of offline search is related to deficiencies in the online channel, increased marketplace search intensity should lead to lower levels of online search satisfaction.

Hypothesis 6B (H6B). Marketplace search intensity is negatively associated with online search satisfaction.

## 4.5. Controls

We include an extensive set of controls in the model to ensure that we capture the information-based effects on the shopping outcomes. We first discuss controls associated with demographics, psychographics, search effort, and Internet expertise used in equations predicting purchase-related outcomes and information retrieval. We then discuss how additional controls related to the portfolio of OISs used may influence the consumers’ retrieval of price and product information.

Demographics: In predicting the shopping outcomes discussed, we control for the age, education, income, gender, and race of the consumers. Older consumers may be less motivated to search because of their accumulated investments in brand-specific knowledge (Maddox et al. 1978, Ratchford 2001) and well-developed heuristics that obviate the need for extended information search (Bettman 1979). Consumers with high education and income levels have shown evidence of more extensive search (Kiel and

Layton 1981) and larger consideration sets (Maddox et al. 1978). There is also evidence of discrimination by new vehicle dealers on the basis of physical characteristics such as gender and race (Scott Morton et al. 2003, Ayres and Siegelman 1995), and Furse et al. (1984) find that inexperienced, single, less-educated, lower income, and female buyers tend to rely on the advice of others in their car purchase.

Psychographics: Psychographics capture a broad spectrum of individual traits and have been found to be important drivers of consumer behavior (e.g., Kunreuther and Ginsberg 1978, Pennings and Smidts 2000). In predicting the shopping outcomes discussed, we control for the price sensitivity and the involvement of consumers. Price sensitivity refers to the weight attached to price in consumer valuation of a product’s overall attractiveness. Involvement refers to the intensity of interest with which consumers approach their dealings with the marketplace (Laurent and Kapferer 1985) and is based on the extent of personal relevance of the decision to the individual in terms of basic values, goals, and self-concept (Zaichkowsky 1985). The salience of these dimensions has been validated in prior research in the auto retailing context (Zettelmeyer et al. 2004).

Search Effort and Internet Expertise: We also control for consumers’ search effort and Internet-related expertise in examining the identified outcomes of search. Search effort has been examined as a foundational driver of shopping outcomes (Charnes and Cooper 1958) and has been extensively studied in the context of auto retailing (Ratchford et al. 2003, Ratchford and Srinivasan 1993). The efficacy of consumer information retrieval, however, depends on consumers’ search effort and their competencies in using OISs. In general, expertise is considered a prime determinant of search behavior because it influences one’s ability to learn about products and the costs of doing so (Alba and Hutchinson 1987). In the online search context, Internet-related expertise affects the efficiency of the information retrieval process (Klein and Ford 2003), and consumers with high levels of expertise may be more familiar with navigation schemes and advanced features such as personalization (Johnson et al. 2003).

OIS Portfolio: In predicting the level of price and product information retrieval, we control for the portfolio of OISs visited—including whether the consumer visited manufacturers, dealers, newspapers, product-specialist OBSs, and price-specialist OBSs. Each of these five types of OISs in the auto retailing context are differentiated in their motivations and ability to supply different categories of information. For example, although manufacturers allow a consumer to custom configure a car and evaluate the cost of multiple vehicle options, they typically do not provide price information that involves cross-make comparisons or offer insights into actual “street” prices much beyond suggested retail prices. Dealers usually provide pricing information for their inventory and product information for the brands that they sell. Newspaper sites provide links and referrals to a variety of dealers; however, the information about vehicles is limited due to content being tied to their print versions. In contrast, independent OBSs generally provide the best coverage of information because they are positioned as neutral third parties and offer unbiased content. Even among the OBSs, however, distinctions exist. Past research and industry literature discriminates between OBSs that specialize in providing price information and those that specialize in providing product information (Viswanathan et al. 2007). For example, price-specialist OBSs such as Edmunds and Kelly Blue Book provide detailed price information, while product-specialist sites such as Consumer Reports and Car and Driver provide detailed performance and reliability information.

## 5. Data and Methodology

We first describe the general characteristics of the data used. We then discuss the specific measures used in establishing the constructs. Finally, we detail the procedures used to test the overall model.

## 5.1. Data

The data for this study are drawn from secondary sources and based on surveys of new vehicle purchasers conducted by a leading market research organization. The sample was randomly selected based on registration data to reflect actual sales of new cars in 2003–2004. The sampling process was designed via quota sampling and a sales-weighting scheme to accurately reflect the overall market for new cars. An incentive of \$1 was provided for participation in the study. Two versions of the questionnaire were used with a different order of response elicitation to check for bias due to respondent fatigue or programmed responses. A total of 26,361 surveys were returned out of the 116,317 mailed out, a response rate of 24%. This data set has a number of notable features. It is representative of the overall U.S. automobile consumer population, captures details of OISs used in the consumer search process, and includes detailed questions related to consumer characteristics. In addition, the surveys are each linked to a recent automobile purchase.

The analyses presented here are restricted to consumers who reported using the Internet to aid their automobile purchase process (16,188). To further reduce the impact of model-related effects, using the vehicle identification numbers (VINs) we restricted our sample to the vehicles (make, model, trim) where at least 25 consumers bought the same vehicle, resulting in a sample of 7,050. Additional missing variables resulted in 6,971 respondents in the final sample. These respondents had an average age of 46 $( \mathsf { s } . \mathsf { d } . = 1 3 . 7 5 )$ and 61.6% were male. Their ethnicity was reported as 87.5% White, 3.4% Black/African-American, 4.3% Asian, and 3.4% Hispanic. In addition, 76.7% of the individuals reported attending at least some college, and they had an average income in the range \$80,000–\$89,999. Overall, we found that the consumer visited an average of 2.72 (s.d <sub>=</sub> 247) OISs during their search process, and information need and information retrieval were higher for price information than for product information. A full summary of descriptive statistics can be found in Table A.1. Nonresponse bias was assessed by comparing the incidence of referred purchase between respondents and nonrespondents for each customer characteristic. No significant differences were detected for any demographic or psychographic variable (p > 005 for all t-tests).

## 5.2. Measures and Constructs

The measures for the survey were developed by market research professionals with extensive knowledge of auto retailing. Information needs were measured through a list of eight questions (five for price and three for product) in which respondents indicated the importance of different information on a 10-point scale. An example of price-related information includes “dealer cost/invoice of new vehicle,” and an example of product information includes “road test and reviews about vehicles by automotive writers.” Information retrieval was then captured by a binary variable in which the respondent indicated whether he/she had obtained the information online. As a result, there was a direct correspondence between the questions used to assess information needs and information retrieval.

To establish the convergent and divergent validity for the price and product information measures, we used both principal component analysis and confirmatory factor analysis (using EQS version 6.1). The principal component analysis indicated appropriate construct loading, with measures for need and retrieval of price information loading together and measures for need and retrieval of product information loading together (see Table A.2). The confirmatory factor analysis included (1) vehicle information need, (2) price information need, (3) vehicle information retrieval, and (4) price information retrieval. The overall fit for the measurement model was as follows: Chi-square <sub>=</sub> 627648 (p < 0001); RMSEA <sub>=</sub> 0041 (0.038–0.044); CFI <sub>=</sub> 0978; SRMR <sub>=</sub> 0066; NFI <sub>=</sub> 0975; NNFI <sub>=</sub> 0968; IFI <sub>=</sub> 0978. This level of fit exceeds the three criteria suggested by Hu and Bentler (1999). The RMSEA was less than 0.06, the CFI greater than 0.96, and the SRMR less than 0.10. Overall, these results support the convergent and discriminant validity of the two categories of information.

For controls, single-item measures were used for demographics (race, gender, age, education, income) as well as for search expertise and search effort. Psychographic traits were captured with five questions (two for involvement and three for price sensitivity) in which the respondents indicated their level of agreement with statements about themselves on a five-point scale. We confirmed involvement and price sensitivity as distinct dimensions of consumer psychographics using principal component analysis (Table A.3). Finally, respondents provided extensive details on the portfolio of OISs visited, including the manufacturers, dealers, newspapers, and OBSs visited. Manufacturer, dealer, and newspaper sites were each coded as binary variables indicating whether the respondent had visited that type of site. OBSs were categorized based on the type of information they provide—i.e., those that primarily provide product information and those that primarily provide price information. This was done independently by the authors by examining each OBS website. Sites in which there was any disagreement were not used. The final list of sites used was verified by industry experts. Product specialist OBSs include sites such as Car and Driver and Consumer Reports, while price specialist OBSs included such sites as Edmunds and Kelly Blue Book.

## 5.3. Analysis

Our empirical analysis is based on three-stage least squares (3SLS) estimation. The 3SLS procedure is used to derive the parameters of the full system because endogenous variables in some equations of the model are used as explanatory variables in other equations. Further, there is a possibility of correlation among error terms across regression equations due to each case being based on data from the same respondent. 3SLS combines two-stage least squares (2SLS) and seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) methods to take into account both dependent regressors and cross-equation correlation of errors, and 3SLS is the recommended approach for triangular structural systems (Lahiri and Schmidt 1978), as in this study. We centered the information needs and retrieval variables to address the possibility of multicollinearity due to inclusion of interaction terms for testing the moderation hypothesis. Because referred purchase is a dichotomous variable, we follow Angrist (2001) by using the predicted values from a probit specification. The probit model includes four exogenous variables to predict the probability of referred purchase—the impact of online search on make-model choice, dealer choice, price paid, and financing choice.

## 6.2. The Outcomes of Information Retrieval and Information Needs

Table 2 summarizes the results of the 3SLS analysis which are used to test the main research hypotheses. Finding greater price information is associated with a higher likelihood of a referred purchase (H1A), while finding product information significantly reduces the likelihood of referred purchase (H2A). For marketplace search, finding more price information online leads to more physical dealer visits (H3A), while finding more product information online results in fewer physical dealer visits (H4A). Although not specifically hypothesized, retrieval of product information online leads to greater online search satisfaction, while retrieval of more price information does not lead to significantly greater levels of satisfaction. Although referred purchase leads to greater levels of online search satisfaction (H6A), greater marketplace search intensity does not significantly impact online search satisfaction (H6B).

Table 2 shows mixed results for the hypotheses that information needs moderate the impact of information retrieval on shopping outcomes. The findings suggest that price information needs significantly moderate the impact of price information retrieval on marketplace search intensity (H3B), but do not for referred purchase (H1B) or online search satisfaction (H5A). Product information needs significantly moderate the impact of product information retrieval on marketplace search (H4B) and online search satisfaction (H5B), but not on referred purchase (H2B). ## 7. Discussion and Implications

This study was motivated by the observation that consumer information search processes in a multichannel environment and the interplay between marketplace search, marketspace search, and other shopping outcomes are not well understood. We investigated the contingent nature of the relationship between information needs and information retrieval, arguing that greater fit between these two would create more value for consumers (in the form of online search satisfaction and lowered need for physical search) and for automobile dealers (through referred purchase). Further, we examined the differences in these outcomes resulting from consumers finding and needing different information categories—i.e., price versus product information. Overall, our empirical results provide support for the majority of hypothesized relationships.

## 7.1. Limitations

Prior to discussing the implications of the findings, we acknowledge the limitations of this work. First, our data are cross-sectional, and although causality is implied in our theoretical model, the data does not permit truly causal inferences. Second, the study is based on responses collected through a single survey instrument, leading to the possibility of a common method bias. The likelihood of this bias is somewhat mitigated by tying the survey closely to a verified car purchase. Harmon’s single factor test (Podsakoff and Organ 1986) also suggests that a substantial amount of common method variance in our data set is unlikely. There is a possibility of a recall bias because the consumers reported on their online search process after the purchase. On the other hand, the retrospective approach allows consumers to provide more deliberate responses covering their completed search.

Finally, although we performed a confirmatory factor analysis to establish the validity of the multidimensional information needs and information retrieval categories, we have not tested the measurement and structural models simultaneously. An alternative approach would have been to test both the measurement and structural models using structural equation modeling. We did not attempt this due to a complex model as well as the presence of categorical variables and interaction terms leading to a violation of the required multivariate normality assumptions (Schumaker and Marcoulides 1998). The procedures needed to accommodate these issues are very demanding and have not yet been fully developed in the literature (Goerzen and Beamish 2003, p. 1295). Alternative estimators such as asymptotically distribution-free estimators that can handle departures from normality are known to be computationally demanding (recognized as becoming impractical beyond more than 20– 25 measured variables), and have not been proven to recover population estimates consistently (Hoyle 1995, p. 64). We should also point out that we cannot rule out some alternate explanations for the findings related to product information. There may be some unobserved vehicle or consumer characteristics that drive both the search for online product information as well as the need for sensory gratification that is fulfilled by physical marketplace searches. However, our study controls for consumer involvement, gender, income, and education, and search competence, which would be expected to be linked to these outcomes.

## 7.2. Implications

The findings of our study have several implications for researchers and managers. For researchers, our study proposed and empirically tested a model that encompasses online information search, information retrieval, and shopping-related outcomes. Past research has suggested that the consumer satisfaction paradigm can provide rich insights into the processes that affect a user’s postusage cognitive evaluation of an information service (Oliver 1997, Susarla et al. 2003). However, much of this work has typically focused on a single information source and on userinterface aspects. We adapted the basic needs fulfillment framework to the context of online information. As a result, we are able to better understand consumers’ interactions with a portfolio of information providers in the online setting and how search extends from the marketspace to the marketplace. Further, in contrast to prior studies that have largely focused on the referral services provided by online intermediaries in the auto retailing context (e.g., Chen et al. 2002), our study sheds light on the less understood information-provision role of these intermediaries. More importantly, our findings highlight the importance of not treating information needs as an undifferentiated bundle, but rather examining them in terms of relatively fine-grained categories.

For managers of firms in the auto retailing sector, results underscore the value of understanding how consumers’ information needs and information search strategies influence their use of online as well as offline channels. Consumers who seek and find price-related information are more likely to purchase based on the referral provided by the online infomediary. On the other hand, consumers who seek and find product-related information are less likely to purchase based on online referrals. These differences in consumer behavior have important implications for revenue models, linkages among players, and the overall information provisioning strategies for the intermediaries in the auto industry.

Currently, the two dominant revenue models for OBSs are fees for referrals provided to traditional dealers and revenues from advertising by manufacturers. Our findings imply that OBSs that provide price information would be more successful as referral intermediaries because consumers using them are more likely to purchase based on the referrals provided by them. In contrast, product-specialist OBSs are less likely to be successful in their referral services. However, they would be more attractive to advertisers and manufacturers because their customers are more likely to seek product-related information. Consequently, product-specialist OBSs would benefit more from advertising-based revenue models as compared to price-specialists OBSs.

Our findings also have implications for the linkages between channel members in the online marketspace and the traditional marketplace. Although traditional dealers may be inclined to limit their presence on such price-specialist infomediaries (because they could expect that consumers will be better informed on price), our findings suggest that these partnerships may be more beneficial for referral relationships. On the other hand, consumers using product-specialist infomediaries are less likely to use online referrals, but may be more open to invitations to visit the dealership to further examine the vehicles. As a result, traditional dealers might benefit from closer advertising linkages to online infomediaries providing product information compared to those providing price information. This assumes greater importance in light of recent industry reports that highlight the growing significance of the distinction between price and product information online: “Initially, the Internet was primarily thought of as a tool for getting the best price on a vehicle. Today, consumers are even more impacted by the Internet in which vehicle they buy than how much they pay for it” (Power 2005).

Given the complex and high involvement nature of the typical automobile purchase, it is likely that the information provided by product specialists is being sought by consumers to get a better product fit. Our findings suggest that product specialists need to facilitate a seamless transition for the consumers from the marketspace to physical dealers. More importantly, information provision strategies and cross-channel services that integrate and exploit the complementarities of the online and offline channels would benefit online infomediaries specializing in product information. This is particularly important given the rapidly changing structure of the auto retailing industry. Although legislation has thus far protected auto dealers’ position in the value chain, a recent article in The Economist (2006) suggests that the emergence of online intermediaries that sell cars directly to consumers is likely to further revolutionize auto retailing. Our findings indicate that such sites promoting direct online sales of cars are more likely to appeal to consumers seeking price information because they have a greater likelihood of consummating their purchase online.

Another distinction between price and product information lies in the different patterns of the moderating effects of information needs. The results suggest that when needs are high, retrieval of both price and product information is associated with greater satisfaction. However, the moderating effect is significant only for product information—with high needs shifting the incremental effect of information retrieval upwards. This implies that at the margin, finding incremental product information is more valuable for consumers who have a greater need for it. An implication for this finding is that product information should be selectively targeted toward those that value it more, and OBSs providing such information should strive to craft their promotional campaigns and messages for these consumers. Although consumers purchase vehicles relatively infrequently, OBSs can provide continuing value through product information about accessories, enhancements, and future vehicle introductions.

## 8. Conclusion

As Peterson and Merino (2003, p. 100) observe, “There is a paucity of systematic conceptual, analytical, or empirical research indicating that the Internet will in fact lead to more and better information, which in turn will lead to better consumer decision making.    likewise, the manner in which consumers search for, process, and use information is a complex phenomenon that is not completely understood.” The study reported here is among the first to examine how consumers’ use of online informational sources influences their behavior in traditional channels, offering insight into how consumers straddle the marketspace and the marketplace and make choices in regard to where to acquire information and when to terminate the search process.

Many opportunities for future research remain. Our focus has been on the auto retailing context, but similar channel choice issues arise in other contexts. Future research can validate this model in other industries. Recent research has alluded to the promise of information-based strategies, suggesting that “informational competence” may be an important component of operational excellence for intermediaries (Balasubramaniam et al. 2003). Extending the theoretical model proposed here with informational competence as an infomediary characteristic might yield additional insight into consumer information retrieval. Future research can also explore more fine-grained typologies of information needs and consumer psychographic dimensions.

A second avenue for future research is to examine dependencies between the information provision strategies of infomediaries and their relationships with dealers. Our study focused on the demand side of online infomediaries; however, an equally important issue is the relational linkage between online infomediaries and traditional auto dealers as well as the resulting implications for end-consumers. Examining infomediary strategies on the supply side promises to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the dynamics of the value chain.

Third, future research can test alternative specifications for a subset of variables to analyze nonlinear relationships. Ratchford et al. (2003), for example, modeled consumer age with four different intervals in the prediction of Internet search effort. Such finer operationalization may be fruitful if the results can be interpreted and theoretically supported. Researchers can also consider the stage of a consumer in the overall search process to better understand differences in information needs.

Finally, there are several other ways in which the theoretical model tested in this study can be extended. For example, variables related to negotiations with the dealer, as well as personal relationships as a source of information, could be considered. We did not consider the cognitive costs (Shugan 1980) associated with using OISs that may have a significant impact on consumer satisfaction. Recent research suggests that the usability of Web interfaces plays an important role in shaping consumer perceptions related to quality of information, attitudes toward the site, repeat visits, and overall consumer satisfaction (Agarwal and Venkatesh 2002). Future research can examine website usability in terms of characteristics such as ease of use, in addition to the information provided by OISs.

In conclusion, the importance of developments in the information space for firms as well as consumers cannot be overemphasized. Value-creation and extraction in the “information marketspace” is very different from that in the traditional marketplace, necessitating a radical shift in thinking (Rayport and Sviokla 1994). This study, drawing on a large data set in the retail auto industry, sheds light on the dynamics underlying consumer behavior in the context of new digital businesses. It underscores the need for a deeper understanding of consumer need fulfillment in the evolving information marketspace and in the context of multichannel information search.
