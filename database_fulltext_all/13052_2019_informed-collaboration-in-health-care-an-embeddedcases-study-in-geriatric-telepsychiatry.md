# Informed collaboration in health care: An embedded‐cases study in geriatric telepsychiatry

## Abstract

Telemedicine, the delivery of medical consultation and services, is enabled by information and communications technologies. Participants in telemedicine consultation services (ie, clinicians, patients, and other parties) use video conferencing, special‐purpose software and/or hardware, and data and notes from electronic medical records and other systems to formulate effective treatment plans. Telemedicine is a particular mode of informed collaboration—information and communications technology–supported collaborative activity in which collaborators rely (wholly or in part) on digitally stored information retrieved from various sources to pursue their collective objectives. We report on an embedded‐cases study of a long‐running geriatric telepsychiatry clinic in the north‐eastern United States. Our analysis draws on the theory of distributed cognition (DCog) to explicate the ways in which information and cognitive effort are distributed across individuals and artefacts to support psychiatric consultation from a distance and how these processes play out over time in routines. On the basis of this case study, we offer a refinement of DCog theory, focusing on four interrelated dynamic processes that, we propose, act as critical enablers of informed collaboration: cognitive offloading, visibility of action, intersubjective understanding, and knowledge redundancy. We discuss practical implications for designing telemedicine services and tools, and we offer suggestions for further research on informed collaboration.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

In this paper, we present findings from an embedded‐cases study of a long‐running geriatric telepsychiatry clinic provided by a city‐based tertiary‐care hospital, “RuralHub,” to rural nursing homes in the north‐eastern United States. The study contributions are both theoretical and practical. First, we explicate how information and cognitive effort are distributed across individuals and artefacts to support patient‐present telepsychiatric consultation and how these processes play out over time in routines. Second, we refine the theory of distributed cognition (DCog) (Hollan, Hutchins, & Kirsh, 2000; Hutchins, 1995). Third, building on our theoretical insights, we identify and discus implications for informed collaboration environments, tools, and practices.

We propose the term informed collaboration to refer to information and communications technology (ICT)–supported collaborative work in which participants rely (wholly or in part) on digitally stored information retrieved from various sources. The modifier informed is inspired by Zuboff's (1988) coining of the word “informate” to describe how workers both use system‐produced digital information and contribute information to the system Among other things, Zuboff observed that to use digitally stored information and tools effectively, workers needed to acquire new knowledge and skills. In many contexts today—in business, education, health care, and other sectors—technology‐mediated synchronous collaboration involves a great variety and volume of information and data (which requires new knowledge and skill to be used effectively).

In the decades since Zuboff's seminal work, software, hardware, and networking technologies evolved dramati cally to alter the transparency of interaction between physical and virtual worlds. Many collaborators today experi ence little dissonance in their work‐related technology‐mediated encounters; they feel physically distant partners are real and present (Mueller, Hutter, Fueller, & Matzler, 2011; Schultze, 2010),<sup>2</sup> as do parents and grandparents who use interactive video tools like Skype or Facetime to virtually “hang out” with their families from a distance As technologies for collaboration and information exchange continue to evolve, the knowledge and skills needed to work effectively are similarly transformed.

Thus, informed collaboration that involves synchronous, technology‐mediated interaction (eg, via real‐time interactive video) merits our attention, due to its potential to change, complement, or displace traditional collaboratio activities. In a recent study, almost half of human resource managers reported that their organizations regularly use virtual teams—of which, 32% reported that the distribution of work is a major obstacle to successful deployment (Society for Human Resource Management, 2012). Another recent study found that 85% of corporate employees engaged in global business activity work on global virtual teams and that the top challenges they face relate to the impact technologies have on how work is accomplished (eg, decision‐making issues and participation issues) (RW<sup>3</sup>, 2016). Many questions remain with respect to how collaboration tools interact with work processes, the distributio of work, and the support of informed collaboration

In this paper, we explore a particular mode of informed collaboration—patient‐present telemedicine. Distributed cognition theory emerged as a useful sensitizing framework during our analysis of the case findings (rather than being applied to the case data a priori). Sociotechnical theories such as DCog help researchers to guard against granting primacy to ICT capabilities (Mort & Smith, 2009) by emphasizing the importance of human cognition and human interaction. They are particularly helpful for studying the interplay among humans and technological artefacts—the “mangle of practice” (Pickering, 2010). However, sociotechnical theories in general (and DCog, in particular) have been critiqued for a lack of specificity and clear conceptual boundaries (Halverson, 2002; Lamb & Kling, 2003; Majchrzak & Borys, 2001)

By closely examining informed collaboration in the context of geriatric telepsychiatry, our case study analysis supports a midrange theory (Merton, 1968) about a specific, contextualized instance of informed collaboration. We then develop conceptual scaffolding to extend this midrange focus to guide future research and theorizing and to inform practice. Consistent with prior DCog studies (Hollan et al., 2000; Hutchins, 2000), our analysi reveals multiple mechanisms for the social, structural, and temporal distribution of cognitive effort. In addition we identify four dynamic properties of distributed cognitive interaction, which we label cognitive offloading visibility of action, intersubjective understanding, and knowledge redundancy. Drawing on the case study findings, we develop three propositions concerning the temporal sequencing and interplay of DCog properties in informed collaboration. We further propose and explain the positive influence of cognitive offloading on the other three dynamic properties and consider how visibility of action and knowledge redundancy affect intersubjective understanding.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: First, we review prior research on telemedicine use and efficacy. Next, we review relevant literature on DCog theory. We then describe our research method, followed b key findings from the study. Drawing on these findings, we propose a model of DCog in informed collaboration. We discuss implications and conclude with suggestions for further research relevant to DCog in telemedicine and to other types of informed collaboration in health care and other contexts.

## 2 | TELEMEDICINE AND DISTRIBUTED COGNITION

## 2.1 | Telemedicine

Telemedicine refers to use of ICT to provide medical services at a distance (Ekeland, Bowes, & Flottorp, 2010; Perednia & Allen, 1995). Relatively few early studies tested or proposed theory (Gammon, Johannessen, Sørensen, Wynn, & Whitten, 2008), but telemedicine's clinical efficacy was demonstrated in many specialties (eg, Coelho Arnold, Nayler, Tischkowitz, & MacKay, 2005: Mever et al., 2008: Shaikh, Lehmann, Kaleida, & Cohen, 2008: Trief Teresi, Eimicke, Shea, & Weinstock, 2009), including psychiatry (Ekeland et al., 2010). The American Psychiatric Association endorses telepsychiatry, based on the accumulated evidence of its efficacy.

Many studies investigated telemedicine adoption and alignment with organizational processes (eg, Chau &, Hu 2002; Cho, Mathiassen, & Gallivan, 2009; Gilman & Stensland, 2013; Grigsby et al., 2007; Hu, Chau, Sheng, & Tam, 1999). Other studies focused on ICT elements that support telemedicine (eg, LeRouge, Garfield, & Hevner, 2002: Raian, Seidmann, & Dorsey, 2013: Tulu, Chatteriee, & Maheshwari, 2007: Wang et al., 2014), Some research focused on sociomaterial facets, such as how telemedicine becomes embedded into clinical practices and patient experiences. Telemedicine users play multiple roles within and across organizations, resulting in a plurality of rules governing actors' choices and engendering structural changes (Boonstra & Van Offenbeek, 2010) that influence (and are influenced by) the “hybrid and dynamic materiality” of ICTs and boundary relations among users' work, visibility, and skills (Barrett, Oborn, Orlikowski, & Yates, 2012; Kaufman et al., 2009).

Several telemedicine studies adopted a practice perspective, which sees knowing as taking place within the context of directed action. Mort, Finch, and May (2009), Mort, May, and Williams (2003), and Mort and Smit (2009) called for telemedicine research focusing on the localized practice of health care delivery and aiming to identify ways to reinforce (rather than undermine) relationships between patients and clinicians. Oborn, Barrett, and Davidson (2011) explored how ICT resources are integrated in and adapted to emerging multidisciplinary practices. Analysis of a telemonitoring service for chronic cardiac care (Nicolini, 2011) demonstrated how knowing is revealed through practices of verbal exchange, timing of activities, material mediation of practice, and collaboration among diverse participants. Nicolini, Mengis, and Swan (2012) argue that digital and material artefacts are not merely mechanisms for bridging professional communities; they motivate collaborative action and establish the foundationa infrastructure that enables action to occur.

Our study builds on this practice approach, by applying DCog theory to better understand how the retrieval, creation, transformation, and storage of clinical information affects chronic care in geriatric psychiatry. We recognize that a cognitive perspective must not grant primacy to ICT capabilities (Mort & Smith, 2009), and we also recognize the importance of cognition. knowing as directed action. and human relationships in the lived practice o telemedicine. Our telepsychiatry case study focused on the interplay among human and nonhuman elements—th “mangle of practice” (Pickering, 2010).

Physicians use a range of clinical reasoning strategies. Prior research revealed examples of the transfer of knowledge and skill from physicians to other clinicians (Farand, Lafrance, & Arocha, 1998)—including knowledg and skills that enabled some nurses to take on higher‐level tasks (Nicolini, 2006). Telemedicine can enable a redistribution of work among both human and nonhuman elements, and this can be “much more than a simple redistribution of what was already there” (Nicolini, 2007, p. 889). Inspired by these prior studies, we focused on how clinicians reasoning about a patient's diagnosis and treatment is distributed and transformed over time. Our analysis considered both the physical embodiment of information and the dynamic practices through which information and knowledge are enacted and revealed

## 2.2 | Cognition and distributed cognition

As we describe in Section 3, during the theoretical review portion of our data analysis process (Urquhart & Fernandez, 2013), DCog theory emerged as a key organizing framework for the study. Cognition refers to the ability to acquire, transform, store, and apply information gathered from the environment (Shettleworth, 2009). Cognitive effort thus includes such diverse functions as attention, evaluation, memory, communication, decision‐making, and problem solving (Anderson, 2005; Metzler & Shea, 2011). Within cognitive science, such activity has been specificall understood as a computational process, that is, processing information by creating and manipulating symbol systems (Johnson‐Laird, 1989; Simon, 1980). While much of cognitive science emphasizes the workings of the brain, othe theorizing expanded that focus (Osbeck, 2009; Salomon, 1997).

In a prime example of this shift, DCog theory emphasizes ways in which cognitive activity extends beyond th mind of an individual to include the interaction of multiple individuals as well as items in the external environment Distributed cognition theory arose from studies of teamwork in complex contexts, such as marine navigation (Hutchins, 1995), air traffic control (Halverson, 1995), and engineering (Rogers, 1993), in which researchers observed that information processing effort is distributed among team members and that much of a group's cognitive workload is “shouldered” by technical artefacts. By considering social, cognitive, and technological elements under one theoretical umbrella. DCog extends the unit of analysis from individual cognition to a system of human and nonhuman entitie attending to a specific objective.

Hutchins (1995) argued that cognitive processes are equally applicable to internal (mental) and externa (environmental, interpersonal) structures; he sought “to preserve a concept of cognition as computation, and … as applicable to events that involve the interaction of humans with artifacts and with other humans as it is to events that are entirely internal to individual persons …” (p. 118). He defines cognition as “the propagation of representational state across representational media” (Hutchins, 1995, p. 118). A representational state is “a configuration of the elements of a medium that can be interpreted as a representation of something” (Hutchins, 1995, p. 117). Media that carry representational states include human memory, language, and facial cues, as well as diagrams, reports, and device settings. A representational state propagates from one (human or nonhuman) medium to another, during which it may remain static or be modified. Distributed cognition theory has informed research in information systems, computer science, human‐computer interaction, software engineering (Aranda & Easterbrook, 2006; Flor & Hutchins, 1991), and computer‐supported collaborative work (Halverson, 2002; Hollan et al., 2000).

Three primary forms of cognitive distribution are proposed: social, structural, and temporal (Hollan et al., 2000 Hutchins, 2000):

• Social distribution is the interpersonal allocation of cognitive workload. Each participant plays specific roles with respect to creating or using information and initiating action. For example, per Hutchins (1995), navigation of an aircraft carrier requires input from a navigator, quartermaster, bearing recorders, plotters, and others. The set o cognitive tasks necessary for navigation are not localized to any one participant.

• Structural distribution involves the interplay of human cognition and structural elements, including aspects of the natural setting—such as visible landmarks to determine position in space—and artefacts designed to suppor knowledge work—such as a calculator for mathematical computations. Distributed cognition thus focuses on cognition “in the wild”—how collaborators incorporate structural elements into their collective thought processes.

• Temporal distribution: Earlier actions and outcomes influence subsequent cognitive effort. When a physicist undertakes a new line of research, she need not explore and test fundamental physical properties of a material; she can retrieve information from existing texts, to begin where prior research left off. Similarly, information she creates and stores in an artefact (such as a journal paper) can be subsequently retrieved and used by others.

Few studies have closely examined how social, structural, and temporal distributions interact. Yet researchers (eg, Hazlehurst, Gorman, & McMullen, 2008; Patel, Zhang, Yoskowitz, Green, & Sayan, 2008) have argued that collective knowledge‐sharing processes across humans and artefacts do need to be studied in the context of health care. A strength of the DCog perspective, in our view, is that by focusing closely on how information is retrieved, used, created, and transformed, DCog complements other sociotechnical theories that focus on the larger interplay and relationships among stakeholders, technologies, and institutions. Significant theoretical perspectives in this domain are summarized in Table 1. Compared with DCog, some theories focus more on one side of the social‐structural divide or on relatively narrow aspects of cognitive activity. For example, actor network theory studies tend to focus on why certain sociotechnical networks emerge or gain dominance, with less emphasis on how actors interact to process information (Fomin & De Vaujany, 2008). The theory of transactive memory systems (TMS) emphasizes how one cognitive function (memory) is distributed among members of a work group (ie, social distribution), whereas DCog simultaneously considers social structural and temporal aspects of diverse cognitive activities in collaborative processes and contexts

Critiques of DCog theory express concern that it treats human and artificial elements as conceptually equivalent “nodes in a system” (Nardi, 1998, p. 87). Halverson (2002) notes that DCog theory suffers from a relative dearth of named constructs. However, a strength of DCog theory is that it offers the flexibility to consider the richness of interactions that emerge in a collaboration context. By focusing closely on cognition within a social system, DCog analysis can complement other sociotechnical theories within the broader domain of research addressing the interplay of stakeholders, technologies, and institutions.

Health care practice often involves informed collaboration, with extensive information flow between human anc nonhuman elements and widely distributed information processing. During a consultation, clinicians, patients, anc family members may individually and collectively contribute information about a patient's history or current condi tion. Human participants rely also on information from diverse artefacts, such as blood pressure cuffs, test results, vital‐signs monitors, and clinical notes. Representational fidelity (ie, extent to which a signal accurately depicts what it purports to represent) is a challenge, as is temporality; while some needed information is available in real time (synchronous), other captured information may not be available until later (asynchronous)—sometimes too late (as when a patient with a serious allergy dies because a clinician does not receive this information). To improve clinica care, it is important to understand the full cycle of interactions among participants and know to what extent each participant can retrieve and use timely, complete, accurate, and unambiguous information

One prior telemedicine study applied DCog to understand the workflow of a telemedicine programme for homebased chronic care of diabetics (Kaufman et al., 2009). That study set out to discover how a telemedicine service was intended to work, how it actually worked, and implications for gauging the current state of the patient—the key representational state in the cognitive processing of the diabetes care service. Their findings focused on how the workflow was embodied by system resources and communication patterns that emerged over the course of a patient's care. Kaufman et al. (2009) caution that their work might not generalize to a broader patient population and that “a telemedicine intervention has many ‘moving parts’ that introduce layers of complexity and the potentia for workflow disruption” (p. 591). Our study addresses this issue and extends their work in two important ways. First, we confront Kaufman et al.'s (2009) concern about the “many moving parts” and “layers of complexity” by taking a deeper dive into work processes, in order to tease apart the modes of cognitive distribution and identify and explore dynamic properties that affect social, structural, and temporal modes of cognitive distribution in telemedicine Second, in applying the DCog perspective to telemedicine practices in a different care context,<sup>3</sup> our study moves the work towards stronger generalizability.

Having outlined the conceptual foundations for our analysis, we can now state our research questions in theo retically distinct terms as follows:

RQ1 . In informed collaboration, how is cognitive effort distributed across individuals, artefacts, and time?

While RQ1 enables us to open the black box of informed collaboration to understand how cognitive effort is distributed and what mechanisms are used, we are fundamentally interested in how DCog enables or enhances informed collaboration. Therefore, we look to understand, in a specific context (geriatric telepsychiatry), whether certain properties of social, structural, and temporal mechanisms of cognitive distribution are evident, whether they are related (via dynamic interaction), and how these properties and their interactions enable or support informed collaboration:

RQ2 . What properties affect social, structural, and temporal mechanisms of distributed cognition and in what ways do such properties enable informed collaboration?

## 3 | RESEARCH METHOD

## 3.1 | RuralHub background and site selection

The geriatric chronic‐care RuralHubTelepsych case is one of several embedded case studies (Stake, 1995) in our broader set of longitudinal case studies that examined telemedicine services provided by RuralHub in multiple medical specialties (dermatology, paediatric intensive care, vascular surgery, and other clinical domains). Initial access to RuralHub i 1996 was made by one author who conducted interviews addressing their federally funded telemedicine pilot tests in pathology, gastroenterology, vascular care, and other specialties. Subsequently, we revisited RuralHub in 2008 and conducted the Telepsych case study in 2009 to 2011.<sup>4</sup> By focusing here on geriatric telepsychiatry, we constrain our scope to one hub provider, one chronic‐care medical specialty, and one form of patient‐present telemedicine, following advice by Yin (2003) to treat each case (or set of embedded cases) as a natural experiment in a series.

Eleven “spoke” institutions utilize this Telepsych service. For the embedded‐cases study, we were given permission to conduct interviews at RuralHub and two spoke nursing homes (each nursing home is a “spoke” cas embedded within the RuralHub Telepsych case). Data were gathered by means of documentary sources and semistructured, on‐site interviews.<sup>5</sup> Appendix A summarizes the 29 interviews conducted in the broader set of RuralHub studies; these interviews helped us understand RuralHub's overall telemedicine programme and its broade institutional context. Appendix A also summarizes the 14 additional interviews that specifically addressed geriatric telepsychiatry (highlighted in red).

## 3.2 | Interviews, data preparation, and analysis

In 2008, two authors visited RuralHub for an update on its telemedicine services and to learn about new services on offer, including geriatric telepsychiatry. We conducted seven interviews relevant to the RuralHub telepsychiatry case. The Clinical Director of Telemedicine (a psychiatrist) described current telemedicine services and pilot tests in psychiatry, dermatology, trauma, and paediatrics (at that time psychiatry and dermatology accounted for most RuralHub telemedicine consultations; pathology and gastroenterology had declined, and vascular surgery consultations remained at about the same level as in 1996). Six individuals in technical or administrative roles provided useful background about technologies in use and interinstitutional arrangements between RuralHub and nursing homes. They also discussed implementation issues, current technical and operational choices, and desired future functionality. A RuralHub psychiatrist then agreed to introduce us to staff at two participating nursing homes (NHs):

NH1 (75 beds, about 3‐h drive from RuralHub). Its parent hospital had used a RuralHub telemedicine ser vice for vascular surgery consultations and other needs for a long time and had requested the Telepsych service, which was launched in 2003.

NH2 (105 beds, about an hour from RuralHub) was also affiliated with a local hospital that used other RuralHub telemedicine services (such as paediatric intensive care). NH2 began participating in telepsych a few years after NH1.

Two authors visited NH1 and NH2 in 2009 for the two embedded telepsychiatry case studies. We conducted on‐site semistructured conversational interviews (30‐90 min long) with three individuals at NH1 (social worker and two nurses) and four at NH2 (Director of the Dementia and Special Care service [a social worker], another socia worker, a nurse, and the NH2 system administrator). All clinicians were asked to discuss benefits, challenges, and issues in geriatric telepsychiatry and to specifically describe one or more recent telepsych sessions they had experienced (including their recollection of the patient's condition, decisions required and care provided during and after the session, and how they felt during consultations). At the larger nursing home (NH2), we asked the system administrator to discuss implementation issues, current technical and operational choices, and desired future functionalit (at NH1, the social worker and nurses spoke to these issues). In all 14 interviews, we further probed regarding telemedicine barriers and facilitators. All interviews were conducted in pairs, with one researcher focusing o questioning while the other took extensive field notes. Interviews were recorded, professionally transcribed, and reconciled with our field notes.

To prepare each transcript for analysis, at least one interviewer listened to the recording and compared it with the transcript and field notes, inserting corrections (eg, spelling of names and medical terminology) and interview segment identifiers to facilitate subsequent coding. Contextual notes (eg, terminology; description of setting) wer also inserted. To analyse the data, we used the constant‐comparative method (Strauss & Corbin, 1998), iterating between preliminary findings from interview data, data in public materials (web pages, announcements), materials provided by participants (brochures, conference presentations, etc), and several theoretical frameworks (describe below). Several forms of coding were done:

Factual coding. Coding of key events and facts (eg, cost of equipment and date of first telepsych consulta tion; Major & Savin‐Baden, 2010). Information obtained in interviews was triangulated against publicly available documents such as web pages, presentation materials, news accounts, and RuralHub doctors' journal publications.

Open coding. Per Strauss and Corbin (1998), we categorized interview segments into themes and subthemes suggested by the data but not anticipated a priori. Examples include affect recognition, patient/family comfort, psychiatrist skill, asymmetric information sharing, social presence, unique aspects of geriatric care, and break downs/glitches/adaptation. Several passes were made through the data to accomplish this coding and to suppor a process of interpretation (identifying linkages across and between themes; probing into the significance of events opinions, and contextual aspects). A purpose of this approach is to achieve some degree of categorical and concep tual saturation in which there seems to be no additional information generated from additional interviewee (Creswell, 2012).

Comparative coding. Next, the research team conducted two independent rounds of comparative coding. First we focused on comparing codes that emerged in the initial, open coding rounds with previously identified themes from earlier rounds of analysis in the broader RuralHub case study. For example, among 20 high‐level themes coded in the 1996 to 1997 study were as follows: strategic vision for telemedicine, consultation examples, usage by specialties, lifelike (subsequently termed “transparency”), telemedicine support, technical limitations, avoid travel and reimbursement. In this second round of comparative coding, we also did a theoretical review (Urquhart & Fernandez, 2013), which juxtaposed the preliminary codes with principles articulated in various theories, including some noted in Table 1. Our initial theoretical coding suggested significant parallels with several of those theorie (eg, heterogeneity of knowledge across participants, interpersonal distribution in information processing, and extensive use of diverse artefacts). Throughout this theoretical review, the research team took great care to avoi forcing observations into existing theoretical constructs (Glaser, 1992; Seidel & Urquhart, 2013).

Through our iterative coding process, our team came to view DCog as a strong candidate theory to incorporate into our analysis, given that it allowed for simultaneous consideration of social, structural, and temporal facets of the cognitive activity of telepsychiatric care delivery.

Theoretical coding. Accordingly, the research team conducted a final round of coding based on principles established (though not all of which were clearly named) in prior DCog studies—including social, structural, and temporal cognitive distribution (Hollan et al., 2000; Hutchins, 2000). Other codes were based on related constructs identified in prior literature (eg, artefacts, knowledge, collaboration, and roles) and dynamic properties DCog suggested or implied in prior DCog studies; we came to label these cognitive offloading, visibility of action intersubjective understanding, and knowledge redundancy.

Four researchers separately coded all transcripts, after which we met to identify areas of overlap or difference in coded themes. We consolidated some into broader themes and broke some out into narrower subthemes (eg, artefact/depression scale, artefact/electronic records, artefact/notes; role/nurse, role/physician, role/socia worker, role/technical support). Lastly, all four authors independently revisited the transcripts again with th themes and subthemes in mind to substantiate or refute them. ## 4 | FINDINGS

Consistent with prior DCog studies (Hollan et al., 2000; Hutchins, 2000), our analysis of the RuralHub telepsych case findings revealed how cognitive effort is distributed across individuals, artefacts, and time using multiple mechanisms of social, structural, and temporal distribution. We describe this confirmatory evidence first, and later we describ evidence pointing to several dynamic DCog properties that influenced informed collaboration in this case: cognitive offloading, visibility of action, intersubjective understanding, and knowledge redundancy.

## 4.1 | Modes of cognitive distribution in the RuralHub telepsych service

We address RQ1 by describing the extent to which social, structural, and temporal forms of cognitive distribution are observed in RuralHub telepsychiatry consultations and roles played by these various elements in the associated cog nitive tasks.

## 4.1.1 | Social distribution

The distribution of cognitive effort across individual social actors is a prominent feature of the RuralHub telepsych case. Distinct roles—including patient, family member, psychiatrist, nurse, social worker, primary care physician (PCP), and technical support staff—vary in significance and centrality to the relevant cognitive processing about a patient's mental state. Patient, psychiatrist, nurse, and social worker are central to the cognitive effort of evaluating patient's behaviour and mental state, while family member, tech support staff, and PCP play comparatively peripheral roles. Whether central or peripheral, each role provides some unique information or authority that contributes to th patient's care.

## 4.1.2 | Structural distribution

A number of distinct types of artefacts are critical in the functioning of the RuralHub telepsych service. Aside from th obvious designation between digital and analogue artefacts, structural elements can be distinguished based on roles each artefact plays within the cognitive system.<sup>6</sup> We discerned two broad roles for cognitive artefacts—(1) information repositories that create an external memory function for the human actors (eg, case notes, medical histories, and laptop devices), and (2) information processing resources that enhance human perception and simplify information processing tasks (eg, video camera, depression scales, and paper‐and‐pen). ## 4.1.3 | Temporal distribution

We identified three key temporal sequences or phases through which representational states propagate within the RuralHub Telepsych system. Telepsychiatry is a temporally recursive service, with participants progressing iterativel through three phases of activity: work completed before a consultation (preconsultation), interaction during a consul tation (during consultation), and preparation of a new or revised treatment plan (or decision to make no changes to a treatment plan) after consultation (postconsultation). Each phase entails a different set of activities and different configuration of human actors and artefacts.

## Preconsultation

Extensive collaboration between the psychiatrist, nurse(s), and social worker(s) ensures that each party can achieve an up‐to‐date understanding of a patient's condition. Sometimes the team confers with a patient's family members, to seek their insights on the patient's behaviour and mood. Participants use various artefacts, most of which functio as external memory stores in the system, including EMR, medical histories, clinical notes, depression scales, and a minimum data set, described by Nurse 1 at NH1:

NH1 Nurse 1: We give [Psychiatrist] a background on each resident. We do medication reconciliation … If it's an initial visit, we talk about what's going on with the resident, give … their social history, family history and their medical history. We have already faxed or mailed their most recent MDS [minimum data set], most recent nurses' notes, progress notes, things like that; so that he has all that information in front of him … It basically gives a brief synopsis of about maybe 100 questions of what the resident is like

## During consultation

The discussion between psychiatrist and patient is the focus of the consultation. Evaluative work is distribute among members of the clinical team (psychiatrist, nurses, and social workers), in conversation with the patient and sometimes including family members (who may or may not be present). The psychiatrist explained:

Most of my interactions take place, not only with patients, but 100 percent of the time with a nurse facilitator on the other end, and 90 percent of the time with a social worker there as well, and probably 70 percent of the time with one or more family members. So it's a pretty crowded room on the othe end [at the nursing home].

If present. family members may take an active or passive role. Other members of the clinical team (nurse, socia worker) primarily observe and take notes, allowing the patient to focus on the one‐on‐one conversation with the psy chiatrist. A nurse or social worker will occasionally intervene to assist the psychiatrist (eg, touching the patient's arm and speaking to the direct patient's attention):

NH2 Social Worker 1: If for any reason the patient is not focused, [Psychiatrist] would expect and hope that we can help them focus. So I would be saying, ‘John look; look at the screen.’ Just to get him focused in. And to adjust the headset—that sort of thing

A variety of artefacts are used during a consultation, including notes, physician‐controlled camera, videoconfer encing and recording equipment, and writing implements. Two social workers explained typical scenarios:

NH2 Social Worker 2: Part of the dementia screening is having people perhaps draw a clock [or] write a sentence. So, we give them the paper and pen and [the psychiatrist] zooms right in so he can see what they're doing. Sometimes we turn it around and show it to him, but very often he's observing that as though he's sitting across the table.

NH1 Social Worker: [The psychiatrist] might ask them to hold up a pencil or hold out three fingers. He′ll have them write out their name and he'll zoom in on that.

A nurse usually brought a laptop into the consultation, in case the psychiatrist requests clarification about a recent medication change or other information.

## Postconsultation

Clinical team members again interact (synchronously or asynchronously) to devise and deliver a new or revised treatment plan and update the patient's medical records and team notes. The patient's PCP is generally included, both to ensure continuity of care and to perform any proposed medication changes. A nurse (who sees asynchronous interaction as “lag time”) explains

NH2 Nurse: … The lag time between what [psychiatrist] recommends … we need to get that to the pri mary care physician, who has to get it back to us. If there's some issues trying to contact him, he's usually fairly accessible, that's usually not an issue. But … it's the back and forth …

## 4.2 | DCog properties in informed collaboration

In turning to RQ2 (What properties affect social, structural, and temporal mechanisms of distributed cognition and in what ways do such properties enable informed collaboration?), the RuralHub TelePsych case study reveals four dynamic properties that supported informed collaboration through coordination of effort and distribution of cognitive work load. We label these (1) cognitive offloading, (2) visibility of action, (3) intersubjective understanding, and (4) knowledg redundancy. As noted in Section 3, these properties were identified in our open coding processes and then associated with DCog theory during the theoretical review phase of coding. The properties complement extant DCog theory, in that each is alluded to, but not explicitly articulated nor rigorously studied in prior research. As explained in Section 5 isolating and separately analysing these four dynamic properties extends DCog theory and points to potentially useful avenues for future research using this perspective.

## 4.2.1 | Cognitive offloading

The structural form of cognitive distribution centres on the principle that information processing and management can be simplified when some of the cognitive workload is transferred from human actors to artefacts (Bose, McLaren, Riley, & Mohammedali, 2001; Larkin & Simon, 1987). Offloading to artificial elements doe not merely shift cognitive effort from one location to another. Hutchins (1995) contends that artefact us fundamentally alters the information processing required of human collaborators: “significant functions are achieved by a person interpreting material symbols, rather than by … recalling those symbols from his or he memory” (p. 280).

Two forms of cognitive offloading were evident in this case: (1) simplification of information processing tasks through the integration of artefacts (ie, information processing resources) and (2) use of external structures to retain information for later use (ie, information repositories). An example of a structure that simplifies human information processing is the psychiatrist's ability to remotely observe a patient performing a simple “paper‐and‐pencil” evalua tion task (eg, draw a clock and write a name). This standard psychiatric technique is enhanced in the telemedicine service by the psychiatrist's ability to remotely direct the camera's focus and observe hand movements more closely than when physically present in the room. A social worker observed:

NH2 Social Worker: [The psychiatrist would say] “I was using the camera and I focused in, looking at he tremor; it was different from the last time I saw her. And then I said this and she changed her behavior.” You know, he's really using it [the video camera] and it's really beneficial in him assessing them. It's not just that he wants to see a face. He wants to look at every aspect of them. I would think in a one‐to‐one rela tionship, you probably couldn't focus that much. Otherwise the person would say “Why are you staring at me like that?!”

The RuralHub psychiatrist reinforced this interpretation when he stated

If you're interviewing a patient, there's a certain distance that you can get; closer than that is uncomfortable for someone. Without having binoculars on I can only see so much. But if I zoom in, that television can be the same distance, but I can zoom in on your face and see tears forming much sooner, I can see a twitch in the corner of the eye or face. Just as interesting and important, I think, is I can be —let's say you're my patient, I can be interviewing you, knowing I'm going to ask this next question that's going to be emotionally charged. I'll twist my camera over to a family member and ask the question, and look for their response—which is sometimes way more telling than that of the patient

The second type of cognitive offloading focused on information retention and retrieval using artefacts (information repositories). An EMR was available during consultations (eg, to confirm medication changes or other pertinent information). The nurse's and social worker's ability to call up information from the EMR is a departure from standard practice at these nursing homes; in in‐person psychiatry, these clinicians were not usually present.<sup>7</sup> During a traditional in‐person session, the psychiatrist would refer to the EMR before the consultation (or possibly at the start of the consultation) but would usually not want to disrupt the flow by referring to it during the session. In RuralHub's telepsychiatry service, a nurse or social worker does refer to the EMR on a laptop during the consultation, without disrupting the exchange between psychiatrist and patient.

While both analogue and digital tools were used during consultations, digital tools—especially the EMR and consultation recordings—were particularly helpful before and after consultations (see Appendix C for full dat analysis of artefact use). As a social worker stated:

NH1 Social Worker: [Walking through the process …]the doctor usually wants to know quite a bit. A lot of times we will send a lot of information to him prior, the current med list and any current labs. Then that day, he'll want the vital signs that morning, anything that's changed, and usually I have to give him details about their prior history, any psych information, … [With the patient present?] No. No I always give that to him first.

The psychiatrist mentioned the use of consultation recordings after the consultations:

What's neat is that you can take this recorded interaction and then a day or two later or as long as you want to, you can say to a colleague, ‘you know I'm wondering, is this Parkinson's Disease or tardive dyskinesia, or both? I'm wondering if you could take a look at this.’ So you can use that.

## 4.2.2 | Visibility of action

Prior DCog research suggests collaborators benefit from being able to “see” what other human and nonhuman collaborators do (Kirsh, 1999; Pea, 1993). Importantly, visibility need not be limited to physically observing collaborators' behaviour; visible outcomes of collaborators' actions may also be significant. The RuralHub case evidence reveals that visibility of action is explicitly supported through direct telemedicine‐facilitated discussions among participants and collective engagement with the technology, combined with conventional face‐to‐face communication While cognitive offloading primarily centres on the structural form of cognitive distribution, visibility of action ma reflect both social and structural mechanisms:

NH2 Social Worker: In this [telepsychiatry] process, I'm able to meet with the resident beforehand, gather information, provide it to the physician, interact with the physician who wants more information [via telemedicine], see the response of the resident, have discussions with the doctor about how the resi dent reacted during that meeting, and then have a follow up and other appointments

A nurse mentioned how additional information sometimes surfaces, due to their ability to observe actions the previously would not have access to in the traditional consultation environment:

NH2 Nurse: We have been surprised by some of the information that pops out that they [patients] have not told us before. We're usually quiet … And they will tell [the psychiatrist] stuff that they've not told us before. It's good for us because then we're aware of issues we might not have been

We also identified instances when visibility was actively inhibited. For example, the psychiatrist sometime confers with family members before or after a consultation, without the patient present. Other members of the clinical team also sometimes confer without the patient present:

NH1 Nurse 1: “[Team members] will meet with both [patients and family members] and then ask the res ident [patient] to leave and meet with the family afterwards.”

We further observe that, before or after the consultation, a nurse, social worker, and the hub doctor often meet with neither family members nor patient present, for a variety of reasons

[First one of you is alone in the room talking to [psychiatrist] to give him the back‐ground, then patient and family come in and have the consultation, then patient and family leave and you're alone with him again to get the orders?] NH2 Social Worker: Yes. Although [psychiatrist] pretty much tells [patient] exactly what's going to happen. He talks to us beforehand, then he brings that person in, and confirms, “Yes, that's what want.” [He] tells them, and then we talk some more.

These findings reveal some tension between perceived benefits of visibility of action and a desire for some asymmetry of information, similar to physicians' concerns about patient access to physician notes stored in EMRs (Boonstra & Broekhuis, 2010; Vishwanath & Scamurra, 2007). In another example, the psychiatrist explained that he often adjusts the video display so the patient does not see himself or herself on screen (can be confusing for some patients):

I can look at the patient with the large screen, and … a small square on the side shows me what I look like to the patient … so I know if I'm making eye contact. If you look directly at the TV screen, it looks like you're looking down, so you have to train yourself to look at the camera rather than the screen, and it's not that easy to do … We purposely disable that on the other side, so that little picture‐in‐picture won't be a distraction to patients

Above, the psychiatrist alludes to the challenge of “making eye contact” via telemedicine. Disabling the screen‐in‐screen display underscores the intertwining of brain and body, particularly during a telepsych consultation. The aforementioned effort by social workers to direct a patient's attention through touch also supports this finding.

## 4.2.3 | Intersubjective understanding

Effective collaborators are aware of how each participant contributes to the collective goals: “Intersubiectivity ... is what permits human actors to intend and find meanings in many nonverbal behaviors and in the aspects of verba behaviors that go beyond the literal locutionary force of the utterance” (Hutchins & Klausen, 1996, p. 24). Building on this principle, intersubiective understanding reflects how collaborators position their own action in relation to the knowledge, roles, and actions of others. For a collaboration to function effectively, human participants must understand the propagation mechanisms through which the system moves towards resolution of collective objectives. The statements below illustrate intersubjective understanding as observed in the RuralHub case:

NH2 Social Worker: [In traditional consultations, only the patient] would go to the appointment. So we wouldn't have that connection. We also wouldn't get the feedback for a while … We wouldn't hear what the practitioner feels, what they think. We get a lot more from [the telepsych process]. We know exactly what [the psychiatrist] thinks.

NH2 Systems Administrator: [The psychiatrist] educated me … and gave me some examples of how he might move the camera slightly to someone else in the room, other than the person who he was having a consult with. He might be more interested in the reaction to a question of a family member or a spouse that's sitting in there, or zooming in on someone's hand movements to see if they're agitated. I quickly real ized that he would need some more advanced equipment that he could control and do that with.

These statements reveal that creation of a shared experience was a perceived benefit of telepsychiatry. Clinical collaborators felt that their participation during telepsych consultations increased their understanding whereas in traditional psychiatry consultations (in these particular nursing homes), nurses and social workers would only be consulted before or after the doctor‐patient consultation. Participants perceived that intersubjective understanding increased their clinical knowledge and efficacy, highlighting a clear and positive impact on informed collaboration.

In an important trend in health care, team‐based care—often involving colocated clinicians and other staff (Ghaye, 2008; Yarnall et al., 2009)—intersubjective understanding is a likely positive side effect, whether or not collaborators rely on digital tools. The RuralHub psychiatrist is located at a distance, yet he and other members of the team (at the nursing home) believe his consultation is at least as effective as a conventional in‐person consultation. Our interviewees noted that the amount of consultation time the psychiatrist spends with a nurse or socia worker present is far higher in telepsych than was previously the case at these nursing homes.

## 4.2.4 | Knowledge redundancy

Some degree of overlap in knowledge held by different actors is needed to create resilience in a cognitive system —“robustness is made possible by the redundant distribution of knowledge among the members of the team” (Hutchins, 1995, p. 223). To the extent that their knowledge overlaps, team members can fill in when another member is absent or incapacitated; if a team member goes on leave, others' understanding of his role and function compensates for his absence (suggesting a link between intersubiective understanding and knowledge redun dancy). Since knowledge and roles change over time, knowledge redundancy—ie, presence of commonly held knowledge among human participants—is a dynamic DCog property that may improve the resilience of informed collaboration.

Our case study evidence reveals that knowledge redundancy is fostered through multiple means. Integration of artefacts in the telepsych process (ie, cognitive offloading)—particularly in the form of repositories that create an external memory function—was a prominent driver of knowledge redundancy. For example, video recordings of consultations were used for training psychiatry resident physicians and for tapping other clinicians' opinions (eg, are symptoms consistent with Parkinson or tardive dyskinesia?). Knowledge redundancy was also pursued through social exchange, including online or via telephone conversations between the psychiatrist, nurses, and social workers and by cross‐training staff on telepsych procedures (eg, how to set up video equipment and bring a laptop into the room for EMR access). These examples occurred in periods before or after consultations (ie, preconsultation, postconsultation) and presumably reduced barriers that would otherwise impede telemedicin effectiveness (many prior papers report that small technical challenges can have a large negative impact on telemed icine use; see, for example, Gogan, Karra, & Garfield, 2013).

The case evidence also suggests that knowledge redundancy is tied to enhanced visibility of action during consultations. For example, a social worker indicated that observing the psychiatrist interacting with a patient expanded her knowledge of the patient's experience:

NH2 Nurse: I really think it's very beneficial, because we're all in the room. When we work with a traditional psychiatrist, they [patients] are going there. I'm not coming, the nurse is not coming, so we're not able to make that connection and provide that information that's really important. It's just the person that's transporting that hopes that they have the information needed. So I love this process … it's much more comprehensive.

Presumably, awareness of a patient's behaviours and expressed concerns translates into improved understanding of the patient and improved care by the observant nurse. On the other hand, while the above quote suggests that acquiring new knowledge is a benefit of telepsychiatry, some participants believed that insufficient knowledge redundancy affected certain aspects of the telepsych service. A social worker felt the service relied too heavily o a single psychiatrist whose expertise would not be easy to replicate:

NH2 Social Worker 2: I have a true respect for [the psychiatrist] and his ability. I know that if someone is going to get it right, he is. When we have been able to actually get another practitioner to come in, I haven't felt really confident that what they're suggesting is going to work …

To summarize, the case evidence reveals how the distribution of cognitive effort supports collaborators collective understanding, both of psychiatric practices and of a particular patients' mental health condition, through four dynamic DCog properties: cognitive offloading, visibility of action, intersubjective understanding, and knowledge redundancy.

## 5 | DISCUSSION

In comparing our case study findings with the one previous study that applied a DCog perspective to telemedicine (ie, Kaufman et al., 2009), we see points of similarity and difference. In line with Kaufman et al. (2009), our findings highlight the value of distinguishing between temporal sequences before, during, and after telemedicine consultations. In contrast to that study, our telepsychiatry embedded‐cases study reveals a much more heterogeneous DCog environment, with respect to both social and structural distribution of cognitive load. This distinction is particularl evident regarding social distribution; in the RuralHub TelePsych service, we observed a more diverse set of actors engaged in every phase of service delivery.

Our observations also differ in terms of how social and structural forms interact to drive the tempora distribution of information processing. Through recursive sequences, the TelePsych clinical team seeks to stabilize or improve a patient's mental health over multiple consultations. With respect to structural elements, a couple of temporal patterns are worth noting. Artefacts that provide an external memory function (medical history, medical imaging, case notes, etc) are particularly prominent in preconsultation and postconsultation periods. During these phases, members of the clinical team focus on knowledge retrieval and sharing, as they prepare for a consultation or debrief from a consultation (they strive to achieve a common understanding of the patient's mental state, the effectiveness of the current treatment protocol, and decide whether to modify the treatment plan). During the consultation greater emphasis is placed on artefacts that enhance perception and simplify information processing (camera and other telemedicine equipment, analogue paper‐and‐pen, etc). This underscores the central aim of the consultation, which is to achieve a clear understanding of the patient's current mental state. Significantly, this use of artefacts to simplify information processing is different from merely using an external artefact to handle data manipulations and storage; it fundamentally enhances the quality of information to which the psychiatrist and other clinicians have access, creating greater support for the process of informed collaboration

## 5.1 | Theorizing the interplay of DCog properties

Perhaps most significantly, the RuralHub case evidence points to a dynamic interplay between social and structura elements to create the four DCog properties observed. Accordingly, we propose several extensions to DCog theory. Figure 1 summarizes the four dynamic DCog properties identified in the three phases of chronic TelePsych care illustrating (1) how social and structural elements interact to affect system objectives (ie, improved understanding, which presumably enables more effective patient care), (2) temporal sequences through which representational states propagate in different phases of care, (3) key variations on the process as indicated by our respondents (shown as core versus “contingent” elements), and (4) relative prominence of the four dynamic DCog properties during different phases of care.

As revealed in the case findings, psychiatric care is temporally recursive, with participants progressing iteratively through three essential phases of activity: work completed and information exchanged before a consultation, interaction during a consultation, and preparation of a new or revised treatment plan following the consultation. All four DCog properties were observed to some degree in each phase. However, based on our temporal analysis, the prop erties vary somewhat in prominence or centrality across the three periods, as does the configuration of human and artificial elements. By integrating our findings regarding RQ1 (ie, focusing on social, structural, and temporal distribution forms) and RQ2 (ie, focusing on four DCog properties), we are able to build a stronger understanding of the forms of—and supports for—informed collaboration in successful chronic‐care telepsychiatry.

## 5.1.1 | Preconsultation

Our findings reveal that two DCog properties are particularly prominent in the preconsultation phase (see RQ1 findings on preconsultation and RQ2 under knowledge redundancy). Enhancing knowledge redundancy is a primary objective at that time; the aim is to ensure that everyone is “on the same page.” Cognitive offloading is also promi nent (see RQ1 findings on preconsultation and RQ2 under cognitive offloading); much communication is mediated through exchange of analogue artefacts (eg, depression scales and notes) and digital artefacts (eg, EMR). By offloading memory and representational effort onto structural elements, the processing of required information is simplified.

## 5.1.2 | During consultation

In this phase, visibility of action is critical; all participants see and hear the discussion. Visibility of patient action is essential for the psychiatrist; his analysis attends to physical symptoms (such as a tremor) and also takes a patient's physical and behavioural reactions to questions and tasks into account, as much as the words exchanged. The psychiatrist takes advantage of the remote focus feature to occasionally zoom in on family members to gauge their reactions to various comments. Visibility is also important for nurses, social workers, and (sometimes) family members; even when unobtrusive “flies on the wall,” they gain insights about both the patient and psychiatrist. Team interaction similarly underscores the importance of intersubjective understanding during consultations. Nurses, socia workers, and family members learn to interpret the psychiatrist's intentions, often without explicit discussion and explanation.

Cognitive offloading is evident in the psychiatrist's reliance on artefacts. The ability to zoom in on physica reactions of a patient or family member is a helpful affordance, which simplifies the psychiatrist's information processing demands<sup>8</sup> (augmenting the cognitively intensive task of interpreting words with the relatively simple task of observing physical signs). This digitally supported dynamic is also augmented by using simple artefacts (eg, pen and paper) to observe the patient and gather information about him/her.

## 5.1.3 | Postconsultation

Knowledge redundancy is again prominent following a consultation. The psychiatrist, nurse, social worker, and famil members work to achieve a shared appreciation of the patient's behavioural symptoms and medical needs, in consultation with the primary care provider, who acquires new knowledge about the patient, other providers, and the proposed adjustment to the treatment plan. The PCP provides an important control, since this clinician may have knowledge about other medical or social issues that can affect the planned course of treatment for this patient Cognitive offloading is also important at this time. In addition to the memory function served by analogue and digital artefacts, video recording enables the psychiatrist to collaborate with other clinicians beyond the primary team (eg, specialist physicians and psychiatric residents in training)

In assessing the relative prominence of each DCog property in different phases of care, the relationship between knowledge redundancy and intersubjective understanding warrants additional comment. These properties are significantly intertwined (see also Proposition 3 below), making it difficult to determine which property is foremost in a given temporal phase. A key distinction in our coding was that knowledge redundancy reflected activities or efforts in which participants specifically sought to create a shared base of knowledge (both through direct communication and through use of artefacts). Intersubjective understanding reflected occasions when an individual participan reflected on others' actions in light of his/her own role or actions. While the distinction between these properties is analytically useful, it might not be possible to entirely disentangle these dynamic DCog properties in a lived practice of informed collaboration.

Considering the varying prominence of individuals, artefacts, and DCog properties across the three phases reveals an interesting insight. Activities in preconsultation and postconsultation periods focus largely on exchanging explicit knowledge about the patient (knowledge redundancy). At these times, active processing of information— beyond attaining a common base of understanding between members of the clinical team—was comparatively limited. During consultation, active information processing seems critically important, as reflected in both the artefacts used and prominent observed DCog properties. This is the phase that explicitly seeks to characterize the patient's current mental state. During a consultation, interpreting others' actions and positioning one's role relativ to these actions (intersubjective understanding) is central, and it is critically supported by visibility of action (seeing those actions, both literally and figuratively).

While the integration of artefacts to bear cognitive load (cognitive offloading) is pronounced in all three periods, use of the artefacts varies across the three phases, based on the primary cognitive role each artefact serves (eg, external memory vs simplification of information processing). This variation in emphasis is insightful, yet it is nevertheless important to recognize that all four dynamic DCog properties were observed in each phase. The interplay between properties makes complete disentanglement of their effects impossible. Next, we explore this interplay in greater detail.

## 5.2 | Propositions

Our case study findings lead us to offer several preliminary propositions regarding the interplay of DCog properties First, the RuralHub case suggests that cognitive offloading enhances the effectiveness of informed collaboration through several means:

Proposition 1. Cognitive offloading positively influences …

1a. informed collaboration efficiency and effectiveness

1b. visibility of action

1c. knowledge redundancy

Prior research finds that by exchanging cognitively taxing activities (eg, mental problem solving and communicating an emotional state) for comparatively simple ones (eg, consulting a chart and observing behaviour), collaboration performance effectiveness and efficiency improve (Turner, Bowker, Gasser, & Zacklad, 2006). Our case study findings reveal two key types of cognitive offloading that contribute to both effectiveness and efficiency of informed collab oration: (1) artefacts that enhance perception and simplify information‐processing tasks, and (2) artefacts that provid an external memory function for human participants. Both classes of cognitive artefacts contribute to efficiency by reducing the mental effort demanded of participants. Presumably, they improve effectiveness, since collaborators are able to take into consideration a greater amount of relevant information as they plan a change in treatment.

We further argue that cognitive offloading influences other DCog properties. By offloading some cognitive work load to artefacts accessible to multiple collaborators, visibility of action increases (Inbar & Tractinsky, 2012). That is, by introducing external representations of information (eg, psychiatrist's reasoning processes) that were formerly internally represented, cognitive offloading enhances visibility of action for some social actors. Knowledge redundancy is als achieved through access to artefacts bearing the load of memory and representation Most notably in the preconsultation and postconsultation phases, clinicians actively enhanced knowledge shared both within the team and beyond the team, through exchange of artefacts. Use of an EMR, patient medical history, clinical notes, depression scale, and minimum data set–supported knowledge redundancy between the psychiatrist and nurses, and access to session recordings supported knowledge redundancy with physicians in their psychiatric residency training programme. These observations resonate with prior health care studies that investigated information sharing throug cognitive artefacts (Tang & Carpendale. 2007). Therefore, cognitive offloading enhances knowledge redundancy

We next propose that visibility of action is important to informed collaboration because “seeing” enhances understanding:

Proposition 2. Visibility of action positively influences collaborators' intersubjective understanding

The case evidence suggests that visibility of action has a significant impact on informed collaboration in telepsychiatry, by enabling clinical team members both to gain and exchange knowledge and to obscure knowledge from others, as deemed necessary. Through both social and structural mechanisms, visibility of action allows each col laborator to perceive broader patterns of action and meaning. As with “heedful interrelating” (Weick & Roberts, 1993), visibility enhances intersubjective understanding by improving collaborators' ability to interpret each other's actions. During a consultation, digital video enables visibility of action, which enhances each participant's ability to position his or her behaviour relative to those of other participants, yielding critical insights (eg, social worker: “We know exactly what [the psychiatrist] thinks.” Nurse: “They will tell [the psychiatrist] stuff that they've not told us before …”).

Knowledge redundancy can occur because two participants have had similar training, or it can occur through telemedicine‐enabled visibility of action and cognitive offloading. It follows that knowledge redundancy contributes to collaboration effectiveness by improving participants' intersubjective understanding:

Proposition 3. Knowledge redundancy positively influences collaborators' intersubjective understanding

In a given work context, some shared knowledge is needed for collaborators to comprehend and interpret others intentions. This idea is similar to the “bridging of individuals' thought worlds” by fostering knowledge redundancy through job rotation or cross‐functional training (Fægri, Dybå, & Dingsøyr, 2010; Gebert, Boerner, & Kearney, 2006). In RuralHub's TelePsych service, intersubjective understanding (demonstrated during consultations) is affecte by visibility of action (discussed in Proposition 2) as well as by clinicians' efforts to explicitly share knowledge in preconsultation and postconsultation phases. And intersubjective understanding appears to be reinforced throug recursive interactions over time.

Our case study findings about knowledge redundancy suggest a point of contrast between DCog and TMS theory (Wegner, 1987). While analysis of the RuralHub TelePsych case suggests that knowledge redundancy is a mechanism that enhances system resilience, TMS sees it as a limitation on collaboration efficiency (some TMS papers argue that individuals may resist taking responsibility for remembering certain types of information, if they believe other collaborators possess that knowledge; see, for example, Wegner, 1987). Transactive memory systems also sees redundant knowledge as giving rise to wasted effort, because individuals must expend effort clarifying who knows what (Lewis, 2004).

Our case study findings also suggest that intersubjective understanding might be higher in a telemedicine consultation service than in a conventional geriatric chronic care service.<sup>9</sup> Further study is required to verify this and to identify the relative importance of videoconferencing for fostering intersubjective understanding, as compared with process and role changes (which could be implemented, albeit at higher cost compared with telemedicine, in conventional care settings).

The propositions advanced here warrant further empirical exploration in other collaboration contexts. We earlier noted that an absence of named constructs in prior DCog theory (Halverson. 2002) might reflect the difficulty of demarcating distinct system properties. Our preliminary theorizing suggests opportunities for future studies to test and further refine DCog theory with an emphasis on the four properties revealed in this case study. In addition to proposing these DCog properties as theoretical constructs, this study represents a first step in theorizing how they interact during informed collaboration

## 5.3 | Practical implications for supporting informed collaboration

The four DCog properties have implications for designing improved software and devices to support telemedicine and other types of informed collaboration. We discuss some implications here. For purposes of clarity, we organize these practical implications around the four DCog properties explored in the case. In practice, the dynamic interplay of the properties means that any proposed intervention in the design of a telemedicine service would draw upon or influence multiple facets of cognition and informed collaboration in its delivery. We will provide several examples of how these properties may interact when new software and devices are designed.

Cognitive offloading confers value not merely by transferring cognitive effort but also by transforming the leve and type of effort required of human collaborators. Our study findings revealed that the use of both digital artefacts (eg, EMRs) and analogue artefacts (eg, pen and paper) simplified information processing tasks. Advances in artificia intelligence and machine learning hint at other opportunities to use clinical decision support tools for cognitive offloading (Abidi, 2001; Kokol, Podgorelec, & Zorman, 2000; Musen, Middleton, & Greenes, 2014). Probabilistic models using fuzzy logic could analyse patient behavioural indicators in clinical histories prepared before and after interventions. Real-time behavioural analysis technologies. based on pattern recognition, could also enhance clinicians' individual or collective abilities to recognize emergent patterns in a patient's response to specific treatments (such as new medications or dosage changes). Such tools might help a psychiatrist to consider whether a particular clinical intervention is helping. Future studies could investigate whether technologies such as these are equally applicable across the phases of care delivery.

Visibility of action was identified as an important DCog property, yet in the RuralHub telepsych case, we saw that some information flowing into and from clinical collaborators was fragmented. Telepsych services would likely benefit from use of an online platform for secure exchange of clinically relevant information (Ciolfi, Fitzpatrick, & Bannon, 2008: Kordzadeh & Warren, 2013), A collaboration platform would create a common online space where participants could share both structured information and their clinical impressions. Such a platform might provide expanded access to session recordings for use by other members of a clinical team; these might be useful for train ing nurses, social workers, and PCPs for integration into a telepsych service such as that offered by RuralHub.<sup>10</sup> Note. however, that our case findings also revealed that asymmetric visibility (or selective invisibility) is sometimes desired in psychiatry (and presumably in other medical specialties). Thus, a platform designed to enhance visibility o action should also incorporate ways to control who and what is visible to whom. Getting visibility right require consideration of regulatory implications (eg, HIPAA) as well as evidence about the level of visibility that is mos effective in given clinical situations.

Intersubjective understanding was evident in nurses' and social workers' reports that they achieved greater insight into psychiatric consultation by being present during consultations and their reports that discussions with the psychiatrist before and after consultations helped them to understand and contextualize his recommendations at a deeper level. Increased intersubjective understanding seemed to increase their trust in and respect for him. This finding implies there may be opportunities to redesign clinical team member roles—by transferring some activities currently performed by a psychiatrist or external PCP to nurses and social workers. Our research team does not include physicians, so we are not qualified to suggest that a nurse or social worker take on any particular role currently performed by a psychiatrist. However, we do observe that nurses have taken on new or expanded roles in other clinical domains (Mickan & Rodger, 2005; Pullon, 2008), so this possibility seems promising

## 5.3.1 | Knowledge redundancy

While a single definitive source is a critical requirement for structured patient medical data, reliance on a single source of know‐how or of other type of knowledge could be counterproductive. Overreliance on one source can constrain a team's capacity to dynamically allocate roles and responsibilities (Michel, 2007). Although we observed some useful knowledge redundancy, we also saw that RuralHub's telepsych service relies heavily on one consulting psychiatrist; collaborators expressed concern about the possibility of his retirement. Also, at these two nursing homes, only a few nurses and social workers participate regularly in the telepsych service. To reduce knowledge related dependency risks, RuralHub could add a second consulting psychiatrist, make greater use of recordings to give medical students and resident physicians greater exposure (both to psychiatric care practices in general and to nuances of telemedicine care). A formal training programme can increase the number of nurses, social workers, and psychiatrists who are qualified to support telepsych consultations

## 5.3.2 | Interaction between the DCog properties

Above, we put forth a set of practical design implications that arose from our case study of telemedicine as informed collaboration. Many changes that we mention will have implications across two or more of the DCog properties. For instance, if clinical decision support tools are incorporated into a telemedicine platform (cognitive offloading), it is possible that multiple parties would have better visibility of action (given an ability to “see” a variety of clinical intervention tactics depicted in the decision support tools). A nurse who interacts with a decision support system may come to better understand why a physician asks patients particular questions during consultations and come to better understand the care protocols that the doctor prescribes. The nurse's new knowledge may enable him or her to more clearly “see” what the doctor does.

Thus, adding smart functionality to a collaboration tool or platform (cognitive offloading) might give rise to improved visibility of action, which in turn might increase both intersubjective understanding and knowledge redundancy (by capturing in electronic format nuanced information that would otherwise reside only in the doctor's head). If stored and retrievable by other clinicians, such a platform can support improved clinician understanding and knowledge, and in turn, patient care teams should work more effectively together. However, this presupposes that different clinicians (such as nurses, social workers, and psychiatrists) share a sufficiently similar foundationa knowledge base that will enable them to perceive and understand one another's actions. There are many other reasons why, in the dynamic interplay among human beings, the positive paths that one hopes would lead to improved understanding and knowledge might not materialize. Further study is needed to shed light on these possibilities.

Addition of video annotation functionality to telemedicine session recordings (Feng, Manmatha, & Lavrenko 2004; Miyamori & Iisaku, 2000) might also be helpful. For example, the RuralHub psychiatrist could use annotation to direct other clinicians' attention to revelatory aspects of the patient's (and his) behaviour (Xiao et al., 2007) Well‐written annotation (another form of cognitive offloading) should improve viewers' intersubjective understanding and knowledge redundancy—important for ultimately improving the stability and resilience of this telemedicine service (by revealing what the psychiatrist does and how he thinks about his actions and the patient's responses, the system provides functionality that helps educate potential successors after he retires)

These suggestions illustrate the practical value that DCog theory offers for designing useful tools to support informed collaboration, while recognizing that complementary human and organizational resources will be necessary for these tools to achieve their potential. Our practical implications are far from an exhaustive list; other design concepts are described in Appendix D.

## 5.4 | Limitations

Many previous studies attempted to explain poor telemedicine diffusion by pointing to problems related to reim bursement, licensure, and regulation, Our study focused on geriatric psychiatry for chronic care, We chose to study RuralHub's service because it has been in place for more than 10 years; this longevity leads us to the reasonable inference that their service is safe and clinically effective for routine geriatric psychiatry, consistent with other stud ies (Hilty et al., 2013). Our case study points to some reasons for the success of this service. The case findings revealed that four DCog properties interact in an apparently virtuous cycle; collaborators' intersubjective understanding and knowledge redundancy increase, largely due to the offloading of cognitive effort onto artefacts, plus the enhanced visibility, of action. We use, the adiective, “apparently” because, alternative, interpretations of our case findings could be offered. For example, friendship ties and trust (which we did not closely study), likely also have influenced the longevity of this telepsych service

Another limitation of our study (true for many IS studies in health care) is that we did not observe actual patient consultations; we relied on participants' retrospective accounts. In a “double hermeneutic” process (Giddens, 1984), we interpreted interviewees' interpretations of their ongoing experiences with telepsychiatry. Also, because we are not clinicians, we also did not directly measure quality of care (clinical efficacy). However, many well‐controlled studies previously concluded that telepsychiatry is effective and safe (Hilty et al., 2013)

The data for this case study were gathered in 2009, and in the intervening years, technology has continued to advance and computational costs have dropped. The particular telemedicine hardware and software in use in 2009 may have been replaced by (for example) smartphones or tablets. So it is very important to consider the particular technology affordances that this RuralHub psychiatrist used: a system that allowed for his remote control of the camera and unobtrusive access to supporting data. Because of the camera and screen set‐up at NH1 and NH2, it was necessary to bring the patient to a particular room in each nursing home. A smartphone or tablet would offer greater portability, but at a price: The psychiatrist might not be able to remotely control the camera. Increased portability and reduced ability to control the camera zooming in and out would likely change how the psychiatrist works during telemedicine consultations. We believe we identified the salient technology design features that influenced collaboration in this case; future studies also need to clearly describe the technologies in use (and specific relevant affordances), as well as other important contextual variables.

## 5.5 | Contributions and concluding reflections

By applying DCog theory to the RuralHub telepsych case study, we contribute a unique new “brick” to the “wall” of knowledge about telemedicine (and informed collaboration more generally). It is important to note that the logic of replication in case studies is similar to the logic of experiments: No single experiment (or case) proves a theory (Lee, 1989); many experiments (and many case studies) are needed before a theory is well delineated and robust (Yin, 2003). The case described here focused on geriatric patients and one type of chronic‐care telemedicine service delivery, in a specific geographic and institutional context. Our analysis supports theoretic or analytic generalizabilit (Eisenhart, 2008; Yin, 2003) to telemedicine in other clinical contexts. The findings should generalize to other telemedicine services for routine geriatric care—such as for diabetes, renal, and other chronic conditions. Our study findings can be fruitfully compared with studies of routine telepsychiatry services provided to different populations, such as young adults or children. Since RuralHub's service was not designed to support consultation during acute psychiatric episodes, acute geriatric psychiatry care would be another useful context for a future case study, as would studies of acute psychiatric care for other patient populations

Our findings can also be fruitfully compared with findings from other studies that drew on different theories or used different research methods. For example, Boonstra and Van Offenbeek (2010) used structuration theory to examine complex interactions, implementation inconsistencies, and tensions in a telecare case study and concluded

Between a telecare application and its successful appropriation there is a grey area of interaction between a ‘seemingly straightforward’ technology and diverse actors whose sense‐making may diverge, without them even being aware of this. … These actors have to develop a common, thorough understanding of the care services and the target groups involved so that they can align their visions and arrive at a consistent implementation mode where development and use activities coincide

Experiments (supported by scenarios, video clips, or simulations) should be useful for testing hypotheses based on our propositions (such as to reveal similarities and differences in how cognition is distributed and in the interplay among dynamic DCog processes). Two socioinstitutional concerns also deserve further study in future telemedicine research: addressing dependencies and modifying roles. Since the enhancements that we suggest have implication for telemedicine system design, process efficiency, and quality of care, interdisciplinary research involving investigators from medicine, computer science, information systems, organizational behaviour, process management, and operations management would be especially valuable.

Given the declining cost of high‐quality hardware, software, and connectivity and improved usability, there is growing interest in using telemedicine to improve patients' access to routine and specialty health care. Distributed cognition theory is well suited to telemedicine research, since clinical care entails both interpersonal interaction and collaborator engagement with digital and nondigital artefacts. The RuralHub embedded‐cases telepsychiatry study revealed (consistent with prior DCog studies) evidence of social, structural, and temporal distribution, as well as evidence of four distinct DCog properties, which we label cognitive offloading, visibility of action, intersubjective understanding, and knowledge redundancy. We identified interrelationships among these properties and offered propositions for further research and design suggestions for supporting effective informed collaboration in health care and other contexts. We encourage other researchers to consider applying, extending, and refining DCog theory in future studies of informed collaboration in a variety of contexts, within and beyond health care.

APPENDIX D

# POTENTIALLY USEFUL NEW TOOLS FOR TELEMEDICINE: FURTHER THOUGHTS

## Augmented Reality

The RuralHub case study findings underscore the benefits of cognitive offloading and visibility of action. New technologies may help further simplify information processing in telemedicine. For example, augmented reality technology (Haller Billinghurst and Thomas, 2007) ‐‐ in which computer‐generated images are overlaid on a visual field in real time ‐‐ could help a psychiatrist and other clinicians detect indications of a patient's mental state. Changes in a patient's eye contact, pupil dilation, facial patterns, or physical behaviors during a consultation could be highlighted, enhancing collaborators ability to discern emotional reactions or detect behavioral shifts that would otherwise be more difficult to detect via conventional video or face‐to‐face.

A relatively simple way to implement augmented reality would involve using the current videoconferencing system's picture in‐picture functionality so that the psychiatrist can view diagnostic imaging (such as a brain scan) while still attending t the patient. Similar functionality has been applied in teledermatology consultation services (Ackerman and Locatis, 2011; Lam and DeSouza, 2011).

Use of virtual/augmented reality technology has been applied to psychiatric treatment of phobias and eating disorders (for a helpful review, see Riva, 2005), but applications in geriatric telepsychiatry have yet to be explored. Further research would aim to identify conditions under which augmented reality improves collaborator perception and conditions under which i impedes such perception (e.g., because of information overload or other factors).

## Assistive Technologies

A concern mentioned by several Nursing Home staff was the challenge of hearing impairment (common in a geriatric care setting), which necessitated the use of special headphones. Some patients felt headphones were physically awkward, uncomfortable or inconvenient. Focused audio technology (also referred to as sound laser.' 'phaser.' or ‘saser’) creates directional sound beams that channel sound to a desired location or person (Kelloniemi and Mettälä. 2006: Wu and Abhayapala, 2011). This functionality, with personalized audio settings can be used for each collaborator, without any physical intrusion. The technology is currently in an embryonic state, but it holds good promise for improving the convenience and natural feel of a telepsychiatry session.

## Other Sensory Enhancements

Many elderly patients suffer from poor vision due to macular degeneration and other conditions. The screen image of the psychiatrist can easily be adjusted in a telemedicine encounter. Other visual enhancements could perhaps be applied to electronic media for performing Rorschach tests or other visually‐intensive diagnostic tests, helping to ensure that the psychiatrist's interpretation is not clouded by a patient's visual impairment. Beyond magnification, other adaptive technologies could also be helpful. Patients with dementia or paranoia might be offered the ability to control the images they choose to see during a session. For example, a patient may choose to turn off the psychiatrist's image and just attend to his voice.

## Enhanced System Integration

An inefficient aspect of RuralHub's telepsychiatry service is weak data and system integration (a common challenge in telemedicine (Kaufman et al., 2009)). RuralHub's clinical information systems were only minimally integrated with nursing homes and their affiliated local hospitals. The pre‐consultation work of transmitting information from nursing home to RuralHub relied heavily on facsimile, emails, and U.S. mail. Fragmented information exchange gives rise to redundant dat entry, limited digital search capability, and a significant need for manual coordination. In turn, poorly integrated information place limits on cognitive offloading and might affect collaborators’ intersubjective understanding. Telepsychiatry effectiveness would likely be enhanced through systems integration within networks of participating institutions, and through improved capabilities for electronic storage, imaging, and exchange of all patient documentation
