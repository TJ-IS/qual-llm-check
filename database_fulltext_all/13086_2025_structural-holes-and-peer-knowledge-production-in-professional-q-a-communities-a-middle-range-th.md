# Structural Holes and Peer Knowledge Production in Professional Q&A Communities: A Middle-Range Theory of User Engagement

Langtao Chen,<sup>1</sup> Aaron Baird,<sup>2</sup> Detmar Straub<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>The University of Tulsa, USA, langtao-chen@utulsa.edu <sup>2</sup>Georgia State University, USA, abaird@gsu.edu <sup>3</sup>Temple University, USA, straubdetmar@gmail.com

## Abstract

Professional question-and-answer (Q&A) communities have become important online social media platforms for peer-to-peer knowledge production. Such communities are vital for users seeking technical solutions and innovative methods for developing new products and features. A key challenge is ensuring information flows across areas of specialization, as such communities thrive on access to diverse expertise. Yet we know little about how a user’s network brokerage (i.e., the extent to which the user bridges structural holes between groups in the Q&A social network) and related reinforcement mechanisms impact continued user engagement, particularly in contexts where balancing efficient solutions and innovative recommendations from diverse users creates tension. Drawing on the molar theories of structural holes and reinforcement, this study proposes a moderated mediation research model set at the middle range (i.e., with deep connection to the empirical context) that explains the underlying mechanisms in the relationship between Q&A community network brokerage and continued knowledge production. Based on a panel dataset from Game Development Stack Exchange, we find that: (1) network brokerage indeed fosters knowledge receipt, which in turn reinforces users to continue knowledge seeking and contribution; (2) knowledge receipt partially mediates the effects of network brokerage on continued knowledge seeking and contribution; and (3) user tenure negatively moderates the indirect effects of network brokerage on continued knowledge seeking and contribution through knowledge receipt. This research offers theoretical and practical implications for designing and managing online communities dedicated to peer knowledge production.

Keywords: Structural Holes, Network Brokerage, Peer Knowledge Production, Professional Q&A Communities, Knowledge Seeking, Knowledge Contribution, Middle-Range Theorizing, Moderated Mediation

## 1 Introduction

Since the turn of the millennium, knowledge seekers and providers have increasingly exchanged information in web-based platforms rather than only through existing physical social ties (Ray et al., 2014). The use of online question-and-answer (Q&A) communities has significantly expanded the scope and implications of knowledge sharing and production. Professional Q&A communities apply a Wiki-like crowdsourcing approach to collectively produce knowledge and innovatively solve problems encountered by peers in a professional domain (Haas et al., 2015). For example, the Stack Exchange Network provides a variety of communities for professional peers to share knowledge and solve problems online. By participating in these online communities, users are freed from geographic and temporal constraints that can otherwise limit the effectiveness of knowledge production and exchange, especially when highly specialized knowledge is sought or provided.

Such communities facilitate not only the exchange of technical solutions for specific problems encountered but also the exchange of innovative ideas, often requiring diversity of knowledge and knowledge recombination (Dong & Yang, 2019; Xiao et al., 2022). In fact, it is essential that online communities dedicated to professional knowledge production foster both technical expertise and innovative solutions. If such knowledge depth and recombination can both be supported and rewarded, user engagement is likely to be sustained or increased over time (Huysman & Wulf, 2006). However, achieving a balance between depth and breadth of knowledge from a crowd of distributed peers is not trivial, as it heavily relies on continued user engagement.

Therefore, from a design perspective, a central challenge for Q&A communities is to provide mechanisms that ensure participants have the opportunity to contribute to knowledge production, and at the same time, are motivated to continuously engage in such efforts (Huysman & Wulf, 2006). This implies a need for a sociotechnical perspective (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977) on Q&A community design that integrates the interrelated technical and social systems underlying the knowledge production environment. The technical system provides a topic-centered communication space that facilitates the exchange of innovative ideas and the generation of solutions to problems, whereas the social system offers a membercentered communication space concerned with human attributes and relationships among people (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977; Huysman & Wulf, 2006).

Although many Q&A communities have adopted gamified features (i.e., game-design elements applied in a nongame context), such as user levels and reputations in the social system (e.g., Chen et al., 2022; Goes et al., 2016; Khansa et al., 2015), scant research has comprehensively considered social network structural position as an important design feature of the social system that provides an advantage in terms of fostering knowledge production.

To address missing links in the literature, this research aims to comprehensively assess how Q&A community network brokerage (network brokerage, hereafter)—the extent to which a user serves as a bridge that links otherwise unconnected groups in the Q&A community social network—affects continued user engagement. Information brokerage provides a structural opportunity for quality peer knowledge production. Meanwhile, associated reinforcement mechanisms incentivize members across the entire community to continuously engage in knowledge seeking and contribution. By unveiling the theoretical mechanism of the effect of network brokerage, this research highlights the relevance of social network structural positions to online community dynamics and management, while offering implications for information systems (IS) design theories focused on peer knowledge production in online communities.

This study considers how network brokerage, as well as associated reinforcement mechanisms, affect user engagement in online peer knowledge production, primarily using the lenses of structural hole theory (Burt, 1992) and reinforcement theory (Skinner, 1953). A central proposition of Burt’s structural hole theory is that an individual who facilitates connections between otherwise isolated groups can enhance performance (Burt, 1992, 2001). In the setting of Q&A communities, since knowledge and ideas are more homogeneous within rather than between groups (Burt, 2004), participants bridging a higher proportion of structural holes between groups in Q&A communities benefit from access to a wealth of diverse and novel information and knowledge. Yet questions remain as to whether and how network brokerage can facilitate continued user engagement in peer knowledge production in Q&A communities, given that they have dual goals (i.e., efficiency of technical solutions vs. novelty of ideas), which may result in unwanted competition between ideas and the reduction of user engagement due to conflicts or lack of specifics.

Pointedly, structural hole theory can be adapted to explain how network brokerage benefits individuals in achieving knowledge production goals. We consider receiving knowledge from the community to solve one’s own problems as the most important utilitarian goal of user engagement in professional Q&A communities. Reinforcement theory can then be adapted to understand the empirical specifics of our context of study and specifically explain how knowledge receipt reinforces user engagement in continued knowledge seeking and contribution. Reinforcement theory, a learning and attribution theory, suggests that individuals attribute a causal linkage between previous behaviors and their consequences and use specific associations developed through this learning process to guide future behaviors (Komaki, 2003).

Although previous research has established a general relationship between social network structures and online knowledge contribution behavior (Beck et al., 2014; Chiu et al., 2006; Safadi et al., 2021; Wasko & Faraj, 2005), little is known about the role of network brokerage in facilitating continued knowledge contribution and how reinforcement mechanisms facilitate knowledge contribution. This work considers an individual to have a higher degree of network brokerage if the individual is positioned in a network in a way that connects disparate clusters of users. This implies diversity of knowledge and boundary spanning capabilities. Such diversity, coupled with the reinforcement of user actions that sustain and refresh communities, is vital for keeping communities invigorated with new perspectives and preventing echo chambers (Burgio et al., 2022), as professional knowledge production is often most successful when it is both efficient and innovative. Thus, understanding network brokerage and related reinforcement mechanisms has important implications for professional Q&A communities in that knowledge flows and innovation have direct impacts on product quality, innovation, and ultimately business revenue.

In addition, limited attention has been paid to simultaneously studying both knowledge seeking and contribution, the two essential balanced components of user engagement in peer knowledge production (Khansa et al., 2015; Phang et al., 2009). A hitherto underexplored notion is whether the effect of network brokerage extends to knowledge seeking behaviors. Consequently, ignoring the mechanisms and processes that underlie the effect of network brokerage, while also primarily focusing on knowledge contribution, leads to an incomplete and limited understanding of user engagement in online communities. In light of this, this work considers the impacts of both network brokerage and associated reinforcement mechanisms on knowledge seeking and contribution rather than just one of these theory bases.

Theoretically, this research contributes by developing a middle-range theory (Bourgeois, 1979; Merton, 1968) of user engagement in professional Q&A communities characterized by specific technical needs as well as a need for continued innovation. IS scholars have strongly advocated middle-range theorizing for the creation of innovative IS theories that are theoretically relevant and at the same time close to observed data and practice (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015; Hassan & Lowry, 2015). Further, “Research claiming to exist at the middle range must demonstrate three elements: clear positioning within a specified domain of knowledge; direct extension/clarification of established findings; and an explicit focus on causal mechanisms and the contexts in which they produce outcomes” (Stank et al., 2017, p. 14). With regard to these criteria, our positioning is within the domain of peer knowledge production in professional Q&A communities that need both technical proficiency as well as innovation. We extend the findings in this area by focusing on the moderated mediation mechanism of how Q&A community network brokerage indirectly affects user engagement via knowledge receipt as well as the conditional boundary of this mediation effect and related reinforcement mechanisms. Thus, our focus is deeply embedded within the context of online peer knowledge production (Pellathy et al., 2018). By proposing a moderated mediation research model at the middle range, this research offers a step forward in solving the challenge of under- or nonengagement of online community participants. Simply looking for an effect of social network structure on user engagement, which would be consistent with general or grand theorizing, is insufficient; rather looking deeply within the process of this effect is preferable in that it is consistent with middle-range theorizing (Kaidesoja, 2019).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. First, structural hole and reinforcement theories are discussed as our theoretical bases. A middle-range theorizing approach is introduced and is connected to the empirical context of online peer knowledge production, followed by an integrative research model proposed to unlock the effect of network brokerage on continued knowledge seeking and contribution. Hypotheses are tested using a rich panel dataset collected from a community-based Q&A website. Research contributions to theory and practice are then discussed.

## 2 Theoretical Background

In this section, the fundamentals of structural hole theory and reinforcement theory are discussed first. The empirical context—user engagement in professional Q&A communities—is introduced next with a middlerange theorizing approach that connects structural hole and reinforcement theories to this context. Then, in the major section that follows, the theoretical model and hypotheses are examined.

## 2.1 Structural Hole Theory

Structural hole theory, one of the most influential social network theories, was originally proposed by Burt (1992) and then further extended about a decade later to the generation of ideas through information arbitrage (Burt, 2004). The core proposition of structural hole theory is that agents can derive benefits by accessing innovative and diversified information and resources through their social network positions that bridge across structural holes, aka, brokerage (Burt, 1992, 2004). According to this theory, unconnected individuals in a social network form structural holes that are key to the diffusion of innovative ideas and resources (Burt, 1992, 2001, 2004). Nodes that bridge disconnected groups can fill the structural holes and are thus called structural hole spanners or between-group brokers. Burt (2004, p. 356) stated: “People whose networks span structural holes have early access to diverse, often contradictory, information and interpretations, which gives them a competitive advantage in seeing good ideas.” With such a vision advantage of accessing diverse information and developing good ideas, structural hole spanners are more likely to enhance their own outcomes and those of others in social interactions. Extant research has documented a relationship between network brokerage and social interaction outcomes such as work performance, job security, innovation, creative performance, content sharing, knowledge contribution, and community responsiveness (Hauser et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2018; Park et al., 2017; Shi et al., 2014; Wu, 2013; Wu & Chang, 2013).

Although structural hole theory seems to explain the same phenomenon as the strength-of-weak-ties perspective originally articulated in Granovetter’s seminal work (1973), the structural hole perspective provides a more compelling theoretical basis for directly explaining the causal effect of a network brokerage position that spans structural holes. In contrast, tie weakness is often considered a necessary but not sufficient condition for brokerage (Brashears & Quintane, 2018; Burt, 1992). As Burt (1992, pp. 27-28) argued: “The structural-hole argument captures the causal agent directly, providing a stronger foundation for theory and a clearer guide for empirical research.”

While the general impacts of network brokerage on social interaction outcomes are well established in the literature, its specific impacts and associated mechanisms in online communities are not as well understood. Although there has been some work in this area related to open source communities (Lei et al., 2024) and online social networks (Lin et al., 2022), the impacts on user engagement have yet to be fully considered. Further, a common assumption among social network theories is that network structure affects the efficacy and efficiency of knowledge sharing as well as individuals’ ability to create, transfer, access, absorb, and apply knowledge (Phelps et al., 2012). However, the literature to date focuses primarily on the direct effect of social network structure on knowledge contribution (Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chen et al., 2019; Chiu et al., 2006; Faraj et al., 2015; Huffaker, 2010; Johnson et al., 2014; Nov et al., 2012; Safadi et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2011; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Yan et al., 2019) but often overlooks the mechanisms and processes that underlie the social network effect, leaving us with an incomplete understanding of how social network structure affects user engagement. Further, there is a limited number of prior studies investigating the direct relationship between network structure and knowledge contribution mainly from the perspective of social capital theory (Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chiu et al., 2006; Nov et al., 2012; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Yan et al., 2019). This is surprising given the importance of network structure in facilitating socia interaction outcomes and the growing attention it has received in various disciplines such as management and sociology (Aral & Van Alstyne, 2011; Carnabuci & Quintane, 2023; Phelps et al., 2012; Tan et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2018; Zaheer & Bell, 2005).

## 2.2 Reinforcement Theory

Reinforcement theory, initially developed by the noted psychologist B. F. Skinner (1953), posits that human behavior is shaped by the consequences and associations one develops between behaviors and outcomes. In other words, people are motivated to repeat behaviors associated with positive rewards. The association of a behavior and a reward is developed (or manipulated) over time when behaviors result in positive feedback that then, at least partially, reinforces the rewarded behavior. In contrast, undesirable behaviors that are ignored or punished are likely to diminish as they develop negative and often undesirable associations. This aligns well with the law of effect (Thorndike, 1927), a principle that people tend to repeat previous actions that led to positive outcomes and avoid actions that resulted in negative outcomes. Reinforcement theory has been applied to contexts such as knowledge management (Durcikova & Gray, 2009), strategic management (Prahalad & Bettis, 1986), and performance management (Mwita, 2000).

As a theoretical framework for explaining human motivation, reinforcement theory fits nicely into the setting of online peer knowledge production, where it is crucial to understand how to effectively promote desirable user engagement behaviors by creating positive conditions and/or depressing negative factors. Positive reinforcement (i.e., rewards or pleasant outcomes) and negative reinforcement (i.e., punishments/unpleasant outcomes or nonresponses) are the two primary mechanisms in reinforcement theory (Estes, 1972; Michael, 1975). Positive reinforcement refers to the presence of desirable consequences, a circumstance that increases the likelihood of similar future behaviors. On the other hand, punishment is any unpleasant outcome resulting from a behavior that demotivates the repeating of such behavior in the future; the withholding of rewards is also considered a negative reinforcement.

Applied to the setting of professional Q&A communities, reinforcement theory suggests that continued user engagement will be sustained when positive reinforcing factors are present (e.g., positive outcomes of user engagement, such as ways users can improve their online reputation). Perhaps ironically, many online communities have mechanisms that could also encourage negative feedback to detect and filter low-quality content meanwhile motivating users to modify their behaviors (Bandura, 1977; Bian et al., 2008). What is critical, though, is that such negative reinforcement should not be so repressive that users completely abandon the online community. This study seeks to contribute to this literature by extending our current comprehension of how reinforcement bridges the effect of network brokerage on continued user engagement in online peer knowledge production.

## 2.3 Theorizing at the Middle Range: Engaging Users in Online Peer Knowledge Production

User engagement generally refers to the state of being emotionally involved, fully occupied, and intrinsically interested in an activity (O’Brien & Toms, 2008; Pagani & Mirabello, 2011). Two important aspects of user engagement in the context of online peer knowledge production are: (1) seeking knowledge from and (2) contributing knowledge to the online community (Khansa et al., 2015; Phang et al., 2009). Since underengagement or nonengagement (Liebowitz & Margolis, 1994) is a common challenge for online communities, it is pivotal to motivate and engage continued user participation (Wang & Fesenmaier, 2004) and reduce knowledge withholding (Jiang et al., 2018; Shen et al., 2019; Strik et al., 2021). Accordingly, the sustainability of professional Q&A communities relies predominantly on mechanisms that can effectively incentivize continued knowledge seeking (i.e., submission of problems) and knowledge contribution (i.e., timely supply of high-quality solutions).

To more deeply engage with the empirical aspects of research context, as recommended for middle-range theorizing (Hassan & Lowry, 2015; Stank et al., 2017), this work connects the high-level or molar theories of structural holes and reinforcement to the empirical context. <sup>1</sup> Middle-range theorizing is a strongly advocated approach for building contextualized, innovative IS theories that are theoretically robust while retaining their testability using empirical data (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015; Hassan & Lowry, 2015; Hong et al., 2014). Specifically, we focus on professional Q&A communities as the empirical context. In point of fact, professional Q&A communities are online peer production communities that tie decentralized peers sharing common interests together in a specific area of practice (Jiang et al., 2018; Wenger & Snyder, 2000). As more peers engage actively in knowledge seeking and contribution, the repositories of knowledge grow more attractive to new users due to positive network externalities (Katz & Shapiro, 1985; Liebowitz & Margolis, 1994). Nevertheless, without a sufficient level of continued user engagement, professional Q&A communities are less able to leverage collective intelligence (Malone & Bernstein, 2015) and establish effective knowledge flows and the introduction of new, innovative ideas that further encourage community members to continue participation. Maintaining continued user engagement in peer knowledge production poses a key challenge for such communities, as dual goals must be achieved: (1) efficient exchange of needed information (i.e., answers to technical questions) and (2) novelty of solutions (i.e., new ways to solve problems that go beyond simple technical fixes).

Previous research on online peer knowledge production suggests that user engagement results from a variety of social and technical factors, as summarized in Table 1. While prior research has investigated a number of factors influencing user engagement in online communities, limited research has paid attention to how mechanisms associated with structural aspects, such as network brokerage, influence both continued knowledge seeking and contribution. For instance, outcome expectations (both personal- and community-associated) are strong motivating forces, as participants see their engagement to be enriching knowledge, seeking help, solving problems, or simply obtaining social recognition and respect (Chiu et al., 2006; Hsu et al., 2007; Lou et al., 2013). Consequently, goal attainment is an essentia driving force of knowledge contribution (Chen et al., 2022; Goes et al., 2016). Achieving goals of knowledge exchange can enhance user perceptions of community resources (Kankanhalli et al., 2005b; Lai et al., 2014), which, in turn, ensure that social interactions are congruent with user goals of information search, problem solving, and even learning cutting-edge ideas for problem solving or the generation of new ideas. In this way, participants are incentivized to continue to engage with the community. Naturally, user perceptions such as perceived usability, sociability, relative advantage, and compatibility are significant explanatory variables for user engagement in seeking and contributing knowledge (Chen & Hung, 2010; Phang et al., 2009). From the perspective of social capital theory (Coleman, 1988; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998), structural capital (such as social interaction ties and network centrality), cognitive capital (such as shared language, shared vision, self-rated expertise, and tenure), and relational capital (such as trust, commitment, and norm of reciprocity) are important explanatory variables for knowledge contribution (Chiu et al., 2006; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Wiertz & de Ruyter, 2007).

## 3 Research Model and Hypotheses

Figure 1 previews a proposed moderated mediation research model explaining how an individual’s network brokerage in a professional Q&A community affects the user’s engagement in continued knowledge seeking and contribution in the community. This model is consistent with middle-range theorizing advice suggesting the evaluation of questions such as: “How is information produced in practice?” (Hassan & Lowry, 2015, p. 13). As shown in the model, knowledge receipt is posited to mediate the positive effects of network brokerage on continued knowledge seeking and contribution behaviors. User tenure is proposed as an important contingency factor that negatively moderates the indirect effects of network brokerage on continued knowledge seeking and contribution. The theoretical rationale for the research model is explained in the following subsections.

## 3.1 Q&A Community Network Brokerage and Knowledge Receipt

Users participate in professional Q&A communities mainly to collaboratively produce knowledge to solve their own problems (Faraj et al., 2016; Haas et al., 2015). Network brokerage in the Q&A community can facilitate the utilitarian goal of receiving diverse and heterogeneous knowledge from peers. According to Burt’s structural hole theory (Burt, 1992, 2004), a user’s network brokerage (i.e., the extent to which the user bridges structural holes in the social network) provides the user with a vision advantage in accessing diversified expertise and ideas. This implies that network brokerage allows for the linkage of more heterogeneous people and the synthesis of diverse expertise from various sources, thereby increasing opportunities for innovation, knowledge recombination, and improving the efficiency and effectiveness of peer knowledge production (Aral & Van Alstyne, 2011; Kwon et al., 2020; Xiao et al., 2022). Network brokerage is particularly critical for peer knowledge production in professional Q&A communities that rely on the generation and exchange of highly specialized innovative solutions and ideas. The network position of brokerage plays a pivotal role for users to receive valuable knowledge from the community to solve their problems. These users not only have access to diversified information related to their problems at hand, they are also among the first to recognize new opportunities and strategically seize such opportunities to enhance their problem solving performance (Wu, 2013). Thus, users with higher network brokerage are expected to be more likely to receive knowledge to solve their problems. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: Users with higher Q&A community network brokerage are likely to receive more knowledge from the community.

## 3.2 Knowledge Receipt and Continued User Engagement

Knowledge receipt from the community, facilitated by a participant’s network position of brokerage, can in turn incentivize continued user engagement. This can be explained from the perspective of reinforcement theory (Skinner, 1953). Applied to the setting of online peer knowledge production, reinforcement theory suggests that for users to be motivated to further seek and/or contribute knowledge, positive outcomes need to be presented to them and negative ones suppressed. Obtaining favored outcomes from online peer knowledge production is among the various incentives that drive continued user engagement. The premise of reinforcement theory is that current user behaviors can be understood by considering how users perceive the consequences of their past behaviors. Hence, reinforcement theory approaches peer knowledge production behavior from the standpoint of proxies visible to others for outcomes, the most prominent of which is solving a participant’s own problems by receiving knowledge from peers.

This study focuses on the amount of knowledge received from the Q&A community as an important utilitarian goal of user engagement in the community. According to reinforcement theory, the fact that users have received knowledge from the community to solve their problems reinforces their belief that the community contains the resources required to solve their future problems when needed. Consequently, users choose to continue knowledge seeking in the community. This effect can be further explained from the perspective of resource availability. Studies generally treat resource availability as an important antecedent of technology adoption (Aubert et al., 2012; Chau & Tam, 2000; Taylor & Todd, 1995) and an important factor for maintaining online social structures (Butler, 2001). In the setting of organizational electronic knowledge repositories, users perceiving a higher level of resource availability are more likely to seek knowledge (Bock et al., 2006; Kankanhalli et al., 2005b). The above reasoning leads to the prediction that previous experience in receiving knowledge from the community reinforces the perception of platform resource availability, which further ensures that continued knowledge seeking will be rewarded by finding relevant knowledge or resources when required. Holding a positive expectation of obtaining needed knowledge from the community, users having received knowledge from the community tend to continue engaging in knowledge seeking. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H2a: User reception of more knowledge from Q&A communities leads to users being more likely to continue engaging in knowledge seeking.

Similarly, receiving knowledge from the community positively reinforces continued knowledge contribution to the community. Such a reinforcing effect can be explained from the perspective of reciprocity. Prior studies suggest that reciprocity is a powerful reinforcing source that governs user behaviors in knowledge exchange (Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chiu et al., 2006; Kankanhalli et al., 2005a; Pai & Tsai, 2016; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Wiertz & de Ruyter, 2007). Norms of reciprocity are a commonly accepted social rule that obligates people to return favors in trade or appreciation for what they have received (Gouldner, 1960). As Davenport and Prusak (1998, p. 32) explained about knowledge markets: “A knowledge seller will spend the time and effort needed to share knowledge effectively if he expects the buyers to be willing sellers when he is in the market for their knowledge.” Specifically, prior research on online peer knowledge production suggests that the perception of reciprocity associates positively with knowledge sharing behavior (Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chen & Hung, 2010; Chiu et al., 2006; Kankanhalli et al., 2005a; Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Wiertz & de Ruyter, 2007).

Norms of reciprocity are particularly important for professional Q&A communities focused on peer knowledge production to mitigate the risk of a one-way knowledge transfer in which the knowledge contributors are exploited without benefits or compensation (Wiertz & de Ruyter, 2007). Derived from the basic tenets of reinforcement theory, reciprocity is thought to be learned from previous social interaction which then further reinforces continuous behaviors that are assumed to be mutually beneficial. Given the general norm of reciprocity that guides peer knowledge production behaviors in professional Q&A communities, participants who have received knowledge from the community to solve their own problems are hence motivated to contribute back to the community. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

H2b: User reception of more knowledge from Q&A communities leads to users being more likely to continue engaging in knowledge contribution.

## 3.3 The Mediation Effect of Knowledge Receipt

Research has established that social networks have positive direct effects on knowledge contribution, an important dimension of user engagement. In the setting of electronic network of practice (a web-based message board), Wasko and Faraj (2005) found that a participant’s degree centrality has a positive effect on both the volume and helpfulness of knowledge contribution. Chiu et al. (2006) found that social interaction ties are positively associated with participants’ knowledge sharing quantity. In the context of online health communities, Chen et al. (2019) found a positive impact of social network centrality on informational support provision, an important type of knowledge contribution.

This research complements extant literature by focusing on network brokerage, a well-established construct indicating the degree of spanning structural holes and having structurally nonredundant contacts in a social network. As argued in Subsections 3.1 and 3.2, the literature strongly supports the notion that network brokerage directly impacts knowledge receipt; meanwhile, knowledge receipt is theoretically predicted to reinforce continued knowledge seeking and contribution behaviors. Given the solid theoretical bases as well as empirical studies supporting an effect of network brokerage on user engagement, this work theorizes that the role of network brokerage in motivating continued user engagement is cultivated through the reinforcing effect of knowledge receipt. Specifically, we propose the following two hypotheses.

H3a: The amount of knowledge received by a user mediates the effect of Q&A community network brokerage on continued knowledge seeking.

H3b: The amount of knowledge received by a user mediates the effect of Q&A community network brokerage on continued knowledge contribution.

## 3.4 The Moderation Effect of User Tenure

Middle-range theorizing embraces empirical specifics of the context under study; hence, the following hypotheses focus on user tenure as an important moderating factor of network brokerage. Further, Burt’s (1997) contingency view of social capital offers a theoretical framework to comprehend the nuanced effects of Q&A community network brokerage on knowledge receipt. Burt (2000) specifically suggested a range of contingency factors such as personality and culture, types of relationships, peers and task uncertainty, and network closure. According to this contingency view, the structural advantage of network brokerage in accessing diverse and innovative information depends on a set of contingency factors. Prior studies have applied this contingency view to explore a variety of structural and environmental factors that impact the role of network brokerage. For example, Aral and Van Alstyne (2011) suggested a trade-off between network diversity and bandwidth that can negatively impact the competitive advantage of accessing diverse and nonredundant information through a network position of brokerage. Tan et al. (2015) suggested network density as an important contingency factor that could change the direction of the relationship between network brokerage and firm-level innovation performance.

The current study extends the contingency view of social capital by incorporating user tenure into the network mechanism involving brokerage. Tenure refers to the length of time a user has spent in peer knowledge production in the professional Q&A community. The current literature has mixed and contradictory findings regarding the relationship between tenure and user engagement. Watson and Hewett (2006) suggested organizational tenure as an indicator of the individual’s commitment to knowledge exchange and found organizational tenure to positively relate to knowledge contribution frequency. Treating tenure as a dimension of cognitive capital that fosters shared interpretations and meaningful interactions, Wasko and Faraj (2005) found user tenure positively affects the volume of the user’s knowledge contribution in an electronic network of practice. In the setting of Q&A communities, however, Khansa et al. (2015) found user tenure to be negatively associated with continued knowledge seeking and contribution. These contradictory findings suggest a need for more deeply examining the relationships between user tenure and user engagement behaviors.

Thus, user tenure may be an important moderator affecting the mediating role of knowledge receipt in the relationship between network brokerage and continued user engagement. In the context of professional Q&A communities, peer knowledge production relies on a creative problem solving process that involves multiple steps such as problem definition, idea generation, and transforming ideas into solutions (Kashani-Vahid et al., 2017; Treffinger, 2007). For the creative problem solving process to work, participants need to engage in both divergent thinking (generating multiple ideas and solutions) and convergent thinking (evaluating alternatives and choosing the optimal solution). However, studies generally suggest that tenure is an obstructing factor for tasks related to creativity (Ng & Feldman, 2010; Yang et al., 2022). A well-established theoretical explanation is that long-tenured users tend to have a high level of cognitive fixation—the tendency to be mentally stuck on a particular solution and unable to explore other problem solving approaches (Duncker, 1945; Lu et al., 2017). Due to cognitive fixation on their past knowledge and experience, Q&A community participants with lengthy tenures are less likely to actively engage in divergent and convergent thinking in the peer knowledge production process. Although network brokerage brings a structural opportunity for an individual to be exposed to a wide range of ideas and resources, such a brokerage opportunity does not necessarily lead to increased knowledge receipt and subsequently continued engagement in knowledge seeking and contribution. When participants with long tenure are mentally fixed to old-fashioned approaches, they lack the incentive to explore diverse ideas and knowledge accessible by the structural advantage of network brokerage (Burt, 2000). Thus, it is expected that a user’s tenure in the Q&A community will be a contingency factor that negatively moderates the indirect effect of network brokerage on continued user engagement in peer knowledge production. Hence, we hypothesize:

H4a: A user’s tenure negatively moderates the indirect effect of Q&A community network brokerage on continued knowledge seeking via knowledge receipt such that the indirect effect is weaker when the user has a longer tenure in the community.

H4b: A user’s tenure negatively moderates the indirect effect of Q&A community network brokerage on continued knowledge contribution via knowledge receipt such that the indirect effect is weaker when the user has a longer tenure in the community.

## 4 Methods

## 4.1 Methodological Setting and Data Collection

A field study was conducted by analyzing an archival dataset collected from Game Development Stack Exchange, a US-hosted Q&A website for game developers and IT professionals. This website is a community-based platform dedicated to peer production of knowledge related to game development programming frameworks, languages, and tools. Users across the globe participate, seeking knowledge to solve their game development problems and sharing game development knowledge with others. Such knowledge production via online communities is essential for the success of game development, which requires innovative ideas and solutions to be prototyped, evaluated, and refined through an iterative process before finalizing the product or features. Indeed, the setting of online Q&A communities has been widely investigated in previous studies to gain insight into peer knowledge production behaviors (Cavusoglu et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2018, 2022; Lou et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2016).

On the study website, users register to participate for free. They can ask questions regarding game software development or provide answers to questions posted by other users. The platform also implements incentivebased features, including achievement badges and reputation scores for knowledge seeking and contribution. Badges are granted to users to recognize their achievements in knowledge production based on certain criteria. For example, a user can earn a “popular question” badge if the user has submitted a question that receives 1,000 or more views. A “refiner” badge is granted for edits and answers to 50 or more questions. Achievement badges are categorized into three levels including gold, silver, and bronze. Calculated using all criteria related to user participation, an overall reputation score is also displayed in one’s user profile.

The full dataset includes 28,238 questions and 33,804 answers posted within this community from November 2014 to November 2020. As a result, an unbalanced monthly panel dataset of 117,958 observations for 26,216 unique users across 73 months (from November 2014 to November 2020) was obtained. To calculate social network brokerage, a weighted directional network was constructed for each month t (t = 1, 2, …, 73) according to problem solving relationships occurring before month t. Users are represented as nodes in the monthly social network. A tie from user i to user j indicates the former user has provided answers to the latter user; the strength of the tie is defined as the frequency of the problem solving relationship. Similar to Wu (2013), we counted the problem solving activities within the last 6 months to deal with the potential erosion of network relationships over time. The social network constructed from problem solving relationships for November 2020 is shown in Figure 2, where the size of each node is proportional to its degree centrality (i.e., the number of the node’s direct connections). The sample network clearly shows major clusters and isolated cliques in the network, suggesting the potential of network brokerage to foster collaboration and innovation within the community.

## 4.2 Variables

Table 2 describes the variables used in this study, including the primary dependent and independent variables and controls. The unit of analysis is the individual; the sampling frame is all users who participated in the platform during the time frame of data collection.

Network brokerage is defined as the extent to which a user’s network position bridges otherwise unconnected groups in the social network. Following previous studies (Aral & Van Alstyne, 2011; Kim et al., 2018; Sajuria et al., 2015; Wu, 2013; Wu et al., 2018; Zaheer & Bell, 2005), network redundancy (or the lack of structural holes) was measured as Burt’s network constraint by using the following formula (Burt, 1992):

$$
C _ {i t} = \sum_ {j} \bigl (p _ {i j t} + \sum_ {q} p _ {i q t} p _ {q j t} \bigr) ^ {2}, q \neq i, j\tag{1}
$$

where $p _ { i j t }$ is the proportion of the strength of user i’s tie(s) with user j compared to the total network strength of all ties of user i in month t. Let $a _ { i j t }$ denote the strength of the tie from i to j in month t. The proportional tie strength $p _ { i j t }$ is defined as:

$$
p _ {i j t} = \frac {a _ {i j t} + a _ {j i t}}{\sum_ {k} (a _ {i k t} + a _ {k i t})}\tag{2}
$$

For isolated users, network constraint $C _ { i t } = 1$ . Burt’s network constraint measures how concentrated a node’s connections are within a group of interconnected nodes. A higher network constraint indicates a higher degree to which a node is constrained in its own local group and thus is more limited to access information and resources from other groups. In other words, a higher network constraint means a lower opportunity for a node to broker between groups in the social network. Therefore, the extent of Q&A community network brokerage (QACNB) by user i in month t was measured as the opposite of network constraint using the following formula:

$$
Q A C N B _ {i t} = 1 - C _ {i t}\tag{3}
$$

## 4.3 Model Specification and Estimation

A dynamic panel regression model was used to test the proposed hypotheses by including lagged levels of dependent variables to control for potential persistence in individual activities in the professional Q&A community across time periods (Khansa et al., 2015; Menon & Kohli, 2013). The two-step system GMM (system generalized method of moments) estimator by Blundell and Bond (1998) was used, given that: (1) fixed-effects (FE) approaches were used to control for individual heterogeneity, which is often not directly observable in online community datasets; (2) traditional static fixed-effects or random-effects estimators may lead to biased estimates in the presence of lagged dependent variables as regressors; (3) the panel dataset was short (a large number of users across relatively a limited number of time periods); (4) heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation potentially existed within users; and (5) the independent variables in the proposed research model might have been endogenous (Arellano & Bond, 1991; Roodman, 2009). By conducting a Wooldridge test (Drukker, 2003; Wooldridge, 2010) for serial correlation in the panel data, we rejected the null hypotheses that there is no first-order autocorrelation in the idiosyncratic errors (p-values < 0.001 for knowledge receipt, continued knowledge seeking, and continued knowledge contribution models). The presence of serial correlation suggests that a static panel regression model with random effects or fixed effects would be invalid, thus supporting the choice of a dynamic panel regression model. The system GMM estimator has been widely applied in prior IS studies (Aral et al., 2012; Bhargava & Mishra, 2014; Chen et al., 2019; Khansa et al., 2015; Menon & Kohli, 2013) to deal with similar autocorrelation and endogeneity issues as those present in this research.

## 6 Discussion

Professional Q&A communities offer a convenient platform for producing knowledge and collaboratively solving problems with peers, both for technical fixes and the generation of innovative ideas. Knowledge recombination is particularly important to innovative processes (Xiao et al., 2022) but, in the online community context, would require contributions from users who have knowledge outside of the bounds of the highly cohesive local groups. Thus, structuring Q&A communities to allow for knowledge transfer between disconnected groups is posited to be vital for motivating ongoing user engagement. Yet a thorough assessment in this context remains to be done. As eloquently stated by Faraj and Shimizu (2018, p. 11) in their summary of online community research, “the mere existence of digital platforms does not guarantee vibrant social interactions and deep engagement in the community.” Therefore, using the community itself and automated platform features to maximize user engagement, while also seeking to aid in the moderation of content being produced and exchanged, is nontrivial (Charband & Navimipour, 2016; Zhang et al., 2013, 2014; Zhao et al., 2016). Further, while a variety of models have been tested before in the context of online knowledge production, a deeper understanding of user motivations is still needed (Charband & Navimipour, 2016; Tedjamulia et al., 2005).

The contribution of the present study to structural hole theory is that the empirical setting of our middle-range theory generally supports molar-level conceptualizations. Research has shown that structural hole theory covers a wider range of phenomena than the largely face-to-face networks that this theory was originally developed for. Im and Straub (2015) referred to this as successful “theoretical generalization” and argued that theories become more valuable and useful when the application of a theory to new settings results in higher external validity. Indeed, they pointed out that external validity is critical for advancing organizational theorizing.

In the case of reinforcement theory, whereas scientific evidence in favor of reinforcement theory is voluminous, most such studies look at traditional behaviors in response to stimuli that are physical in nature and overtly extrinsic (Kumari et al., 2021; Luthans & Stajkovic, 1999; Skinner, 1953). The online Q&A community setting is quite different in that individuals are represented symbolically in virtual environments. User engagement in virtual communities has all the hallmarks of anonymity and invisibility— compared to settings where people are known for their physically identifiable features (at least as far as people faithfully represent themselves) and behaviors.

Through our generalization to the online world, we were able to determine that the human behavior being acted out in such contexts is remarkably similar to what occurs in traditional, face-to-face settings. Thus, both structure hole and reinforcement theories appear to extend nicely to online Q&A communities and perhaps even to other community types in the online world. This research provides robust evidence that the network brokerage position in the Q&A community social network is essential to the diffusion of critical problem solving information and knowledge (Burt, 1992). Specifically, we found that having a greater extent of network brokerage helps users find answers by receiving more knowledge from the online community. This finding is in line with previous IS research that has demonstrated associations between network brokerage and a variety of individual, community, and organizational outcomes such as work productivity and job security (Wu, 2013), creative performance (Wu & Chang, 2013), content sharing on Twitter (Shi et al., 2014), community responsiveness (Kim et al., 2018), and product sales (Lin et al., 2017). Our research goes deeper, however, by showing that participants indeed benefit from a network position that brokers disconnected groups in the social network established from prior peer knowledge production. This implies that integrating network brokerage into the theoretical consideration of user engagement, as this research does, is imperative for fully understanding user engagement behaviors in online communities.

With respect to the reinforcement mechanism of knowledge receipt, our findings support that the success of receiving knowledge from online peers reinforces user beliefs that the online community works for them personally, thus motivating further efforts in seeking knowledge from and contributing knowledge back to the community. This finding leads to a deeper understanding of how reinforcement works in such communities, where the reinforcement is generated through knowledge receipt over time. Thus, we demonstrated that user engagement is a reinforcement process rather than an isolated event and must be continually cultivated through positive experiences with peer knowledge production. This extends previous studies mainly focused on general usage/continuance of online communities (Bateman et al., 2011; Kuem et al., 2020; Tiwana & Bush, 2005; Wang et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2017).

Further, our empirical results provide evidence that the effects of network brokerage on continued knowledge seeking and contribution are mediated by the amount of knowledge received from other peers. Extending prior studies that have only investigated the general relationship between social network structure and knowledge contribution, our findings show that this effect of network brokerage also applies to knowledge seeking, another integrating component of user engagement in Q&A communities. This provides significant implications for continuously motivating users to produce professional knowledge. Given that those users with high network brokerage regulate and maintain information flow and knowledge production, it is critical to continuously motivate their high-quality engagement. We argue that knowledge receipt is one such motivating force that helps to sustain online communities.

Finally, our results provide evidence that a user’s tenure in the online community is an important contingency factor that negatively moderates the indirect effects of network brokerage on continued knowledge seeking and contribution behaviors via knowledge receipt. This finding supports the contingent view of structural holes and extends prior studies on the boundary conditions of network brokerage (Aral & Van Alstyne, 2011; Burt, 2000; Tan et al., 2015). Our results support that user tenure is an obstructing factor that weakens the structural advantage of network brokerage. This finding extends the online knowledge production literature which generally regards tenure as a dimension of cognitive capital that fosters shared interpretations and meaningful interactions (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). We argue that online community users with lengthy tenure tend to be influenced by a cognitive fixation on their past experiences, which negatively impacts their motivation to actively apply creative problem solving processes to innovatively generate, recombine, and evaluate ideas and solutions. Thus, this research contributes a nuanced understanding of user tenure as a contingency factor that impacts users’ efficacy in leveraging the network brokerage opportunity in the context of professional Q&A communities.

## 6.2 Practical Implications

The findings in this study have practical implications for the design and management of online communities. In particular, the contextualized findings of this research provide opportunities to solve many of the challenges associated with under- or nonengagement in online communities.

To start with, our robust empirical evidence shows that context-specific information-rich networks, containing users with a high degree of network brokerage, play an important role in achieving participants’ goals of peer knowledge production. To enhance the effectiveness and efficiency of such peer knowledge production, community designers should consider not only the community features that directly facilitate social interaction for knowledge seeking and contribution (such as easily searching and replying to questions asked) but also the social network structure that is embedded in the community. For instance, social networking features can be implemented to foster the building of information-rich networks that benefit users in receiving knowledge from a diversified pool of potential experts and subsequently engaging in continued knowledge seeking and contribution. Perhaps statistics of network brokerage can be applied to show participants’ social network characteristics, or badges based on certain brokerage scores can be granted to participants who are potentially more important for maintaining the sustainability of the community.

This study also finds that participants who have received more knowledge from peers are more likely to continue engaging in both knowledge seeking and contribution. In other words, participants may be motivated to either continue or discontinue use as they experience different levels of knowledge receipt. This finding suggests that community designers and managers need to seriously consider effective strategies for responding to longunanswered questions. One solution would be to incentivize high-reputation participants to provide a response in order to initiate the engagement process. Such incentives could be something along the lines of “conversation starter” badges or identification of “top voices” for a particular category, as LinkedIn has started to do. Given that the sustainability of the online community depends on the success of knowledge exchange between participants, online Q&A communities should be designed and moderated by adopting user-centered approaches, potentially with the goal of facilitating knowledge receipt rather than simply negative reinforcement (i.e., downvoting without additional suggestions as to how to improve an answer). The online community can sustain and continue to provide public good only when individual users’ goal of knowledge seeking has been fulfilled.

Finally, this study finds that participants with a lengthy tenure in the community are less likely to leverage the advantageous position of network brokerage to more efficiently engage in peer knowledge production. As long-tenure users are a critical asset for maintaining the vibrancy of online peer knowledge production, motivating them to actively engage in peer knowledge production is key to the success of online communities. With the primary goal of engaging users, online community designers and managers should implement and optimize community features that help users explore innovative solutions and ideas, leveraging the network position of brokerage and at the same time alleviating the potential negative impact of having a long tenure with the community.

## 6.3 Limitations

This research focuses on user knowledge seeking and contribution behaviors in professional Q&A communities where user engagement is voluntary and there are seldom tangible economic benefits for the efforts spent by the users. Future research can extend the work here to other settings such as knowledge production in organizations (Huang et al., 2018; Pu et al., 2020) where knowledge contribution is closer to being obligatory and where direct or indirect economic benefits or compensation for engagement exist.

Furthermore, reinforcement theory suggests not only positive reinforcement mechanisms but also negative ones. On online platforms, a typical negative stimulus is negative usefulness votes (or down-votes) by other users. Such negative votes are widely used, together with positive votes, to rank or filter user-generated content on a wide range of online peer production platforms. Consequently, users’ reputation scores, rankings, and privileges on the platform are negatively impacted when negative votes are received. If punished for previous peer production behaviors, users may significantly change future behaviors. This research considers knowledge receipt as a positively reinforcing factor, largely ignoring other potential negative reinforcing elements. Thus, future research could evaluate potential negative reinforcement mechanisms in order to comprehensively understand how to reduce the quantity of low-quality content while at the same time not adversely affecting user engagement.

Additionally, this research only considers the user’s tenure as an important boundary condition that negatively moderates the indirect effects of network brokerage on continued knowledge seeking and contribution via knowledge receipt. Further research could systematically explore other contingency factors that change the role of network brokerage in peer knowledge production. Exploring those contingency factors offers potential opportunities to reveal nuanced insights and even reconcile conflicting theoretical perspectives and empirical findings.

Last, our empirical results show that the amount of knowledge received from the online community partially mediates the effect of network brokerage on continued user engagement. Future studies can further investigate other potential mediators. For example, the quality of knowledge receipt as well as other outcomes of peer knowledge production such as reputation gains may also have a mediating role in the motivating mechanism of network brokerage. This can potentially provide a more comprehensive and holistic understanding of the detailed mechanisms and processes under which social network structures influence user engagement.

## 7 Conclusion

Online community users drop out for various reasons. Maintaining user engagement is a key challenge for online social media platforms. Grounded in structural hole theory and the reinforcement theory of human motivations, this study shows how Q&A community network brokerage affects continued user engagement. We theorized at the middle range by proposing a comprehensive moderated mediation research model that theoretically integrates network brokerage, knowledge receipt, user tenure, and continued user engagement. The research model was tested by applying a robust system GMM method on a large panel dataset from an online Q&A community focused on professional peer knowledge production. This research not only offers a substantive theoretical understanding of the effect of network brokerage on peer knowledge production in professional Q&A communities but also provides practical implications for motivating continued user engagement in online communities.

Based on the weighted directional network, we can create a matrix of proportional tie strength. The proportional tie strength $p _ { i j }$ is defined as:

$$
p _ {i j} = \frac {a _ {i j} + a _ {j i}}{\sum_ {k} (a _ {i k} + a _ {k i})}
$$

where $a _ { i j }$ denotes the strength of the tie from i to j. As an example, the proportional tie strength $p _ { A B }$ is calculated as:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} p _ {A B} = & \frac {a _ {A B} + a _ {B A}}{(a _ {A B} + a _ {B A}) + (a _ {A D} + a _ {D A}) + (a _ {A C} + a _ {C A}) + (a _ {A E} + a _ {E A}) + (a _ {A F} + a _ {F A})} \\ & = \frac {0 + 2}{(0 + 2) + (0 + 4) + (4 + 0) + (1 + 0) + (3 + 0)} = \frac {2}{1 4} = \frac {1}{7} \end{array}
$$

## Appendix B: Robustness Checks

## The Impact of Q&A Community Network Brokerage on Knowledge Receipt

In our main analysis, knowledge receipt is measured as the number of answers received by the focal user in a given month that solved the focal user’s problems. This more conservative measure encompasses both the quantity and quality of knowledge received by the focal user. Since the dependent variable is truncated at zero, we first evaluated the robustness of our findings using the Tobit linear regression method (left-censored). Then, we dichotomized the dependent count variables (coded as 1 if the dependent variable ≥ 1, otherwise coded as 0) and then applied system GMM to estimate the relationships.

Although not every answer received from the community would completely solve a given problem or generate a new idea, answers often ignite more in-depth discussions and provide insights for continuously exploring and refining solutions. To extend the measurement of knowledge receipt, which only considers the number of answers that solve the focal user’s problems, we further used the number of received answers that may or may not solve the focal user’s problems as an alternative measure of knowledge receipt. Given the conversational nature of interactions that occur in Q&A communities, as well as the incremental nature of peer knowledge production, the number of answers received represents another way to capture the advantages of network brokerage and having access to diverse knowledge and expertise available within the Q&A community. With this less restrictive alternative measure of knowledge receipt, we also estimated the parameters by applying system GMM, Tobit, as well as system GMM with a dichotomized dependent variable. ## The Impact of Knowledge Receipt on Continued User Engagement

To check the robustness of the main findings from the knowledge seek and knowledge contribution models, we first used the number of answers received as an alternative measure of knowledge receipt. Then we applied the Tobit model and system GMM with a dichotomized dependent variable with the two measures of knowledge receipt to alternatively estimate the effects. Tables B2 and B3 summarize robustness checks on the effects of knowledge receipt on continuous knowledge seeking and contribution respectively.

As Table B2 shows, all estimation methods result in consistent results regarding the significant positive effect of knowledge receipt on continued knowledge seeking. As similarly shown in Table B3, all estimation methods lead to consistent results regarding the positive effect of knowledge receipt on continued knowledge contribution. The results of the robustness checks suggest that applying alternative estimation methods and alternative definitions of the key independent and dependent variables leads to results consistent with our main analyses.
