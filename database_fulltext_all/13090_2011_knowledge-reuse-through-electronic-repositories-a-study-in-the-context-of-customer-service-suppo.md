# Knowledge reuse through electronic repositories: A study in the context of customer service support

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Knowledge reuse Knowledge repository capability Extrinsic and intrinsic motivation Performance benefits Customer service

## A B S T R A C T

Organizations are implementing electronic repository systems to facilitate knowledge reuse but with varying degrees of success. There is a lack of understanding of how individual and technical factors interact in determining knowledge reuse and the performance benefits that could be derived from electronic knowledge repositories. We proposed a model to explain the impact of user motivation and the user’s perception of the value of the available knowledge repository on knowledge reuse and the individual’s performance benefits through using the system. Through a field survey, we found that perceived knowledge repository capability and intrinsic motivation positively affected knowledge reuse, which in turn impacted the benefits derived from using the system. We also found that perceived knowledge repository capability moderated the relationship between extrinsic reward and knowledge reuse.

## 1. Introduction

Knowledge has become an important organizational resource [22]. Consequently, organizations are engaging in knowledge management (KM) efforts to manage their knowledge resources effectively in order to enhance their competitiveness [3]. The activities or processes in KM include knowledge creation, capture, distribution, and application or reuse [2].

Knowledge reuse has been considered as a major justification for KM, i.e., offering productivity gains by not having to reinvent solutions [1]. Examples of knowledge reuse include transfer of best practices from one part of an organization to another and the reuse of employee knowledge captured prior to their departure from the firm [20].

It is important to investigate the use of IS to support knowledge reuse, because IT investment is expensive and the effectiveness of IT in facilitating KM remains unclear [11,33]. The class of IS and technologies that are implemented to support KM processes including knowledge reuse are the knowledge management systems (KMS). They can have various forms, such as electronic repositories, yellow pages, and forums to support communities of practice. Of these, repositories are commonly used to share codifiable knowledge in organizations. Previously created knowledge is entered into the repository so that it can be retrieved and reused without the cost of its reinvention.

While understanding why employees attain differential knowledge reuse and performance benefits through a repository is of practical concern, little research has been performed to address this issue. However, for KMS to yield organizational benefits they need to be successfully used by individual employees [5]. Therefore, the impact of KMS within an organization needs to be investigated at the individual level. However, most of this individual-level research has focused on examining the antecedents of knowledge seeking through repositories (e.g., [6,14]). A few studies have tackled related problems, e.g., Boh [8] studied how help from the provider can facilitate knowledge reuse from a KMS, while Majchrzak et al. [20] investigated how person-to-person knowledge reuse takes place in radical innovation. We intended to extend this work by considering the outcomes of knowledge seeking from KMS. Thus, our study was intended to explain individual-level impact of the use of a KMS (specifically, a knowledge repository) in supporting knowledge reuse and thereby enhancing employees’ work.

Prior studies have also explored various kinds of enablers of KM, including user motivation (e.g., [13,31]) and technology capability, but with mixed results. Therefore, based on a socio-technical perspective, we decided to determine how perceived technology capability and users’ motivations interact to influence knowledge reuse and work benefits when using a repository. Specifically, we investigated the impact of two types of user motivation, extrinsic and intrinsic, on knowledge reuse. This is because these two fundamental types of motivation operate in different ways and are stimulated differentially.

The context of our study is customer service in knowledge intensive industries, specifically the banking industry. The banking sector is knowledge intensive due to the complexity of products and services. Customer support is often more critical there, because customers find it difficult to understand the nature of their services. Quick and responsive customer service may require successful knowledge reuse; the customer service officer needs to understand previously created knowledge and adapt it to meet a specific customer’s needs. Furthermore, with improvements in information and communication technologies, expectations from customer support have reached unprecedented levels; customers expect 24-h service and instant solutions to their problems. Therefore, a customer service center was an appropriate setting for our study.

## 2. Conceptual background

Common theoretical bases of prior studies on knowledge repositories are technology acceptance models, social exchange theory, and knowledge transfer models. A few studies have also investigated person-to-person knowledge reuse using these perspectives (e.g., [27]). While technology acceptance models [30] focus on IT beliefs that determine people’s use of a technology, our objective was to go beyond technology usage and study how outcomes such work benefits could be obtained through the use of a repository. Further, social exchange theory, as applied to KM research, highlights the costs and benefits behind knowledge contribution or seeking. We hoped to understand other outcomes such as knowledge reuse through repositories and resultant work performance benefits. A study by Boh applied the knowledge transfer view to investigate the problem. However, the focus of that study was on the effect of help from the provider in facilitating knowledge reuse.

Our objective was to understand how motivation and technical (repository capability) factors interact in influencing knowledge reuse and performance benefits through a repository. For this purpose, a socio-technical perspective was employed because it emphasizes the interdependencies of technical and human factors in determining the outputs of a work system. The outputs of the work system are thought to be the result of interactions between these two systems. Thus, this perspective has been used as a lens to investigate how IS characteristics together with human motivations determine system use (e.g., [12,15]). In KM too, the sociotechnical perspective has gained popularity (e.g., [7,17]) because KM entails the intertwining of human practices and technology. In accordance with the socio-technical perspective of KM, our model therefore depended primarily on two streams of KM literature, i.e., KMS capability for knowledge reuse and KMS user motivation.

## 2.1. KMS capability for knowledge reuse

The processes leading to knowledge reuse consist of capturing knowledge, packaging knowledge for reuse, and distributing knowledge [21]. Capturing knowledge in a repository involves coding employees’ knowledge, which can be done as a part of the work process. Knowledge about a company’s products, services, etc. is now commonly captured online. Packaging knowledge is the process of culling, cleaning and polishing, structuring, formatting, or indexing documents against a classification scheme. Knowledge packaging in a repository includes filtering or pruning as well as organizing the contents. Distribution can be passive, where users search for relevant knowledge, or active due to receiving assumed relevant knowledge from the KMS. Reusing knowledge thus involves users evaluating search results or updates sent to them to see if they meet their needs as well as actually applying the knowledge.

Knowledge in a repository is subjected to the three processes: capturing, packaging, and distributing. Thus, perceived knowledge repository capability is composed of the perception, of knowledge reusers, about these three processes of the repository.

Researchers (e.g., [32]) have highlighted the perceived capabilities of specific IT as the source of individual and organizational impacts. However, technology capabilities (e.g., perceived knowledge repository capability) need to be complemented by human factors (e.g., user motivations) for KM success. While prior studies have examined the effects of KMS perception and motivational factors separately, the interaction of these factors is not well understood.

## 2.2. KMS user motivation

Reusers are driven by either extrinsic or intrinsic motivations [14,19]. Extrinsic motivation involves performing a behavior because it may lead to separable outcomes (e.g., earning money). Intrinsic motivations involve performing the behavior for its own purpose (e.g., enjoying helping others).

Motivations have been studied to determine their contribution to knowledge sharing [19]. For example, extrinsic motivation in the form of economic rewards has been studied [4] and found to play a facilitating role [13,29]. Further, intrinsic motivation in the form of knowledge growth was found to affect knowledge seeking positively. Thus, we expected extrinsic and intrinsic motivation to impact knowledge reuse.

## 3.1. Knowledge reuse

Knowledge capturing capability involves the way that users perceive the functionality of their knowledge repository which helps acquire knowledge. Knowledge packaging enables filtering, organizing, and indexing content in a systematic manner. Knowledge distributing is the user’s perception of the functionality of the repository in dissemination of knowledge to reusers.

Provision of a high knowledge repository capability is likely to lead to greater knowledge reuse. Tippins and Shoi [28] found that organizational learning was facilitated by the perceived IT-based capabilities. Thus we hypothesized,

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 } } .$ Perceived knowledge repository capability is positively related to knowledge reuse.

Rewards and incentives are integral components of a KM initiative. Incentives include increased salary, additional bonuses, and career advancement prospects. Hence extrinsic reward is likely to have a positive effect on knowledge reuse through a repository. Nonetheless, we expected that extrinsic rewards needed to be complemented with the perceived capability of the knowledge repository to promote reuse.

Indeed, extrinsic rewards and IT capabilities have been found to complement each other in generating better performance. In our study, we proposed that when perceived knowledge repository capability was poor, extrinsic incentives were likely to have a weaker impact on knowledge reuse. Conversely, when the knowledge repository is perceived as providing high capability, extrinsic rewards can provide additional motivation to reuse the knowledge in the repository. Therefore, we hypothesized,

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } .$ Extrinsic reward is positively related to knowledge reuse under conditions of high perceived knowledge repository capability.

Intrinsic motivation involves engaging in an activity (in our case, knowledge reuse) for its own sake. Two types of intrinsic motivation are likely to occur: improved self-image and knowledge growth. Both are considered as motivations intrinsic to the activity itself.

Based on the premise that knowledge repositories can expand the pool of knowledge resources available to knowledge workers, reusers are likely to increase their knowledge when they seek it from repositories. Such self-learning is intrinsically motivated by the perception of knowledge growth [10]. Thus, the benefit of knowledge growth can also serve as intrinsic motivation for knowledge repository users. Therefore, we hypothesized,

$\mathbf { H } _ { 3 } .$ Intrinsic motivation is positively related to knowledge reuse.

Both extrinsic motivation and repository capability can be seen as external facilitating conditions leading to greater knowledge reuse. On the other hand, intrinsic motivation is derived from internal desires. Therefore, we did not expect to see any interaction effect between intrinsic motivation and repository capability.

## 3.2. Performance benefits

Knowledge reuse is considered as an intermediate outcome that enhances work performance. The outcomes include faster, better, and less costly service since the reuser does not have to reinvent solutions already present in the repository. The utility of a customer service system is also manifested in the form of reduced training costs. Knowledge leverage facilitated by IT has been found to produce better outcomes, such as knowledge combination, product innovation, and sales [26]. Thus, we expected that knowledge reuse would result in better work performance outcomes. Therefore, we hypothesized,

$\mathbf { H } _ { 3 } .$ Knowledge reuse is positively related to performance benefits.

## 4. Research method

Our model was tested in a field setting using survey methodology.

## 4.1. Construct operationalization

A systematic process of construct operationalization was undertaken to ensure reliability and validity of the survey instrument. First, to achieve content validity, wherever possible we developed our items based on the relevant literature in order to use well-established theoretical perspectives and psychometric properties proven to be valid in existing measurements.

Second, to ensure face validity, the initial items were given to two employees (a customer service officer and a manager) in the target organizational unit for their comments. This was to ensure that the items made sense in the context of the system under study and that the terminology used was familiar to the respondents. The practitioners knew the details of the customer service processes and the use of their knowledge repositories. We provided the items and definitions of each construct (including each of the capabilities) to the experts as well as giving them the definitions of other key words used in the questionnaire. The experts’ replies helped us to refine the definitions and survey items.

After refinement based on expert comments, the next step was to carry out the conceptual validation procedure consisted of two stages, i.e., unstructured and structured sorting. In unstructured sorting, the construct labels were not specified and were created by the judges as they found appropriate. In structured sorting, the construct definitions were made known to the judges. Based on the sorting results, minor changes were made to the survey instrument. The final list of items remaining after the conceptual validation are shown in Table 1. All questions were anchored on a 1–7 point Likert scale where 1 corresponded to ‘‘Strongly Disagree’’ and 7 corresponded to ‘‘Strongly Agree’’. The details of the derivation of the scale items are given in Appendix A. In addition, we also requested and used the demographic variables of gender, age, experience, and tenure as controls in our model analysis.

## 4.2. Data collection

We surveyed the customer service officers supporting phone banking services in a multi-national bank. Apart from serving customers, these officers were also responsible for training new staff members. The department faced similar challenges to those of most customer service departments. First, customers expect instant answers to questions. Second, there are attempts to reduce call handling times and continuous tracking and assessment of efficiency. Also, there is increased pressure to contribute to the company’s bottom line profits by cross-selling products to customers. Overall, the department was required to adapt quickly to new channels and integrate them with existing ones in serving customers.

Additionally, the banking and financial sector requires its employees to have knowledge about the complex products and services offered. The range of customer enquiries is wide and often required knowledge of the company’s entire product line. Further, the banking industry is regulated, making updated knowledge of the changing legal and regulatory landscape a necessity. Also, the time-to-market for a new product is relatively short. As a result, customer relationship management drives KM more in banking than in other knowledge intensive industries.

## 5. Data analysis and results

We used the partial least squares (PLS) structural equation modeling technique for our data analysis. It can model formative indicators and their interactions and was primarily intended for causal-predictive analysis during early stages of theory development. Also, it does not require multivariate normal distribution or large sample sizes. As our study was an early attempt to develop a model for knowledge reuse and work performance benefits and that the model contained formative indicators, PLS therefore was appropriate.

## 5.1. Measurement model evaluation

Our questionnaire consisted of both formative and reflective constructs whose validities were evaluated differently. Reflective constructs feature indicators that are caused by the construct, and thus covary. In contrast, formative constructs are caused by their indicators, which precede the construct. Accordingly, formative indicators were not correlated as they did not measure the same underlying phenomenon. The test for whether any set of indicators should be viewed as formative or reflective was to question whether a change in the underlying construct would necessarily result in similar changes in all the indicators [24]. For our study, two constructs were reflective: extrinsic reward (ERW) and intrinsic motivation (IMV). There were three formative constructs: perceived knowledge repository capability (PKRC), knowledge reuse (KRU), and performance benefit (PBN). PKRC was composed of perceived capturing, packaging, and distributing capabilities of the repository. KRU was composed of the extent of knowledge application and reuse effectiveness. PBN was composed of better information providing to customers, reduced time in serving customers, and reduced time in training new staff.

## 5.1.1. Construct validity

For reflective constructs, the strength of the measurement model was demonstrated through their convergent validity (the extent to which two or more items measured the same construct) and their discriminant validity (degree to which items that measure different constructs differed). The convergent validity of these constructs was assessed in three ways: (1) item reliability, (2) composite reliability of the construct, and (3) average variance extracted by the construct. As shown in Table 3, the minimum item loading for reflective constructs was 0.73 (IMV2) which was higher than the threshold of 0.5 considered appropriate for item reliability. The generally accepted threshold for composite reliability is 0.7 while the threshold for average variance extracted is 0.5. Thus we concluded that the convergent validity of the reflective constructs was satisfactory.

## 5.1.2. Common method bias test

We took a number of steps to reduce the possibility of common method bias due to a single source of data. These included appropriate instrument design and data collection procedures [25] such as neutral wording of the items and multiple items for each construct. We further reduced the threat of common method bias by ensuring anonymity of the respondents, assuring them that there were no right or wrong answers, and requesting that they answer each question as honestly as possible. In addition, we tested our data for common method variance using Harman’s single-factor test, which involves an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) of all measurements to determine whether the majority of the variance was accounted for by one general factor. Our test showed that the first factor accounted for 29% of the total variance. We further carried out principal component analysis using varimax rotation. A total of five distinct factors emerged. The results revealed that each of the five principal components explained similar amounts of the total variance of 70.2%, ranging from 10.8% to 21.9%. This result indicated that our data did not suffer from common-method bias.

## 5.2. Structural model evaluation

Given a satisfactory measurement model, our hypotheses could then be tested by examining the structural model. The explanatory power of the structural model was assessed based on the amount of variance in the endogenous construct (performance benefits) for which the model could account. Our structural model explained 27.5% of the variance for performance benefits and 36.5% of the variance in knowledge reuse. These exceeded 10%, which has been suggested as an indication of substantive explanatory power. After computing parameter estimates for all paths in the structural model, PLS employed a bootstrap resampling technique to compute the significant level (t-values) for all paths (see Table 6). Given that each hypothesis corresponded to a path in the structural model, support for each hypothesis could be determined based on the sign (positive or negative) and statistical significance of its corresponding path. The results of testing the PLS structural model are shown in Fig. 2.

It can be seen that all four hypotheses are supported (see Table 6). We included the demographic variables of gender, age, experience, and tenure as controls in our model. None of these variables had a significant effect on KRU or PBN.

## 6. Discussion and implications

In our study, a theoretical model relating technology and motivational factors and their interaction to knowledge reuse and work performance benefits was formulated and empirically tested. Perceived knowledge repository capability and intrinsic motivation of reusers were found to be positively related to knowledge reuse. Additionally, perceived knowledge repository capability was found to moderate the relationship between extrinsic reward and knowledge reuse. As a mediator of the other antecedents, knowledge reuse was found to positively affect performance benefits.

Fig. 2. Results of structural model. Notes: \*\*p < 0.01; \*p < 0.05.

Our study provided support for our prediction that perceived knowledge repository capability is positively related to knowledge reuse (H ). This finding is in agreement with previous studies that found IT to be a facilitator of knowledge management [2]. This result has implications for the design of knowledge repositories. It suggests that capturing, packaging, and distributing capabilities of a repository need to be refined to increase knowledge reuse.

Capturing knowledge is the first step and the improved capability can enhance knowledge repository capability. Packaging capability involves culling, structuring, formatting or indexing documents such that they are converted from input to a format better suited for knowledge reuse. For example, using customized taxonomies in indexing the captured knowledge is likely to provide better packaging of knowledge [23]. Finally, distributing capability helps to facilitate the movement of well-packaged knowledge to the reusers’ hands as and when needed. Adequate online help and better search capabilities may work to augment this capability.

Another more salient finding of our study was that perceived knowledge repository capability moderated the relationship between extrinsic reward and knowledge reuse (H ). Previous studies have shown the role of incentives in promoting knowledge sharing and reuse [3]. However, insignificant or negative impact of incentives on KM efforts has also been observed. A possible reason for such contradictory findings could be the presence of moderators, such as task characteristics which have been suggested while investigating the relationship between incentives and system usage.

However, technology moderation for this relationship has not previously been considered. A practical implication of this finding is that incentives could be implemented to support knowledge reuse, but they would be far more effective when the perceived knowledge repository capability is high; it is not valuable to provide incentives to use a low capability system.

We also found that reuser intrinsic motivation was positively related to knowledge reuse (H ). This was consistent with previous studies. Intrinsic motivation can take various forms including growth of knowledge from using the repository and enhancement of self-image as a result of reuse. Therefore, to encourage knowledge reuse, managers should explain the need for learning and self-image when using knowledge repositories. Further, the strong direct effect of intrinsic motivation, as compared to extrinsic motivation, on knowledge reuse suggested that instilling intrinsic motivation was far more effective than providing rewards to increase knowledge reuse. Therefore, organizations should consider ways to stimulate intrinsic motivation through persuasion and communication rather than sanctions or rewards.

We also conducted a post hoc analysis to test a potential moderating effect of perceived knowledge repository capability on the relationship between intrinsic motivation and knowledge reuse. Fig. 3 shows the results of this post-hoc analysis.

According to the results, perceived knowledge repository capability does not have a moderating effect on the relationship between intrinsic motivation and knowledge reuse while it does have a significant moderating effect on the extrinsic reward– knowledge reuse relationship. Thus there was no interacting relationship between intrinsic motivation and IT capabilities in the context of knowledge reuse as expected. We also conducted a post hoc analysis to explore if PKRC, ERW, PKRC  ERW, and IMV had direct effects on PBN. This analysis showed that only IMV had a direct effect on PBN, which was fully mediated by KRU.

Finally, our study indicated that knowledge reuse was positively related to employee performance benefits $\mathrm { ( H } _ { 4 } \mathrm { ) }$ attainable through the repository; reuse of knowledge via the repository appeared to improve customer service by providing better knowledge and reducing response time, as well as reducing time needed for new employee training.

However, our findings should be interpreted in light of the limitations of the study. First, while a sample size of 102 was enough for an exploratory study, a larger sample size would allow for greater statistical power. Second, the study was conducted in one organization. Third, while the explanatory power of the model (27.5% for dependent variable and 36.5% for mediating variable) was acceptable, it could potentially be enhanced through inclusion of additional antecedents, such as. task characteristic, vision sharing, organizational training [34], and organizational culture could potentially add to the explanatory power of the model at the risk of losing parsimony.

## 7. Conclusion

Our study contributes to the KM literature in five ways. First, it developed and tested a theoretical model for knowledge reuse. Second, we investigated individual-level KMS outcomes, including knowledge reuse and the benefits for employees’ tasks through utilization of a knowledge repository. Third, we employed a sociotechnical perspective to investigate the interaction between technology factors and individual motivations in KM. While our objective was to view the use of work systems as the result of interactions between people and technologies, there has been a lack of investigation of their interaction effects. Fourth, our finding about the interaction between perceived knowledge repository capability and individual’s extrinsic motivation can add to previous knowledge about the effects of extrinsic rewards in KM. Last, we distinguished between the impact of intrinsic and extrinsic motivations on knowledge reuse because they can be differentially stimulated. We proposed two distinct roles of intrinsic motivation and extrinsic reward in leading the employee to reuse knowledge and confirmed our theoretical arguments through the main and post hoc analyses.

## Appendix A. Item scales

Perceived knowledge repository capability (PKRC) was measured as a formative construct using three items involving its constituent capabilities, i.e., capturing, packaging, and distributing capabilities. Respondents were asked their perception on multiple ways of knowledge capture as well as efficiency of knowledge packaging and distributing. The definition of efficiency was also provided to the respondents.

Extrinsic reward (ERW) was measured through three items identified in the literature on incentives provided by organizations to stimulate reuse through repositories, i.e., better pay, bonus, and career advancement. We verified that these forms of reward are available in the organization under study.

Intrinsic motivation (IMV) was measured through three items representing knowledge growth and self-image. These items were derived from previous literature [6]. Subsequently one of the items was dropped due to poor reliability.

Knowledge reuse (KRU) was assessed through two distinct items, which represent how often and well the individual can perform reuse through the repository. Both items were derived from previous literature [16] and the construct is considered as formative, with an item each for the two dimensions. One item is a direct assessment of effectiveness of reuse while the other explains the extent of knowledge application and reuse.

The concept and measures for performance benefits (PBN) were derived from the previous measures of customer service success. In the context of our study, performance benefits was measured as a formative construct through three items involving better information provision to customers, reduced time in serving customers, and reduced time in training new staff. These three items were derived from previous literature [18]. The measures were refined through the internal interviews with representatives of the target organizational unit.

For testing our model, we used partial least square (PLS) which allows the formative loadings of multiple items for a construct. Hence, we used the original values of the indicator items, i.e., on 1–7 Likert scale, for each respondent.
