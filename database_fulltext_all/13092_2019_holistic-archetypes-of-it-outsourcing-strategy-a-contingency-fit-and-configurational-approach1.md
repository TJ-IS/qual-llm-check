# HOLISTIC ARCHETYPES OF IT OUTSOURCING STRATEGY: A CONTINGENCY FIT AND CONFIGURATIONAL APPROACH<sup>1</sup>

Jae-Nam Lee Korea University Business School, 145 Anam-Ro, Seongbuk-Gu, Seoul 02841 KOREA {isjnlee@korea.ac.kr}

YoungKi Park Information Systems and Technology Management, School of Business, The George Washington University, 2201 G. Street NW, Washington, DC 20052 U.S.A. {ykpark@gwu.edu}

Detmar W. Straub MIS Department, Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA 19122 U.S.A. {straubdetmar@gmail.com}

Yunmo Koo Barun ICT Research Center, Yonsei University, 50 Yonsei-ro, Seodaemun-gu, Seoul 03722 KOREA {ymkooh@barunict.kr}

The goal of this paper is to determine holistic archetypes of information technology (IT) outsourcing strategy. It does this through an overarching theoretical framework that integrates three dominant theories of interorganizational relations in the IT outsourcing literature, namely transaction cost economics, resource dependency, and social exchange theories. A contingency fit theoretical framing is married to a configurational approach to explicate the intricate relationships that spring up between a focal firm and its outsourcing vendors when they are working toward specific strategic objectives under varying contingencies. In line with this theoretical objective, we used qualitative comparative analysis (QCA), a set-theoretic configuration method that can handle the interdependent complexity among multiple elements of IT outsourcing. The technique was applied to a sample of 235 companies that have outsourced some or all of their IT functions. Findings at the project level of analysis empirically reveal two sets of configurations of strategic IT outsourcing elements, one set of configurations resulting in high economic benefit and the other set leading to high strategic benefit. Next, we compare similarities and differences among multiple, equifinal configurations and infer archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy internally congruent in terms of the strategic objectives as well as matching specific contextual contingencies. Our holistic archetypes take the form of theoretical propositions integrating the previous fragmented and inconsistent knowledge in IT outsourcing resulting from the causal ambiguity and complexity inherent in IT outsourcing projects as well as from divergent theories in the literature. Furthermore, by defining specific contingency boundaries, our archetypes provide managers with context-specific guidelines for strategic decisions regarding their relationships with outsourcing vendors, helping different sized firms to effectively succeed in IT outsourcing, contingent on the IT type being outsourced. Finally, we discuss new insights and implications of this study for complementing and extending the extant theories in IT outsourcing.

Keywords: IT outsourcing, archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy, transaction cost economics theory, resource dependency theory, social exchange theory, equifinality, contingency fit theory, configurational approach, qualitative comparative analysis (QCA)

## Introduction

Information technology (IT) outsourcing has emerged as a major strategic alternative to in-house IT operation and management (Han and Mithas 2013; Lee et al. 2004; Loh and Venkatraman 1992; Weigelt 2009). According to a 2015 market research report, the worldwide IT outsourcing market size had reached US \$314.9 billion and was predicted to grow at a 6.2% compound annual growth rate from 2015 through 2022 (Stratistics MRC 2016). Furthermore, cloud computing, the most recent form of large-scale IT outsourcing, has proliferated over the last decade, garnering significant attention from top management (King and Torkzadeh 2008; Wang et al. 2011). This trend in increasing adoption of IT outsourcing and its related business opportunities and risks make evident the importance of developing an appropriate IT outsourcing strategy, defined as the organizing logic underlying a firm’s configurations of IT outsourcing elements for achieving success in IT outsourcing.

Prior literature suggests that IT outsourcing strategies provide a firm with guidelines, under different conditions, on how to configure its strategic elements with regard to specific goals in IT outsourcing projects (Lee et al. 2004). A successful IT outsourcing strategy enables a firm to find ways to access external resources, specialized knowledge, industry best practices, and learning opportunities, thereby achieving economic and strategic benefits from IT outsourcing (Grover et al. 1996; Han and Mithas 2013; Mitchell and Singh 1996; Mowery et al. 1996; Poppo and Zenger 1998).

Unfortunately, however, firms often experience difficulties in developing a successful IT outsourcing strategy and thus are likely to fail in IT outsourcing projects, ending up with higher costs and unfulfilled goals. Such difficulties and failures are mainly due to the complexity and causal ambiguity of coordinating IT outsourcing projects across diverse outsourcing activities, particularly in the relationships between a focal firm and its outsourcing vendors (Barthélemy and Quélin 2006; Martinsons 1993; Poppo and Zenger 2002; Ravindran et al. 2015). For one, to succeed in IT outsourcing projects, a firm should effectively handle the problems caused by information asymmetry and conflict of interest with its outsourcing vendors (Handley and Benton 2009; Loh and

Venkatraman 1992) and balance the priorities of the multiple IT functions to be outsourced, congruent with specific outsourcing objectives across varying settings.

To develop archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy that can effectively tackle such complexity and related challenges of interorganizational relationships (IOR), three theories with an inherent focus on IOR were selected as a grounding for the work: (1) transaction cost economics theory (TCET), (2) resource dependency theory (RDT), and (3) social exchange theory (SET). Our systematic perusal of extant studies related to theories relevant to IOR (e.g., Evan 1965; Jarillo 1988; Uzzi 1997) reveals a few, equally viable key mechanisms that enable firms to effectively manage IOR complexity and achieve outsourcing project success. These are cost efficient transactions, control of critical resource dependency, and reciprocal relationship building. Such mechanisms are well reflected in three respective theories (i.e., TCET, RDT, and SET), all of which have been widely adopted and are commonplace theoretical framings in the IT outsourcing literature.

Studies based on these theories have greatly contributed to our understanding of the relationship between a client and its vendors. From these theories, we extracted four key IOR elements related to outsourcing strategy: degree of IT outsourcing (Ang and Straub 1998; Dibbern et al. 2012; Lacity and Willcocks 1996), relationship type (Fitoussi and Gurbaxani 2012; Gopal and Koka 2012; Handley and Angst 2015), period of the IT outsourcing contract (Domberger et al. 2000; Goo et al. 2007; Ravindran et al. 2015), and the number of outsourcing vendors (Ngwenyama and Bryson 1999; Poston et al. 2009; Su and Levina 2011). Embedded in the three designated, prominent theories noted above, these core mechanisms have led scholars to have different foci on these four IOR elements in investigating ways to fulfill the unique objectives of IT outsourcing projects. Not unexpectedly, extant studies with such different approaches have developed useful but limited understanding of only part of the whole picture.

Simply stated, studies based on SET generally argue that firms opt to establish long-term reciprocal partnerships to share risks and strategic benefits with their vendors (Martinsons 1993; McFarlan and Nolan 1995; Ravindran et al. 2015; Ren et al. 2010). In contrast, TCET-based studies suggest a short-term fee-for-service contract as an effective pathway to achieving cost efficiency (Fitoussi and Gurbaxani 2012; Lacity et al. 1996; Willcocks et al. 2011) whereas RDT studies argue that a firm can increase its strategic advantage by minimizing its resource dependency on others with a selective outsourcing, buy-in contract or by controlling key resources via cooperative alliances with its vendors (Grimpe and Kaiser 2010; Ulrich and Barney 1984).

Furthermore, even studies adopting the same theory report inconsistent findings. In TCET studies, Lacity and Willcocks (1998) and Loh and Venkatraman (1992) find that selective outsourcing serves firms better than total outsourcing while Bengtsson and Dabhilkar (2009) conclude that total outsourcing outperforms selective outsourcing. Likewise, some studies contend that a single vendor contract is superior due to the lesser complexity in coordinating outsourcing activities (e.g., Ngwenyama and Bryson 1999) with others arguing that the multi-vendor contract works better because it enables firms to lower switching costs (e.g., Susarla 2012).

In addition, extant IT outsourcing studies adopting these theories have predominantly stressed the net effect of individual IOR elements and paid little attention to their combined, complementary effects on IT outsourcing success. Thus, we contend that overall knowledge about IT outsourcing strategy to organize the key IOR elements remains fragmented, incomplete, and contradictory. All of this leaves managers uncertain about managing IT outsourcing and researchers unclear about which theories are most relevant in studying IT outsourcing projects.

To fill in these knowledge gaps, this study derives archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy by simultaneously considering the four above-designated IOR elements and other contingency factors. We posit that there are multiple, equally valid logics for a firm to effectively configure these IOR elements. Built on this premise of equifinality, we adopt a contingency fit framework with a configurational approach to generate archetypes that are internally congruent in terms of the strategic objectives of IT outsourcing projects and matching specific contingencies such as outsourced IT types and firm size (Drazin and Van de Ven 1985). To achieve this research goal, we use qualitative comparative analysis (QCA), a settheoretic configurational method to handle the statistical intransigence of interdependency among the multiple IOR elements (Fiss 2007, 2011; Ragin 2008). QCA is applied to a sample of 235 Korean companies that have outsourced their IT functions, in part or wholly. The technique empirically uncovers two primary sets of configurations of the IOR elements, one set exhibiting high economic benefit and the other set exhibiting high strategic benefit (Grover et al. 1996; Lane et al. 2005). We then compare similarities and differences between the equifinal configurations and suggest a set of holistic archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy.

Adopting this approach, the present study makes significant contributions to the strategic IT outsourcing literature. First, this study develops an overarching theoretical framework to integrate the three dominant theories widely adopted in IT outsourcing studies. This framework provides a foundation that enables researchers to build a holistic theory that can integrate fragmented and incomplete knowledge in the current IT outsourcing literature. Second, it makes contributions to extant theories by complementing and extending the extant core mechanisms of the dominant theories. Our proposed archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy explicate multiple equifinal pathways to the outcomes, ones that are internally congruent in terms of outsourcing project objectives as well as matching IT outsourcing contingencies. Each archetype represents social, economic, or hybrid mechanisms to simultaneously configure the four IOR elements, showing different organizing logics depending on firm size and outsourced IT type. These archetypes provide new insights that complement and advance our understanding built on the foundational theories. In our view, the hybrid archetype suggests a new mechanism that cannot be explained by the logic of a single theory alone but only by combining the logics of multiple theories.

Overall, our archetypes, empirically determined within the overarching theoretical framework, enable us to integrate fragmented and inconsistent knowledge residing in the IT outsourcing academic research stream. By defining specific strategic objectives and contingency factors for IT outsourcing projects, the practical guidelines implied in the emergent archetypes give managers context-specific strategies for forming effective relationships with outsourcing vendors, thus helping different-sized organizations flourish across different IT types. Methodologically, the study describes how to apply a set-theoretic QCA method to IT outsourcing research, thus providing specific procedures for how to use QCA to create a configurational theory of IT outsourcing and innovative theories in other IT topical arenas.

## Theoretical Background

## IT Outsourcing Strategy

Outsourcing is a process of coordinated or negotiated exchanges between parties, specifically an exchange of tangible and intangible resources between organizations to attain specific goals. In this study, which adopts the client firm perspective, we define IT outsourcing as the process of commissioning all or part of an organization’s IT assets, IT people, and/or IT operations to external vendor(s).

Studies on IT outsourcing have emphasized the importance of developing effective strategies for outstanding outcomes in IT outsourcing projects (Goo et al. 2009; Han and Mithas 2013; Lacity and Willcocks 1998; Lee et al. 2004). The strategic management literature recognizes strategy as a set of guidelines to deliver on corporate objectives (e.g., Barney 1991; Porter 1991). It is generally defined as “determination of the basic long-term goals and objectives of an enterprise, and adoption of course of actions and the allocation of resources necessary for carrying out these goals” (Chandler 1962, p. 13). Referring to specific types of strategy, Lee et al. (2004) explain that an IT outsourcing strategy is a logic used in making decisions on a firm’s IT outsourcing portfolio. Thus, IT outsourcing strategy is a primary way for organizations to leverage the value of IT outsourcing to execute on corporate strategic objectives.

Based on these relevant literature streams, we define IT outsourcing strategy as the organizing logic underlying a firm’s configurations of IT outsourcing elements for achieving success in IT outsourcing. An efficacious IT outsourcing strategy enables firms to garner not only the economic benefits of lowered costs relevant to IT operation and management but also strategic benefits via access to emerging technologies and skills that capture long-run market opportunities as well as a renewed focus on core competencies (Grover et al. 1996; Han and Mithas 2013; Mitchell and Singh 1996; Mowery et al. 1996; Poppo and Zenger 1998). At the same time, the literature has also recognized the difficulty of developing a winning IT outsourcing strategy due to the inherent complexity and causal ambiguity of coordinating diverse outsourcing activities between a focal firm and its outsourcing vendors (Barthélemy and Quélin 2006; Martinsons 1993; Poppo and Zenger 2002; Ravindran et al. 2015).

In that IT outsourcing is firmly seated as an interorganizational relationship, it has the typical principal-agent problems of vendor opportunism caused by a client firm’s bounded rationality to cope with outsourcing complexity and coupled with conflicts of interest and information asymmetry between parties. Moreover, the literature clearly states that a firm needs to balance the priorities of outsourcing multiple IT functions, such balancing often required across several vendors. A firm must also keep these congruent with specific strategic objectives that vary according to certain contingencies. These factors increase IOR uncertainty and complexity and exchange costs in the marketplace. Therefore, a firm should search for smart ways to coordinate and control the critical aspects of its interactions with outsourcing vendors to acquire both economic and strategic benefits (Grover et al. 1996, Lane et al. 2005).

## Dominant Theories and Key Elements of IT Outsourcing Strategy

With the focus on the relationship between a focal firm and its outsourcing vendors, we proffer an integrated theoretical framework based on IOR theories of exchange, collaboration, and resource dependencies (e.g., Evan 1965; Jarillo 1988; Uzzi 1997). In this focused domain, we specifically hone in on three dominant theories (namely, TCET, RDT, and SET) because they focus on IOR and set forth strategic core mechanisms, respectively: (1) cost efficient transactions, (2) control of critical resource dependency, and (3) reciprocal relationship building.<sup>2</sup>

As summarized in Appendix A, these theories have been widely adopted and are dominant in the IT outsourcing literature. Based on the strategic core mechanisms articulated in these theories, extant IT outsourcing studies have developed a rich understanding of the relationship between a focal firm and its vendors, all being mobilized in quest of excellence in outsourcing projects. They also identify four key IOR elements: degree of IT outsourcing, relationship type, period of IT outsourcing contract, and number of outsourcing vendors.<sup>3</sup>

Degree of IT outsourcing is defined as the extent to which a firm outsources its functions and related business (Ang and Straub 1998; Lacity and Willcocks 1996; Weigelt 2009). In past work, scholars have measured the degree of IT outsourcing as the percentage of the total IT budget spent for outsourcing, often categorized into three groups: minimal outsourcing, selective outsourcing, and total outsourcing (Kaiser and Buxmann 2012; Kotabe et al. 2008; Lacity et al. 1996). Relationship type is defined as a kind of outsourcing contract between a focal firm and its outsourcing vendors (Fitoussi and Gurbaxani 2012; Gopal and Koka 2012; Lee and Kim 1999; Ren et al. 2010). In general, the outsourcing relationship can be placed into three major types: (1) fee-forservice contract, (2) partnership, and (3) buy-in contract. A fee-for-service contract represents a relationship in which a firm pays a fee to a service vendor in exchange for the management and delivery of specified IT products or services. The partnership contract is a collaborative relationship that involves resources from both a client firm and its vendors to increase mutual benefits for both parties. The potential value of partnership relies on complementary resources and voluntary allocation of resources, which eventually strengthens the partnership (Dyer and Singh, 1998). Thus, authority over resource management tends to be internalized within the partnership relationship over time. Last, the buy-in contract means that a client firm purchases vendor resources (e.g., an ERP or cloud services) and internally manages these purchased resources itself to minimize its resource dependency on external vendors (Lacity and Willcocks, 1998).

Period of outsourcing is defined as the duration of an outsourcing contract (Domberger et al. 2000; Goo et al. 2007; Ravindran et al. 2015). Some studies codify period as shortterm, medium-term, and long-term instead of a temporal, continuous metric like years. Finally, the number of outsourcing vendors is defined as the actual number of external vendors involved in an IT outsourcing project (Bapna et al. 2010; Koo et al. 2017; Poston et al. 2009).

Each of these elements has been shown to be sine qua non for successful IT outsourcing projects, but there is lingering causal complexity and ambiguity since they are neither independent nor mutually exclusive in yielding IT outsourcing success (Heide et al. 2014; Lee et al. 2004; Misangyi and Acharya 2014). In reality, organizations should consider all these elements simultaneously in their strategic decisionmaking taking place at the early stage of a project.

Nevertheless, prior studies based on these three theories have mostly centered on the net effect of individual elements. Few studies have focused on the combined, complementary effects of the four elements with regard to outcomes. In fact, core mechanisms of the three theories have induced scholars to investigate unique objectives of IT outsourcing projects via different foci on these IOR elements.

First, studies built on TCET concentrate primarily on a strategy that yields economic benefits through reductions in transaction and production costs (Ang and Straub 1998; Fitoussi and Gurbaxani 2012; Lacity and Willcocks 1996). This stream of research argues that outsourcing enables a firm to achieve economies of scale, resulting in lower production costs (e.g., cheaper delivery of IT products or services), but at the risk of substantially higher transaction and coordination costs (e.g., costs like searching for vendors, negotiating, monitoring, and enforcing contracts) (Thouin et al. 2009; Willcocks et al. 2011; Williamson 2008). Specifically, TCET-based studies insist that outsourcing vendors cannot truly be strategic partners due to conflicts of goal interests and opportunism. Thus, a firm should choose either a fee-forservice or a buy-in contract rather than a partnership relationship (Fitoussi and Gurbaxani 2012; Lacity and Hirschheim 1993; Lacity et al. 1996). TCET studies also argue that a firm should avoid a long-term contract to precisely analyze the cost implications of their outsourcing decisions, recover fast from mistakes, and easily switch vendors (Lacity and Willcocks 1998; Willcocks et al. 2011). Regarding the degree of IT outsourcing and the number of vendors, TCET studies yield incompatible findings. For example, some studies find that selective outsourcing is a better option than total outsourcing (e.g., Lacity and Willcocks 1998; Loh and Venkatraman 1992) while others argue that total outsourcing is superior (e.g., Bengtsson and Dabhilkar 2009) or the degree of outsourcing has no significant impact on outsourcing performance (e.g., Bhalla et al. 2008). Moreover, some studies contend that firms should prefer a single vendor contract to reduce the coordination complexity of outsourcing activities (e.g., Ngwenyama and Bryson 1999), whereas others insist that a multi-vendor contract is preferable because of its low switching costs (e.g., Susarla 2012). In addition, no study considers all four elements simultaneously and thus analyzes their combinatorial effects in achieving either economic benefits or strategic benefits or both.

Second, RDT-based studies assume that IT outsourcing is a means by which firms acquire critical resources from external vendors while minimizing their dependency on vendors for sustainable competitive advantage (Barney 1991; Slaughter and Ang 1996; Weigelt 2009). This stream of research typically recognizes a market environment as a set of organizations engaged in resource exchange relationships and interprets the IOR as a resource dependency (Pfeffer and Salancik 1978). Firms attempt to acquire valuable resources across firm boundaries and thus depend on other organizations to cover the gap between resources needed and those owned. A firm also may be vulnerable when vital resources are controlled by other organizations. Hence, it strives to be as independent as possible by controlling key resources itself (Daft 1992). Some RDT studies argue that a good IT outsourcing strategy consists of a short-term, buy-in contract for selective IT functions so that a firm can increase its strategic advantage by minimizing its resource dependency on others (Grimpe and Kaiser 2010; Ulrich and Barney 1984), especially for rare, difficult-to-imitate resources (Barney 1991; Weigelt 2009).<sup>4</sup> On the other hand, other RDT studies argue that firms can maintain control and power over critical resources in environments by establishing a stable IOR based on cooperative partnership such as strategic alliances and joint ventures (Alter and Hage 1993; Pfeffer and Salancik 1978), both of which would likely require a long-term contract for either selective or total outsourcing. Regarding number of vendors, no established guidelines in the literature appear in RDT studies.

Third, studies based on SET primarily address the issue of managing interorganizational ties in outsourcing (Lacity et al. 2010; Martinsons 1993; McFarlan and Nolan 1995; Ravindran et al. 2015; Ren et al. 2010). SET studies dwell on interfirm relationships as a dynamic process involving specific interactions in which a client and its vendors cooperate in carrying out key, outsource-related activities (Blau 1964; Emerson 1962; Ravindran et al. 2015). Thus, these studies consider exchange relationships to be most crucial for success, all the while narrowing in on gathering resources for the development and maintenance of the exchange relationship (Dwyer et al. 1987). In particular, intangible factors, such as trust, commitment, sharing of risk and reward, and shared values, are emphasized as the most important determinants of success (Ang and Inkpen 2008; Cao and Lumineau 2015; Goo and Huang 2008; Lee and Kim 1999). Thus, this stream of research is more likely to stress strategic benefits rather than cost efficiency due to the centrality of such intangible factors. SET studies have, for example, argued that firms tend to establish a partnership with their vendors to manage complex outsourcing relationships by sharing risks and benefits (Grover et al. 1996; Lahiri and Kedia 2009; Lee et al. 2004; McFarlan and Nolan 1995; Ren et al. 2010). This stance suggests that a long-term contract based on a partnership relationship increases financial predictability and thus reduces the risk and uncertainty associated with mission-critical business processes (Martinsons 1993; Ravindran et al. 2015). This choice of long-term contract, in turn, allows firms to reduce superfluous complications and bureaucracy and reassign internal staff from routine operational tasks to more value-added functions (Goo et al. 2007). With respect to degree of IT outsourcing and number of vendors, SET studies have not explained how to configure these along with the other two IOR elements.

Finally, the three dominant theories for IT outsourcing strategy research are obviously not mutually exclusive. In fact, they overlap one another and often yield findings that are contradictory with regard to configuring IOR elements to effectuate strategic objectives. These inconsistencies occur not only across multiple theories but also between studies adopting the same theory. Furthermore, prior works have, as a rule, fixated on the net effect of individual IOR elements and paid little attention to their combined, holistic effects. Thus, the overall knowledge about appropriate IT outsourcing strategies can be enhanced by organizing the four IOR elements simultaneously so that they are no longer fragmented, contradictory, and incomplete.

## Theoretical Framework

In the current study, we attempt to bridge the above-identified knowledge gaps by developing archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy based on the contingency fit theory as an overarching conceptual framework, which enables us to integrate the three foundational theories. Figure 1 depicts this theoretical framework.

## Developing Archetypes of IT Outsourcing Strategy

The strategic management literature defines archetypes as a series of different, although very frequently occurring modes, which represent multiple, congruent gestalts of holistic and ordered patterns of relationships among organizational, environmental, and strategic elements (e.g., Miller and Friesen 1977, 1978, 1980). Thus, well enunciated archetypes allow organizations to create a strategy to manage and control the scope and magnitude of key elements in a way that matches best the contingencies of the project setting. Hence, in the current study, we define an archetype of IT outsourcing strategy as a gestalt of holistic and ordered patterns of relationships amongst interorganization relationship elements that are internally congruent with the strategic objectives of IT outsourcing projects and matching internal and external contingency factors.

In the literature, archetypes are empirically manifested via configurations composed of conceptually distinct but mutually supportive elements that commonly occur together (Meyer et al. 1993). The configurational approach enables researchers to explore interdependencies among multiple elements; it also shows how all elements can be simultaneously combined into bundles that produce the outcome of interest (Fiss 2011; Lee et al. 2004; Meyer et al. 1993; Miller 1987, 1996).

We posit that superior performance in IT outsourcing projects can best be explained by systemic configurations of all elements rather than by any of these elements in isolation. This leads us to the basic premise of this study: that there are multiple logics for a firm to configure strategic IOR elements in ways that fit specific strategic objectives and contexts, all of which imply equifinality. In other words, no single IT outsourcing strategy can be suitable for all types of IT outsourcing projects. Even within a firm, the combination of strategic IOR elements will change across IT outsourcing projects as IT outsourcing success will depend on specific objectives under specific project contexts.<sup>5</sup>

## Overarching Theoretical Framework of Contingency Fit

We next advance an overarching contingency fit theoretical framework (Figure 1) integrating the three dominant theoretical models, thereby suggesting successful archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy. Built on the articulated equifinality premise, this framing sets forth a configurational approach to investigate the intricate relationships between a focal firm and its outsourcing vendors under different contingencies. This systems contingency fit approach accentuates our ability to find consistent patterns among elements of organizational context, structure, and performance. It accomplishes this by incorporating the equifinality concept to model “fit” as feasible sets of multiple alternative patterns. Each pattern is internally congruent with specific economic and strategic outsourcing objectives and matches internal and external contingency factors (Drazin and Van de Ven 1985; Gresov and Drazin 1997).

Thus, the theoretical framework articulated in this study consists of two parts: (1) development of the profiles of the four IOR elements in IT outsourcing projects that are internally congruent with strategic outsourcing objectives while, at the same time, matching the internal contingency of outsourced IT type, and (2) identification of congruent profiles that, in addition, match an external contingency of firm size.

First, based on the extant studies adumbrated in Appendix A, we put forward two specific objectives that a firm aims to achieve via an IT outsourcing project: economic benefits from lower costs through economies of scale for IT operations and management and strategic benefits through access to emerging technologies and key IT skills from outsourcing vendors. The latter goal allows a firm to attend to its core competencies as well as capture long-run market opportunities (Grover et al. 1996; Lane et al. 2005). In the process of matching internal and external contingencies to levels of success, new congruent profiles can be derived to reflect two or all three dominant theories, resulting in a hybrid theoretical configuration. In addition, a firm may attain both economic and strategic benefits through IT outsourcing.

With respect to contextual factors, we chose firm size as an external contingency factor and outsourced IT type as an internal contingency factor.<sup>6</sup> Both have been shown to closely relate to the four IOR elements and their impact on IT outsourcing projects. Firms need to keep a healthy balance in prioritizing multiple IT functions for reaching specific strategic objectives (Bapna et al. 2010; Weill and Aral 2006). For example, an outsourced IT type is typically associated with both relationship type and the number of outsourcing vendors (Koo et al. 2017). The typical argument is that IT functions for core business processes ordinarily need to be outsourced to a single vendor through a partnership relationship due to strategic importance and security concerns. Likewise, depending on firm size, a contingency factor external to projects, available resources, and size of budget for IT outsourcing vary significantly. Thus, firm size (implying slack resources) is known to have a major influence on the degree of IT outsourcing and number of vendors through its direct effects on the IT unit (e.g., Ang and Straub 1998).

Archetypes can be determined via a top-down conceptually derived typology (e.g., Miles et al. 1978; Mintzberg 1979) or a bottom-up empirically based taxonomy (e.g., Miller and Friesen 1977, 1978). To take the advantage of both approaches, particularly for reconciling the inconsistencies in extant IT outsourcing studies, we adopt an abductive theory building approach (e.g., Bensaou and Venkatraman 1995; Van Maanen et al. 2007). This abductive theory building approach is particularly useful for social science research topics in which knowledge is fragmented and inconsistent, such as the present research domain.

Following the abductive theory building approach, we defined and articulated the theoretical framework (Figure 1) with its key elements and integrated the multiple theoretical models for deriving archetypes. Now, in the following discourse involving taxonomy, we empirically investigate how these elements combine naturally into multiple, equifinal configurations. As such, derived archetypes will permit firms to tailor their IT outsourcing strategies to mitigate IOR complexity and its related challenges and to effectively respond to contingencies in the quest of strategic project goals. Thus, by utilizing derived archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy, we will eventually posit a middle-range theory in the form of configurational propositions (e.g., Bensaou and Venkatraman 1995; Misangyi and Acharya 2014) where contingencies rule and where theoretical law-like statements are highly contingent, that is, theoretical propositions that specify the set of conditions under which a given outcome can occur (Eisenhardt and Bourgeois 1988).

## Research Methods

The unit of analysis in this paper is an IT outsourcing project and the main constructs are shown in our theoretical framework (Figure 1). The research method was a field study of 235 Korean firms that have been engaged in substantive IT outsourcing projects. To gather data, we designed a questionnaire as instrumentation for our field study and, accordingly, developed question items based on constructs that have been validated and administered in numerous other outsourcing studies.<sup>7</sup>

## Sample and Data Collection

Data were collected from organizations listed on the Korea Exchange and the initial sampling frame was the set of 1,000 companies covered by the annual corporation reports of Maeil

Business Newspaper, a Korean nationwide business newspaper. Previous studies in IT outsourcing have used data collected from Korean companies (e.g., Goo et al. 2009; Lee and Kim 1999; Lee et al. 2004) because, across diverse industries, many Korean organizations have adopted IT outsourcing and the trend of IT outsourcing remains upward. Korea is also well known for its IT-intensive economy.

The CIO or the top IT manager in each firm was identified from the book of listed firms published by the Korea Stock Exchange. Next, 98 IT service providers were removed from the sample because this study focuses on the IT outsourcing strategy of outsourcing service receivers, not service providers. Before the questionnaire was mailed, the top IT managers of 902 firms were called to explain the research objectives and invited to participate in the survey. Out of the 902 firms, 750 firms responded positively to our invitation for data collection. Accordingly, we mailed the questionnaire to the top IT managers of 750 firms with personalized cover letters that explained the study and guaranteed the confidentiality of the collected data. In this survey, the respondents were asked to select the most important outsourcing project they had within the last five years and answer questions regarding that specific project and its outsourcing service providers.

To increase the response rate, the total design method proposed by Dillman (1991) and Sivo et al. (2006) was deployed. A follow-up post-card was mailed one week after the original mailing and the same questionnaire was mailed again three and six weeks after the original mailing. After four rounds of solicitation, we received a total of 298 responses (39.7% response rate).

Nonresponse bias was tested before conducting the final analysis. A bias test compared the total sales revenue and the number of employees across the sampling frame (Babbie 1973). For this test, 50 companies were randomly selected from the nonrespondents and then their total sales and the number of employees were compared with those of all respondents, respondents from the first mailing, and respondents from the second mailing. The results of the t-test of these timed waves showed no difference among the four comparisons at an alpha protection level of 0.05. Given the assumption that late respondents are reasonable surrogates for nonrespondents, there appears to be no overt bias in this respect (Armstrong and Overton 1977). We also conclude that respondents were at the high level of management, so they could be expected to knowledgably address the questions in the research instrument.

To increase generalizability, we carefully screened the types of relationships between a focal firm and outsourcing vendors unique to Korea, specialized relationships that could compromise the integrity of the data. In Korea, conglomerate groups such as Samsung, LG, Hyundai, and other chaebols often conclude IT outsourcing contracts with their affiliated firms within the same conglomerate group. The potential issue with such strong affiliation relationships is that IT outsourcing contracts are often secured mainly due to the same group affiliation, thus making the relationship different from a more independent relationship between an IT service provider and client firm. This case may be true not only in Korea but also in other countries in that they typically focus on generic systems management services rather than creating unique and innovative IT solutions tailored to each customer. Thus, we further culled out the 42 chaebol responses. It turned out that 10 responses did not have an IT outsourcing project and were thus discarded, whereas 11 responses were removed due to incomplete data, thus resulting in a total of 235 responses. Respondent characteristics in terms of industry type, number of total employees, and total sales revenue are summarized in Table 1. Descriptive statistics are shown in Table 2.

## Data Analysis with Fuzzy-Set QCA

In keeping with the theoretical objective and the configurational approach of the study, we used fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA), a set-theoretic configurational method which uses set-theory, Boolean algebra, and counterfactual analysis to effectively handle the interdependency of outsourcing elements and contingency factors (Bell et al. 2014; Crilly 2011; Fiss 2007, 2011; Ragin 2008). QCA is increasingly used in strategic management, organizational, and sociological research to develop a configurational theory (e.g., Bell et al. 2014; Crilly 2011; Fiss 2011; Garcia-Castro and Francoeur 2014; Greckhamer et al. 2013). It has also been introduced and used in IS research (e.g., Dawson et al. 2016; El Sawy et al. 2010; Park et al. 2017; Rivard and Lapointe 2012). QCA does not assume independent conditions<sup>8</sup> but rather a congruent combination of causal conditions to generate the outcome of interest (Ragin 2008), which is often the case in fused digital phenomena (Bharadwaj et al. 2013; El Sawy 2003) and thus particularly appropriate for our study. Unlike general linear regression models that handle at best a three-way interaction effect (see Drazin and Van de Ven 1985; Fiss 2007; Ganzach 1998), QCA can explain a multi-way relationship of all elements in a study and enables scholars to find multiple, equifinal configurations (e.g., Fiss 2007; Gresov and Drazin 1997), in our case archetypes of all IOR elements that fit both strategic objectives of IT outsourcing projects and internal and external contingencies.

## Calibration of Set Memberships

Using fsQCA, we first calibrated all elements and outcomes of a case into set-membership, a process that transforms the variable values into set-membership scores. For example, a case (i.e., a single IT outsourcing project) might have a high or low membership in the mathematical set of high economic benefits in outsourcing. We used Ragin’s (2008) direct method of calibration, implemented in fsQCA software, an approach based on three qualitative anchors: full membership, full nonmembership, and the crossover point of maximum ambiguity regarding membership of a case in the set of interest. Given that this approach uses the metric of log-odds and the distance of variable values from the three anchors, the resulting fuzzy membership score is between 0 and 1, where 0 indicates a full nonmembership and 1 indicates full membership. Researchers determine these three anchors based on their empirical and theoretical knowledge of the context and cases (Ragin 2008).

First, to calibrate the outcome variables, we defined the three anchors by following recommendations of prior methodological studies (e.g., Fiss 2011; Park et al. 2017; Ragin 2008). In our seven-point Likert scale, the value 4 in each item qualitatively means neither “in” nor “out” of the set and thus we made 4 the cross-over point. Next, we defined 6 as the anchor for full membership in the set of high performance (i.e., high economic benefits and high strategic benefits) in an outsourcing project and 2 for full nonmembership. For the strategic benefit outcome, we used the sum of values of its five items to define the three anchors. Thus, for high strategic benefits, the values of 30, 20, and 10 were designated as anchors for full membership, cross-over, and full nonmembership, respectively. In the same way, for economic benefits, we used the sum of values of the three items and thus put the values of 18, 12, and 6 as anchors for full membership, crossover, and full nonmembership, respectively.

Second, we also calibrated fuzzy-set memberships for degree of IT outsourcing, period of outsourcing, and firm size. Extant studies have defined an outsourcing project as total outsourcing if its degree of outsourcing is greater than 80%, selective outsourcing if its degree is between 20% and 80%, and minimal outsourcing if its degree is less than 20% (e.g., Kaiser and Buxmann 2012; Lacity et al. 1996). Thus, we made 80, 50, and 20 percent the three anchors for full membership, cross-over, and full nonmembership for total outsourcing. In the same manner, for period of outsourcing (i.e., the temporal metric of years), we made 8, 5, and 2 the three anchors for full membership, cross-over, and full nonmembership for long-term contract (Lacity and Willcocks 1998). For firm size, we selected 5,000, 1,000, and 200 employees corresponding to around 90, 50, and 10 quantiles as the three anchors for full membership, cross-over, and full nonmembership for a large firm.<sup>9</sup>

Finally, subsequent to this robustness check, we coded other elements into multi-value sets or crisp-sets (either 1 or 0). Due to its clear categories in the relationship (contract) type, we coded relationship type into three crisp-sets by adding three binary crisp-set elements (i.e., fee-for-service, partnership, and buy-in). A fee-for-service contract consists of four specific contract types—standard, detailed, loose, and mixed contract—as shown in Appendix C (Gopal and Koka 2012; Lacity and Willcocks 1998); thus we coded it as 1 if a contract type was one of the four types (1 to 4); otherwise it was coded as 0. We coded the number of outsourcing vendors into either single vendor (0) or multi-vendors (1) and coded outsourced IT type as IT application outsourcing (1) and IT infrastructure outsourcing (0).<sup>10</sup>

Table 3 shows the summary of calibrations. As such, our QCA with regard to outsourcing elements includes both crispset and fuzzy-set memberships (Ragin 2008; Rihoux and Ragin 2009), a setup that reflects the current knowledge on outsourcing elements. This calibration approach allowed us to create relatively clear qualitative boundaries between configurations and thus helped us to effectively identify distinct archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy. After calibration, the next step in fsQCA is applying the truth-table algorithm (Ragin 2008), an algorithm that identifies sufficient configurations of elements for producing the outcome of interest; it does this by examining whether firms sharing a same set of outsourcing elements consistently resulted in outsourcing success. A detailed explanation of this Truth-Table algorithm is given in Appendix F.

Each rectangle in this figure (e.g., A1, B1) describes a single configuration of conditions in the intermediate solution in Table 4. A graphical expression of configurations enables us to more effectively interpret and compare the complex structures of configurations in a way that explains how the elements simultaneously and systemically combine to result in the outcome and the role of each element in the dynamics of high performance.

There are two types of measures for validating the solutions: consistency and coverage (Ragin 2008, pp. 44-68). First, consistency is the degree to which the cases sharing a given combination of conditions agree in identifying the outcome in question (Ragin 2008, p. 44). Thus, it is similar to the p-value (vis-à-vis the alpha protection level) in correlational analysis and shows how a configuration consistently becomes a sufficient solution for the outcome. The overall solution consistency measures the degree to which all configurations together consistently result in the outcome. Our overall consistencies for economic benefits and strategic benefits are 0.87 and 0.89 correspondingly, far above the conventionally acceptable level of 0.80 (Ragin 2008).

In general, raw coverage is the extent to which each configu ration covers the cases of outcome. More exactly, it is the proportion of cases that have membership in its respective path to the outcome, a measure similar to explained variance (R<sup>2</sup>) in correlational analysis. Unique coverage of a configuration is the portion of coverage that is explained by the configuration, without its overlapping with other configurations. Thus, coverage shows an empirical relevance and effectiveness of the configuration for producing the outcome, although a higher coverage does not necessarily mean higher theoretical importance (Ragin 2008, p. 44). It is clear that organizations can attain high performance through multiple, different configurations, each of which demonstrates equifinality. Thus, individual configurations can differ in structure and empirical importance and explanatory power. In our equifinal configurations, the coverage of configuration A1 and B1, “total outsourcing with long-term partnership,” is the largest, meaning that it is empirically the most relevant and effective in achieving both economic benefits and strategic benefits in IT outsourcing (Ragin 2008, p. 44).

## Configurations for Economic Benefits

As shown in Figure 2, there are seven configurations that produce high economic benefits (A1–A7); for all configurations, NOT buy-in contract was key to achieving highly efficient IT outsourcing. A1 is a configuration representing “total outsourcing with long-term partnership relation,” which achieves sizable economic benefits regardless of the number of outsourcing vendors, outsourced IT types, and firm size (meaning that this is a more generalizable solution). Furthermore, its coverage is highest among all configurations, empirically most relevant, and its raw coverage and unique coverage are the same, meaning that it is distinct and not overlapping with any other configurations. A2 is a configuration of “total outsourcing with a long-term, fee-for-service contract with multi-vendors for IT infrastructure outsourcing,” for high economic benefits. A3 is a configuration for smaller firms that achieve high economic benefits with “total outsourcing using a long-term, fee-for-service contract with multi-vendors regardless outsourced IT types.”

A4, A5, A6, and A7 are configurations of NOT total (i.e., selective outsourcing) and a fee-for-service contract with a single vendor. A4 is for smaller firms while A6 and A7 are for large firms and A5 is for all-sized firms. Unique coverage of these configurations is very small, meaning that these configurations do overlap significantly with one another. A4 overlaps with A5 while A6 overlaps with A7. A4 and A5 are configurations that achieve high economic benefits by outsourcing a part of the IT infrastructure selectively with a single vendor, fee-for-service and the period of outsourcing does not matter (A4) for smaller firms; firm size likewise does not matter (A5) with regard to a shorter-term contract. A6 and A7 are configurations for large firms to achieve high economic benefits with a selective, single vendor outsourcing and fee-for-service contract. In this case, the period of outsourcing contract does not matter (A6) for application outsourcing. Nor do outsourced IT types matter for a shorterterm contract (A7).

## Configurations for Strategic Benefits

There are four configurations that produce high strategic benefits (B1–B4), for which there are three core elements as presented in our Table 4 parsimonious solution. Interestingly, the structure of configurations for high strategic benefits are the same as the structure of configurations for high economic benefits—that is, (B1, A1), (B2, A2), (B3, A3), and (B4, A4) have the same structure except for the role of each element as core or peripheral. This means that a firm can earn both economic and strategic benefits with the same configuration, having a different level of focus on each element. For example, total outsourcing with a long-term partnership contract (B1, A1) can enable firms to get both high economic and strategic benefits; indeed, the coverage shows that this configuration is empirically highly relevant. A firm may choose other types of configurations (e.g., B2/A2, B3/A3, B4/A4), possibly due to practical, economic, legal, political, or other reasons. In other words, given the several configurations to achieve high economic benefits and/or strategic benefits, a firm can select one by considering its contingent contexts.

## Discussion and Implications

To derive archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy with an abductive theory building approach (Bensaou and Venkatraman 1995; Misangyi and Acharya 2014; Van Maanen et al. 2007), we first defined an overarching theoretical framework that allowed us to integrate the three theories of IOR and then empirically uncovered multiple equifinal configurations using fsQCA. In Table 5, we summarize the empirical findings: two sets of configurations, one set exhibiting high economic benefits (A1–A7) and the other set exhibiting high strategic benefits (B1–B4) in IT outsourcing projects. Now, we interpret the findings theoretically with regard to the similarities and differences across these multiple equifinal configurations based on extant outsourcing studies from the dominant theories and suggest the archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy in the form of configuration propositions.

Some configurations (e.g., A4/B4, A6) have unique structures that are applicable only to a specific IT outsourcing context, whereas others (e.g., A1/B1) have more general structures applicable to different contexts. Furthermore, multiple configurations are isomorphic (i.e., A1/B1–A4/B4), meaning that a configuration with a same structure can simultaneously achieve both economic and strategic benefits.

First, the hallmark of the pattern in the isomorphic configurations of A1 and B1 is that “total outsourcing with a long-term partnership relation” attains both high economic benefits and high strategic benefits. As explained, its greatest coverage among all the configurations with a same value between raw coverage and unique coverage implies that this type of configuration is empirically distinct and most relevant. This pattern reflects the social perspective of both SET and RDT. The stream of research based on SET has historically emphasized the importance of long-term partnerships between a focal firm and its outsourcing vendors (Rai et al. 2009; Ravindran et al. 2015). While some RDT studies view partnerships as a way to stabilize its resource dependency on others, a firm, in order to be successful, needs to keep control over critical resources in business environments by developing cooperative relationships such as long-term strategic alliances and joint ventures with its outsourcing vendors (Pfeffer and Salancik 1978; Ulrich and Barney 1984). As such, SET and RDT emphasize the role of two IOR elements (i.e., period of outsourcing and relationship type) but do not provide clear, unambiguous guidance on configuration of the other two elements (i.e., degree of outsourcing and number of vendors) for the success of IT outsourcing projects. Furthermore, SET and RDT do not clearly explain how the interdependent dynamics of the four elements change across different contingencies. Our results in configuration A1/B1 highlight long-term, partnership contracts with total outsourcing regardless of the number of outsourcing vendors, outsourced IT types, and firm size. This particular pattern enables us to explicate the role of all four IOR elements and suggest a more holistic, specific underlying mechanism effective over diverse contexts, thus sharpening and expanding the core mechanisms of both SET and RDT.

This finding can be theoretically explained by both SET and RDT as follows: both theories see a firm’s business environment as a network of other firms that own critical resources and recognize IOR as a dynamic process of specific interactions between parties (Blau 1964; Emerson 1962). This social aspect of SET and RDT considers relationships to be crucial, enacted by garnering resources for the achievement of shared business goals (Dwyer et al. 1987). Intangible contexts and factors, such as trust, commitment, sharing of risk and reward, and shared values, are thus considered to be important determinants of successful outsourcing (Ang and Inkpen 2008; Goo and Huang 2008; Klepper 1994; Lee and Kim 1999).

In the outsourcing context, this social mechanism of SET and RDT explains how a firm embracing closer relationships can overcome agency problems with its outsourcing vendors and why, in the long run, the nature of outsourcing is changing from a more formal buyer-seller relationship to a strategic partnership (Grover et al. 1996; Rai et al. 2009; Willcocks and Choi 1995). Furthermore, a close, personalized relationship facilitates efficient governance (Granovetter 1985) and yields relational advantages (Dyer and Singh 1998). Whereas a hierarchical arrangement facilitates “knowledge application” (i.e., a firm’s ability to leverage the knowledge of its employees; Grant 1996), social arrangements have been found to be superior in their ability to facilitate knowledge transfer and acquisition (e.g., Liebeskind et al. 1996; Powell 1998; Saxenian 1996). In this approach, resource exchanges occur “through stable networks of exchange partners who maintain close social relationships” (Uzzi 1997, p. 36). The strength and stability of the relationship emerges in large part from both parties being committed to a long-term relationship (Dwyer et al. 1987; Jarillo 1988). Personal ties and evolving trust provide the relational governance substitutes for detailed and formal contracts (Granovetter 1985) and opportunism is curtailed by engaging in a trust-based long-term relationship.

Moreover, since partners are motivated to share knowledge that may not be available in the marketplace (Coleman 1988; Hansen 1999; Portes and Sensenbrenner 1993), they undertake joint problem-solving and promote mutual and Paretooptimal advantages (Uzzi 1997, 1999), thus creating unique value within the relationship (Dyer and Singh 1998). By implication, a firm that focuses on nurturing long-term partnerships with its vendors is more likely to pursue total outsourcing because such relationships allow the firm to leverage a key part of the value chain by bringing in a strong partner that complements its skill base; this, in turn, helps to reduce issues related to resource dependency. In addition, a partnership, with its requirement for long range planning, implies forming a mutually beneficial relationship with competent vendors based on mutual trust (Klepper 1994; Lee and Kim 1999; Lee et al. 2004; Mehta and Mehta 2010; Rottman 2008).

Given these social aspects of SET and RDT, the number of vendors should be less of a concern. Firms may either engage in an exclusive, dyadic relationship with a single vendor that eventually builds a highly integrated, strong, and long-term relationship or choose to develop a network of close relationships with multiple vendors, thereby increasing the chances of capturing market opportunities (Bapna et al. 2010; Herz et al. 2012; Wiener and Saunders 2014). The comparative benefits from dyadic relationships or networks of relationships are informed by theories of social structure (e.g., Blau 1977; Burt 1992). The regulatory and resource advantages accrue from one’s membership (Bourdieu 1983; Portes and Sensenbrenner 1993) or position in a network (Gnyawali and Madhavan 2001; Uzzi 1996). Thus, in this pattern, a firm is free to choose either a single vendor or multiple vendors (Bapna et al. 2010; Koo et al. 2017; Willcocks and Choi 1995). Based on the finding and its theoretical interpretation, we posit the first archetype of IT outsourcing strategy empirically manifested via configurations A1/B1, which complements and expands the core mechanisms of SET and RDT:

Proposition 1. Social Archetype of IT Outsourcing Strategy: Total outsourcing with long-term partnership relations can achieve both economic benefits and strategic benefits in all types of IT outsourcing projects regardless of firm size.

Except for the unique configuration A1/B1, we found significant overlap between other configurations. Therefore, we decided to compare all configurations (A2–A7, B2–B4) by narrowing in on similarities and differences in configurational structures. Specifically, we conducted an intersection analysis in the set-subset concept between configurations of high economic benefits and configurations of high strategic benefits to find a more general solution (Frambach et al. 2016; Ragin 1987), one that would bring about both benefits, if possible. As explained above, configurations A4/B4 and A5, A6, and A7 overlap, sharing the same structure of selective outsourcing, fee-for-service, and single vendor. Configurations A4/B4 and A5 together show that not-large (i.e., small/medium-sized) firms can achieve both high economic and strategic benefits, regardless of the period of outsourcing, but only when outsourcing IT infrastructure. On the other hand, configurations A5, A6, and A7 are applicable to large firms and we find that this combination allows large firms to achieve high economic benefits in an IT application outsourcing project (A6), but it does not ensure high strategic benefits. For IT infrastructure outsourcing (A5, A7), large firms should add a shorter-term contract to this combination.

These configurations are typically viewed as the de facto strategy in the IT outsourcing literature, particularly in studies based on TCET. As mentioned earlier, regarding the strategic objective of IT outsourcing, TCET emphasizes the economic benefit by focusing on the role of costs in achieving the outsourcing success. Our finding reveals more diverse types of TCET-based configurations for economic benefit (four configurations A4–A7) than strategic benefit (single configuration B4), which may partly show the economics-oriented aspect of TCET.

For the IOR elements, conventional TCET studies insist that outsourcing vendors cannot truly be strategic partners and thus a firm should avoid the partnership mode. Also, a firm should avoid a long-term contract to more precisely analyze the cost implications of their outsourcing decisions, recover faster from unforeseen mistakes, and more easily switch vendors (Lacity and Willcocks 1998; Willcocks et al. 2011). However, TCET studies offer incompatible findings regarding the other two IOR elements (i.e., degree of outsourcing and number of vendors) and no guideline for configuring the four IOR elements depending on firm size and IT type to be outsourced.

We argue that our findings enable us to complement and extend extant TCET mechanisms in IT outsourcing by explicating how all four IOR elements matter for the success of IT outsourcing projects in different contexts. To theoretically explain the findings, the traditional mechanism of TCET has emphasized the necessity of a fee-for-service contract with outsourcing vendors. Fee-for-service contracts typically provide a highly standardized task service that reduces uncertainty in outsourcing activities and, furthermore, is accompanied by a detailed contract, including service level agreements (SLAs), measures of performance, and penalties for low performance. These effectively reduce information asymmetry and opportunism, resulting in significantly lower transaction costs (Lacity and Willcocks 1998).

Some studies based on TCET argue that total outsourcing outperforms selective outsourcing (e.g., Bengtsson and Dabhilkar 2009), but at the same time TCET provides a logic that selective outsourcing exposes only some IT functions to vendor opportunism and thus can be more effective in reducing coordination and control costs compared to total outsourcing (Lacity et al. 1996; Willcocks et al. 2007). Therefore, the risk of possible vendor opportunism and information asymmetry posed by a single vendor contract can be mitigated via mechanisms provided by fee-for-service and selective outsourcing. In addition, a strong relationship with a single vendor controlled by these mechanisms can allow the firm to reduce performance assurance costs and communication costs (Chen and Bharadwaj 2009; Cronk and Sharp 1995). Regarding the period of outsourcing, a short-term contract enables a firm to more easily switch between vendors, and the possible opportunism posed by a long-term contract may be managed by the selective and fee-for-service logics.

Furthermore, depending on firm size, available resources and the size of the budget for IT outsourcing vary in major ways, hence having a major influence on outsourcing relational elements (Ang and Straub 1998). Firm size has been one of the main contingency factors that can affect organizational structures, allocation of resources and authority, IT investment, and information processing and decision-making processes (Galbraith 1973; Harris and Katz 1991; Mabert et al. 2003; Roberts and Grover 2012; Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999). In general, due to the relatively simpler structure of small firms and their IT systems, the configuration of selective, fee-for-service, and a single vendor can be sufficient for them to gain strategic and economic benefits for both IT infrastructure and applications. On the other hand, large firms, being more complex in organizational structures, business processes, and decision-making, are thought to be burdened by diversity, complexity, and interdependencies (Galbraith 1973; Harris and Katz 1991; Roberts and Grover 2012; Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999). That being said, though, IT infrastructure, unlike IT applications, consists of multiple components (e.g., hardware, software, and network) that are standardizable, modularizable, and separable (Roberts and Grover 2012; Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999), a factor that lowers switching costs. To get this benefit from standardized IT infrastructures, a large firm needs to have a shortterm contract so that it can switch to a different vendor in a short time frame, thus preventing vendor opportunism. Therefore, we suggest our second proposition that complements and extends the core mechanisms of TCET:

Proposition 2. Economic Archetype of IT Outsourcing Strategy: Selective outsourcing with a feefor-service contract with a single vendor enables smaller firms to achieve both economic benefits and strategic benefits in individual outsourcing projects for IT infrastructure. This combination also enables large firms to achieve economic benefits in IT application outsourcing while a shorter-term contract added to this combination will also achieve economic benefits in outsourcing IT infrastructure.

Third, via intersection analysis, we found that configurations A2/B2 and A3/B3 likewise overlap, sharing the same structure of total outsourcing, fee-for-service, long-term contracts with multi-vendors. This structure enables smalland medium-sized firms (SMEs) to actualize both high economic and strategic benefits irrespective of outsourced IT type. However, for large firms, configuration A2/B2 qualifies only in the case of IT infrastructure and thus a large firm with this structure effectuates both high economic and strategic benefits, but only when outsourcing IT infrastructure.

We argue that this new pattern represents a hybrid mechanism that cannot be explained by a single theoretical logic, but can be fully explained by both economic and social logics from the foundational theories. In other words, firms that adopt this pattern can not only maximize the advantages of both economic and social archetypes but also minimize disadvantages. The very high consistencies (0.98 for A2/B2 and 1.00 for A3/B3) imply that these configurations are highly reliable in producing both economic and strategic benefits, thus supporting our argument.

According to the economic logic, fee-for-service provides an effective way to reduce opportunism and uncertainty with highly standardized task services and SLAs, thus lowering transaction costs (Lacity and Willcocks 1998). Furthermore, a multi-vendor contract allows a firm to minimize vendor switching costs and vendor opportunism (Levina and Su 2008; Su and Levina 2011; Venkatraman et al. 1994). Indeed, fee-for-service makes it easier for a firm to hire and switch between multiple vendors. Thus, a fee-for-service contract with multiple vendors may be a more effective way to overcome the opportunism risk and reduce the coordination and control costs for outsourcing activities.

On the other hand, according to TCET-based studies, total outsourcing and long-term contracts open the door for deliberate opportunism, leading to higher outsourcing costs compared to selective outsourcing and a shorter-term contract (Lacity et al. 1996; Willcocks et al. 2007). Interestingly, as mentioned earlier, social mechanisms stress the importance of building trust-based relationships that usually take a long time to establish, but enable both parties to achieve mutual benefits and reduce risks. Such close relationships are mutually beneficial in the context of the need for long-range planning (Willcocks and Choi 1995), whereby a firm can clearly offset such short-term contract problems (Mohr and Spekman 1994). In addition, the problems inherent in total outsourcing can be effectively managed by the fee-for-service, multi-vendor contract mechanisms.

In practice, many firms have a tendency to nurture and maintain long-term relationships with their outsourcing vendors by choosing total outsourcing through a continuing fee-forservice contract. This reduces the various transaction costs and enhances relationship quality, thereby increasing the possibility of IT outsourcing success (e.g., Goo et al. 2007).

As explained previously in describing the economic archetype, the logic of firm size and outsourced IT type also applies here. The simpler structures of smaller firms and their IT systems allow them to simultaneously capitalize on strategic and economic benefits for both IT infrastructure and IT applications. Large firms that adopt total outsourcing with a fee-for-service contract are more likely to outsource the standardized IT infrastructure to multiple vendors and thereby gain bargaining power over them, promoting lower costs and higher vendor performance. Based on these patterns, we offer our third proposition that suggests a new hybrid archetype built on both economic and social theoretic logics:

Proposition 3. Hybrid Economic and Social Archetype of IT Outsourcing Strategy: Total outsourcing with a long-term, fee-for-service contract with multivendors can achieve both economic and strategic benefits in individual IT outsourcing projects. For large firms, this archetype is applicable only for IT infrastructure outsourcing projects while for smaller firms it can be applicable for both IT applications and infrastructure.

## Theoretical and Practical Contributions

The IT outsourcing strategic literature suffers from the causal ambiguity and complexity inherent in IT outsourcing projects and thus has difficulty in stating, unequivocally, what is a successful IT outsourcing strategy. Studies built on the dominant theories in IT outsourcing have revealed and tested core strategic mechanisms, including cost efficient transactions, control of critical resource dependency, and reciprocal relationship building, all well reflected in three foundational theories (i.e., TCET, RDT, and SET). However, the IT outsourcing literature built on these theories has not been able to clearly explain appropriate degrees of outsourcing, relationship type, duration of engagement, number of vendors, nor, finally and most importantly, the combinatorial fit of all of these elements. Indeed, these dominant theories do not match all these IOR elements simultaneously with the strategic objectives of organizations nor do they contextualize their guidance by IT type or firm size. Some studies put forward generalized IT outsourcing strategies (Ali and Green 2012; Kim et al. 2013; Lacity and Willcocks 1998; Larsen et al. 2013; Murray et al. 2009; Weigelt 2009), but these scholars focus mostly on the additive net effect of individual elements, mainly adopting the point of view of a single theoretical mechanism. Little attention has been paid to combined or complementary effects.

As a result, findings of previous IT outsourcing studies on how to configure all the strategic elements are often inconsistent, not only across multiple theories but also between studies adopting the same theory. Thus, overall knowledge about IT outsourcing strategy to organize the key IOR elements remains fragmented, contradictory, and incomplete. Hence, in this study, we sought to build holistic archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy that can bridge these identified gaps by integrating the three main theories with an overarching contingency fit framework.

By doing so, this study makes significant contributions to the IT outsourcing literature in several ways. First, this study develops an overarching theoretical framework, based on contingency fit theory that enables us to integrate three dominant theories widely adopted in the IT outsourcing studies. As mentioned earlier, from an overarching contingency fit perspective, it is not strange that we would find fragmented, inconsistent knowledge in the current literature. Given that a synergistic combination of all IOR elements cannot be fully explained by the logic of a single theory alone, it can be better explained by the logics of multiple theories. Thus, our framework provides a solid foundation that enables researchers to build a holistic theory that can integrate fragmented and incomplete knowledge.

Second, with the proposed archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy (i.e., social, economic, and hybrid) we make contributions to theories by complementing and extending the core mechanisms of the three dominant foundational theories. By specifying strategic objectives of both economic and strategic benefits and the contingency factors of IT outsourcing projects, we offer a contextually specific middle range theory consisting of specific fit propositions. Our first social archetype of IT outsourcing strategy prescribes long-term partnership relations for total outsourcing to achieve both economic and strategic benefits in all types of IT outsourcing projects regardless of the number of vendors and firm size. This archetype complements and further sharpens the core mechanisms of SET and RDT (i.e., reciprocal relationship and control of critical resource dependency). As we explained in our propositional development, both mechanisms explain how to configure the two IOR elements (i.e., period of outsourcing and relationship type) but do not show clearly how to configure the other two elements (i.e., degree of outsourcing and number of vendors) to achieve success in IT outsourcing projects and both are largely silent on the contingency effect on the interdependent dynamics of the four elements. Our social archetype explicates how to configure all four IOR elements at once in a way that achieves both economic and strategic benefits regardless of outsourced IT type or firm size, thus complementing and extending SET and RDT.

Our second economic archetype of IT outsourcing strategy prescribes to smaller firms a fee-for-service contract with a single vendor for selective outsourcing of IT infrastructure to garner both economic and strategic benefits. For large firms, this combination can be used for IT application outsourcing to achieve economic benefits, and for IT infrastructure outsourcing a shorter-term contract should be added. Thus, this archetype advances our understanding of how TCET plays out in IT outsourcing. Prior studies adopting TCET argued that a short-term, fee-for-service contract enables a client firm to overcome agency problems, resulting in successful IT outsourcing projects. Unfortunately, findings regarding the other two IOR elements (i.e., degree of outsourcing and number of vendors) were inconsistent. Thus, TCET failed to explain how the dynamics among the IOR elements change across different contingencies. Our economic archetype explains how to configure the four IOR elements from the TCET perspective in order to achieve success for large versus smaller firms as well as for IT infrastructure versus IT applications, thus extending the core economic logic.

The third hybrid archetype is composed of total outsourcing with a long-term, fee-for-service contract with multi-vendors. For large firms, this archetype is only for IT infrastructure while for smaller firms it is for both IT infrastructure and applications to realize both economic and strategic benefits. This hybrid archetype also suggests a new mechanism that cannot be explained by a single theoretical logic, but can be fully explained by both economic and social logics from the foundational theories. The hybrid configurations are highly consistent and reliable in producing both economic and strategic benefits, which not only maximize the advantages of both economic and social archetypes but also minimize their disadvantages. Thus, our three archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy provide a deeper and more comprehensive understanding that can overcome the limitations of prior studies, and complement and extend the application of these individual theories in the IT outsourcing research stream.

Third, we empirically apply the concept of equifinality to IT outsourcing strategy. As IT functions in organizations are no longer homogeneous in the current, rapidly changing business and technological environment, IT outsourcing decisions should not be limited to a narrow range of options that are thought to be applicable to all situations. Likely there are several viable paths to reach the strategic objectives of projects. This may explain why previous studies adopting various theoretical models in IT outsourcing report inconsistent and contradictory findings. In this sense, the three archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy draw clear contingency boundaries within a middle range theory, boundaries which provide more specific guidelines for researchers who have a serious concern about the relevance of the current set of theories being applied to the phenomenon.

This study also yields several implications of practical and methodological importance. First, the basic premise of equifinality as empirically manifested in the form of multiple configurations suggests that multiple viable alternatives can exist for organizing strategic outsourcing elements that match conflicting functional demands via structural options. This implies that a firm has multiple options for laying out an IT outsourcing strategy that organizes interorganizational relational elements to meet objectives.

Second, the three archetypes provide valuable and contextspecific guidelines for firms in their strategic decision-making with regard to their relationships with outsourcing vendors depending on firm size and outsourcing IT type, as prescribed in detail in our three archetypes. Furthermore, the three archetypes identified in this study provide organizations with a benchmark against which they can compare their own outsourcing strategies.

Finally, this study also makes a methodological contribution to the IT outsourcing literature by showing how to apply a set-theoretic QCA method to IT outsourcing research; this is an alternative to other pattern-finding techniques like cluster analysis,<sup>11</sup> thereby providing specific guidelines for using QCA in IT studies.

## Limitations and Future Research

As with other studies, our research has its limitations. First, this study uses a dataset collected from the CIO or a top IT manager of each firm. While the CIO is perhaps the best person to provide good, quality information about IT outsourcing (and is thus a typical source of data for most IT strategy studies), there can be a selection bias due to the single key informant. Therefore, future research could collect data from multiple respondents and sources. Furthermore, if longitudinal data can be collected, future research could develop a deeper understanding of how the archetypes of outsourcing strategy are actually configured and the effects on IT outsourcing success over time. Also, as the performance of an IT outsourcing project was measured with perceptual items collected from managers, a future study can use relatively objective performance data, such as public firm’s accounting records to perhaps confirm and extend our findings.

Second, although we focused on the four key IOR elements based on our overarching theoretical framework, there is the potential to generate further knowledge by adding other outsourcing elements to the configurational analysis. In a similar manner, we classified outsourced IT types into two typical categories, application and infrastructure, but other researchers might embrace a more comprehensive coding scheme for specific IT functions and investigate their varying effects on IT outsourcing success. Future research can also include other contextual and environmental factors to put forward a more context-specific middle range theory. This could be even more useful for praxis.

Third, we note that a buy-in contract does not appear in any configuration that consistently achieves IT outsourcing success.<sup>12</sup> This finding implies that the argument of previous studies built on the mixed mechanism of RDT and resourcebased view theory (e.g., Bustinza et al. 2010; Holcomb and Hitt 2007; Quinn 1999) does not appear to hold when all elements are considered simultaneously. The buy-in contract implies that the firm should internally retain most of the management of critical purchased resources in order to minimize its dependency on external vendors (Grimpe and Kaiser 2010; Ulrich and Barney 1984). However, it appears that firms cannot effectively offset the benefits of resource independency by developing their own IT competencies through the buying-in of external resources. Thus, future research should investigate, reify, or dispute our finding that a buy-in configuration is inferior to the contending archetypes of partnership and fee-for-service contracts.

Last, our data sample includes only Korean firms that were outsourcing key IT functions. Although we attempted to generalize our findings to other contexts, future studies can and should test the generalizability of our archetypes to other world regions with different cultural, social, and economic characteristics. Replications of this study with datasets from geographically diverse areas will help to fine-tune the archetypes proposed in this study.

## Conclusion

In the current, pervasively digitized business environment, more and more organizations are paying attention to the strategic importance of IT outsourcing to sustain competitive advantage. In this study, we first devise an overarching theoretical framework to integrate the dominant theories widely adopted in IT outsourcing studies. Using fsQCA, we then empirically uncover multiple, equifinal configurations that reap strategic and economic benefits from IT outsourcing. Based on the empirical findings, we theoretically determine holistic archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy congruent with strategic objectives of IT outsourcing projects and matching specific contexts. We believe that by virtue of applying these archetypes, outsourcing scholars can start to remedy the fragmented and inconsistent knowledge that has arisen in IT outsourcing work. We hope it will encourage other researchers to adopt this theoretical framework and create more holistic, generalizable knowledge on the increasingly important topic of IT outsourcing strategy.

## Appendix B

## Dimensions of Interorganizational Relationship Strategy

This study focuses on strategic decisions that determine a firm’s outsourcing relationships at the early stage of IT outsourcing projects. These guide the entire outsourcing process and are thus different from other factors such as actions that emerge during implementation or execution of specific outsourcing relationships. That is, outsourcing strategic factors, by our definition, impact the overall process of outsourcing while other factors have their effects only on a part of the whole process. In this study, other such factors arising in the later stages are not considered to be relevant strategic elements of outsourcing. For example, according to the general process model of outsourcing (Chaudhury et al. 1995; Huber et al. 2013; Lee 2008; Lee and Kim 1997), such factors as specific contract negotiation, outsourcing implementation, actual contract management, and performance feedback are implemented and completed only after decisions on outsourcing strategic elements have been made during the earlier stage

In their pursuit of interorganizational relationships (IORs), firms adopt diverse strategies in managing or governing those relationships (e.g., Baker 1990). In order to understand the dimensions that distinguish among IT outsourcing strategies, we searched for the literature on the IOR governance forms.

Following Granovetter’s (1985) landmark article outlining the embeddedness thesis, researchers on IOR across several disciplines have identified two types of relationship strategies. While they allude to these strategies utilizing different names, the characteristics that distinguish among the strategies are largely similar. Dwyer et al. (1987) characterize buyer–supplier relationships as discrete transactions or relational exchanges. In a discrete transaction, an easily measurable commodity is traded for money. The terms of a relational exchange are less definite and may include noneconomic satisfaction in addition to economic advantage. Baker (1990) describes IOR based on the nature of interface. A relational interface is one wherein “inter-firm contracts can be so strong that they act as functional substitutes for hierarchy” (Baker 1990, p. 594). Conventionally, this type of interface has a long-term, exclusive tie with a single vendor. By contrast, a transactional interface is transitory and based solely on competitive pricing. Thus, the transactional interface assumes multiple, interchangeable service vendors, induced to act more competitively and yielding competitive prices (Baker 1990). Accordingly, transactional interfaces avoid problems incurred with a relational interface (e.g., dependence on a sole source). However, such a transactional relationship is not well suited to activities that require cooperative endeavors.

Dyer and Singh (1998) distinguish between arm’s length relationships and alliances. Arm’s length relationships are non-idiosyncratic, i.e., sellers are interchangeable (Dyer and Singh 1998), while alliances are formed by a firms’ commitment to invest in relation-specific assets, substantial knowledge exchange, and complementary resources or capabilities (Dyer and Singh 1998). Alliances assume a long-term, committed relationship.

Jarillo (1988) distinguishes classic market relationships from strategic networks. Parties to a classic market relationship perceive the relationship to be competitive rather than cooperative. In the alternative, firms strategically create networks of complementary asset-specific functions. Similar to Dyer and Singh (1998), Jarillo (1988, p. 32) sees these networks as “long-term, purposeful arrangements among distinct but related for-profit organizations that allow those firms in them to gain or sustain competitive advantage vis-à-vis their competitors outside the network.” While Dyer and Singh’s alliance strategy addresses the relationship between a focal and a single vendor, Jarillo’s strategic networks can also refer to a firm’s relationship with multiple vendors who have embedded relationships with each other. Such firms within the network favor a hierarchical relationship, i.e., their contracts are relatively unspecified and the relationship is expected to be long-term.

In Uzzi’s (1996, 1997, 1999) account , interorganizational strategies may be arm’s length or embedded. An arm’s length strategy is one in which transactions take place through loose collections of individual who maintain impersonal and constantly shifting exchange ties. In such relationships, actors constantly switch to new buyers and sellers to maximize cost advantages and/or to avoid dependence on a single vendor. In contrast, an embedded strategy is one wherein economic exchanges occur “through stable networks of exchange partners who maintain close social relationships” (Uzzi 1997, p. 36). In such relationships, both parties are committed to a long-term relationship, oriented toward information exchange. Based on Table B1, it is clear that there are four variables that distinguish among strategies in IOR: degree of outsourcing, number of vendors, relationship type, and period of outsourcing. The first distinguishing characteristic of arm’s length versus embedded strategies made evident in Table B1 is exclusivity. Baker (1990) studied exclusivity in firms’ relationships with banks and therefore refers to it as a relationship with a single external organization. However, we view exclusivity in its broader connotation as the degree of vertical integration as well as the number of trading partners used for a specific type of transaction. In other words, a client can be nonexclusive by insourcing a portion of its IT functions and by employing multiple providers. Thus, in the outsourcing context, exclusivity also pertains to the degree to which IT functions are outsourced. We note several other references to the number of vendors employed by a firm in strategic characteristics such as small numbers bargaining (Uzzi 1996, 1997) and the size of one’s network of trading partners (Dwyer et al. 1987; Uzzi 1999). IORs also differ in the specified duration of the relationship (Dwyer et al. 1987; Uzzi 1999). An embedded relationship tends to be long-term while an arm’s length relationship tends to be short-term (Dwyer et al. 1987; Jarillo 1998). We refer to this dimension as the period of outsourcing.

Finally, Dwyer et al. (1987) refer to the nature of the specified obligations. More specifically, Uzzi (1996, 1997) distinguishes among types of interorganizational relationships in terms of the extent to which price is a determining factor in the relationship, whether or not they have a written contract, whether reputation is considered important, and the extent to which both parties invest in the relationship. In a similar vein, Dyer and Singh (1998) allude to the extent to which IOR manifest relationship-specific assets and complementary resources. These characteristics speak to the type of relationship that differentiates pure market relationships from extended hierarchical relationships (Baker 1990; Williamson 1994). Fee-for-service contracts set up market relationships wherein the identity of the buyer or seller is unimportant (Williamson 1994). Price is still a decisive factor as is a detailed contract. In this and other types of highly specific and price-driven relationships, reputation, resource complementarity, and mutual asset-specific investments are unimportant (Dyer and Singh 1998; Jarillo 1988). Partnerships represent an extension of a firm’s hierarchy when the transaction involves high asset-specificity and the identity of the buyer or seller is important (Williamson 1994). Partnerships are less specific in contract terms and price is less of a factor. Such relationships hinge on resource complementarity and mutual asset-specific investments. Relationship-specific investments signal credible commitment and enable the relationship to develop into a rare, inimitable source of competitive advantage (Dyer and Singh 1998; Jarillo 1988).

As suggested by Porter (1980), different relationship strategies play out via different outcomes. A variety of outcomes of IORs are evident in Table B1. The literature provides some support for a distinctive relationship between the nature of the relationship and the types of outcomes that accrue from the relationship. Arm’s length relationships minimize transaction costs by relying on multiple, inter-changeable partners, and detailed contract stipulations (Williamson 1985). Such relationships are therefore useful for reducing costs (Dyer and Singh 1998). In contrast, an embedded strategy yields knowledge and competence-related advantages (Dwyer et al. 1987; Uzzi 1996, 1997)

## Measure Development

Measurement development went through three stages: (1) item generation based on prior literature and extensive feedback from four academics who have published outsourcing papers in prominent journals and three practitioners who have experience in outsourcing practices for more than 10 years; (2) a pretest involving 15 IT outsourcing managers from different firms who have experience in outsourcing practices for more than 20 years; and (3) a pilot test using 30 firms in diverse industries that have implemented at least some degree of IT outsourcing.

The data gathered for our key strategic elements are relatively impersonal in that our questionnaire items do not measure a personal opinion but instead relatively objective project features. The number of outsourcing vendors involved in an IT outsourcing project, period of outsourcing contract, relationship type and budget for the IT outsourcing project (as a proxy for the degree of IT outsourcing) are not latent but essentially data-driven measures. On the other hand, outsourcing performance is markedly perceptual as we measure managers’ assessment of economic and strategic benefits emerging from their IT outsourcing projects. We adopted well-defined and empirically validated items from extant IT outsourcing studies, ones that were specifically designed for measuring economic benefit and strategic benefit as outcomes of IT outsourcing projects (Grover et al. 1996; Lane et al. 2005). Given these formative outcome variables, we report the test results for formative construct validity in Appendix E.

## Appendix D

## Instrument Validation Prior to Primary Data-Gathering

As a first step, a questionnaire based on prior work was prepared and evaluated by seven IT professionals for content validation. For example, the degree of outsourcing was measured by the actual amount of the outsourcing as a percentage of the total IT budget in each organization (Kaiser and Buxmann 2012; Lacity et al. 1996) because it is fairly objective, requiring a quantitative response and does need to consider each organization’s specific situation and intentions in IT outsourcing. The period of outsourcing and the number of vendors are likewise less personal opinion than relatively objective aspects of outsourcing projects (Lacity and Willcocks 1998). For the relationship type, respondents were asked to select one among three major types—fee-for-service contract, partnership, and buy-in contract—based on their outsourcing contracts (Gopal and Koka 2012; Lacity and Willcocks 1998).

Regarding outsourcing performance, we assessed the economic benefits and strategic benefits of outsourcing project by adopting Grover et al.’s (1996) instrument, which consisted of three and five items respectively with a seven-point Likert scale (1 as strongly disagree and 7 as strongly agree). Grover et al.’s (1996) original instrument was developed as a three dimensional construct to assess the degree of achieving strategic, economic, and technological benefits of IT outsourcing and has been adopted and validated by numerous outsourcing studies (e.g., Lee and Kim 1999; Lee et al. 2004; Schwarz 2014). However, few studies have confirmed their three dimensions of IT outsourcing success and some studies have instead questioned the construct validity of the original three dimensions of IT outsourcing success (e.g., Goo et al. 2008; Lane et al. 2005). In particular, Lane et al. (2005) found that a two dimensional model (i.e., strategic and economic benefits) is more appropriate and reliable than the three dimensional one (i.e., strategic, economic and technological benefits) when assessing outsourcing benefits using Grover et al.’s (1996) instrument. Thus, we decided to adopt a two dimensional outcome construct in this study, consisting of two distinct factors, i.e., strategic benefits and economic benefits. Furthermore, in Appendix E, we present the results of formative construct validity tests for both two and three categories. We also conducted fsQCA analysis for both and ended up with the same results. Please note that the items for strategic benefits (B5\~B8) measure the tangible and intangible outcomes resulting directly from IT outsourcing, particularly from a client firm’s IT resource perspective. However, these items do not measure a firm’s IT capability, that is, a firm’s ability to leverage IT resources to meet business needs. We certainly admit the importance of IT capability, but it is just not the focus of this study. A future study could well investigate IT capability as an outcome of IT outsourcing projects. After drafting the initial version of the questionnaire, four academic scholars and three industry professionals in the area of IT outsourcing assessed the content validity of the items and also provided feedback on the initia items. After considering their feedback, we refined and restructured the questionnaire.

As a second step, a pretest test was conducted with 15 IT outsourcing managers from different companies experienced in outsourcing practice for more than 20 years. The purpose of the pretest was to test the wording, the ease with which the questionnaire could be answered, the appropriateness of the question sequence, and the consistency in meaning. The pretest also helped to validate the scales for the survey items. Once the content of the survey instrument had been polished, back translation (with the material translated from English into Korean, then back to English, then comparison of versions, and finally discrepancy resolution) was carried out by three IT outsourcing professionals who speak both Korean and English; the intent of this procedure was to ensure consistency between the Korean and original English version of the instrument (Mullen 1995; Singh 1995).

A pilot test was then conducted to assess questionnaire construct validity with 30 managers from 30 Korean companies in diverse industrie that have already adopted IT outsourcing practices. The respondents were CIOs or the heads of IT departments. The responses from 30 managers were analyzed for the discriminant and convergent validity for the formatively measured outcome variables. All the items for the economic and strategic benefits of outsourcing performance had item-to-construct correlations more than 0.6 (Campbell and Fiske 1959; Loch et al. 2003).

Multiple phases of instrument development and validation resulted in significant refinement and restructuring of the survey instrument and also established the initial face validity of the measures (Nunnally and Bernstein 1994). These tests showed that the instrument was ready for full scale testing. The final questionnaire items are listed in Appendix C.

## Appendix E

## Validation of Formative Construct

The single questionnaire items for the four outsourcing elements were designed to gather straight-forward factual data from respondents. Thus, there was no real need to test them for construct validity. However, for the IT outsourcing success constructs, we used multiple items that measured the perception of CIOs about the benefits derived from their outsourcing projects in terms of the economic benefits and strategic benefits. Therefore, we conducted a series of tests to evaluate the construct validity of the formative outcome variables using the data collected from the main survey.

Two methods were adopted to examine construct validity. First, a confirmatory factor analysis was performed as part of the PLS algorithm (Gefen and Straub 2005). For outsourcing success, its item weights were then evaluated. As summarized in Table E1, the weights of all items were significant, indicating convergent validity. Second, a modified multi-trait multi-method analysis was conducted to assess both convergent and discriminant validity (Petter et al. 2007). In this process, three items of the economic benefits and five items of the strategic benefits are multiplied by their respective weights, as calculated by PLS. Then, composite measures for both benefits are created for the formative construct by summing the weighted item values. In addition, correlation matrixes were calculated, as summarized in Tables E2 and E3. The tables include two demographical variables, number of employees and total sales revenue, which should not be correlated with either the items of the economic and strategic benefits or their composite value. Tables E2 and E3 show the item-item and item-to-construct correlations for the items of the strategic and economic benefits and their respective composite value. ## Truth-Table Analysis in fsQCA

## Truth-Table Algorithm

After calibration of set-memberships, fsQCA creates a truth-table to identify combinations of elements that produce the outcome of interest. A truth-table displays all possible combinations of the elements and each row corresponds to one combination.

In a truth-table, the “number” column means the frequency of firms allocated to each combination, that is, those sharing the same set of elements. First, we define five as the minimum frequency cutoff for qualifying empirically reliable and relevant combinations. Thus, we exclude combinations with fewer than five empirical instances (Fiss 2011; Ragin 2008). Table F1 is the truth-table for economic benefit outcome of IT outsourcing and Table F2 is the truth-table for strategic benefit outcome that include at least five cases. There are a total of 254 logically possible combinations with eight causal conditions (i.e., 2 to the eight power 2<sup>8</sup> = 256); in both truth-tables only 47 combinations include at least one case, which means that 209 rows do not have any cases, known as limited diversity (Fiss 2007; Ragin 2008). There are theoretical and practical reasons that firms may subconsciously choose some combinations while avoiding other combinations. Counterfactual analysis handles this limited diversity, as discussed below.

Next, to determine which combination consistently becomes a sufficient solution for the outcome, we use set-theoretic consistency, a measure similar to the significance level in regression analysis. It is “the degree to which the cases sharing a given combination of conditions (i.e., a row in the truth-table) agree in displaying the outcome in question” (Ragin 2008, p. 44). Thus, it indicates how closely a combination of conditions becomes a subset of the outcome (i.e., a sufficient condition). In our study, it shows whether firms sharing a same set of outsourcing elements consistently result in outsourcing success.

In fsQCA, there are two types of consistency: raw consistency, which is calculated by giving credit for “near misses” and penalties for large inconsistencies and PRI consistency (a proportional reduction for inconsistency), an alternate measure of consistency that in addition eliminates the influence of cases that have simultaneous membership in both the outcome and its complement (i.e., y and \~y). In this study, we rely on both types of consistency. For rows (i.e., combinations of conditions) that satisfy the frequency threshold, we set 0.85 as cutoff for raw consistency and 0.75 for PRI consistency, meaning that only combinations with a raw consistency of at least 0.85 and a PRI consistency of at least 0.75 are considered as reliably resulting in the outcomes. In the truth-table, outsourcing performance (i.e., economic benefits and strategic benefits columns) has a value 1 for the combinations that satisfy this consistency cutoff, otherwise 0. In this filtering process with consistencies, all rows (i.e., combinations) that include “buy-in” contract type are determined not to produce the outcomes in enough consistency. If we lower the consistency threshold, there could be some configurations with buy-in contract to produce economic and strategic benefits. Further truth-table analysis reveals that such configurations have a common IOR structure, that is, “buy-in, selective outsourcing, not-long term,” which can be best suited for minimizing dependency on other firms for key resources, and thus reflect the mechanism of resource dependence theory (RDT).

Next, using Boolean algebra, the truth-table algorithm reduces the numerous combinations into a smaller number of more parsimonious solutions (Ragin 2008). For example, if a combination A&B and \~A&B result in the desirable outcome, then regardless of whether A is present or absent (A&B + \~A&B = B), the outcome still occurs, where & means AND, + means OR, and \~ means a “negation.” Thus, A does not matter and B alone becomes a sufficient solution for achieving the outcome.

Finally, the truth-table algorithm utilizes counterfactual analysis to handle the rows without empirical cases (i.e., limited diversity) and to further minimize the number of causal conditions in a configuration (Fiss 2011). By using “easy” counterfactuals, QCA deals with an empirically unobserved combination by adding a condition known to produce the outcome to a combination. Furthermore, with “difficult” counterfactuals, it deals with an empirically unobserved combination by removing a redundant condition from a combination (Ragin 2008, p. 162). QCA generates the most parsimonious solution by applying both easy and difficult counterfactuals and the elements in this result are considered to be core conditions that have a stronger causal relationship with the outcomes (Fiss 2011). By applying only easy counterfactuals, QCA creates intermediate solutions that include peripheral conditions that have weaker causal relationships with an outcome as well as core conditions. Thus, with intermediate solutions, we can explain which conditions play a core role or a peripheral role in producing the outcome of interest. We did an additional fsQCA for the three groups of outcomes (i.e., economic, strategic, technological benefits), as explained in Appendices C and E. The resulting configurations for both strategic and technological benefits are much the same, with little difference in consistency and coverage. ## Supplemental Cluster Analyses

Alternative data analysis techniques that have been used in IT strategy studies include cluster analysis, interaction effects, and deviation score analysis, but such techniques have limitations in explaining the holistic interdependencies among multiple elements and could not reveal which elements and how they together produce the outcome of interest. They mostly look only at the impact of pair-wise combinations of outsourcing elements on performance and implicitly premised on unifinality (Fiss 2007).

The main objective of this study is to develop multiple archetypes of IT outsourcing strategy that are internally congruent in terms of the strategic objectives as well as matching specific contexts of IT outsourcing projects. To achieve this theoretical objective, we adopt a configurational approach and use qualitative comparative analysis (QCA), a set-theoretic configuration method which enables us to handle the complexity in the interdependency of the multiple elements of IT outsourcing projects. We empirically uncover two sets of configurations, one set of configurations exhibiting high economic benefits and the other set exhibiting high strategic benefits in IT outsourcing projects. Based on the findings, we could develop propositions that explain how to achieve both benefits simultaneously in IT outsourcing projects.

To explicitly show the benefits and superiority of our approach in reaching our research objective, we compared our results with the results from other techniques. Specifically, we conducted a cluster analysis with the same dataset to examine the congruence among the four outsourcing relational elements. Cluster analysis is a traditional statistical technique widely adopted by prior studies that aim to find multiple clusters of objects (Venkatraman 1989). The resulting clusters should show high internal homogeneity and high external heterogeneity, meaning that the cases within a same cluster are close together whereas different clusters will be far apart. This can be achieved by grouping cases in a way to minimize the Euclidean distance between cases in a same cluster while maximize the distance between cases in different clusters. Here, we used the K-Means clustering technique using the Quick Cluster routine produced by SPSS 22.0 software.

Interestingly, we found clusters similar to the configurations that we found with fsQCA. Although, with cluster analysis and additional supporting statistical analyses, we found some groups that may represent our posited archetypes, there are critical limitations to these findings, especially compared to those from QCA. First, using these tools, it is relatively difficult to validate whether the clusters consistently produce outsourcing success while all configurations resulting from QCA present a consistent solution in producing IT outsourcing success. Results of cluster analysis end up being mixed and thus it is not clear whether each group is valid or not. More importantly, the results from cluster analysis do not show the role of each element and how the outsourcing elements work together to produce the desired outcomes. Therefore, by introducing QCA into IT outsourcing research, the present study opens a new avenue to effectively investigating the complex dynamics among key outsourcing strategic elements.

## Further Details of Testing

To derive a parsimonious set of clusters that could be clearly distinguished from one another, we attempted to extract a different set of clusters consisting of two, three, four, five, six, and seven groups using different options, such as Euclidean and Mahalanobis distance, a standardized form of Euclidean distance. To assess the distinctiveness of each cluster, equality of variable means between the clusters was assessed by the F-test. Finally, a six-cluster solution was selected based on the results of F-test. Table G1 shows means and standard deviations of variables in the six clusters from columns 2 to 7. F-value and the level of its significance related to the test result for equality of variable means between clusters are shown in column 8. The last column shows the results of Dunnett’s multiple range tests for the significant pair of clusters between the variable means.

As shown in Table G1, both F-tests and Dunnett’s multiple range tests show that the group means of these six clusters are significantly different. Five clusters (i.e., Groups 2–6) appear to be internally congruent while Group 1 is noncongruent. Table G2 presents the summary of cluster analyses in terms of the four outsourcing elements.

In addition to cluster analysis, we also did one-way ANOVA to assess the differences between the six clusters in terms of outsourcing performance as measured using the eight performance items for IT outsourcing projects. The results are shown in Table G3. Columns 2 to 7 display the mean values and the standard deviations of outsourcing performance measures for the six clusters. Column 8 exhibits the F-values, degree of freedom, and significance levels. Paired clusters of which mean values are significantly different from each other are identified and shown in column 9. The F-test results indicate that the six groups are significantly different in terms of the outsourcing performance outcomes. Groups 2 to 6, which display a high congruency among four outsourcing elements, achieve different levels of outsourcing success—middle to high, while the noncongruent group 1 shows lower levels for outsourcing success. Thus, the firms in the congruent groups exhibit higher mean ratings on the measures of outsourcing benefits compared to firms in the noncongruent group. Groups 5 and 6 together seem to correspond to the social archetype of IT outsourcing strategy (i.e., Proposition 1), Group 3 may correspond to the economic archetype of a selective IT outsourcing strategy (i.e., Proposition 2) but no cluster was found corresponding to the hybrid archetype of total IT outsourcing (i.e., Proposition 3). Group 4 appears to show that a configuration of the resource perspective and its performance is mediocre for all performance measures, compared to other groups.
