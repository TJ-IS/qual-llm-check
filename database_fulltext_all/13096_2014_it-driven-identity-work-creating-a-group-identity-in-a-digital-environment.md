# IT-driven identity work: Creating a group identity in a digital environment

## a b s t r a c t

While organizational scholars are increasingly interested in issues of identity, identity work, and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, in-depth empirical studies analyzing the process of identity creation have lagged behind, particularly when such process is triggered by the digitization of a work environment. In this longitudinal case study, we take a social constructionist perspective to investigate the identity creation process of a group of librarians in charge of a new information commons library. We call attention to the dialectic forces underlying this process, emphasizing how the librarians image, as re<sup>fl</sup>ected by the patrons, led the librarians to try multiple provisional identities, which were supported by liminal actions reminiscent of either “who they were” and/or “who they could be.” We also consider how technology was appropriated throughout this dynamic and suggest a technology identi<sup>fi</sup>cation process model that parallels the group identity creation process.

## 1. Introduction

In recent decades, technological innovation and digitization have increasingly affected a variety of industries, such as banking, medicine, education, journalism, and librarianship. As these institutions rely more on information technologies and sometimes adopt digital forms (e.g., e-commerce, “citizen journalism,” electronic health records, electronic banking, digital libraries, telemedicine, massive open online courses), specialists in these <sup>fi</sup>elds must adapt their skill sets, service approaches, and relationships with customers (Fourie, 1999); that is, they need to reconsider, and possibly change, their work practices.

Information technology (IT) driven changes in work practices can be greatly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the sensemaking process that organizational members go through as they appropriate new information technologies or digital work environments. For example, Hsiao, Wu, and Hou (2008) studied how taxi drivers ascribed different meanings to an information technology (i.e., a GPS-based vehicle dispatching system) and, thus, devised new work practices, both during the introduction of this technology and during its ongoing use. Many other studies have used a sensemaking lens to explore the manner in which individuals interpret and react to information technologies (e.g., Barley, 1996; Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Orlikowski, 1996). Whereas these studies are insightful in explaining how individuals and groups enact novel work practices as they make sense of new information technologies, they neglect to address one of the core properties of the sensemaking process, the establishment and maintenance of the sensemakers' identity (Weick, 1995).

Indeed, the way organizational members think of themselves within their work context (i.e., their group identity) heavily in<sup>fl</sup>uences their interpretations of their job environment and the work practices they devise. This collective identity de<sup>fi</sup>nes the shared “internalized cognitive structure of what the organization stands for and where it intends to go” (Albert, Ashforth, & Dutton, 2000, p. 13). Thus, workers' group identity is critical for organizations because it provides stability and guidance, both internally within an organization and externally in terms of how outsiders view the organization. This stability and guidance are necessary for the organization's sense of legitimacy and survival (Tripsas, 2009). Assimilation of IT in the workplace, however, often plays a pivotal role in rede<sup>fi</sup>ning workers' roles and serves as a destabilizing force that challenges workers' identities (Alvarez, 2002; Lamb & Davidson, 2005). For example, Bartel and Wiesenfeld (2013) discuss a group of local newspaper journalists whose sense of group boundary and identity had been blurred by technological innovations such as social media and teleworking. Because a group's identity serves as a guidepost for its members, disruptive innovations that alter the group's sense of direction and purpose trigger confusion and force members to consider how to change their existing identity or create a new one (Tripsas, 2009). The mechanisms that underlie this process are important to uncover so that practitioners and researchers alike are able to discern ways to avoid the negative pitfalls of such transitions and instead facilitate the group members' journey in de<sup>fi</sup>ning who they are.

However, despite identity's fairly wide application in the management and organization literatures, the IT-identity landscape is relatively uncharted in the information systems literature and, thus, inadequately understood (Gal & Kjaergaard, 2009; Nach & Lejeune, 2009; Schellhammer, 2010; Stein, Galliers, & Markus, 2012; Tripsas, 2009). In particular, existing IT-identity research primarily views identity as an antecedent or outcome in relation to IT or explores the theme of virtual self-presentation (which, as we explain later, is more akin to the concept of image rather than identity). For the most part, this body of work is limited in its approach (mainly cross-sectional methods) and focus on individual level identity concepts. Therefore, although there are many potential rami<sup>fi</sup>cations of technological transitions on collective identity, current research concerning this topic offers little guidance in terms of how to theoretically frame IT-driven identity dynamics.

One particular lens that we leverage to frame IT-identity dynamic processes is “identity work,” or the activities that an individual, or group of individuals, engage in to create, present, and sustain their identities (Snow & Anderson, 1987). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when a group of individuals is faced with a new technological environment, it may need to reenact a previous, familiar identity (i.e., “who we were”; e.g., Walsh & Glynn, 2008), defend its current identity (i.e., “who we are”; e.g., Corley & Gioia, 2004), or devise a new one (i.e., “who we could be”; e.g., Reger, Gustafson, Demarie, & Mullane, 1994). Moreover, because identity is relational (Weick, 1995), how outsiders perceive a given group (i.e., the group's image) will in<sup>fl</sup>uence the group as it seeks to establish its identity, giving rise to a dialectical interplay between identity and image.

Addressing this important area of research, we investigate the group identity creation process of library professionals who were recruited to run a new unit characterized by a completely digitized work environment. For the most part, these library professionals did not share a common, past identity; most were recruited from other organizational areas (and in some cases, from other organizations) to staff this new work environment, which was hosted in a new building. This group of professionals was transplanted directly into the new digital environment (versus participating in its evolution from traditional to digital), and thus faced the challenge of creating a new identity consistent with its new reality, while considering how patrons viewed the group in this new reality (i.e., its image). Our research seeks to provide theoretical insights into the dynamic nature of group identity creation when a completely digital environment becomes the new work environment of that group.

In the following sections, we <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss the literature on identity, focusing on the dynamic aspect of organizational identity creation and its relationship with organizational image. Based on this literature, we present our two research questions. Next, we elaborate on the case selected for our inquiry and discuss the methodological approach underlying our case study. We then present our results, which we frame as two process models, and later discuss these <sup>fi</sup>ndings. We also suggest a revised framework to explain the identity creation process as triggered by the digitalization of a work environment.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Identity

Identity is a self-referential description that provides answers to the questions “Who am $I ? "$ (at the individual level) or “Who are we?” (at the group, organizational, or societal level) (Ashforth, Harrison, & Corley, 2008). Many disciplines have studied the concept of identity in a variety of contexts, <sup>fi</sup>elds, and levels of analysis, including personal identity (Alvesson, Ashcraft, & Thomas, 2008), social identity (Tajfel, 1974), professional identity (Ibarra, 1999), and organizational identity (Albert & Whetten, 1985; Gioia Price, Hamilton, & Thomas, 2010; Gioia, Schultz, & Corley, 2000; Hatch & Schultz, 2002). Organizationa identity refers to the collective understanding of the features of an organization by its members (Albert & Whetten, 1985) and may pertain to the organization as a whole (Alvesson & Robertson, 2006) or to particular groups within the organization (Terrion & Ashforth, 2002), such as a division, department, or sub-unit. Proposed by Albert and Whetten, organizational identity originally entailed a static concept— that is, it was de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the collective perception of organizational members regarding the presumably central, distinctive, and relatively permanent features of the organization (Albert & Whetten, 1985). Following this conceptualization, most subsequent studies theorized identity as a <sup>fi</sup>xed characteristic, precluding the possibility that it might change over time (Gioia et al., 2000).

However, some researchers have acknowledged the potential dynamism attributable to organizational identity (e.g., Gioia et al., 2010, 2000; Hatch & Schultz, 2002). They suggest that identity can be seen as a work-in-progress: something <sup>fl</sup>uid, unstable, and changeable (Gioia et al., 2000). Identity work <sup>fi</sup>ts under this dynamic view, in that it involves activities concerned with reproducing and transforming identity (Alvesson & Willmott, 2002). Identity work has led to a fairly recent growing body of literature (Beech, 2008; Carroll & Levy, 2010; Sveningsson & Alvesson, 2003; Sveningsson & Larson, 2006). However, the literature on the process of identity work “remains a loosely af<sup>fi</sup>liated body of research that has yet to systematically tackle issues of identity construction” (Pratt, Rockmann, & Kaufmann, 2006: p. 238). Given that identity construction, as an organizational process, compels a dynamic view of identity, we consider this stream of literature next.

## 2.2. Identity as a dynamic proces

A dynamic conceptualization of identity begs the question: what causes identity to change, or to be created in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place? A review of the literature on organizational identity, which by de<sup>fi</sup>nition extends to units or groups within the organization (Terrion & Ashforth, 2002), points to several possible drivers of this process. Crises, such as layoffs, mergers, or acquisitions, are highly disruptive to a work unit in that they typically threaten the identity of the individuals or group affected by such events (Kovoor-Misra, 2009). Elsbach and Kramer (1996), for example, discussed how business schools <sup>fi</sup>nd their organizational identity threatened by a third-party ranking. Another example is found in Jacobs, Christe-Zeyse, Keegan, and Pólos (2008), who studied how changes in managerial practices of the German Police threatened this group's identity. In addition, and although not frequently covered in the literature, crises may also lead to untapped opportunities, such as in the case of a corporate spin-off (e.g., Corley & Gioia, 2004); a merger (e.g., Clark, Gioia, Ketchen, & Thomas, 2010); or the creation of a new unit, group, or department (e.g., Gioia et al., 2010).

But as He and Baruch (2009) argued, we need to go beyond exploring the “why” of organizational identity dynamics (i.e., the causes), and expand our work to gain insight into the “how” (i.e., the process). As mentioned earlier, the literature is particularly sparse in terms of identity creation, that is, the identity work process involved in constructing an organizational or group identity from birth onwards (Dhalla, 2007; Gioia et al., 2010; Pratt et al., 2006; Spyridonidis, Hendy, & Barlow, 2013). A commonality among the few studies with this focus is the macro perspective that they have adopted, privileging a managerial-centered approach, either by acknowledging impression management efforts or by taking an institutional perspective. For example, Czarniawska and Wolff (1998) studied two cases of organizational identity creation (i.e., two newly created universities) within an established organizational <sup>fi</sup>eld (i.e., higher education), emphasizing the view of the founders. Clegg, Rhodes, and Kornberger (2007) rather focused on a non-established organizational <sup>fi</sup>eld (e.g., business coaching), but similarly, favored the view of the principals of the coaching <sup>fi</sup>rms. Foreman and Parent (2008) considered organizational identity creation in the context of iterative organizations,<sup>1</sup> further developing the link between organizational identity creation and institutional theory and thus providing insight as to how institutional context directly affects the process. Yet other notable studies (e.g., Dhalla, 2007: Scott & Lane, 2000) and most recent ones (Foreman, Westgren, & Whetten, 2013: Spyridonidis et al., 2013) have tended to take this macro perspective, either by focusing on how senior managers strategically shape the birth and subsequent development of a new organization or group or by developing models delineating macro-level factors leading to the creation of an organizational or group identity. In essence, in these studies, the locus of identity creation resides in the institutional claims that explicitly articulate what the organization is and what it represents (Corley et al., 2006; Whetten, 2006); these studies take a social actor perspective, where organizational identity is considered as a feature or property that somehow resides in the focal organization (Gioia et al., 2010; Ravasi & Schultz, 2006).

However, whereas a macro perspective sheds light on the managerial and institutional forces on organizational identity creation, it is limited in terms of explaining how the interplay of such process actually occurs at the level of those individuals at the core of this organizational identity. In contrast, a micro perspective treats organizational identity as negotiated by organizational members; such a social constructionist perspective suggests that organizational identity should not only be considered as a phenomenon experienced by the people within the organization, but also perceived by outsiders (Corley et al., 2006). Hatch and Schultz (1997, 2000) are among those in support of this perspective, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally acknowledging how the perceptions of external stakeholders serve as a destabilizing force on organizational identity. As the discrepancy grows between how a group views itself (i.e., “who we are”) and how it perceives external stakeholders view it (i.e., “who they think we are,” also known as “construed organizational image”), motivation to align those perceptions grows stronger (Ashforth & Mael, 1989; Cheney, 1983). This process can be framed as dialectical, as it emphasizes colliding forces (“who we are” versus “who they think we are”) that compete against each other (Robey & Boudreau, 1999; Van De Ven & Poole, 1995).

To our knowledge, little research has taken a purely social constructionist perspective in the study of group identity work in an organizational context.<sup>2</sup> Such a lens positions the locus of organizational identity creation within the organizational members who embody this identity, and more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in the negotiations that these individuals enact with outsiders about who they are as a group. Only Gioia et al. (2010) considered both micro and macro in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the creation of an organizational identity, combining both the social actor and social constructionist points of view. Although they attempted to give equal treatment to both, their results do not shed much light on the aforementioned dialectic forces at play, i.e., identity versus image. Their resulting model is one that follows a life cycle motor of change, where the creation of a group identity is mapped according to a series of steps arranged in a stage-like fashion (Van De Ven & Poole, 1995). It does not delve into the dialectic forces underlying a social constructionist perspective, that is, the impact of the external stakeholders' image on the forming organizational identity.

Whereas the identity creation literature has not taken such a social constructionist perspective and acknowledged these dialectical forces, the identity change literature has frequently leveraged this perspective. Indeed, the works of Dutton and Dukerich (1991), Gioia et al. (2000), Elsbach and Kramer (1996), Gioia and Thomas (1996), Corley and Gioia (2004), and Ravasi and Schultz (2006), among others, examine the relational processes through which identity change occurs, and thus supply some grounds for understanding organizational identity formation since similar dynamics are involved (Gioia et al., 2010).

A model of identity change that encompasses concepts from this literature was suggested by Kovoor-Misra (2009), who proposed that threats, opportunities, and image-identity mismatches that trigger the need for identity change evoke different reactions, and thus give rise to different organizational identity change processes. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, threatening situations tend to cause groups to cling to the known idea of “who we are." while opportune situations impel groups to expand toward a vision of “who we could be." In other words, when there is an impetus for organizational identity to change, a group will likely attempt to resist change it perceives as a threat, whereas it will be inclined to act on a change it perceives as an opportunity. Moreover, when there is a misalignment between identity and image, which Kovoor-Misra refers to as “negative image” or “perceived identity gap,” the group will be more disposed to change its stance of resistance to one of enactment, shifting its focus from “who we are” to “who we could be” and pursuing dialectical work to change its identity. We are interested in leveraging the previous literature on both identity creation and change to answer, from a social constructionist perspective, our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question, asking: How does the dialectical process of group identity creation unfold over time?

## 2.3. IT and identity creation

Whereas many studies have explored information technology (IT) in relation to organizational change dynamics (e.g., Berente, Hansen, Pike, & Bateman, 2011; Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Orlikowski, 2000; Volkoff, Strong, & Elmes, 2007), fewer have examined IT in relation to identity. A meta-review of IT-identity studies published in six leading IT journals identi<sup>fi</sup>ed only 11 such manuscripts (Gal & Kjaergaard, 2009). A larger review targeting 30 IT journals between 1997–2007 singled out 25 articles that explored identity in an IT context (Nach & Lejeune, 2009).<sup>3</sup> While IT-identity research has further accumulated subsequent to these reviews, gaps still remain in the literature, particularly with respect to group identity creation. We delve into this literature next.

A common theme within the IT-identity literature investigates how technology can indirectly impact identity by changing work practices and roles. Works in this area span various IT artifacts, such as enterprise systems (Alvarez, 2008), electronic trading systems (Barrett & Walsham, 1999), health information systems (Korica & Molloy, 2010; Mishra, Anderson, Angst, & Agarwal, 2012; Van Akkeren & Rowlands, 2007), and scienti<sup>fi</sup>c technologies (Lamb & Davidson, 2005). On the one hand, this research stream emphasizes that IT may threaten identity by leading to the deskilling of professionals by, for example, rendering certain types of knowledge obsolete (Lamb & Davidson, 2005), removing job responsibilities (Alvarez, 2008), or introducing new clerical tasks (Van Akkeren & Rowlands, 2007). Mishra et al. (2012) also introduced the idea of “identity deterioration,” which re<sup>fl</sup>ects compromised autonomy and status as a result of IT implementations. On the other hand, research has also shown that IT can provide opportunities for job enhancement and reskilling by allowing professionals to develop new areas of unique knowledge and skills (Alvarez, 2008; Barrett & Walsham, 1999; Lamb & Davidson, 2005).

Technology can also directly impact one's identity as it is appropriated and interpreted by individuals— a less developed research area. Among this research stream is the work of Ravasi and Canato (2010), which shows how an organizational technology can function as an identity referent for the creation of organizational identity. Other work has taken this direct relationship between IT and identity a step further, conceptualizing IT as part of the extended self. For example, Nyberg (2009), in his study of call centers, <sup>fi</sup>nds that call center operators frequently see themselves as inseparable from their computers. Turkle (2011) also re<sup>fl</sup>ects on this idea, pointing to the extent to which some people “become” their devices. Stein et al.'s (2012) research encompasses much of the aforementioned work and also suggests that professional identity manifests itself in different patterns of IT use.

A third major theme with respect to IT-identity research centers on the “virtual self” and various manifestations of this concept within the context of online or virtual communities (Schultze & Orlikowski, 2010). These studies tend to focus primarily on how online communities in<sup>fl</sup>uence the virtual self, such as users' individual self-presentation (e.g., Kim, Chan, & Kankanhalli, 2012; Mehdizadeh, 2010; Schau & Gilly, 2003; Zeng & Wei, 2012; Zhao, Grasmuck, & Martin, 2008), identity deception (e.g., Donath, 1999), or identity shifting (e.g., Campbell, Fletcher, & Greenhill, 2009; McKenna & Bargh, 1998). However, upon closer inspection, these studies mainly focus on the concept of self-image as opposed to identity. In other words, these studies explore the manner in which users present their virtual selves and manage other users' impressions of their virtual selves. tapping at concepts of impression management and self-presentation (Goffman, 1959; Leary, 1995; Schlenker, 1980). These individual level concepts are akin to the organizational level concepts of corporate impression management and projected image (Alvesson, 1990; Bernstein, 1984; Gioia et al., 2000). Thus, whereas these works reinforce the notion that image is indeed relevant to understanding identity creation, and despite the fact that some of these studies claim to address the concept of identity creation (e.g., Fang & Neufeld, 2009; Salimkhan, Manago, & Green<sup>fi</sup>eld, 2010; Zhao et al., 2008), they do not actually focus on the concept of identity or the mechanisms underlying identity creation.

While this body of literature enhances our understanding of the multi-faceted impacts of information technology on the identity concept, it has largely been limited in terms of focus and approach. Indeed, most of this research has focused analysis at the individual level, neglecting group and organizational perspectives. Also, most of these studies apply cross-sectional methods, an important limitation because identity creation, as a process, is unlikely to exhibit a stagnant role of IT. Given the ubiquity of IT in the workplace, and in particular considering its potential to be a disruptive force leading to radical change (Lyytinen & Newman, 2008), a more nuanced understanding of IT's role in identity dynamics is warranted. Hence, an exploration of the group identity creation process within the context of IT should leverage a temporal lens and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally account for the group members' understandings and interpretations of technology (i.e., the sensemaking process) as they forge their new identity. Accordingly, our second research question asks: What is the role of information technology in the process of group identity creation?

## 3. Methodology

At the start of our study, because investigation into the relationship between the transformational potential of information technology and organizational identity was scant, particularly regarding how digitization of traditional work environments in<sup>fl</sup>uences identity creation, we selected an exploratory approach for the purpose of theory building in this area. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we conducted an in-depth longitudinal case study guided by the interpretive tradition of information systems studies (Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1993). Our methodological description echoes the structured-pragmatic-situational (SPS) approach proposed by Pan and Tan (2011) to increase the replicability of exploratory studies by making them more transparent. The discrete steps of the SPS methodology, particularly those comprising the “Framing Cycle” (conceptualizing the phenomenon, collecting and organizing initial data, and constructing/ extending the theoretical lens) and the “Augmenting Cycle” (con<sup>fi</sup>rming/validating data, selective coding, and theory-data-model alignment) can be mapped to the steps described in this section

Our research approach resembles that of prior qualitative researchers (Barrett & Walsham, 1999; Boudreau & Robey, 2005) who entered the <sup>fi</sup>eld with some knowledge of potentially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant literature. Allowing for researchers' prior exposure to a range of biases, including an awareness of extant theory and relevant literature, we adhered to a “Straussian” strain (Locke, 1996) of grounded theory research (Strauss & Corbin, 1990) throughout our data collection and analysis. In our case, we were initially inspired by works on organizational culture but very quickly observed that issues of organizational identity and image were prominent. Thus, we shifted our focus to organizational identity and image and, drawing upon literature in these areas, re<sup>fi</sup>ned our investigation and analysis accordingly.

## 3.1. Case description

The organizational unit selected for this study was a group of reference librarians within a university library at a large southern university in the United States. These librarians were selected to staff an Information Commons<sup>4</sup> Library (ICL), located in a new building that opened in the central part of campus during the summer of 2003.

In the late 1990s, the university faced pressures from a regional accrediting organization to increase the university's library and classroom space and upgrade computer labs. At the time, there were two main libraries available to students on campus: the North library, which served the arts and humanities, social sciences, and business, and the South library, which served the physical and life sciences. Thus, the University Librarian conceptualized a visionary facility, the ICL, which was designed to consist of “state-of-the-art” technology throughout the building and to serve as an electronic library space. When the ICL opened in 2003, it included over 500 workstations, 30 classrooms for general and specialized instruction, nearly 100 group study rooms, a coffee shop, and a “reading room” with a limited number of books from non-circulating gift collections.

This case was suited to address our research questions for several reasons. For one, the case involved a group of individuals with little shared history or prior sense of collective identity coming together in a digitized environment. Previous studies have examined how employees attempt to rede<sup>fi</sup>ne themselves in order to adapt to challenges to their identity introduced by the adoption of novel information technology in the workplace (e.g., Alvarez, 2008; Barrett & Walsham, 1999; Korica & Molloy, 2010; Lamb & Davidson, 2005; Mishra et al., 2012; Van Akkeren & Rowlands, 2007). However, none has done so in the context of a newly created group as it originates in an innovative, and thus unfamiliar, type of organizational setting. Furthermore, in the studied situation, the ICL embodies not just a new information system, but a radically digitized workplace. Finally, the close association of one of the researchers with the site<sup>5</sup> allowed for access to many then-current and former librarians associated with the ICL.

## 3.2. Data collection

Both primary and secondary data were collected for this study. Primary data collection in the forms of interviews and non-participant observation took place between 2007 and 2011. During that period, the team conducted 23 semi-structured interviews with 15 respondents, including original, former, and newly hired ICL librarians. All interviews were audio-recorded and later transcribed. To begin each interview, researchers asked the respondent to draw his/her former and current work environments; these graphical representations, which worked well as ice-breakers and were rich in underlying concepts, were included in the data analysis. Additional primary data comprised <sup>fi</sup>eld notes derived from non-participant observation of staff meetings and reference desk shifts in the ICL.

Secondary data were collected to capture a more complete picture of the relevant timeline associated with the identity creation process, comprising materials from the late 1990s (when the ICL was being conceptualized) through the end of 2011. Analysis of secondary data permitted a deeper exploration of the processes associated with the ICL librarians' image, as much of the secondary data re<sup>fl</sup>ected perceptions of external stakeholders. Examples of secondary data include press releases and news stories, patron surveys, focus group summaries related to the ICL, and staff meeting notes (see Table 1 for a complete list of the secondary data). In total, the researchers collected and analyzed over 1100 pages of materials (274 documents). Data collection ceased once theoretical saturation was reached.

## 3.3. Data analysis

All primary and secondary data were analyzed by undergoing three phases of coding: open, axial and selective (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). Our initial coding scheme underlying open coding was fairly generic (including high level codes related to culture and identity). but as we transitioned from open coding to axial coding, we immersed ourselves to a greater extent in the organizational identity literature (as identity issues were frequently brought up by our respondents), and re<sup>fi</sup>ned our coding scheme to include a greater variety of codes related to organizational identity and image literature. Examples of added codes include “construed external image” (Dutton & Dukerich, 1991; Dutton, Dukerich, & Harquail, 1994), “projected image” (Alvesson, 1990; Bernstein, 1984), “who we were,” “who we are,” “who we could be” (Gioia & Thomas, 1996; Kovoor-Misra, 2009), “threat,” “opportunity” (Kovoor-Misra, 2009), and “image-identity clash.” Within Atlas.ti software, we re-coded interview transcripts separately (i.e., for each interview, two of the three researchers proceeded to the open and axial coding) and then thoroughly deliberated any discrepancies until reaching consensus, while continually <sup>fi</sup>ne-tuning the coding scheme as our understanding of the identity creation mechanisms evolved. Our team engaged in several analytical discussions of the coded data to ensure accurate correspondence among observation, interpretation, and the evolving coding scheme. We commenced a second (in 2009) and third (in 2011) major iteration of data collection, revisiting original interviewees to ascertain the impact of several intervening years on experiences with (and perceptions of) the ICL environment. At this point, the intangible and untraceable yet critical components of creativity and imagination (Pozzebon, Petrini, de Mello, & Garreau, 2011) were again applied, this time in order to enable us to apply selective coding and decipher and conceptually frame the most interesting insights from our data.

Consistent with assumptions of interpretive research, we focused on the subjectively expressed thoughts and feelings of librarians regarding their experiences working in the ICL. Concurrent with the prospective longitudinal analysis of the ICL librarians' identity creation process, we retrospectively reconstructed the phenomenon's historical context in an attempt to more accurately grasp the identity creation process from the inception of the new digitized work environment. To facilitate the longitudinal analysis of the case, we developed a timeline of events following the framework proposed by Street & Ward (2012) and also integrated research activities into the timeline, such as when we conducted interviews and attended ICL staff meetings. By leveraging both primary and secondary data in conjunction with the timeline, we were able to triangulate our data and ensure that we were including the relevant sequence of events to capture the processes under study.

As per grounded theory convention, we tightly coupled data collection with data analysis, engaging in both phases simultaneously. As data analysis informed our evolving interpretation of the phenomena under scrutiny, we revisited extant data in light of new constructs derived from tangential literature, and honed subsequent sampling and analysis according to our evolving understanding of the mechanisms at work.

## 4. Results

We framed our results accordingly as two inter-related process models (Figs. 2 and 4) illustrating how the group of librarians in charge of the ICL created its identity over time, and how it appropriated technology throughout. First we describe how the group of librarians created their new identity by enacting provisional identities (i.e., trial identities that the librarians experimented with tentatively), in response to threats and opportunities, until settling into their current realized identity. We then elaborate on how librarians came to appropriate technology over time.

## 4.1. The process of group identity creation over time

## 4.1.1. Past realized identity: “librarians as custodians”

Prior to appointment to the new digital library environment, each of the librarians from our sample worked within traditional (but different) library environments, with ample print media. Within their respective unit, each of the librarians' organizational identity was then aligned with the image that the patrons had of them: librarians and patrons saw eye-to-eye as to who the librarians were and what they had to offer. Librarians were perceived, by others and themselves, as keepers of the physical library space and the print materials within this space. Many librarians' drawings of their traditional environmen depicted their co-presence with print resources. One librarian, when describing her drawing, noted:

“So the traditional library setting, we're basically surrounded by books… There are also a lot of computers too but for some reason I didn't think to include them in my picture.”<sup>6</sup>

As suggested, when librarians described their traditional library work environment, the mention of computers or technology was an afterthought. Another librarian explained:

“In a traditional print library, you're still seen somewhat as a custodian or a curator of the materials that are within that space.”

Although the labeling of librarians as custodians of the books, as indicated by the quote above, may be somewhat of a stereotype (Stoddart & Lee, 2005), it was, in this situation, how the librarians perceived themselves in their previous work environment, one which embodied “who they were,” or their past identity. Furthermore, they believed that patrons also viewed them the same way, as one librarian explained:

“People assume that you have books…but as far as other services or what we're willing to help with, they might not know.”

During the period preceding the ICL, librarians were custodians of the library space.

## 4.1.2. Digitization of the workplace: both a threat and opportunity

When, in 2003, the electronic ICL environment was created, the librarians quickly realized that they had no established organizational identity that de<sup>fi</sup>ned and explained them as a group. Although they all brought a legacy associated with their previous work environments, their old identity did not <sup>fi</sup>t well with the new, digitized environment. Moreover, the librarians were not all familiar with each other, and their traditional library backgrounds were mostly from different units or even institutions. Thus, an important challenge that quickly emerged had to do with the creation of a new organizational identity for their group:

“We did have to sit down and say, all right, what are we? What are we doing? Are we going to do this or are we going to do this? And everything here is kind of fluid.”

“And we've had to […] rethink our identities a bit. And some of it has been a little bit uncomfortable, you know.”

They were both excited and fearful of their new work environment, perceiving this transition as both an opportunity and a threat:

“Everyone was really excited about it!”

“I am very afraid that I'm going to forget how to use the print resources.”

To address the need for a new identity, the librarians started by enacting a provisional identity that re<sup>fl</sup>ected a shared vision of innovation among the members. At the same time, the librarians could not ignore the patrons' perceptions of them, which posed threats and opportunities to their provisional identities, prompting them to create new provisional identities as trials over time.

## 4.1.3. Provisional identity: “librarians as cutting edge”

The <sup>fi</sup>rst trial identity that the librarians assimilated identi<sup>fi</sup>ed them as “cutting edge.” They saw their role within their novel digitized space as one that would push innovation and new ideas. Soon after the building opened, the librarians began to give tours to outside groups, such as other universities' library departments looking to the ICL for ideas on how to improve their respective library spaces. The librarians emphasized how they were the <sup>fi</sup>rst university library group in the nation to open a physical library space with exclusive digital information resources and services. In this regard, the librarians originally saw themselves as thought leaders. One of the librarians explained:

“The original librarians who were chosen, there was some sort of idea of them being…I don't want to say technology savvy…I feel like, cutting edge.”

Another librarian described the group of librarians as “maverick” when they initially transitioned to the new environment. Thus, their <sup>fi</sup>rst provisional identity was that of being cutting edge and represented an ideal notion of “who they could be.”

4.1.4. Misaligned external image: “librarians as invisible”

However, following the digitization of the librarians' work environment, patrons largely ignored the librarians. Indeed, despite thousands of visitors to the ICL every day, patrons posed relatively few research questions to the librarians. The total number of in-depth reference questions and ready reference questions (i.e., quick, factual questions) were both less than four percent, according to reference desk statistics collected by the ICL librarians. Thus, patrons did not recognize that librarians were available in the ICL:

“People just did not see us that way [as librarians].”

“I have yet to come to look at the ICL as a library per se.” (patron)

Librarians sensed that they were not visible to the patrons. Surveys and focus groups conducted a year after ICL's opening corroborated this impression. Indeed, through these devices, the librarians learned that few patrons were aware that librarians and research help were available at the ICL; most patrons rather considered the North and South libraries as the prime place to conduct their research. The ICL librarians, accordingly, felt invisible:

“Also there has been […] this sort of vague idea that it's a library but where are the librarians?”

## 4.1.5. Provisional identity: “librarians as central”

The image re<sup>fl</sup>ected by the patron population, one that did not recognize the presence of the librarians at the ICL, was not acceptable:

“I don't think we're comfortable entirely disappearing physically and being only on call […] I think we'd all be pretty uncomfortable becoming invisible.”

The issue of visibility became a dominant point of discussion at the librarians' monthly meetings. Because their image did not align with their forward-looking provisional identity (cutting edge), they assumed a new provisional identity more reminiscent of their past, or “who they were” (in their previous, traditional library work environment), which placed librarians as a central resource in academia.

The librarians engaged in a few different initiatives in order to convince patrons that they were a central resource. Among these initiatives was the improvement of signage, with banners, <sup>fl</sup>yers, and name tags that better identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the librarians and the services they could offer. Banners used catch phrases to invite help requests from patrons (e.g., “Need Help? Ask Us!”). Flyers were posted on the elevators and bathrooms (where a temporary captive audience could be found), informing patrons of the services offered by the ICL librarians. Name tags were changed to include a clear label of “Reference Librarian.” In addition, librarians decided that, at the beginning of every semester, they would visit each class to conduct a one-minute “blitz,” that is, a condensed description of the services they offered within the ICL and an invitation to the students to tap into their library knowledge.

Furthermore, the librarians found that their location in the building, which was in the east wing rather than centrally situated, was quite problematic. Thus, they decided to move to a central location on the busiest <sup>fl</sup>oor of the ICL and built a new reference desk at this spot. The new reference desk was custom-made and imposing—a piece of furniture that was conspicuous. Thus, when patrons ascended the main staircase to the ICL, the newly constructed central reference desk staffed by librarians was the <sup>fi</sup>rst thing the patrons saw. The new location of the librarians restored the concept of centrality, which was enduring of their previous work environment.

## 4.1.6. Misaligned external image: “librarians as clerks”

These efforts were met with some success: the patrons acknowledged, in greater number, the librarians' presence, and the number of questions for the librarians increased. However, these questions, in vast majority, were either directional (e.g., “where are the bathrooms?”) or clerical (e.g., “could you help with the printer's paper jam?”). As illustrated in Fig. 3, the librarians' drawings of their new work environment often emphasized such clerical artifacts, such as printers, staplers, and copy machines:

![](/api/attachments/E547TZMC/fulltext/images/860195774b0285b85d6f7280caa20dbada32909b14e8cfa80a5ebbc473bd12da.jpg)  
Fig. 3. Drawings representing the ICL environment.

Not surprisingly, the librarians quickly became annoyed with this new perceived image that pictured them as clerks:

“Oh, God, I'm so sick of this, and if anybody asks me one more question about the copier, I'm going to scream. I'm so angry.”

This new image was not one that they were willing to adopt as their new identity; it con<sup>fl</sup>icted in a fundamental way with who they were and what they had to offer: their sense of worth. The perception of librarians as clerks was at odds with who they actually were:

“There's definitely an amount of swallowing your pride…refilling the stapler and doing things that you just normally wouldn't have to do…I had this sense of wasted talent and resources because you have a very highly educated person who spent six years of their life going to school to learn how to help people with sort of high level research, and then to sort of have no questions, except for, ‘Where's the stapler? Where's the copy machine?’”

## 4.1.7. Provisional identity: “librarians as ubiquitous”

Because the librarians were not willing to internalize the external image of themselves as clerks, they engaged in additional initiatives to clarify that they had much more to offer than providing directional and clerical assistance. They increased their marketing efforts to not only make themselves more visible, but more importantly, to make sure that the patrons knew about the services they could provide. In order to achieve this end, the librarians put forth efforts that simultaneously tapped at “who they could be” and “who they were.”

To break free from their traditional workspace and put emphasis on “who they could be,” the librarians decided to put greater efforts into venturing into the patrons' space: online. Social media was thus considered, and in 2007, the librarians created an ICL Facebook. Realizing that a Facebook group without “fans” was useless, a librarian was asked to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to increase the fan base group, so as to reach out to more individuals:

“I wanted to recruit people to be our Facebook fans, which I did, and how I did that was an in-person thing. I basically… For a week, I just sat there and during class change times, I would try to overcome my reticence and get people to sign up to be our Facebook fans right then on our laptop…”

During the same time period, librarians also created a Twitter account, updated a digital library blog, and adopted an instant messaging program<sup>7</sup> to increase reference questions online. Acknowledging students' love affair with computer games, librarians also created “Wii<sup>8</sup> Study Break” sessions in 2008. These sessions were of<sup>fi</sup>cially created to allow students to take a break from studying and to unwind while playing Wii games and sports; unof<sup>fi</sup>cially, they were meant to attract more students to the ICL, give them an opportunity to meet with librarians, and increase their awareness about ICL being a library (e.g., a study break poster claimed “Wii Love the Library!”). A librarian re<sup>fl</sup>ected on the <sup>fi</sup>rst instance of the Wii study break:

“It was more of a social thing; get people in here, get people to see us, that sort of thing. It didn't really have any concrete, learning objectives for that, you know, just have a little fun. I was really worried that nobody was going to come.”

Because these initiatives involved modern technologies, patrons were at ease with them. These initiatives were, however, fairly novel for most librarians, because they departed from the traditional work environment familiar to them. Librarians were thus challenged in novel ways.

In addition to engaging in novel initiatives that were forward-looking, the librarians simultaneously spearheaded efforts that were expressive of “who they were.” For example, epitomizing their past traditional work environment, the book was given back some of its symbolic status. On the <sup>fi</sup>rst Wednesday of every month, a rolling cart that held about a hundred books was stationed by the coffee shop, the busiest area of the ICL. Staffed by librarians, this cart included a variety of books that had been selected as being “good reads.” The idea was to send a message to patrons that librarians were available in the ICL.

Furthermore, the librarians chose to spend more time in the classroom to bolster their traditional teaching role with patrons. In fact, the librarians found themselves seeking more teaching opportunities in their new digitized environment. One librarian explained:

“And the teaching role has become maybe the biggest change in all of this. We've always taught classes. A lot of times they'd be one fifty minute session where you're trying to cram in as much as possible about how to search a database…But now we've done a lot to try to expand our role to add on more classes where we can get into things into more depth and emphasize critical thinking and the practice of using the sources for a particular assignment rather than just someone lecturing for fifty minutes on how you use a database.”

Another approach the librarians implemented was “roving,” a trend in reference librarianship that gained popularity in the 1990s (Rettig, 1996). Roving reference is a service method in which a librarian walks around a computer lab and subtly peeks at students' computer screens, and proactively offers help when someone appears to be struggling with a library resource. Following this proven approach, the librarians commenced roving within the ICL.

Thus, the librarians put forth concerted efforts re<sup>fl</sup>ecting both “who they could be” and “who they were” in order to engage more heavily with their patrons. Primarily, they increased their teaching and social activities with patrons to boost face-to-face interactions and, most notably, chose to venture into the Web 2.0 environment (e.g., Meebo chat reference, Twitter, Facebook) to interface virtually with thei patrons.

“We have literally stepped out from behind the desk, stepped into the classroom more in different ways, stepped into a chat environment or a Facebook environment.”

By engaging in both new and old modes of interactions with their patrons, the librarians' presence and the services they offered became more ubiquitous

## 4.1.8. Misaligned external image: “librarians as cybrarians”

Though the ICL librarians' provisional identity tapped at both past and forward-looking practices, the patrons only took note of the virtual practices that the librarians instantiated. The initiatives that attempted to engage patrons in a traditional, face-to-face manner proved mostly unsuccessful. For example, regarding the book mobile initiative that spanned approximately half a year, one ICL librarian expressed how the project “completely bombed” one month. Another librarian explained,

“The book mobile…it was marginally successful really…I don't think we'll do it again.”

Furthermore, the ICL librarians halted their roving reference service after <sup>fi</sup>nding that it did not yield the desired results.

“It turned out with doing the roving reference that people were working on their computers and they might be on Amazon or WebCT or just looking at something else for fun…You could perceive or pick up that they might have thought of it a little bit of an invasion of their privacy.”

On the other hand, the librarians' virtual reference efforts were readily embraced by patrons, especially once the librarians began using Meebo for their online chat tool.

“I find the librarians to be top notch. VERY helpful. I love the ability to AIM the librarians.”(patron, 2010\_LIBQUAL)

“Nine times out of ten, I have easy access to a librarian's assistance. I have especially used this through the Gchat/AIM feature.” (patron, 2010 LIBQUAL)

The patrons increasingly recognized the librarians' virtual presence as reference librarians. While the librarians appreciated the opportunity to reach patrons via virtual channels, they were not ready to embrace a fully virtual identity. One librarian explained that the label “cybrarian” did not fully resonate with the identity of the ICL librarians and only re<sup>fl</sup>ected one dimension of their group identity:

“…It [the term cybrarian] just sounds sort of faddy!”

“I would think that cybrarian kind of describes at least the sort of virtual reference portion of my job…”

Other librarians concurred that they viewed their virtual reach to patrons as a necessary part of their new role in the ICL, but they still valued their physical reach and interactions with patrons. Thus, they were still in search of a way to assimilate their physical role within the ICL into the new identity they were forging as a group.

## 4.1.9. New realized identity: “librarians as techno-present”

After the librarians had undergone several provisional identities, they learned that efforts representative of “who they could be” were consistently more successful compared to efforts reminiscent of “who they were.” Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, patrons were most receptive to initiatives geared toward integrating technology into reference services. In this vein, the librarians decided to shift their face-to-face services to providing tangible technology resources to students while continuing their virtual reference services in parallel.

To achieve their goals, the librarians submitted grant proposals (which were later awarded) that allowed them to provide technical resources to patrons, such as a digital media lab and tablets (e.g., iPads and Kindles) that patrons could borrow. They explained:

“The Kindles, they just lap them up. I think that's that they wanted; that's what they needed. They wanted something that they could read their books on.”

“Doing stuff like the digital media lab…it's more like supplementing the electronic focus and really beefing that up…If they're [patrons] going to see us as just an electronic resource, well then, let's do more electronic things.”

The librarians also sought grant funding for other types of multimedia (e.g., <sup>fl</sup>ip cameras and audio recording devices) to lend to patrons. Thus, they shifted their services in relation to physical artifacts to focus on technology rather than physical books in their face-to-face roles as digital librarians. At the same time, they offered reference services using technology-mediated solutions such as web chat and online courseware, to continue in their role as cybrarians. In this regard, they settled into a new, realized identity of being “techno-present” librarians, which is characterized by offering physical technology services in a face-to-face ICL environment while at the same time providing interactive, reference services in a virtual environment.

## 4.2. The role of IT in the process of group identity creation

Given the central role of technology in the group identity creation process, we speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examined how the librarians appropriated and made sense of technology in constructing their group identity. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest three key perspectives of technology that unfolded over time, as shown in Fig. 4.

## 4.2.1. Technology as a tool

An important factor underpinning the conceptualization of the ICL in the early 2000s was found in the patrons' reference and search behaviors in traditional library environments. Increasingly, patrons moved away from browsing the shelves and pulling print resources and began looking toward electronic resources for reference. This observation helped to inspire the vision of the ICL as a completely electronic library space.

Accordingly, the ICL librarians viewed technology as a tool to provide reference assistance and support to patrons. When the ICL opened, the librarians oversaw use of three of the four smart instructional lab classrooms. The purpose of these labs was to teach patrons research skills and software competencies (committee notes, 6-19-2002). Initially, the librarians had put in place a new service called the “software for students” seminar. These were individualized workshops that targeted speci<sup>fi</sup>c software learning needs, such as online courseware, library databases, and web search engines.

“And so yeah, we wanted them to be aware that they could get help on these databases, in these catalogs…using EndNote, using anything for their research projects that they could get that help at [the ICL], whether it was virtually or face-to-face that it was available for them.”

In this respect, technology was perceived as extrinsic to the ICL librarians. The role of the librarians was to teach patrons how to use technology tools to achieve their research goals.

## 4.2.2. Technology as a tie

During the <sup>fi</sup>rst year of the ICL's operation, it became apparent, as discussed earlier, that patrons were not aware that librarians were available in the ICL to assist them with their reference needs. In fact, the ICL librarians discontinued their software workshop offerings because the sessions were not well attended by patrons. Because patrons viewed the librarians <sup>fi</sup>rst as invisible and then as clerks (Fig. 2), the librarians revised their perspective of technology and envisaged technology as a way to tie them to their patrons. Most prominently, the librarians relied on their online chat service to provide virtual reference to patrons, but they also used other Web 2.0 media and later implemented a text-a-librarian service to reach patrons as well.

“Some of us have done things within WebCT where we're kind of partnered with a class. We're looking at doing wiki's and blogs for a class to where people can discuss what problems they've come up with and we can communicate with them that way. Students are using chat and emailing; we're going to have to move on to text messaging soon on our cell phones.”

“In chat, you know, you're still flexing the reference muscle; you're still answering questions. You're still helping people with research.”

The librarians' technology-mediated initiatives became integral to their efforts to establish ties with patrons. Because the chat reference service became such a key responsibility of the ICL librarians, they appointed one of their librarians to be a chat coordinator. In this role, she coordinated the archiving of chat transcripts, collecting chat interaction statistics, and training librarians on effective chat communication. At every monthly meeting, there was a group discussion on how to improve the librarians' chat reference service. At this point, the ICL librarians shifted their view of technology as merely a tool and instead saw technology as the primary mechanism with which to interact with patrons.

## 4.2.3. Technology as a trademark

Though the librarians' technology-mediated communications with their patrons were very successful, a core dimension of the librarians' identity was to interface with patrons in person, in their physical library work environment. However, for several years in the ICL, the librarians' face-to-face services primarily re<sup>fl</sup>ected their past identity (e.g., waiting for patron questions at the reference desk, implementing the bookmobile, roving reference, etc.) and never fully resonated with their patrons. After trialing many provisional identities that invoked this notion of “who they were” in their past traditional role as librarians, the librarians settled into a group identity in which technology embodied “who they are,” both in face-to-face and virtual interactions with patrons. In their physical work environment, the librarians turned to providing physical technology resources to patrons, rather than waiting for patrons to seek their assistance with electronic resources. This shift in their technology service offering proved to be very successful with patrons. The ICL librarians lent laptops, iPads, and Kindles to patrons and offered social outlets to patrons through initiatives such as Wii study break sessions. Furthermore, the librarians spearheaded the efforts toward creating a digital media lab for patrons. This lab was equipped with high speed computers, multimedia and web development software, and a soundproof audio-recording booth for patrons to work on multimedia projects. Thus, technology became a trademark, a core identity marker, of the ICL librarians.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Group identity creation process

Our results shed light on the identity work underlying the process of group identity creation that librarians experienced as they shifted from a traditional work environment to a digital one, giving special attention to the misalignment between how they saw themselves and how patrons saw them. Based on these results, we provide insight into our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question, which asked how the process of group identity creation unfolded over time. We <sup>fi</sup>rst summarize this identity creation process, then conceptualize it in a model.

The case data illustrates how, from a past realized identity where both librarians and patrons understood librarians as resource custodians, the transition to a new, digitized work environment led to threats and opportunities that librarians had to address. Attending to these threats and opportunities meant that the librarians had to try new group identities and compare and contrast them to the external images that patrons reflected back at them. For example, whereas the librarians' initial focus was forward looking, leveraging “who they could be” and portraying themselves as “cutting edge,” the patrons' external image—librarians as invisible—did not align with this identity. This mismatch, in turn, meant that this identity was only provisional, as it required revision. Librarians then adopted a completely different focus, reminiscent of “who they were” in their previous role of traditional librarians; they improved their visibility and positioned themselves centrally within the new building, such that patrons had to notice them as they went to the ICL and used its resources. However, patrons came to consider librarians as clerks, undervaluing their skills and expertise; librarians had to react to this con<sup>fl</sup>icting image, again. They elected to become more ubiquitous, i.e., to be more present in both the virtual and the physical arenas, inspired from both past work practices (“who they were”) and novel ones (“who they could be”). Another round of adjustments was deemed necessary as the new image re<sup>fl</sup>ected by the patrons, i.e., librarians as cybrarians, was not quite comfortable to the librarians. Librarians' identity morphed into one that we call “techno present;” this is how the group of librarians eventually came to think of itself (“who we are”) in its work environment. “Techno present” described how librarians secured their role at both providing technology services in a face-to-face environment and servicing reference questions in a virtual environment. Rather than provisional. this new identity became realized. as it was aligned with the external image that patrons ended up construing about the group of librarians.

This social constructionist rendition of the identity creation process of the group of librarians staf<sup>fi</sup>ng the ICL provides a rich account of the influence of the patrons' construed external image on this dynamic. It departs from previous research on group identity creation in its emphasis on the sensemaking process that underlies the social construction of organizational identities, as negotiated among organizational members and subjected to periodic revisions in light of outsider's perspective. To date, research on organizational identity creation has mainly taken a social actor perspective, according to which identity claims are based on organizational self-de<sup>fi</sup>nitions proposed by organizational leaders (e.g., Clegg et al., 2007; Czarniawska & Wolff, 1998; Dhalla, 2007; Foreman & Parent, 2008; Foreman et al., 2013; Scott & Lane, 2000; Spyridonidis et al., 2013), or in one case, a balanced approach (e.g., Gioia et al., 2010) in which social actor and social constructionist perspectives were considered together. Our research <sup>fi</sup>lls a gap in that it is the only study that employs a purely social constructionist perspective to shed light on the dialectical interplay between identity and image as underlying group identity creation.

Conceptualizing from both our empirical data and the literature on organizational identity and identity work, we propose a theoretical model representing this dynamic of identity creation. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our model is inspired by another, the Kovoor-Misra (2009) model (Fig. 1) presented earlier. Our reconceptualization indeed incorporates some concepts of this early model. However, our revised model, presented in Fig. 5, suggests important distinctions, given its focus on identity creation rather than identity change.

A <sup>fi</sup>rst aspect of our model that deserves discussion is the fact that previous literature on identity dynamics typically refers to a threat (e.g., Ravasi & Schultz, 2006) or an opportunity (e.g., Corley & Gioia, 2004) as the element triggering the need for identity change or creation. Whereas past research has focused on one or another, it is possible for change situations to be characterized as both threatening and opportune at the same time (Kovoor-Misra, Clair, & Bettenhausen, 2001). For example, when a new digital environment triggers the need for identity creation, the concepts of threat and opportunity are confounded. Indeed, as we saw in this case, we cannot unambiguously classify the digitization of the library environment as either a threat or an opportunity; librarians perceived their new work environment as both, concurrently. Other studies related to identity creation acknowledge the same idea. For example, the identity formation process described by Gioia et al. (2010) recognizes the opportunity of founding a distinctive new college, but also highlights how participants from this effort experienced a meanings void and attempted to <sup>fi</sup>ll such void—a very uncomfortable, threatening position. As Foreman and Parent (2008) describe it, disruption often serves as a catalyst for renewal, such that “there is a sort-of yin and yang about [it]: in certain ways it creates opportunities for the organization, and yet in many other ways it creates risks or presents a potential threat” (p. 226). Our model, instead of representing threat and opportunity as individual, separate concepts (as done in the Kovoor-Misra, 2009), rather combines them, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting that the trigger leading to the need for a new group identity imbeds seeds of both opportunities and threats.

![](/api/attachments/E547TZMC/fulltext/images/c659f528d6c4fbfeb6446e2394657e6be30a2bd8863e15dc3fe1f48b517955a9.jpg)  
Fig. 5. IT-driven identity creation framework

Another important difference between our model and Kovoor-Misra's model is the absence of the “identity continuation” concepts; obviously, when a group identity must be created, there is nothing to continue, as nothing existed to start with. In a similar vein is the impossibility for a new group to attempt to maintain “who we are” as a response to a threat; again, if a group did not start with a given identity, it has nothing to preserve. In our framework, the concept of “who we are” rather becomes the end point, the state where the group identity is <sup>fi</sup>nally realized, when there is alignment between how the group perceives itself and the outsiders' construed image.

Moreover, what Kovoor-Misra's (2009) original model did not represent well is that the progression toward a novel identity often implies a period during which a group experiences “identity ambiguity,” wherein it <sup>fi</sup>nds itself without a <sup>fi</sup>rm sense of who it is. Similarly, a discrepancy between external image and internal identity, combined with a desire to minimize this discrepancy, will lead a group to experience identity ambiguity. When facing identity ambiguity, groups often resolve this uncertainty by changing their identity through a collective sensemaking process (Corley & Gioia, 2004), that is, by “trying” provisional identities. One can think of a group's “provisional identity”<sup>9</sup> as the group level equivalent to the concept of “provisional self” at the individual level (Ibarra, 1999), which serves as trials for possible, but not yet fully elaborated, identities. These interim group identities <sup>fi</sup>ll a gap as a group strives to decide its de<sup>fi</sup>ning characteristics. In this case study, we observed how librarians went from trying to be “cutting edge,” to “central,” and then “ubiquitous,” before <sup>fi</sup>nally settling on a group identity that became stable (“techno-present”), as it was aligned with the patrons' construed image. Provisional identities, resulting from the dialectic between identity and image, are thus to be expected as a group faces identity ambiguity. Because these provisional identities can be numerous, we represent them via a stack of multiple rectangular symbols.

Our proposed model also differs in terms of how we position the concept of “who we could be” and add the concept of “who we were.” Whereas being able to describe “who we are” (i.e., a realized identity) is the goal, we suggest that the concepts of “who we were” and “who we could be” represent tentative actions that are enacted as a means to reach that goal. These are also called liminal actions, in that they are tentative, or provisionally tried out (Gioia et al., 2010; Van Gennep, 1960). These actions can be of two time orientations. i.e., past or future. For example. in this case, past liminal actions were those reminiscent of the features of a traditional library environment (e.g., positioning of the reference desk, displaying of books, etc.), and future liminal actions embraced novel ideas (e.g., using social media, promoting computer games, etc.). Whereas liminal actions can sometimes be enacted exclusively from one another (i.e., work practices leveraging either past of novel practices, but not both), at other times, they can rather be used in conjunction. In our new model, we show these two concepts being leveraged (sometimes together, sometimes separately) when provisional identities are being tried out by the group. This is consistent with previous literature in which past practices have been shown to operate often as a force opposing future possibilities (Robey & Boudreau, 1999), leading to another type of dialectic underlying identity creation.

The Kovoor-Misra (2009) model also indicates that the perception of a negative external imagine will pressure a group to adopt a future orientation and devise strategies of “who we could be” in order to change its identity. For the creation of a group identity, however, we suggest that it is not the negative aspect of the image that in<sup>fl</sup>icts pressures towards change, but the fact that, as a provisional identity is assessed in light of its external image, it may con<sup>fl</sup>ict with it, that is, there may be misalignment between identity and image. Whereas an image may sometimes be negatively perceived (such as when the librarians were considered as invisible), it can also be neutral, or even positive (such as when the librarians were perceived as “cybrarians” by the patrons—an image that was con<sup>fl</sup>icting with what librarians thought of themselves, but not necessarily negative in itself). Thus, we deem “image con<sup>fl</sup>ict” as a better quali<sup>fi</sup>er than “negative image” in the context of identity creation. Moreover, not only will this image con<sup>fl</sup>ict lead to the establishment of a new provisional identity (through the enactment of limina actions, as discussed earlier), but in turn, this provisional identity will invite a new external image to be construed, and thus, new instantiations of image con<sup>fl</sup>ict will potentially emerge. We posit this dialectic between provisional identities and image con<sup>fl</sup>ict at the heart of our model of group identity creation.

In sum, our proposed model of group identity creation, while leveraging concepts from studies of organizational identity change, provides a theoretical contribution in terms of highlighting the dual dialectical processes (one between provisional identities and image con<sup>fl</sup>ict, another between past and future orientations) underlying the social constructionist dynamic resulting in a group's realized identity. The role played by information technology within this dynamic is discussed next.

## 5.2. Technology identification process

Our second research question asked about the role of information technology in the process of group identity creation. Our results show that the group members' appropriation and sensemaking of technology changed over time as they constructed their group identity. Early on, technology was perceived by the ICL librarians as a tool to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll a particular objective, such as increasing patrons' competency with certain software or showing them how to make best use of a database. As a tool, technology was external to the librarians' identity, and considered as a means to an end. However, as patrons' familiarity with technology became more apparent to librarians, the librarians decided to “go where the patrons were” and made greater use of technology to support online communications with them, either synchronously (via chat referencing) or asynchronously (via email, wikis, and blogs). The librarians also realized that these technologies could serve not only to support communications, but also to further support their teaching of research skills. At that point, technology was not only perceived as “just a tool”—a means to an end—but also as a tie, a way to connect to a greater number of patrons wherever they were located, at whatever time of the day. Eventually, as librarians reached their realized identity, one of being “techno-present,” their identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with technology further evolved. Technology became part of who the librarians were; that is, they could hardly disentangle it from their group identity. Of course, technology did not stop serving as tool and tie, as librarians still had to instruct patrons on how to use software and databases, and still communicated with them via digital means. However, librarians, as a group, came to intrinsically identify with technology at a deeper level than before.

Leveraging a sensemaking lens, we suggest framing these results as a “technology identi<sup>fi</sup>cation process,” through which librarians' interpretations of technology were entwined with their creation of a group identity. Identification is a concept closely related with identity and is defined as “the process by which people come to de<sup>fi</sup>ne themselves, communicate that de<sup>fi</sup>nition to others, and use that de<sup>fi</sup>nition to navigate their lives, work-wise or other” (Ashforth et al., 2008, p. 334). The concept of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is usually referenced in relation to a collective, such as organizational identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, workgroup identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, or professional identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (e.g., Vough, 2012), and re<sup>fl</sup>ects the process of identifying with that particular collective (Ashforth et al., 2008). Most studies that have incorporated the concept of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation as it relates to technology have indeed used it this way, i.e., referring to or belonging to a given group. For example, in a study on teleworking, Bartel, Wrzesniewski, and Wiesenfeld (2012) found that virtual work leads to higher perceptions of isolation among employees and, hence, lowers organizational identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Sivunen and Valo (2006) proposed strategies for fostering identi<sup>fi</sup>cation in virtual teams, such as giving positive feedback and emphasizing common goals. Similarly, with respect to online communities, Ren et al. (2012) found that increasing bond-based attachment enhances identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the community.

We adapt the term identi<sup>fi</sup>cation to refer to how groups identify with technology and appropriate it into their identity de<sup>fi</sup>nition. Further, we suggest the technology identi<sup>fi</sup>cation process re<sup>fl</sup>ects a gradual shift (paralleling the librarians' identity creation process) from information technology de<sup>fi</sup>ning the librarians' work practices to becoming the librarians' core identity marker. This entanglement between the self and the technology, in which IT becomes part of an extended self, has been acknowledged within the realm of virtual worlds; investigations into “avatar identi<sup>fi</sup>cation” have shown the more an avatar matches the physical appearance of an individual, the more the individual will identify and feel a sense of oneness with the avatar (Suh, Kim, & Suh, 2011). Avatar identi<sup>fi</sup>cation has also been suggested to vary on a continuum from segmentation to complete integration (Schultze & Leahy, 2009). Outside virtual communities, the work of Stein et al. (2012) empirically illustrates how enterprise systems can become part of one's identity, and the work of Nyberg (2009) supports the same idea in the context of call centers. Entanglement with technology, in all of these works, is represented as an end-point, that is, as a feature of a socio-technical understanding of the relationship between technology and its users.

Our proposed technology identi<sup>fi</sup>cation process rather emphasizes the dynamic via which technology identi<sup>fi</sup>cation occurs: IT does not become part of a group's identity because of the technology's characteristics or functionality, but as a result of a long process (highlighting the time requirement) through which a group of individuals makes sense of the technology, overlaying the process of group identity creation. Indeed, we saw that the librarians' identity creation process involved many liminal actions pertaining to technology (especially those attached to a future time orientation, i.e., “who we could be”). Through these actions, librarians were able to develop the necessary expertise and level of comfort with a set of technologies that, slowly but surely, allowed them to incorporate the technologies into their group identity.

Moreover, most aforementioned studies have emphasized the individual's appropriation of technology, acknowledging how IT can become part of an individual's self-identity. The proposed technology identi<sup>fi</sup>cation process “scales up” to describe the relationship between IT and a larger social collective, which is an under-explored area within the studying of socio-technical systems (Robey, Anderson, & Raymond, 2013). Indeed, how IT can be appropriated, either as a tool, a tie, or a trademark, was a group phenomenon in that each of these three perspectives was shared by all librarians within the group of interest. We argue that such aggregated level of attention allows for the model to be more parsimonious, and thus potentially more useful, than if individually based.

## 6. Conclusion

In this research, we considered how a collective of workers made sense of a new, digital work environment and created its group identity over time. The results of our study clearly demonstrate that this group's identity work was greatly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by how outsiders perceived these group members in their work environment, and how these group members tried out provisional identities over time. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also show that technology can embody both threats and opportunities to identity and provide occasions for groups to rethink, fundamentally, who they are. In doing so, groups draw upon past and future orientations (“who we were” and “who we could be”) in creating a new group identity (“who we are”) that aligns with their external image. We also suggested a technology identi<sup>fi</sup>cation process that conceptually shows how the group appropriated technology over time, delineating perspectives of technology as a tool, as a tie, and as a trademark.

Our study offers contributions to both research and practice. On the research front, our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to engage in a longitudinal inquiry to explore, over time (in our case, over the span of 13 years), the role of IT in the process of group identity creation within an organizational context. As many organizations change their business models to become completely digital, their organizational members need to adapt their work practices over time, which is intertwined with how they perceive themselves. Describing this process at a conceptual level allows us to better understand its underlying mechanisms. Thus, our study informs the IT-identity literature by responding to recent calls to investigate identity dynamics by employing a process research perspective (Gioia, 2012; Pratt, 2012).

Furthermore, our study informs practice by showing that IT-driven workplace transitions, especially those that fundamentally alter work practices, can impel work groups to forge completely new identities. This process of group identity creation can challenge the group, and the group members may struggle to de<sup>fi</sup>ne who they are as a collective. In these cases, organizations should consider allowing the referent group to keep certain linkages with its past identity (“who we were”) while at the same time fostering a future orientation of the desired new identity (“who we could be”). This approach could facilitate the group's efforts to establish a new identity.

As with all research, our study has its limitations. For one, we focused on a single case. However, while our case study investigated a group of academic librarians in an information commons library, workplace digitization is a phenomenon that has diffused across many industries and professional roles, and <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our study could inform these other contexts. At the same time, future research should explore the processes of identity-image dialectic and technology identi<sup>fi</sup>cation in other technology-rich work contexts (e.g., medicine, journalism, education, etc.) to ascertain the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in other settings.

Moreover, as we considered image as an important component of group identity creation, we primarily focused on one type of outsiders re<sup>fl</sup>ecting this image: patrons, and more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, student patrons. The point of view of other types of outsiders was either minimally represented (e.g., faculty) or completely absent (e.g., other academic units, librarians from other institutions, staff, etc.). As the largest group of outsiders, students were the main audience targeted within the focus groups, surveys, and LIBQUAL summaries; as such, we relied on this secondary data to comprehend how librarians were perceived. In fact, our reliance on secondary data to get this outsiders' perspective constitutes another limitation of our research; interviewing students directly might have provided richer insight into their perspective. However, our assessment of the secondary data made available to us was that it was not only voluminous but also authentic in that it included candid student feedback about the librarians.

An additional noteworthy limitation has to do with our neglect of the macro level perspective on the institution's adoption of an ICL. A focus on both macro and micro level perspectives, shedding light on not only the sensemaking process but also the sensegiving one, would have led to more extensive <sup>fi</sup>ndings (and likely different resulting models), but would have also commanded a much more involved research process and resulting paper. Given that macro level in<sup>fl</sup>uences were already quite well covered in the literature, we elected to focus on the micro level.

Future work should continue the exploration of identity creation using longitudinal inquiry but should also collect primary data from multiple stakeholder perspectives. Further, we suggest a need to investigate the dialectical interplay between image and identity and its in<sup>fl</sup>uence on identity dynamics in different industries, such as <sup>fi</sup>nance, publishing, and medicine. Given the widespread transition from physical to digital media and services in the workplace, the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of this type of change is critical to researchers concerned with its implications on identity and, increasingly, on the technology identi<sup>fi</sup>cation process. Our study takes the next step in contributing to this important phenomenon.
