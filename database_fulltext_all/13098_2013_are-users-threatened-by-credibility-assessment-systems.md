Abstrac t: Despite the improving accuracy of agent-based expert systems, human expert users aided by these systems have not improved their accuracy. Self-affirmation theory suggests that human expert users could be experiencing threat, causing them to act defensively and ignore the system’s conflicting recommendations. Previous research has demonstrated that affirming an individual in an unrelated area reduces defensiveness and increases objectivity to conflicting information. Using an affirmation manipulation prior to a credibility assessment task, this study investigated if experts are threatened by counterattitudinal expert system recommendations. For our study, 178 credibility assessment experts from the American Polygraph Association (n = 134) and the European Union’s border security agency Frontex (n = 44) interacted with a deception detection expert system to make a deception judgment that was immediately contradicted. Reducing the threat prior to making their judgments did not improve accuracy, but did improve objectivity toward the system. This study demonstrates that human experts are threatened by advanced expert systems that contradict their expertise. As more and more systems increase integration of artificial intelligence and inadvertently assail the expertise and abilities of users, threat and self-evaluative concerns will become an impediment to technology acceptance.

The ancient Chinese measured changes in saliva excretion by having suspects chew on rice during questioning [19]. If the rice remained dry, it was considered a physiological cue of deception. Information technology has evolved over the years to aid humans in making complex decisions. Nonetheless, the technology required to aid credibility decisions is significantly more advanced than traditional information systems. Credibility assessment systems rely on artificial intelligence or agents to evaluate context, situation, and multiple sensors to render a unique recommendation. In contrast to traditional information systems based on business process automation, agent-based systems make knowledge-based recommendations and exhibit human characteristics such as rationality, intelligence, autonomy, and environmental perception [20]. The ability for agents to adapt and learn from new information makes them ideal for dealing with a phenomenon as complex and diverse as judging credibility.

## Credibility Assessment Technology Acceptance

Whenever a new system is introduced to an organization, there is always the danger that it will not be adopted by users. Assessing credibility is a complex task on which humans perform poorly without aid, and technological tools have been shown to improve accuracy [8, 11]. Considerable time and resources could be spent on developing new technologies for deception detection, but if new users reject the technology, then the effort is for naught. When users are particularly committed to a system, it makes the adoption of new technologies even more difficult. Although some research has demon strated that professionals are not much better than novices at detecting deception [18], Jensen et al. [12] found that novices with no prior training in deception detection were more open to trying a new system and allowing it to influence their judgments than were professionals (Secret Service, police, Internal Revenue Service investigators, and agents at the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms, and Explosives).

Much of the research in the area of technological adoption centers around Davis’s [6] technology acceptance model (TAM). The TAM predicts the probability that a new system will be adopted by examining the mediating role that perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness play in determining both the behavioral intention of adopting a new system and the actual use of the new system. Although the model has been revised, a meta-analysis by Legris et al. [13] suggested that in addition to perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness there are as many as 22 factors that affect adoption and should be included in studies testing the TAM. Venkatesh [17] suggested that one’s perceptions about computers and experience using them can also play a role in adoption of a new system.

The TAM can help explain how polygraph experts can be trained to use a new system as well. Polygraph has received a great deal of scrutiny over the past two decades, and critics of the polygraph, and the control question technique in particular, suggest that the accuracy of an examination is dependent on the examiner’s skill and perception, and as a result, has been ruled by many U.S. courts not to meet the Daubert evidentiary standard necessary to be used as evidence [9, 19]. Experts trained in polygraph, who have spent their careers training and using the device, are particularly sensitive to these criticisms and may be defensive when new technologies are offered that purport to improve upon or replace the polygraph.

## Limitations of the Technology Acceptance Model

During their 32-month field investigation on the transition of a group support system (GSS) aboard the USS Coronado, Briggs et al. [2] identified the limitations of the TAM for predicting the overall success of a technology transition. The result of their investigation was the technology transition model (TTM), which augmented the TAM from an individual to a group or organizational context. The TTM provided explanations for the social and political obstacles to technology that shape and influence not just technology acceptance, but its self-sustaining use. A user could be considered self-sustaining if he or she elected to use the system with no intervention, engaged in a culture of assumed use of the technology and training of new employees, and if the user looked for solutions when the technology fails rather than abandoning the technology.

The TTM sought to extend the TAM at a macro level to account for social and political influences that affect the long and complex path for new technology. Like the TAM, the current investigation is interested at the individual level of technology use; however, we argue that the TAM as it is currently conceptualized may need to revisit the assumption that users accurately and objectively perceive and report a technology’s ease of use and usefulness.

Despite the potential advantages, perceived usefulness, or ease of use of expert systems, such as modern credibility assessment systems, users aided by these systems have not improved their accuracy or accepted the technology. In spite of highly advertised system accuracy, expert and novice lie detectors disregard the recommendations of the system when judging credibility [10]. When asked about new technologies, experts in deception detection are very enthusiastic and interested in new tools and technology. However, when confronted with the actual technology, they reject its use and ignore it all together.

This investigation seeks to reconcile why a system might abstractly appear attractive to experts but when they actually use the system they lose enthusiasm and reject its use. We posit that increasing advances in artificial intelligence has led to sophisticated expert systems that now rival the abilities of human experts. When an expert is faced with a nuanced complex judgment that contradicts their own, they may become threatened. An expert whose self-efficacy was inadvertently threatened by the technology might become defensive and nonobjective in his or her use and perception of the technology.

As technology blurs the distinction between human and computer characteristics, psychological theories, such as self-affirmation theory, are becoming more relevant in explaining these interactions.

## Self-Affirmation Theory

Self-affirmation theory suggests that human experts could be acting defensively to the system’s conflicting recommendation in order to maintain a positive view of the self. Self-affirmation theory posits that humans are motivated to maintain a positive selfregard [16]. These positive feelings toward the self are interdependent with beliefs, attitudes, and social identity (e.g., expert lie detector). Therefore, when agent-based systems contradict human experts, they could inadvertently threaten their self-view.

The threat posed by agent-based systems may be overcome using affirmation. Previous research has demonstrated that affirming the individual in an unrelated area reduces defensiveness to conflicting or threatening information [4, 5]. Affirmation can be induced by allowing an individual to express an important value such as religion or family. In addition, affirmed individuals demonstrate more sensitivity to argument strength (i.e., rate strong arguments as more persuasive than weak arguments) when evaluating counterattitudinal messages [4]. This suggests that human experts should be less defensive than nonaffirmed experts when evaluating conflicting recommendations if they are actually threatened by an agent-based system.

## Research Question and Hypotheses

RQ: Are lie detection experts threatened by agent-based expert systems?

To investigate this research question, three hypotheses are specified and tested in this study. Hypothesis 1 tests threat as a possible explanation for why experts ignore recommendations that contradict their own judgments. This explanation is particularly critical for improving the accuracy of expert judges aided by technology. If experts ignore system recommendations, they will receive no benefit or improvement to accuracy by credibility assessment systems.

In this study, all the experts will make an initial credibility judgment that will be contradicted by the system to induce threat. If the threat, caused by this contradiction, prevents the experts from accepting the system’s judgment, affirmed participants should change their judgments in favor of the system’s recommendation more than nonaffirmed participants:

Hypothesis 1: Affirmed participants will be more accepting of recommendations that contradict their judgments than nonaffirmed participants.

After the system provides its contradicting recommendation to the participants, it will provide arguments in support of this recommendation, such as the person did not make eye contact or was evasive when responding to direct questions. Participants will be presented with both strong and weak arguments. A weak argument for deception would be that a person is lying because he or she looks tired. All the participants will be shown weak and strong arguments in support of the system’s recommendation and asked to rate each argument’s strength and importance.

If participants are experiencing threat, they are predicted to be nonobjective and not carefully evaluate the arguments they find threatening. If the contradicting recommendation provided by the system causes threat in expert users, affirmed participants should be more sensitive and able to differentiate between strong and weak arguments than nonaffirmed participants. Sensitivity reflects the expert’s ability to discriminate between strong and weak arguments provided by the system:

Hypothesis 2: Affirmed participants will be more sensitive to argument strength than nonaffirmed participants.

Finally, if the system causes users to be threatened, they will view the system less favorably. A negative attitude toward the system, mediated by perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness, is predicted to make the system much less likely to be used. If experts are threatened by contradicting system recommendations, affirmed participants should view the system more favorably than nonaffirmed participants:

Hypothesis 3: Affirmed participants will report more favorable perceptions of the expert system after the system contradicts their judgments than nonaffirmed participants.

## Method

## Experimental Setting

To rec ruit pote ntia l use rs of a dec ption de tec tion syste m, the investigators conducted this study at an American Polygraph Association (APA) seminar and a European Union (EU) security workshop for border guards. The APA seminar is a large gathering of several hundred polygraph experts from around the world. They include representatives from government agencies, private companies, research institutions, as well as individual practitioners. The meeting is an opportunity for them to hear presentations on the latest research, attend training workshops, complete certifications, and participate in research. The leadership of the APA has embraced research as a method of demonstrating the effectiveness of the polygraph and occasionally invites researchers to their meetings to both present research findings and conduct new research. The meeting is a unique venue for conducting research because it allows invited researchers access to a large population of polygraph examiners in a concentrated setting, which would be difficult to replicate in an academic environment.

The security workshop, sponsored by the EU border agency Frontex, invites border guards from countries across Europe to attend and participate in activities and discussions to inform border security research. Border guards attending this workshop have an average of 15 seconds to make a decision and either let someone through their checkpoint or send them for secondary inspection. If a border guard at an airport takes longer than this to judge a person’s credibility, the line will become congested and cause people to miss their flights and become frustrated.

One particularly overlooked reality of border security is that it is customer service focused. Border guards at an airport know that they are the first face someone sees when arriving to their country and strive to make a good first impression—to encourage tourism and repeat travel. In sum, border guards must assess credibility, identity, and provide good quality service in 15 seconds or less. This makes valid software for rapid credibility assessment software a necessity in light of increasing international travel and stagnating border security personnel budgets.

## Participants

Our study included 178 credibility assessment experts from the APA (n = 134) and the EU’s border security agency Frontex (n = 44). All of the participants reported that they spent at least 45 percent of their professional day actively detecting deception. Moreover, all the participants reported that detecting deception was important to them (M [mean] = 6.53, SD [standard deviation] = 0.92, on seven-point scale). Only people who find the task important will be threatened and overlap their detection performance with self-concerns.

## Affirmation Manipulation

Affirmation was manipulated by having the participants write an essay on either an important (affirmed) or unimportant value (nonaffirmed). This study replicated the manipulation procedures used successfully in previous affirmation research [5]. The participants were asked to rank order 11 values by level of importance: artistic skills, humor, family relations, spontaneity, social skills, athletics, music, attractiveness, creativity, business, and romance. The ranking interface provided to the participants within the system is shown in Figure 1.

Following the ranking, the participants in the affirmed condition were asked to write a three-minute essay describing why their top-ranked value is important and to describe a time when it proved meaningful to them. In contrast, the participants in the nonaffirmed condition wrote an essay describing why their lowest-ranked value might be important or meaningful to someone else.

## Weak Versus Strong Arguments

A set of candidate arguments was created and pretested on a sample of undergraduate students (n = 30). The students were presented with four semantic differential word pairs (weak/strong, not relevant/relevant, not convincing/convincing, not important/ import) meant to reflect a single dimension of perceived strength for each argument as evidence of lie behavior [21]. Each word pair was rated on a seven-point scale.

A multilevel factor analysis on all 390 observations (30 students × 13 argument ratings) revealed a single-dimension factor for all four items (eigenvalue = 3.09, CFI [comparative fit index] = 0.960). These items were then summed (α = 0.9) to create composites reflecting perceived argument strength for the sample students. The average argument strength was then computed for each argument and the top and bottom arguments were identified and selected for inclusion in the study:

•	 Weak argument: “The person used poor grammar when responding” (M = 12.10, SD = 5.30)

•	 Strong argument: “The person responds defensively” (M = 21.37, SD = 5.92)

These arguments were provided to the participants to support the contradictory judgment made by the system.

## Procedure

The pa rtic ipa nts we re told the y we re going to use a prototype syste m to aid their decision making in a credibility assessment task. The participants were informed of pertinent details about the procedure and seated individually at computers equipped with headphones. They were given brief instructions on how to use the system and then began interacting with the expert credibility assessment system. The system was designed to be fully self-contained, guiding participants step-by-step through the entire process and randomly assigning them to either the affirmed or nonaffirmed condition.

The participants first completed the values essay writing task that served as the condition manipulation. All of the participants were presented with a set of values that they were asked to rank-order in terms of personal importance. The participants were then asked to write a short three-minute essay on the values based on their rankings.

Once the participants finished with the writing task, the system presented them with a video recording of an interview of a potentially deceptive interaction. After viewing the interview, the participants were asked by the system to offer their initial credibility judgment (i.e., innocent or guilty). Immediately following their judgments, the participants watched the expert system perform an analysis of the same videotaped interview. The system ostensibly performed an automated analysis of the verbal and nonverbal behavior of the subject in the video and offered its assessment of that subject to the participant. The expert system was programed to function as a confederate, always returning a judgment that directly contradicted the human expert’s initial judgment to induce threat. After offering its assessment, the expert system was programmed to support its decision by displaying arguments of varying strength (two strong and two weak) to be reviewed and rated by the participant. Figure 2 illustrates the screen presented to the experts immediately after making their initial judgments.

After receiving the system’s judgment and its supporting arguments, the human expert had an opportunity to revise or retain his or her initial credibility judgment; this change in final judgment served as the dependent variable for H1 (change or no change). The human expert was then asked to evaluate the perceived strength of the expert system’s arguments, providing the dependent variable for H2. Finally, the participants were asked to rate their perceptions of favorability toward the expert system, furnishing the dependent variable for H3. Upon completing this final task, the participants were debriefed on the details and intent of the study and thanked for their participation.

## Discussion

The mea sure me nts of a rgume nt se nsitivity a nd fa vora bility reflect the objectivity of the participants toward the system. The nonaffirmed deception detection experts that were threatened by the system did not discriminate as strongly between strong and weak arguments and viewed the system less favorably than the affirmed experts. This suggests that the threat introduced by a contradicting system reduced objectivity and negatively biased their attitude toward the system.

However, despite an increase in objectivity toward the system there was no improvement to accuracy for the affirmed participants. It is reasonable to assume that accuracy of the affirmed participants should increase if more cognitive resources are available for the judgment task. In this study and in the case of all deception studies, an unfair and unrealistic constraint is placed on experts to make snap judgments of deception video recordings, with low media richness, no direct interaction, and no contextual intelligence (e.g., passport, criminal history). Given these impediments, it is likely that experts are judging equivalently to flipping a coin. If this is the case, no additional cognitive resources would improve their accuracy. Moreover, the accuracy of experts reported in deception studies should not be overly generalized as the accuracy in the field (e.g., border guards, polygraph examiners, law enforcement officials).

The participants, regardless of the threat, did not change their honesty rating to agree with the system’s recommendation. Experts may be more objective toward the system when threat is reduced (H2 and H3), but once they make a decision, they maintain it. Considering the high confidence levels in judgments reported by the experts in this study, it may take more than an accurate system to change their minds once they have committed to a judgment. There could be a confirmation bias once an expert becomes anchored to a concrete judgment. Future research should investigate how to design interfaces of expert systems that incorporate the system and human judgment into the decision process. The system should not just dictate a judgment, nor should the human expert make a commitment to a judgment too early during system use.

## Conclusion

This re sea rc h re pre se nts a f irst ste p in e xpla ining a nd unde rsta nd ing the interaction between users and advanced agent-based expert systems, specifically for automated credibility assessment. This is crucial because despite improvements in system performance, the ultimate decision is made by the human. This study demonstrates that human experts are threatened by advanced expert systems. However, steps can be taken to mitigate this to promote objectivity after these advanced systems are implemented and used by experts in the field. This could be accomplished through training, interface design, or even affirmation. Specifically, if affirmation against threat is provided at the correct time (e.g., early in a user’s exposure to the system), the effects of threat do not become cumulative over time, which has been found to facilitate threat defense for months at a time [3]. Affirmation, if found valuable for threat buffering, could be incorporated into trainings and operational procedures.

The focus of this investigation was on deception detection or credibility assessment systems because these systems (1) incorporate artificial intelligence and automated decision support to experts, facilitating the conditions to experience threat, and (2) are currently in the prototype and transition stages to experts [14], making the understanding of the effect of their adoption critical. However, as more and more systems increase integration of artificial intelligence and inadvertently assail the expertise and abilities of users, threat and self-evaluative concerns will become an impediment to technology acceptance.
