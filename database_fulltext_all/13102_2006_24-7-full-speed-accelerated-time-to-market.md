## Abstract

‘It’s only a web site. What could be so difficult about that?’ This quote is from the cafeteria of a start-up business funded by a North American retailer, after the disastrous ‘Black Friday’ of 2000, during which its web site experienced systemic failure. This case describes the dynamics, complexities and consequences of fast tracking an e-business start-up. This consumer electronics retailer created one of the most visited retail web sites, from concept to operation in 6 months. Market analysts were predicting a major increase in online sales while consumers were adopting the Internet at a rate faster than any previous technology. Meeting the multi-channel demands of the dynamic and competitive environment required operational balance, stability, innovative flexibility, organizational fit and the alignment of resource capabilities with technology. This case challenges the reader to comment on how a large company positioned itself and integrated the necessary competencies to compete successfully in a developing market by establishing a spin-off operation, separate from the main company.

## Introduction

A major electronics retailer created one of the most visited retail web sites, from concept to operation, in 6 months. To do that, it set up an e-business initiative separately from the parent company. The strategy was to leverage the parent’s business model, which relied on attracting customers to stores using low-priced specials and to cross-sell products in an exciting retail environment. SellWell.com,<sup>1</sup> the online start-up, was to offer free shipping and low-priced items to attract customers. The order placement process, however, would discourage shipping and encourage in-store pickup.

‘It’s only a web site. What could be so difficult about that?’ This quote is from the cafeteria of the start-up business, after the disastrous ‘Black Friday’ of 2000, during which its web site experienced systemic failure. The web site was built in 6 months by calling on the services of a prime contractor and almost every IT vendor, consultant and service provider, while not disrupting the parent’s aggressive expansion of bricks and mortar stores across the United States and Canada. However, on Black Friday, the web site kept crashing while processing large numbers of customer orders. This case describes the dynamics, complexities and consequences of building and commissioning the web site.

## Disaster and recovery

Thursday 23 November 2000, Thanksgiving Day, was the official start of the Christmas holiday season. While most Americans spent their day with friends and family, the executive and technical staff of SellWell.com were trying to recover from major database failures preventing customers from shopping.

The trigger seemed to be the massive increase in transaction volume, far beyond projected figures. Customers had swamped the site. In the prior weeks and months, managers and technicians had worked to stabilize the systems and create contingency plans. However, the patches did not work and no one could pinpoint the cause of failure. The only work-around was to ‘flush’ all connections to the databases when the number of transactions built up. This required continuous monitoring and ‘flushing’ when the volume indicator reached 80% of the theoretical maximum.

## Holiday shopping

In the United States, Black Friday,<sup>2</sup> the day after Thanksgiving, is the biggest shopping day of the year. Retailers prepare for this day all summer – tuning their product offerings to match trends and shifts in demand. Black Friday brought SellWell.com a business crisis. Under heavy shopping loads, the web site would build up a backlog of transactions and then crash. To manage the crisis, the CIO and Director of Systems Operations created three teams of managers, engineers, developers and business users not only to ‘watch and flush’ but also to monitor events, changes and patterns in the hope of nailing the bug. These teams operated in three 8 hour shifts to provide 24  7 coverage. Within each team, a select group of technicians, called ‘finger monkeys’, were responsible for operating the control consoles.

At that time, SellWell.com was in transition from being a dynamic development shop to an industrial-strength largescale e-tailer, with customers forcing the pace. Leading the IT team were the CIO and the Director of Systems Operations who, along with everyone else, were new to both the spin-off and parent organization. The CIO had joined the organization in August from a catalogue shopping service that had been transformed into an Internet retailer. Before that, he worked for a prominent financial firm. In both of those engagements, he had worked with the newly appointed Director of Systems Operations who had joined the organization only a week before Thanksgiving.

## Background information

## Industry trends

New technology-driven business models have been evolving in parallel with adoption of the World Wide Web. Widely known as e-business, it is simply conducting business via electronic means, especially using the Internet. The most popular forms of e-Business are business-to-business (B2B) – where companies use electronic means to sell to one another, and business-to-consumer (B2C) – where business sells directly to consumers via web sites (Trombly, 1991). Within the B2C sub-group, one promising model is etailing, or ‘direct to customer electronic retailing’ via the Internet.

The dotcom boom also saw the birth of a new creature – the ‘Online Consumer’ – first defined in the ‘1998 Consumer Technographics North American (US and Canada) Benchmark Study’ conducted by Forrester Research (McQuivey, 2003). This emerging breed doubled in numbers from 25 million households in 1998 to 50 million 2 years later. With a booming economy, media attention and the increasing reach of the Internet, consumers were moving online.

The last 2 years of the 1990s saw growth accelerate. Michael Pastore, in his November 1998 article for Cyberatlas, reported that Shop.org and the Boston Consulting Group were projecting growth in excess of 200% per year and that revenues would surpass US \$13 billion for 1998 (Pastore, 1998). After 1 year, Pastore wrote that Forrester Research was predicting 17 million households shopping online in 1999, each spending US\$1,167 and driving online retail sales to US\$20 billion. The projection to 2004 was for a steady increase to 49 million households with average spending climbing to \$3738 and revenues reaching \$184 billion<sup>3</sup> (Pastore, 1999).

Also in 1999, the Consumer Electronics Manufacturers Association forecast that online sales of traditional consumer technologies such as TVs, DVD players, VCRs, computer hardware/peripherals and camcorders would reach at least US\$14 billion or 13% of the total industry by 2002 (CEMA, 1999). CEMA also reported that some people were shopping online to review specifications and prices, but then buying in a store.

Responding to these customers, some companies were offering combined channels, or a ‘bricks and clicks’ business model (Weill and Vitale, 2001). This multi-channel model utilizes traditional product delivery channels such as physical stores and outlets, in combination with ‘virtual store fronts’ by way of web sites. For home video equipment, more than 12 per cent of customers reviewed specifications online, and then went to a store to review and purchase, compared with only 2 per cent buying online. With the exception of computer equipment, all consumer electronic categories saw the same pattern (See Table 1 for details). ‘The Internet and traditional retailing are working together in consumers’ views; they don’t see them as separate realms,’ said Todd Thibodaux, VP of Market Research at CEMA (CEMA, 1999). The ‘Online Consumer’ identified by Forrester Research in 1998 had begun to evolve.

By 1999, traditional retailers, who had been reluctant to put resources into the Internet delivery channel, saw that they could not continue to ‘wait and see’ and ignore the growth posted by the pure-play e-tailers in 1997 and 1998. There was too much at risk in not adopting, or at least experimenting, with the technology-driven business paradigm. The Internet empowered consumers with access to product information and easy price comparisons.

## Competitive market

The consumer electronics and in-home entertainment segment of the retail industry is a highly contested sector.

The growing popularity and affordability of in-home entertainment, such as TVs and stereos in the 1950s and 1960s, further increased in the 1970s and 1980s with the introduction of new products such as cassette recorders, personal audio players (e.g., the Sony Walkman), VCRs and CD Players. Large department store chains, such as Montgomery Wards and Sears, created separate departments for electronics and appliances. Specialty retailers had appeared in cities and towns.

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, a new wave of electronic entertainment took the market by storm. Personal computers and video game consoles became affordable for the ordinary consumer, which allowed video gaming at home instead of at the video arcade. The average American home began to acquire a collection of technology products and appliances. One slogan that became popular was ‘every home should have one’. Specialty retailers such as Circuit City, Silo, Best Buy and the parent company, SellWell, grew quickly, as consumers demanded a mix of entertainment products.

Consider, Circuit City, one of SellWell’s competitors. Customers came to Circuit City stores intending to purchase an advertised item. However, stock levels were low, so consumers often faced a commissioned sales force convincing them to purchase an alternative product – often one with higher margins. In contrast, SellWell always had stock, even of items advertised at low prices. The SellWell strategy was to create a sense of excitement in the store. For example, in 1999 SellWell sourced a very low-cost DVD player from a Chinese manufacturer. The DVD player retailed for \$99, an unheard of price at the time. Supported by a large advertising campaign, SellWell attracted tens of thousands of customers to its stores. In a piece of retail theatre, an entire pallet of DVD players would be driven into the store on a forklift with warning sirens and flashing lights announcing the forklift’s progression. The staff would unwrap the pallet, unpack one player onto a nearby counter and put a huge \$99 price card on top of the pile. Customers would go crazy. Nearby, there would be huge piles of compatible electronic equipment. The other goods would have higher margins, but still have competitive prices. Without a salesperson in sight, the customers would scramble for the goods and head for the checkouts.

The early and mid-1990s also saw the arrival of new Internet-based competitors. By 1999, SellWell competed directly with competitors such as Circuit City, Best Buy and Silo; department stores such as Sears and Montgomery Wards; discount retailers such as Wal-Mart; software retailers such as Electronic Boutique and Egg Head Software; catalogue music retailers such as Columbia House; pure-play online retailers such as Buy.com and Amazon.- com; and direct online vendors such as Dell. SellWell also had to deal with customers informed by product information sites of companies such as Sony and IBM.

## Business information

From its 1960s beginning, SellWell became a leading North American consumer electronics retailer, with total revenues in the tens of billions and net earnings of hundreds of millions of dollars. It operated more than one thousand stores in both the USA and Canada.

At the end of 1999, SellWell decided to join the Internet world by transforming its existing ‘brochure-ware site’ into a comprehensive e-Tailing site. It would use a ‘bricks and clicks’ model to exploit its established fulfilment channels of shops and distribution centres. SellWell created a separate e-Commerce company to operate the Internet retailing that would offer customers cross-channel services such as store pickup and return, online catalogue services for customers’ research before going to a store, discounts through electronic coupons and in-store ‘kiosk ordering’ in case the store ran out of stock.

The bricks and clicks strategy were designed to allow SellWell to offer immediate gratification in a way that neither Circuit City nor Amazon could. Circuit City’s low level of stock precluded it from offering stock-level information on its web site. If customers could see that there was little or no stock, then they would be unlikely to drive to the store. Amazon, and other pure-play online retailers, by definition had no physical presence and therefore could not offer immediate customer satisfaction. Bricks and clicks also fitted SellWell’s overall strategy of attracting customers to the store in search of specials and cross-selling other products in an exciting environment.

SellWell announced the Internet initiative in a January 2000 news release (see Figure A1 in Appendix A for a timeline of events). The President of the new e-tailer startup commented: ‘We believe that the consumer electronics market on the Internet will be substantial, and this e-Tailing operation is uniquely positioned to seize market leadership. We intend for this operation to be the best consumer resource on the web for technology, and entertainment products and services.’ A related announcement revealed a large-scale strategic partnership with a major technology vendor. In exchange for technology support, the vendor received premier advertising positioning.

After 6 months, the Internet retail site was launched. In the first phase, customers could access only a portion of the company’s merchandize. All features and access to the full merchandize offering were to be available at the beginning of the holiday season.

## Operating structure

SellWell.com was established as a separate company from the large bricks and mortar operation of the parent for three reasons. First, at that time, the SellWell strategy was working so successfully that new stores were opening up at a rate of about one per fortnight. Infrastructure and IT were expanding at a breakneck pace just to keep up with growth in the number of stores. Some felt that SellWell.com, if kept inhouse, could easily disrupt the existing IT function’s core business of running the tills, managing stock flow and placing orders. Quarterly reporting to Wall Street showed a cadence in growth that analysts had fully priced into the shares. The stock market would quickly punish any drop in growth.

At the same time, the shares of pure-play Internet competitors were increasing in price. This created the second reason to spin out SellWell.com. Attracting talent was challenging and the ability to offer share options on high-growth dotcom shares would be a major incentive. Competitors were offering top technical staff the opportunity to become millionaires and SellWell would be able to follow suit.

The third reason for spinning off SellWell.com was to protect the initiative itself. The culture for the new business was incompatible with that of the established SellWell operation. In comparison, SellWell.com had a younger workforce, a highly creative culture, and a fluid highpressure environment. Keeping it within the parent corporation could have negatively influenced that.

Sales estimates put SellWell.com at about the size of a large format or ‘super’ store – small in comparison to the parent company’s overall operations, but unique in its full autonomy. It had its own marketing, merchandizing and various other operating and customer support functions (see Figure A2 in Appendix A). Very few staff came from the parent company, other than a few product and IT specialists. SellWell.com would use existing distribution mechanisms extensively, in particular the stores and warehouses. Customer orders could be placed on the web site and, while free home delivery would be offered, the prominent positioning of the store pickup option would encourage customers to collect their goods immediately. The intention was to entice customers to buy other items while in the store. The start-up would not use the existing IT infrastructure other than an electronic interface to query stock levels and place electronic orders. See Figure 1 for a schematic of the business model.

Creative flexibility was present in every area of the business, driving the culture and staff attitudes towards work. The facility housing SellWell.com had a colourful, modular cutting-edge design filled with music, recreation and fun. The cafeteria was equipped with computers dedicated to popular PC games and video game consoles such as PlayStation 2 and Xbox. Other facilities included table tennis, pool and mini-hockey, along with tennis, basketball and volleyball courts. (The head of building security often asked the employees: ‘Are you kids getting anything done at all?’) However, major parts of the Technical Operations Group were located at the corporate facility several miles away. Attending meetings and getting teams together became a challenge, especially during the frequent winter snowstorms.

Owing to the urgency and tight delivery schedule, consultants and contractors were hired to create the site and establish operations. Employees were a small minority. Accenture, as the prime contractor, managed relationships with the other suppliers. Almost every large IT subcontractor – Accenture, Rainier, Spherion, Microsoft, Software Architects, Modis, Talent, Borne, Compaq, etc – had representatives on-site. To meet the speed to market requirement, the site was hosted by a major vendor who could assist with rapid set-up, technical expertise, security and other needs, and had experience with engaging with remote clients.

SellWell.com planned and implemented multiple projects simultaneously, with half focused on the technical and business adjustments needed to operationalize the site and the other half developing new functionality. Each project team contained a mixture of a few SellWell.com people and many people from different IT/IS contracting firms, reflecting an extensive diversity in skills, culture and operational backgrounds. Subject matter experts, nicknamed ‘Smees’, came from different functional units of the parent company to develop business and operational protocols within the projects. Each project also used Business Liaisons from the SellWell.com Systems Operations Group to guide business operatives on the capabilities and limitations of systems and technologies.

## Issues and challenges

SellWell.com’s implementation plan drew on the parent’s proven marketing and sales approach developed over the years for launching new stores in the traditional retailing channel. This aggressive approach allowed no slack for operational or technical e-Business challenges, and rushed the design and development into production in 6 months – the time typically taken to launch a new physical store. This resulted in frequent troubleshooting cycles that consumed vital time and resources in the effort to establish operational stability. In frustration, the technical leader for software distribution, the group responsible for site stability, stated: ‘We better do something about this quickly. If we don’t, it’s either going to kill us or destroy our marriages.’ As the holiday season approached, testing and quality assurance cut corners to get the site to market. Significant time and resources were committed to revising and trouble shooting the site’s functional modules. According to one operative: ‘It is like a voyage of discovery heading into the unknown, where you constantly adjust your course trying to avoid hazards that may lie ahead.’

Keeping the pressure on, both online and traditional competitors were launching campaigns, requiring Sell-Well.com to react and change the site’s specifications. Half way through the development, the parent company intervened and ‘tweaked’ the business model. SellWell.com would have to synchronize its promotional activities with those of the ‘brick’-based campaigns, which varied geographically by product, price and timing. This was an attempt to create consistency between the activities of the electronic and traditional channels. SellWell.com had to react to competitors’ online campaigns and the campaigns of an aggressive physical parent, while it dealt with the already complex work of stabilizing the web site for the holiday season. A commonly heard phrase was: ‘If only we were given more time’.

Most of the technology issues stemmed from inadequate standards, poor coordination between the development teams and insufficiently rigorous testing and quality control. For example, one developer would use a version of code to implement a new feature in one package, while another revised the same code to correct a problem in another package. The later revision would overwrite the earlier one, effectively discarding the new feature. Without policed standards and strict enforcement of development guidelines, developers from different consulting firms reverted to their idiosyncratic methods and processes, further compounding the problems.

The builds were particularly problematic. A full build of the site took 8 hours. The build was a manual process that collected, compiled, packaged and deployed new features and bug fixes. The ‘build meister’ was kept isolated for the entire length of the work to minimize inadvertent errors caused by distractions. This lengthy precision process meant that a bug fix for the production site took at least 8 hours to apply. Add the time needed to develop, test and approve the fix, and the time stretched to days instead of hours. This was not a satisfactory response time for a customer-facing system. In addition, it prevented the business from quickly responding to competitive market changes.

## Implementing stability

In an address to SellWell.com’s Operations Group upon his appointment, the CIO acknowledged the lingering technical and operational issues and the diminishing time to fix them, as the holiday shopping season loomed closer. He expressed hope that all personnel would continue to work hard to keep the site functioning optimally during the impending holiday season rush. By September 2000, development was slowing and the majority of people were working on initiatives to stabilize and prepare the site for the holiday season. One August initiative of the Development Operations Team was to clean up code and data. A September initiative by the Systems Operations Team aimed to improve infrastructure performance.<sup>5</sup> As comprehensive as these preparations were, they did not address standards, coordination and quality control.

In response to the lack of focus on management issues, the Senior Technical Consultant conducted a comprehensive analysis of the entire Systems and Development Operations and presented a set of recommendations to improve management control. Those recommendations formed the Software Delivery System (SDS) initiative, intended to bring stability and focus into the reactive and increasingly chaotic environment.

The recommendations were:

 Form a Software Architecture Team to establish software development standards.

 Create a Release Management Team and release procedures to coordinate the entire software delivery and data synchronization process.

 Improve the version control process and use a robust configuration management system.

 Simplify the management of the site through the implementation of a comprehensive Site Management System (SMS).

 Create test cases as part of a set of QA test criteria in the code promotion process.

 Create a consolidated project web site, or Developers’ Portal, that would propagate information and distribute important utilities and components to development, systems and business teams.

Owing to time and resource constraints, only a portion of the SDS initiative received approval. This included the Software Architecture Group, Release Management Team, Developers’ Portal, and the redefinition and restructure of the Quality Assurance practices. Active discussions about the merits of implementing the rest of SDS continued for a few weeks before the Director of Systems Operations paved the way towards site and operational stability by providing the necessary resources to complete the remainder of the SDS initiative. This placed added focus on the SMS that controlled the distribution and management of software components to the web site and backbone systems.

On November 13, SellWell.com implemented a code freeze to stabilize the site for the holiday season. Everybody worked hard to ensure an excellent customer experience for those visiting the site and few customers experienced problems – until November 23, Thanksgiving Day.

Keynote, a web metric service organization, issued a special ‘Black Friday’ report on web retailers’ performance (Keynote Systems, 2000). According to the report, many companies were struggling that day. SellWell.com’s site availability was at less than 50%. For customers who did find the site, the average response time was 11 seconds, slower than competitors but, more importantly, it exceeded the ‘8-second rule’, which stipulates that users will click away from a site if the page does not download within 8 seconds.

Throughout the holiday season, SellWell.com’s ‘watch and flush’ teams vigilantly monitored the site, fixing problems as they appeared. Their efforts and dedication led to a maximum daily transaction rate of 7000 orders, with total web site visits over the season in excess of 13 million (see Table 2). The code freeze lifted in January of 2001 and SellWell.com had survived the greatest test of its existence. As a show of gratitude, every member of the ‘watch and flush’ teams received gift certificates for purchase of company merchandize. In his post-holiday season address to all employees, the SellWell.com President acknowledged the team members and all their efforts – ‘if not for these people, we all would be out of a job right now’.

The organization faced overwhelming difficulties as it fought to gain control over the web site and stabilize its operations. It learned about and implemented new technologies, processes, structures and skills, each intended to reinforce others. One technology initiative was the centralization of the infrastructure to support the new SDS initiative. The Director of Systems Operations re-architected the entire infrastructure, consolidating functions into industry-proven integrated systems.

The SMS, which began implementation early in 2001, was another technology project proposed by SDS to address the problematic builds. With its completion in June 2001, partial builds of the site became a reality. A full build, if required, took less than 10 min with a single click of a button!

The most remarkable capability came in August 2001, with the first site rollback. SMS could manage partial or full site rollbacks to any previous release. This allowed for very quick recovery while maintaining full synchronization between data and system components in multiple environments. This supported the development and web teams’ creative experimentation and accelerated processes for testing, release and deployment.

Implementation of the Release and Deployment Process (RDP) had begun a few months earlier, in November 2000. Before the RDP implementation, the project managers and the lead developers were responsible for controlling the release of new functionalities and bug fixes. The coordination became unwieldy and required many meetings to make sure that everyone knew what was happening. Participants became increasingly fed-up with these meetings that they saw as a waste of productive time. When asked about them one developer said: ‘Different circus, the same clowns.’

RDP helped relieve much of the brewing frustration by establishing controls over the release of features and fixes onto the site. A major part of RDP was the Developers Portal, where details about each release were published in advance, allowing developers to discuss the details, update and finalize the release before requesting deployment approval. In time, these processes integrated tightly with SMS and identified potential problems, while capturing knowledge about changes and reducing guesswork.

There was also a major re-structuring of roles and responsibilities. The Front-end and the Back-end Development Groups became responsible for development coordination and code handling through SMS and RDP. The Release Management Team, Data Management, Systems Operation and Architecture Groups clarified and re-aligned their functional and service boundaries. As the realignment took place, stress reduced as everybody’s work became more meaningful, with clear starting and ending points, and defined hand-off procedures and conditions. ‘Now, you feel that you don’t have to do everything, as you are assured that your colleagues will not drop the ball when you hand it off’, said one developer. Business operatives received comprehensive rules of engagement to obtain services from the Technical Operations Group.

Recognizing the need for advanced software development and project management, executives established training programmes for all personnel, including training on the new technology and delivery processes. Training in groups helped build relationships to support the new structural arrangements, particularly for task-dependent roles requiring hand-offs. It became much easier to communicate functionality, delivery and performance implications.

Before the implementation of SDS, production systems could receive undocumented changes with little testing, often with destabilizing effects. Subsequent attempts to re-stabilize the site would require extensive diagnosis and bug fixing. However, applying bug fixes often overwrote previous undocumented fixes, destabilizing the site again. Despite its late application, the implementation of the SDS initiative supported the necessary controls to eliminate these risks. By supporting standards, policies and procedures, along with changes to the Release Management Team, Developers’ Portal, Configuration Management System and the RDP, SMS began to establish stability for SellWell.com.

## The new era

In October 2001, with any prospect of a full spin-off destroyed by the dotcom bust, the parent company decided to absorb SellWell.com into its regular operations. The web site had been stable in the lead up to the 2001 holiday season. Key business and technical personnel integrated into the SellWell IS and business units to assimilate the learning, while the rest remained in the new Web Operations Group to operate the web site and develop the next generation.

At this time, the Architecture team had completed the blueprints for the next-generation site. The new design intended to utilize a selection of the best of breed technologies, moving away from the prime contractor arrangement put in place by the parent company during conception. The prime contract arrangement had presented numerous technical and capability constraints, limiting the implementation of some important initiatives. For example, moving away from this arrangement allowed alternative technologies to reduce the number of servers employed from ninety machines to just four, significantly reducing license and maintenance cost without any penalties in functionality and performance.

Reducing the time to market had made SellWell.com run before it could crawl, but run it did as the volume of transactions soared. After its first year of operation, SellWell.com was one of the fastest growing and most visited Internet retail sites.

## Bricks and clicks

Since the holiday season of 2000, commercial use of the Internet has matured quickly. The convergence of Internet and traditional retail channels, now known as ‘Bricks and Clicks’, has proved successful. Table 3 shows the performance comparisons between ‘pure play’ and ‘Bricks and Clicks’ retailers at the end of 2000. Organizations developed co-branding and partnership activities between ‘click’- and ‘brick’-based organizations in an effort to become profitable. In 2001, Amazon.com, an e-Tailing giant with only one profitable quarter (Abrams, 2002), started pursuing cobranding alliances with Toys-R-Us, Borders Books and others to strengthen its cross-channel position (Flint and Spieler, 2001). Other e-tailers such as Buy.com and eBay were also seeking synergistic partnerships with complementary brands and suppliers in a similar approach. Smaller operators merged into bigger operators, and many closed as the dotcom bubble burst.

The emerging Online Consumer had also experienced a transformation. In a Forrester Research report, James L McQuivey (2003) noted that they ‘multiplied’ – increasing in numbers as more consumers adapted to the online channel.

They then ‘mainstreamed’ – began to resemble the average US population. Finally, they ‘matured’ – became skilled and discriminating in their use of the web (see Table 4).

The mature Online Consumer had become a demanding ‘multi-channel consumer’, fully embracing the web without abandoning efficient offline channels. This maturation is indicated by the steady increase in the types of media consumed online, banking activities conducted online and purchases performed online (see Table 5), without further decreases in the activities performed in the traditional channels. Consumption of online and offline products and services had increased, complementing each other.

In comparison with its competitors, SellWell had better capabilities in satisfying multi-channel consumers. It had the virtual marketing reach and online service delivery of its web site along with support from the bricks and mortar operation to integrate in-store fulfilment. For SellWell, the purely online delivery channel contributed less than 2 per cent of total revenue, which translated into sales of US\$300 million for 2001.

For SellWell.com, the ‘brick’-based fulfilment channel worked. Home deliveries were largely on time, stores handled customer collections efficiently, while returns and exchanges operated flawlessly. The difficulties faced during the turbulent holiday season of 2000 may have proved fatal if not for the reliable fulfilment channels. Despite the problems and limitations in receiving customers’ orders at the frontend, fulfilment of orders received was timely at the back-end.

## 2006 update

In an effort to concentrate its efforts on its core competencies, SellWell outsourced its entire IS operations to Accenture, including the SellWell.com technical support function. SellWell continues to explore other competitive options such as opening a wholly owned sourcing operation in Shanghai to exploit low-cost Chinese production capabilities, and SellWell stores may appear in the Asia-Pacific region. However, before that, customers in some countries outside North America may be able to purchase certain items through SellWell.com.

## Conclusion

Most business initiatives that are worth undertaking are difficult, especially those using leading-edge technology when the company has few capabilities in the technology. SellWell.com succeeded by creating a dynamic development environment and sourcing capabilities through a prime contractor. By complementing the existing business model of the parent company, SellWell.com experienced a surge in demand just as it began the transition from development to production. SellWell.com worked 24 h a day to overcome this obstacle and deliver what is now one of the world’s busiest web sites.

## Appendix B. About the research

The SellWell case is the result of a programme of research at the Australian Graduate School of Management<sup>6</sup> into the business value of information technology. The objective has been to describe how e-businesses initiatives can be effectively developed and executed, and to explore influencing factors on why some survive and prosper while others do not. SellWell.com is one of the largest retail sites in the world and the lead researcher worked there during its establishment, adding personal experience to the case study.

Data collection followed two approaches. One focused on SellWell.com, developing a narrative time line and detailing the operating structures. The other focused on events in the wider industry and competitor performance. The research team collected multiple types of data, drawing on the considerable publicly available information.

Within SellWell.com, the researchers selected respondents based on a purposive sampling to develop an understanding of the company from the executives to the engineering and support functions. Respondents were involved in the decision-making, execution and benefits capture. The wide range of respondents provided maximum diversity to enable the research team to compare and contrast data (Carson et al., 2001). The collection procedures involved reflection on experience, telephone calls, public and private database searches, emails, questionnaires, and both individual and group online chat sessions, to assist in triangulation of findings.

The lead researcher initially communicated with people in SellWell.com by email. The response was enthusiastic, reinforced by the purpose of the research and the confidentiality agreement, but perhaps also buoyed by a sense of pride. Participants included the CIO, the Director of Systems Operations, managers from marketing, merchandizing and fulfilment, and key members of the Creative Development and Technical Operations groups.

All respondents received an email containing a two-page questionnaire to establish the timeline of major events. The researchers compared the results and consolidated a narrative timeline that they then sent back to the participants for confirmation and/or revision. This process iterated until a timeline was substantially agreed.

The researchers needed a more interactive mechanism than asynchronous email to enable richer exchanges, but visits to the physically distant SellWell.com were not a viable option and demands on the respondents’ time made it difficult to secure appointments for telephone calls. Further complicating communication was the large time zone difference between the subjects and the researchers. Internet chat was the solution.

Participants received an invitation to join an Internet chat group. Nine accepted, which may reflect the technical background of the participants. Those who did not join remained accessible via email and telephone. Chat sessions lasted for up to 4 h and were active at various times of the day and night (approaching 24  7), to conform to the respondents’ availability. Sessions had up to five participants, who sent documents, diagrams and other information back and forth, as discussions took place, providing timely responses to questions and confirmation on specific information. The researcher recorded all chat sessions with the application’s tracking function and kept a journal reflecting on conversations and information. The journal’s unformatted conversation data is 2 Mb and the journal contains 10 Mb of file transfers.

In parallel to the focus on SellWell.com, a separate enquiry established the operating structure of both SellWell and SellWell.com. The lead researcher used questionnaires focusing on the structure and emphasizing how SellWell.com fitted into the operations of the larger parent company. In addition, company reports, past and current organizational charts, financial and sales performance figures, sales growth estimates, profits, transaction volumes and other relevant figures were collected and reviewed.

The industry-focused approach was simultaneously building an understanding of the state of the industry and the dynamics of the e-business market in which SellWell.com competed. Here, the objective was to establish a separate timeline to represent the external events influencing alignment of the case organization. To accomplish this, the researchers extensively scanned and collected relevant published materials on consumer electronics retailing, online retailing (e-tailing), Internet consumerism and trends in information technology. Although the researchers referenced academic and proprietary databases, the most relevant information was publicly available on the Internet. More than 32 articles provided reference data for the industry timeline and analysis of direct competitors’ performance.

As the SellWell.com timeline and operating structure and the industry timeline and competitor performance data developed, so the research moved into an analysis of the findings. The first step was to synchronize the two timelines, which surfaced and validated external factors affecting the organization. Rich local events then attached to the consolidated timeline. In a hermeneutic process, this illuminated the industry events, allowing adjustments to preserve and strengthen the joint narrative timeline. All respondents then received the resulting narrative for review. Several chat sessions discussed and validated the case and further resolved the data. The CIO and the Director of Systems Operations, the most senior participants in the study, approved the final case transcript.

The research took place over a 3-year period with data collection concentrated in the 6 months January 2003 to June 2003, and analysis undertaken over an extended period from July to December 2003. Further refinement and input from a number of academic sources and the 2005 Americas Conference on Information Systems contributed to the case.
