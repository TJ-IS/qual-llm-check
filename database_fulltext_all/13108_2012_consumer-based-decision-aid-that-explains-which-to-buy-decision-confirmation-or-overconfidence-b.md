# Consumer-based decision aid that explains which to buy: Decision con<sup>fi</sup>rmation or overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence bias?

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Keywords: Decision aid Explanation Consumer decision making Overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence bias

## a b s t r a c t

Providing explanation to justify product recommendations is critical in the online purchase decision process. Bulk of the extant literature has focused on the provision of decision aids facilitating screening of product alternatives and presenting of <sup>fi</sup>ltered alternatives. In comparison, few studies are conducted to examine decision aids that support the assessment and evaluation of the presented product alternatives prior to actual purchase, i.e., explanation-featured decision aid. This article conceptualizes three implementations of explanation aid differed by the forms of explanation elaboration. Experimental results indicate that a more elaborated explanation aid could heighten a consumer's decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence leading to lesser cognitive effort expended and inferior product choice made.

## 1. Introduction

Consumers habitually go through an evaluation process in which available product alternatives are assessed and compared before making a purchase decision [43]. An online consumer-based decision aid leverages on the collected information about the consumers' preferences to recommend a small subset of product alternatives that are more likely to interest them [4,25,26,45,56]. Product recommendation transpires in two forms, namely 1) the <sup>fi</sup>ltering of product alternatives based on the elicited preferences, and 2) the provision of reasoning based on the presented alternatives with the aim of helping consumers to make the informed choice [68]. Prior researches have ostensibly focused on the former form of product recommendation [4,5,42,45,58,61]. The second form, i.e., how a decision aid could assist a consumer in making decision after presenting a list of product alternatives, is less investigated in great details. This study seeks to bridge this knowledge gap by examining how the provision of explanation feature, i.e., a speci<sup>fi</sup>c instance of the second form of decision aid, could affect consumers' purchase decision. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we seek to understand how the provision of different forms of explanation features impact consumers' behavior differently.

Providing explanation feature could assist a consumer in <sup>fi</sup>nding a reason (hence explanation) on why certain product option(s) is(are)

recommended and which is to be chosen [27,55]. Research on providing explanation-featured decision aid is scare. An exception is the work by Wang and Benbasat [65] who examined a consumer's trust formation in response to the recommendations furnished by the decision aid. This view is in accordance with the conventional wisdom dictating that the provision of explanation-featured decision aid could enhance a consumer's affect towards the aid's product recommendations [18,51,66]. This general presumption has served as the driver for the development of the deluge of explanation-featured decision aid now widely found on the Internet. However, the work of Wang and Benbasat [65] only focused on input explanations, i.e., explanations that guide consumers to express their product preferences. Input explanations are more applicable to the earlier preference elicitation and <sup>fi</sup>ltering stage, i.e., the <sup>fi</sup>rst form of product recommendation, and not at the latter evaluation stage, i.e., the second form of product recommendation.

Intriguingly, the Internet has witnessed an increase in the variations of output explanation-featured decision aid, i.e., the second form of product recommendation, spanning from providing simple explanations (e.g., MovieLens Movie Recommendation) to affording more elaborated reasoning (e.g., CarWale Automated Car Recommendations and Yahoo! Shopping SmartSort). Yet it is not entirely clear how the variants of the output explanation-featured decision aid affect decision performance beyond subjective assessments. This study, hence, seeks to contribute to this knowledge gap by examining the behavioral and performance consequences of variants of output explanationfeatured decision aids. In this paper, we draw from the commercial implementations (e.g., CarWale and Yahoo! Shopping Smartsort) and match against the explanation literature [20] to evaluate and assess three forms of output explanation-featured decision aid, namely Trace, Justi<sup>fi</sup>cation and Strategic.

Our overarching proposition is that the three variations of the explanation-featured decision aid could differ in their impact on the consumer decision making behavior, manifested by decision con<sup>fi</sup>- dence, decision effort and decision quality. To this end, we anchored on the decision overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence paradigm [32,34,37] to posit that a more elaborated explanation aid could heighten a consumer's decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence (i.e., overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence) leading to expending lesser cognitive effort and choosing an inferior quality product option (i.e., lower decision quality). A set of laboratory experiments was subsequently conducted to verify the conjecture. Through deliberating and differentiating the effect of different explanation aids on both the subjective and objective outcomes, we are able to develop a nuanced understanding of the relationship between consumer-based explanation aid supporting the second form of product recommendation and consumer behavior [2,14].

Apart from advancing our theoretical understanding of explanationfeatured decision aid, this research is important for several reasons. In particular, industry practitioners have continued to highlight the importance of providing a highly personalized experience for online shoppers [17]. Online consumer-based decision aid has the potential to help companies reach out to their customers through personalized product recommendations, which increase purchase propensity and present an opportunity to cross-sell [30,49]. In the long term, it is possible to establish customer loyalty [49]. In gist, explanationfeatured decision aid holds the promise to provide a better personalized product recommendation experience to consumers, bene<sup>fi</sup>ting electronic commerce websites and consumers alike.

## 2. Explanation-featured decision aid

Explanation is often provided by the information provider (e.g., the decision aid in this case) to clarify, justify or convince the recipient (i.e., the consumers) to accept the content or the recommendations proposed [48,63]. More elaborately, explanation is furnished when an individual is externally expected by others or internally required by him/her to justify the decisions so as 1) to enhance his/her selfesteem [23] and/or others' perception of his/her wisdom [1], and 2) to account to those who are likely to evaluate his/her choices, e.g., friends and spouse [53]. In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst review the notion of explanation and then build on this to identify three forms of explanation-featured decision aid for investigation.

## 2.1. Theoretical background of explanation provision

Explanation reduces the anomalous in the recommendations [39] and clari<sup>fi</sup>es why the decision aid believes the recommended alternatives are suitable for a consumer [20]. By exposing the underlying reasoning mechanism governing the decision aid's selection and recommendation of the product options, the product recommendation transparency increases [27]. When a consumer is able to understand and better appreciate the product alternatives recommended, consumer's decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence, trust and acceptance of the decision aid as well as customer loyalty are enhanced [4,18,51,65,66,68]. Conversely, a consumer may be more caution of a decision aid's product recommendations should they be perceived as lacking predictability (i.e., reasoning) with respect to his/her preferences or unclear of the steps taken by the decision aids in deriving the recommended product options [22].

It is to our contention that provision of explanation may also lead to unforeseen impact on consumer decision making. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, consumers must make probability judgments on whether the products to be purchased are indeed suited to their actual preferences given the recommendations made by the decision aid. Presumably, consumers would have purchased the best products if the attribute values are mostly closely matched or better than the consumers' preferences. Unfortunately, people are prone to suffer from having excessive con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their knowledge and judgment, i.e., their con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their judgments exceeds the accuracy of those judgments [32,34]. The marketing literature provides further support for the overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence bias among decision makers. For instance, marketing managers are susceptible to overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence especially when dealing with dif<sup>fi</sup>cult or infrequent decisions [38,40]. Overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence bias could be a non-trivial problem in online shopping given the increasing amount of product information being made available to consumers [56]. Even though explanation-featured decision aid can reduce the number of product alternatives that the consumers viewed and evaluated [26], there are still signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cognitive-laden tasks that still need to be performed by the consumers [28]. In particular, consumers must still delineate their preferences and deliberate on the recommendations provided by the decision aid as well as the associated explanations.

According to Grif<sup>fi</sup>n and Tversky [21], the root cause of overcon<sup>fi</sup>- dence bias is that decision makers tend to give greater attention to the strength of the evidences pointing to speci<sup>fi</sup>c uncertain outcomes. The weight or credence of the sources of the evidences and background information, i.e., base rate, are accorded far lesser importance. In spite of this, the fact remains that a piece of strong or weak evidence may come from a reliable or unreliable source. More importantly, when the ability to discriminate between the strength of competing evidences is low, consumers could suffer from overly high con<sup>fi</sup>dence [21]. A key implication of this supposition to our present study is that explanation is intended to clarify, justify and convince the information recipient to accept the content [31] and therefore strengthens the decision aid's recommendations. To this end, we posit that different types of explanation could increase the strength of the decision aid's recommendations to varying degree. Consequently, each type of explanation could lead to various degree of decision overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence that affects the decision quality directly.

## 2.2. Types of explanation-featured decision aid

How then should an explanation aid be designed? To answer this question, we <sup>fi</sup>rst need to understand the underlying philosophy of explanation, which could transpire in two forms [20,39,48]. First, explanation is the associations between antecedents and consequents that accounts for the relationship between cause and effect. Second, explanation could also serve as the causal mechanism relating an individual's past experience to the present. Relating the two means of explanation to decision aiding context would suggest that an explanation-featured decision aid would need to satisfy two important objectives [31]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the system ought to 1) provide knowledge and explanations necessary for the user to carry out his or her task, and/or 2) perform certain actions and then explains the need and rationale for doing so.

Building on the two design requirements for providing explanation, several researchers have proposed several taxonomies (see Table 1). For instance, Chandrasekaran et al. [11] conceived explanation could be inoculated into a system by implanting an information structure containing reasoning elements and then dynamically putting together appropriate elements, known as introspection. Introspection refers to the examination of a record (e.g., a product alternative) of its own problem-solving activity (i.e., using the elicited preferences to evaluate the product alternative) and picking up appropriate traces (i.e., explanations) helpful for a consumer in making the purchase decision. Building the explanation content on introspection of the system's own problem-solving behavior, several scholars proposed different explanation types [20,69].

Among the several other research expeditions, the seminal work by Gregor and Benbasat [20] receives the greatest attention due to its comprehensiveness and enduring relevance. According to the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme of Gregor and Benbasat [20], there are four variations of explanation feature, namely Trace, Justi<sup>fi</sup>cation, Strategic and Terminological.

In this study, we will focus on three types, i.e., Trace (TRACE; Type I), Justi<sup>fi</sup>cation (JUST; Type II) and Strategic (STRG; Type III), which are more applicable to the later stage of the decision process, i.e., product alternative evaluation and choice. Terminological (i.e., Type IV) explanation is not examined because it involves de<sup>fi</sup>nitional information to explain the product attributes. Terminological explanation is more closely resembling the “how explanation” in Wang and Benbasat [65] input explanation classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, which is more applicable to the earlier preference elicitation and <sup>fi</sup>ltering stage and not at the latter evaluation stage.

Our review of the extant commercial implementations offers supporting evidence indicating the popularity of the three types of explanation features selected, i.e., TRACE (for instance CarWale website at http://www.carwale.com), JUST (for instance Yahoo! Shopping SmartSort at http://shopping.yahoo.com/smartsort) and STRG (for instance MovieLens at http://movielens.umn.edu and Shopping.com at http://www.shopping.com) [4,18,51]. Fig. 1 depicts sample system screenshots of some of the explanation implementations. It is to be highlighted that the three studied explanations could fall along a continuum of decreasing amount of reasoning details provided, with TRACE having the greatest amount of details and STRG with the least amount of reasoning details furnished [6,31,39].

The notion that different explanations differ in the degree of elaboration is well studied in the extant literature [11,20] and may be dissected from two dimensions. First, explanations that can provide deep domain knowledge relevant to the decision context typically offer a higher degree of elaboration [20]. This is because deep domain knowledge lends credence to the decision recommended. Second, case speci<sup>fi</sup>c explanations offer a higher degree of elaboration compared to generic explanations [11,20]. The former utilizes data or fact drawn from the particular decision case whereas the latter relies on description of general decision making strategies. Case speci<sup>fi</sup>c explanations can thus explicitly clarify the relationship between the decision maker's preferences and the decision aid's recommendations.

TRACE is conceptualized as a series of steps taken by the decision aid to generate its recommendations [20,69]. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, TRACE provides an intuitive representation of the internal rule used by the decision aid. This representation encompasses the procedure for combining the component information (i.e., the product attribute values and preferences) to derive a value for the recommendation [6]. Based on extant commercial implementations such as CarWale, TRACE can manifest as an extended textual description, tabular data or iconic symbol explaining the generation of the recommendations. The tracing process involves either comparing 1) the consumer's preferences for the speci<sup>fi</sup>c subset of attributes against the actual attributes values of each alternative; or 2) the derivation of the overall quality of each alternative against the consumer's aggregated preference across all relevant attributes. For instance, the TRACE explanation provided by the CarWale Automated Car Recommendations system is based on the former technique (top panel of Fig. 1). TRACE thus focuses more on explaining how the recommendation is derived, i.e., clarifying the relationship between the customer's input preferences to the decision aid's output recommendations [51]. It possesses deep domain knowledge and is case speci<sup>fi</sup>c.

JUST is an explicit description of the underlying rationale for the decision aid's recommendations [69]. In particular, JUST draws on the domain knowledge associated with the decision, i.e., the product attribute values and preferences in our context, to justify the recommendation process [6,20]. However, unlike TRACE, JUST does not detail the exact inferential steps taken by the decision aid. To this end, JUST closely resembles the “why” aspect of the knowledge-based explanation proposed by Wang and Benbasat [66]. In effect, JUST explains to the customer why the decision aid recommends certain product alternatives. Building on these theoretical foundation and extant commercial implementations such as Yahoo! Shopping SmartSort, JUST is conceptualized as a shorter textual description that narrates why a particular alternative is selected or matched to the consumer's preference. This textual description is based on a single attribute or speci<sup>fi</sup>c subset of attributes from the set of all attributes in which a preference is explicitly stated. For instance, the JUST explanation provided by the decision aid on Yahoo! Shopping SmartSort justi<sup>fi</sup>es why a particular alternative is ranked the top three overall higher or better than the next alternative by highlighting the superior attributes (middle panel of Fig. 1). Although JUST also possesses deep domain knowledge and is case speci<sup>fi</sup>c, its domain knowledge lacks the crucial ability to illustrate the inferential process.

STRG de<sup>fi</sup>nes the high level decision strategy used by the decision aid in formulating its recommendations [6,20]. STRG neither details each step of the decision strategy nor attempts to explain the underlying rationale for the recommendations. Rather, STRG focuses on providing straightforward information about the decision strategy employed and thus lacks the reasoning ability of TRACE and JUST [69]. To this extent, commercial implementations of STRG typically do not de<sup>fi</sup>ne the decision strategy. Rather, STRG is conceptualized as a single piece of aggregated information that represents the outcome of the high level strategy used to derive the recommendations. To illustrate, consider a strategy in which the decision aid recommends alternatives that have high overall quality. Overall quality could be determined by calculating the weighted additive score for each alternative [43]. In this example, a typical STRG would simply be “because we want you to purchase the product with the best quality, our strategy is to recommend those items with the highest weighted additive score” [11,20]. Instead of displaying this statement to consumers, most shopping websites implementing STRG would either show the numerical score or a single piece of information corresponding to the numerical score, e.g., ordinal rank of the alternative based on the numerical score.

Indeed, STRG typically manifests in the form of numeric ranking, percentage rating or star rating that summarizes the overall quality of the alternatives with respect to all evaluated attributes. On some commercial websites (e.g., Shopping.com), numerical ranking style STRG is provided implicitly in the format of sorting by closet match or search relevancy (see bottom panel of Fig. 1). ## 3. Research model and hypotheses development

Fig. 2 depicts the research model that assesses the impact of different explanation-featured decision aid on the consumer decision making process and outcome. Explanation-featured decision aids are manifested in three forms (in descending level of the explanation detail provided): 1) TRACE, 2) JUST and 3) STRG. We assess the decision making process and outcome based on two criteria — the subjective assessment and the objective decision outcomes. The subjective assessment is the decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence with the product choice made arising from the use of the explanation-featured decision aid. The objective decision outcome is re<sup>fl</sup>ected by two variables, namely decision time (proxy for cognitive effort) and decision quality. ## 3.1. Perceived confidence of decision made

Prior studies have demonstrated that consumers can develop positive affect towards the decision aid and/or the product choice made as a result of using it [27,66]. Moreover, a decision aid that provides explanation can improve transparency of the recommendation process by illustrating to consumers how and why it derives the recommendation [66]. Such a decision aid can thus induce an even greater degree of trust in consumers [27]. For instance, MovieLens is an online decision aid that recommends movies to users based on collaborative-<sup>fi</sup>ltering techniques [27]. It is observed that the addition of explanation facilities could increase the acceptance of MovieLens and yield greater adherence to the system's recommendations [6]. Through the explanation-featured decision aid, a consumer is able to learn about how the presented product alternatives match/address his elicited preferences, thereby leading to higher degree of perceived con<sup>fi</sup>dence resulted from the decision made.

However, we posit that in the situation where the degree of details offered by different explanation-featured decision aids varies, the decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence could also differ across the various explanation types, i.e., TRACE, JUST and STRG. JUST has the ability to facilitate the consumer in comprehending the exact attributes that are superior or inferior to the consumer own preference as well as relative to another alternative. On the one hand, since TRACE could also achieve the same purpose as JUST but also details the reasoning process taken by the decision aid as it inspect each and every attribute [6,39], we posit that TRACE should be better than JUST in strengthening the decision aid's explanation. On the other hand, although STRG informs the consumer the relative quality of each alternative, it is lacking in details and thus could be inferior to JUST. Both expert and novice users of knowledge-based system have been found to consistently prefer TRACE explanation more, followed by JUST and STRG [39]. This <sup>fi</sup>nding collaborates well with our reasoning that TRACE possesses more details than JUST, which in turn possesses more details than STRG. In other words, TRACE is most useful to users of the knowledge-based system since it contains the greatest amount of details.

The decision overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence paradigm suggests that consumers are likely to give greater attention to the explanation provided by the decision aid to justify its recommendation [21]. This means that consumers are likely to attach a higher credence to the explanations in the belief the products currently under scrutiny are indeed suited to their preferences. At the same time, consumers could disregard other background information such as the values of other attributes not mentioned in the explanation, which might make a particular alternative less attractive. Thus, consumers are likely to suffer from overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence in their purchase decision [32,34]. Extending this notion, we posit that an explanation type possessing a greater amount of details could aggravate the overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence situation more. We thus hypothesize:

H1. The degree of explanation elaboration offered by a decision aid is positively related to the perceived decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence.

## 3.2. Decision time

Explanations on how consumers' preferences and requirements are translated into product recommendations are provided in a decision aid so as to assist the consumers in making the <sup>fi</sup>nal purchase decision [27,50,51,65]. Consequently, consumers can often bene<sup>fi</sup>t from lower search effort [25,26,59] and lower search time [44,64]. Insofar as explanation of increasing strength can increase a consumer's con<sup>fi</sup>- dence, we further postulate that the consumer will be able to make a purchase in a shorter time. Indeed, prior researchers have shown that providing more information to decision makers can make them feel more con<sup>fi</sup>dent regardless of whether the information provided is useful [47]. In addition, higher con<sup>fi</sup>dence levels among consumers have often been associated with a lower perception of mental load involved in the choice process [70,71]. A more elaborated explanationfeatured decision aid could thus lead to lesser time taken to make a decision compared with another that has less detail elaboration (e.g., time taken to derive a decision using TRACE is lesser than time taken to make a decision using STRG).

From another related perspective, consumers who need to explain the rationale for choosing a particular product alternative or to convince others of their choice typically spend more time than if no justi<sup>fi</sup>cation is required [29]. This is due to the need to discover and process greater amount of more complex information to derive the required justi<sup>fi</sup>cation. More elaborated explanation-featured decision aid is capable of providing additional knowledge to support a consumer's choice and thus reinforces his/her conviction to purchase the chosen alternative [13]. In a way, this is similar to the con<sup>fi</sup>dence enhancement effect that is associated with providing decision maker with more information [47]. The extant literature contains evidence supporting this proposition. Compared to expert consumers, novice consumers with minimal product knowledge using explanation-featured decision aid are likely to purchase an alternative that is more closely matched to their preferences [41]. That is, novice consumers feel more con<sup>fi</sup>dent with the decision aid's recommendation when explanations are provided. Since more elaborated explanation-featured decision aid can provide the required knowledge to aid consumers in choosing the required alternative and formulate a justi<sup>fi</sup>cation, the shopping time should be proportionally lower compared to less elaborated explanation-featured decision aid.

According to the decision overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence paradigm, the provision of more elaborated explanation is likely to aggravate the overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence situation more [21]. We should therefore observe shorter purchase time coupled with lesser time spent on seeking self-justi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the use of more elaborated explanation-featured decision aid. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2. The degree of explanation elaboration offered by a decision aid is negatively related to the decision time (i.e., decision effort).

## 3.3. Decision quality

Prior research has shown that decision quality increases if consumers perceive the decision aid as being similar to themselves [2]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, if consumers believe that the decision aid would take into consideration their preferences for different product attributes and/or choose alternatives using similar decision strategy as what they would have used, then consumers would be more inclined to accept the recommendations made by the decision aid. Consequently, consumers are more likely to choose the alternative recommended by the decision aid, which is typically of a higher quality. If a decision aid's explanation can help consumers to understand how the recommendations are generated, this similarity perception should increase and thus improving decision quality. Indeed, the use of decision aid generally leads to improved decision quality in the products purchased by consumers [25,26,28].

However, an important quali<sup>fi</sup>cation to note is that these prior studies [25,26,28] compared consumers who used decision aids against consumers who did not. Under this condition, it is hardly surprising that the use of decision aids led to improved decision quality. Whether or not consumers bene<sup>fi</sup>t from better decision quality if provided decision aids with different features remains an unanswered critical question in the extant literature. A rare exception is the work of Tan and his colleagues [61]. However, even this study only focused on how different screening strategy-featured decision aids that support product <sup>fi</sup>ltering, i.e., the <sup>fi</sup>rst form of product recommendation, affect decision quality. Our present study thus attempts to provide a plausible answer by examining explanation, i.e., the second form of product recommendation, since it is the primary decision aid feature that is widely adopted in commercial shopping websites to help consumers understand how the recommendations are derived from the <sup>fi</sup>rst form of product recommendation.

Nonetheless, even when comparing decision quality among consumers who used decision aids against those who did not, the associated bene<sup>fi</sup>ts have been challenged by some researchers. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a decision aid could induce effort minimization and cognitive laziness in consumers thus leading to blind acceptance of satis<sup>fi</sup>cing outcomes that may compromise decision quality [2]. This counterargument draws parallel with similar <sup>fi</sup>ndings of negative consequences from using of<sup>fl</sup>ine decision aids, i.e., reducing cognitive effort at the expense of increasing decision quality [19,54,62]. One of the key underlying reasons is because consumers who are provided with a decision aid are less likely to actively seek out information that would otherwise have helped them to choose a better alternative [19]. Instead, consumers tend to rely on the recommendations made by the decision aid even though these recommendations might be inferior. Indeed, extant research in the area of psychology has highlighted that more information, i.e., a more elaborated explanation in our context, could indeed cause negative implication. For instance, Einhorn and Hogarth have pointed out that providing decision makers with more information to make their decisions only increases con<sup>fi</sup>dence but not accuracy [15]. In fact, even when the information provided is consistent or extreme, i.e., information characteristics that supposedly increase con<sup>fi</sup>dence, it is still plausible that the decision accuracy might be negatively affected [15,33]. Other researchers have suggested that it is easier for decision makers to estimate the effort involved in decision making compared to accuracy [35]. Consequently, decision makers might focus more on reducing decision efforts instead of increasing decision accuracy [2].

The above line of reasoning is also in agreement with the decision overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence bias paradigm [32,34,37], which suggests that consumers using a decision aid have a tendency to overestimate their knowledge and judgment of whether the chosen product is indeed the best. The explanation provided by the decision aid strengthens the consumer's perception that the current alternative is indeed the best suited to his or her preferences. Such a display of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence could induce cognitive laziness in the consumer, causing him or her to misjudge a particular alternative as the best without evaluating additional ones [2]. This, however, does not imply that the consumer will not expend the necessary effort to search for the desired product. Rather, consumer simply has lesser motivation to lengthen the search process. In fact, the use of decision aid can potentially reduce the effort involved in making a purchase decision [16]. In other words, a genuine consumer will typically expend the required effort to buy a product matching one's preferences [12] although the cognitive laziness induced by overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence will discourage them from searching for the best possible product [2].

Clearly, cognitive laziness is a non-trivial problem when comparing consumers who use a decision aid against those who do not because the decision aid is so effective in helping consumers to identify their desired products to the extent that overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence sets in. In a similar fashion, different types of explanation that are provided by the decision aid have differential effectiveness and thus the degree of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence that is triggered differs too. To this extent, the cognitive laziness problem might be more serious when it comes to certain explanation types and negatively affect decision quality more compared to others. In other words, we posit that an explanation perceived as having greater strength should lead to a higher possibility of purchasing a lower quality alternative due to a higher overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence bias induced [9,24]. Essentially, a more elaborated explanation-featured decision aid could lead to lower decision quality on the product alternative chosen than another with less elaboration on the details. We hence hypothesize:

H3. The degree of explanation elaboration offered by a decision aid is negatively related to the decision quality of the product chosen.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Research design

We employed a one-way factorial design with explanation-featured decision aid as the only independent variable of three treatment levels (TRACE, JUST and STRG). Our study takes a similar approach to Kumar and Benbasat [36] by studying the commercial implementations and extracting the product content of commercial websites to manipulate the decision aids offered for explanation-based support. With respect to the three forms of explanation-featured decision aids, we referenced the explanation types postulated in prior explanation literature [20] as well as existing commercial implementations, e.g., CarWale, Yahoo! Shopping Smartsort, Shopping.com, MovieLens, etc. For TRACE, the underlying mechanism is a tabular format depicting the calculation of the weighted additive score and also indicates that whether the current item's score is better than the participant's preference (left panel of Fig. 3). A value of 0.00 denotes that the participant did not provide a preference for that particular attribute. The standardized values were adjusted so that the maximum score is 100.

JUST was operationalized as a textual element that compares the alternative's attribute weighted values to the participant's preferences for those attributes that the participant had indicated a preference. Depending on the weighted score of each attribute, JUST describes verbosely why an alternative was recommended because a particular attribute was bad, average, good, very good or excellent. For the remaining attributes in which the participant did not indicate a preference, JUST also provides a textual comparison between the current alternative and the next item in the result list (top right panel of Fig. 3).

STRG was operationalized as a numerical rank of the alternative based on the weighted additive score (bottom right panel of Fig. 3). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, STRG consisted of a textual description of the form “This item is ranked XX”, where XX is the numerical rank of the alternative based on the weighted additive score and a lower rank denotes better quality, i.e., 1 is the best.

The product data and images used in the experiment were actual datasets crawled from a commercial shopping website based in the United States. The product <sup>fi</sup>ltering and screening prior to activating the explanation feature was implemented as a variant of weighted additive [2,46,58,60] that is commonly found in commercial shopping websites (for instances Yahoo! Shopping SmartSort and Moneysupermarket.com at http://www.moneysupermarket.com) (top panel of Fig. 4). Weighted additive considers the quality of each alternative based on the values of all relevant attributes together with the corresponding attribute weights stated by the decision maker [43]. An overall evaluation of the alternative, i.e. the weighted additive score, is calculated by multiplying the weight times the attribute value for each attribute and summing these weighted attribute values over all attributes. Weighted additive thus explicitly takes into consideration the decision maker's preferences. The alternative with the highest weighted additive score, i.e., the best quality, is assumed to be chosen by the decision maker. Our implementation of weighted additive <sup>fi</sup>lters out alternative that are below the consumer's expressed attribute values and weights in order to ease the cognitive load of consumers. In other words, the decision aid only recommends alternatives whose weighted additive score is at least better than the consumer's preferences. The recommended alternatives are displayed to the participants in a random order so that they could not easily <sup>fi</sup>nd the best alternative.

The main layout and design of the website were the same across all treatments with the exception of the explanation-featured decision aids, which were treatment speci<sup>fi</sup>c. More importantly, no other additional online shopping website feature that could aid in the decision making process was provided to avoid any confounding effect. Basic navigation and information elements such as number of result and result set paging as well as viewing of product details were made available nonetheless (bottom panels of Fig. 4 respectively).

In this study we examined the differentiated impacts of the explanation-featured decision aids on three dependent variables, namely perceived decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence, decision time and decision quality. Perceived decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence was measured with a set of three survey items based on seven-point Likert scale. This scale was administered once at the end of each purchase task (see Table 2). Decision quality was operationalized as an objective measure to determine whether the participant had selected the best alternative recommended by the decision aid. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the measure was an indication of the quality of the item the consumer purchased relative to the result set returned by the decision aid. This measure is based on the weighted additive score numerical rank of the item. The formula for calculating the decision quality is as follow:

TRACE illustrates how the weighted additive score for a particular product alternative is calculated and also states the actual score. The scores of all N recommended products are sorted in descending order and the top product is ranked, i.e., R, 1 and the last product is ranked N. R is described in the STRG explanation explicitly. However, TRACE contains much more information than STRG, including detailed attribute-by-attribute comparisons. Thus, TRACE is more elaborated than STRG. Although JUST neither explicitly states the weighted additive score nor R, it provides a verbose description of the quality of each attributes of relevance to the participant based on the weighted score of each attribute. This is similar to TRACE except that the actual numerical weighted scores are not disclosed. To this extent, we will consider JUST to be more elaborated than STRG but less so than TRACE.

## 4.2. Experimental controls

The individual differences of the participants such as age, gender and experience that could potentially affect decision making and its outcomes were controlled by random assignment of participants to different treatments. The physical environment, i.e. the computer laboratory and workstation, and software environment were similar for all participants across treatments. In addition, we took into consideration three theoretical control variables that could confound the results of the experiment.

Prior research study has suggested that there could be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interaction between product category knowledge and decision quality [59]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, participants with high prior product knowledge might not give due consideration to the recommendations and explanations given by the decision aid. Moreover, consumers who lack adequate product knowledge are more likely to exhibit higher degree of con-<sup>fi</sup>dence [37]. Thus, it is imperative to control for the participants' prior product knowledge statistically. Prior product knowledge was measured before each purchase task using a four items seven-point Likert scale as shown in Table 2. The cognitive capacity of the participants was measured as a control to ensure that the participants did not have any dif<sup>fi</sup>culty interacting with the decision aid. Cognitive capacity was operationalized as a three items seven-point Likert scale shown in Table 2. Finally, since novice and expert users of the decision aid could have different preference for the explanation type [6,39], we also controlled for participants' prior knowledge with using the decision aid. This was operationalized as a three items seven-point Likert scale as shown in Table 2.

## 4.3. Experimental task and procedures

The experimental task required each participant to complete four purchase tasks, with the assistance of the assigned explanation-featured decision aid, one from each of four product categories. These product categories were specially chosen based on the problem size classi<sup>fi</sup>cation along the two dimensions of alternative size and attribute size that is commonly used in decision making studies [8]: 1) thumbdrive (easy — 425 × 7); 2) MP3 player (moderate — 801 × 12); 3) digital camera (moderate $- 8 5 1 \times 2 0 )$ ; and 4) laptop (complex — 1581×20). The sequence of purchase in which the participant proceeded was balanced within each treatment.

The experimental tasks were simulated as close as possible to a real online purchase experience but the participants' browsing activities were restricted. The experiment proceeded in the following sequence. Participants reported to the computer laboratory and were then randomly assigned to a vacant workstation. Participants were then briefed thoroughly on the entire experimental procedures. The experiment begins with the participants completing a brief demographic questionnaire and a pre-questionnaire for the control variables. Next, participants read a simple tutorial on how to navigate around the online shopping website and use the decision aid. This tutorial was customized for each treatment and showed the participants what they would expect to see during the actual experimental task. For instance, participants were told that the products were ordered randomly and they could not sort the list. The tutorial also drew the participants' attention to the fact that explanations were available to help them choose the desired products and that these explanations were intended to inform them as to why the decision aid had recommended the particular products. The tutorial, however, did not explain to the participants how the explanations were generated or how to interpret them. Essentially, all participants were shown the same tutorial contents. The only difference was that the sample explanation images shown to the participants across treatments were different. This was done to minimize differences in the experimental conditions and ensured that no other extraneous factors affected the experiment outcome. Finally, the participants were shown an opening narrative, which asked them to imagine that they had just received their <sup>fi</sup>rst salary after graduating from the university and were eager to purchase four gifts for themselves as a reward.

The participants were then asked to commence the purchase tasks proper. For each purchase task, the participants began by using the product <sup>fi</sup>ltering and screening tool to indicate their preferences (see top panel of Fig. 4). After submitting the product preferences, the decision aid will return a list of matching alternatives. Participants browse through the list of alternatives by reading the product title and description and viewing a thumbnail image of the product. Participants could also view the product details, which would show the complete product attribute information for the selected alternative, and the explanations generated by the decision aid. Fig. 5 shows a complete screen layout of the experimental system taken with a screen capturing software. Once the participants had decided on the alternative to purchase, they would click on the respective “Buy Now” link. A con<sup>fi</sup>rmation prompt would be shown to the participants, which allowed the participants to cancel the purchase in case of a mistake. If the participants con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the purchase, they would be redirected to the next purchase task. At the end of the fourth purchase task, the participants would be redirected to the main questionnaire page.

No speci<sup>fi</sup>c time limit was enforced and participants were allowed to complete the purchase tasks at their own pace. The experiment took place in a large public university using 54 student participants, 18 per treatment, recruited through electronic mail. To motivate the participants, they were paid a guaranteed amount of \$5 and an additional random bonus ranging from \$1 to \$10 for about an hour's work. However, the participants were told that the bonus will be pegged to how closely the purchased items were matched to their preferences. This incentive scheme ensured that the participants would work on the purchase tasks seriously and purchase the best item matched to their preferences. The participants were also provided with paper and pen in case they needed to jot down notes to aid them in making the purchase decision.

## 5.1. Hypotheses testing

All statistical tests were conducted at a <sup>fi</sup>ve-percent level of signi<sup>fi</sup>- cance. To control for the possible in<sup>fl</sup>uences of prior product knowledge, cognitive capacity and prior decision aid knowledge on the dependent variables, we included them as covariates in the statistical tests. We also controlled for the product category and purchase sequence. Multivariate Analysis of Covariance (MANCOVA) and Univariate Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) tests were used to assess the effects of the manipulated variable (i.e., explanation-featured decision aids) on decision making process and outcome (i.e., perceived decision con<sup>fi</sup>- dence, decision time and decision quality).

A MANCOVA was <sup>fi</sup>rst performed to test the hypotheses and to control for Type I error in<sup>fl</sup>ation. For the main independent variable of explanation, Wilks' Lambda=0.835, F (6, 408)=5.998, pb0.001. This indicates that there are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between the three treatments for all the dependent variables. The hypotheses were then tested with three separate ANCOVA. Table 4 summarizes the test results.

H1 posits that there is a positive relationship between the degree of explanation elaboration offered by the decision aid and the perceived decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence. From Table 4 (top panel), we observed a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant main effect of the explanation-featured decision aid on decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence. Inspecting the chart at the right-hand side suggests that participants using TRACE (i.e., the decision aid with most detailed elaboration of the explanation) expressed the highest degree of con<sup>fi</sup>dence, followed by those using JUST and STRG consecutively. Hence, H1 was supported. We further performed post-hoc pair-wise comparison of differences among the explanation-featured decision aids on perceived decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence using the Bonferroni's adjustment tests. As detailed in Table 5, the greatest signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference among the three forms' effects was observed between the use of JUST (or TRACE) and STRG. This could suggest that participants with STRG expressed the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, lowest degree of decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence.

H2 argues that the degree of explanation elaboration offered by a decision aid is negatively related to the decision time. From the results of the ANCOVA in Table 4 (middle panel), we observed a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant main effect of the explanation-featured decision aid. Inspecting the means suggest that as the degree of explanation elaboration offered by the decision aid increases, the time taken to make a decision decreases $( M e a n S T R G = 8 . 5 0 9 ;$ MeanJUST=6.932; MeanTRACE=6.626). Hence, H2 was supported. It is interesting to note the post-hoc comparison of differences using the Bonferroni's adjustment tests suggest that users of both TRACE and JUST expended signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lesser time to complete the purchase tasks compared to those with STRG.

H3 dictates that the degree of explanation elaboration offered by a decision aid is negatively related to the decision quality of the product chosen. The ANCOVA results (See Table 4 lower panel) suggest that there is a main effect of the explanation-featured decision aid on decision quality. Inspecting both the mean chart and the mean statistics reveal that as the amount of elaboration of explanation increases, the decision quality decreases $( M e a n S T R G = 0 . 6 5 1$ ; MeanJUST = 0.510; $M e a n T R A C E = 0 . 4 9 2 )$ . Hence, H3 was supported. The post-hoc analysis further revealed that users of both TRACE and JUST yielded signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower decision quality compared to those with STRG.

## 5.2. Test of robustness for decision quality

In this study, product quality was not explicitly varied. The experiment was conducted by having the participants to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the product preference <sup>fi</sup>ltering criteria. The system (i.e., the product <sup>fi</sup>ltering and screening mechanism) then used the de<sup>fi</sup>ned criteria to extract the relevant product alternative. In other words, the list of product alternatives presented to the experiment participants was contingent on the preference <sup>fi</sup>lter criteria provided by the participants. Thus, it was technically challenging to have product quality as an independent variable since the resultant set of product alternatives had to be extracted based on the <sup>fi</sup>lter criteria. If explicit manipulation was to be enforced on product quality, the resultant set of product alternatives might not be what the consumers had wanted. This could potentially confound the empirical investigation. Nonetheless, a post-hoc test of robustness was performed to demonstrate that H3 holds regardless of product quality.

The product <sup>fi</sup>ltering and screening mechanism was implemented as a variant of weighted additive. The weighted additive score, which represents product quality, was <sup>fi</sup>rst calculated for each product item purchased by the participants across all four purchase tasks. Using kmeans clustering, the product items were split into two categories, i.e., low product quality and high product quality. An ANCOVA test was then performed with explanation-featured decision aid and product quality as the two independent variables. As shown in Table 6, mean decision quality scores for all three types of explanation-featured decision aid under high product quality are uniformly higher than those under low product quality. More importantly, under both low product quality and high product quality, mean decision quality scores for STRG are higher than those for JUST, which in turn are higher than those for TRACE. Although the ANCOVA results in Table 7 show that the interaction effect between explanation-featured decision aid and product quality only approaches but does not reach statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance, we may be reasonably assured that H3 holds regardless of product quality.

Based on the prior work on explanation [22,27] and overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence bias paradigm, we propose that a more elaborated explanation-featured decision aid could increase a consumer's con<sup>fi</sup>dence over the purchase decision made, leading to lesser cognitive effort expended but yielding a lower decision quality product choice made. The statistical results from the experiment generally support our conjecture by adding that there is a smaller difference in the impact between an excessively elaborated explanation-featured decision aid like TRACE and a slightly less elaborated explanation-featured decision support like JUST; however, the effect difference of these two explanation features and the least elaborated explanation-featured decision aid of STRG is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant.

Although the statistical results support our conjecture, some readers might have reservation that this seemingly counter-intuitive <sup>fi</sup>nding might be an experimental artifact rather than true causality. An in depth analysis of the experimental design might help to alleviate this concern. First, real product datasets crawled from a large-scale commercial shopping website was used in the experiment. The products in each category varied widely in their attribute values and there are no obvious dominating alternatives. Second, the decision aid used in the experimental shopping website employed a variant of weighted additive decision strategy that <sup>fi</sup>ltered out products that were below the participants' preferences. Thus, all of the recommended products at least met the participants' preferences. In other words, even if a participant had chosen a product with a low decision quality, that product would still at least have met the participant's preferences. Third, the recommended products were displayed to the participants in a random order. The participants were supposed to browse through the recommendation lists and used the explanations provided to decide which products to purchase. Fourth, no other decision aid features beyond the manipulated explanations were provided to the participants.

Fifth, and the most important feature in our opinion, the participants were asked to buy something for themselves, i.e., something that they liked or met their preferences, and not the top ranked or best products. If the latter was indeed the case, then participants could have just as easily found the top ranked products with TRACE, JUST or STRG since all of them are good indicators of decision quality. For TRACE — products with high weighted additive scores, for JUST — products with good qualitative attributes descriptions and for STRG — good ranking products. Sixth, closely related to the <sup>fi</sup>fth feature, the incentive structure was designed to ensure that the participants had purchased products that most closely matched their preferences and not the top ranked or best products. We also believe that the incentive provided suf<sup>fi</sup>cient motivation for the participants to expend the required cognitive effort, to the extent necessary to <sup>fi</sup>nd the desired products, for: 1) expressing their preferences; 2) scrutinizing the recommended products; and

3) reading the explanations. However, and more importantly, there is no reason to suspect that the same incentive had induced participants to expend additional cognitive effort to seek out the top ranked or best products. This was because the participants were never asked to do so in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place.

The above experimental design features ensured that supposed all participants had expended suf<sup>fi</sup>cient efforts in the purchase tasks, they would have all purchased the best products, i.e., decision quality closed to 1, regardless of the type of explanation-featured decision aid provided. Results from our statistical analysis clearly showed that this was not the case. The fact that decision quality among participants differed must be attributable to the differential impact each type of explanation-featured decision aid had exerted on decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence and effort. This was because the type of decision aid used by the participants was the only factor that varied across treatments. If any of the decision aids was biased, we would have observed the decision quality scores of participants assigned to a particular treatment to concentrate within a narrow range. However, the scatter plot of decision quality against explanation-featured decision aid depicted in Fig. 6 suggests otherwise. The scatter plot shows clearly that across the three explanation-featured decision aids, there were participants who obtained decision quality scores in the low, medium and high range. In our opinion, the statistical results re<sup>fl</sup>ect true causality attributable to the decision overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence paradigm.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings contrast and add to the extant understanding of embedding explanation to the extant online decision aiding implementations [65,66,68] by highlighting that offering elaborated explanations may not serve consumers well in term of decision quality despite heightening their perception (i.e., decision con<sup>fi</sup>dence). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our study suggests that the provision of output explanations by the decision aid to explain its recommendations to the consumers could potentially have an ironic twist with regard to decision outcomes. Explanations that confer greater strength to the decision aid's product recommendations boost consumers' con<sup>fi</sup>dence to the extent that the decision time taken becomes shorter [70,71] and they purchase alternatives that might not be the best.

Moreover, our observation on decision effort and decision quality is consistent with the effort–accuracy tradeoff notion [43]. That is, consumers are willing to trade off decision accuracy in order to minimize the amount of effort required to make the decision [20]. In other words, consumers need not always purchase the best alternative among the provided recommendations but instead place an equal, if not higher, emphasis on a positive shopping experience that is less cognitive taxing [7]. This appears to be the case for participants using the TRACE explanation-featured decision aid. Participants using STRG explanation-featured decision aid are seemingly willing to expend more effort to purchase higher quality product.

## 6.2. Limitations and future research

As in all research studies, our present study also has four limitations that readers should be cautioned. First, this study examined the use of explanation-featured decision aids for purchasing consumer electronic products, which are essentially search products [67]. Given that product attributes information, which form the main content of the explanation, can only effectively communicate consumption bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for search products [3], future research should examine the usefulness of providing explanation to aid in purchasing experience and credence products [67]. Second, this study uses the variant of weighted additive decision strategy for the <sup>fi</sup>ltering and screening of the product alternatives. Since different decision strategies can vary greatly in their characteristics [43], future research should examine other relevant ones. For instance, the elimination-by-aspect decision strategy is also commonly used in commercial implementations of decision aid (for instances mySimon at http://www.mysimon.com and CNET Shopper.com at http://shopper. cnet.com). Third, although this study has shown, contrary to popular misconception, that providing more information could be worst off for the consumers, further research is required to determine if this <sup>fi</sup>nding may be generalized to other decision aid contexts. For instance, do presentation format and provision mechanism of the explanation [16] moderate the impact of the degree of explanation elaboration? Derivations of TRACE, JUST and TRACE, which differ from our operationalizations, as might be used in other commercial shopping websites should also be the subjects of future research.

Last, this study uses student participants. Thus, the generalizability of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this study is contingent on how closely student participants behave compared to actual shoppers. We reason that at least with respect to the four product categories used in the experiment, the use of random participant assignments and statistical controls of prior product and decision aid knowledge should ensure a high behavioral similarity between the student participants and actual shoppers. Despite the above limitations of the study, which also highlight future research opportunities, this study has made several noteworthy contributions.

## 6.3. Contributions

Theoretically, prior research has hitherto focused only on input explanation features that support the initial <sup>fi</sup>ltering of product alternatives based on the elicited preferences. Thus, our present study <sup>fi</sup>rstly focuses on the provision of output explanations to help consumers evaluate the consideration set generated from the initial step such that the consumers make a more informed purchase decision. To this extent, we propose the taxonomy of output explanation – namely TRACE, JUST and STRG – for decision aid's recommendation and provide precise conceptualization and operationalization of the various explanation types. This complements the input explanation taxonomy put forth by Wang and Benbasat [65].

Second, prior studies that examined input explanations tend to focus only on consumers' subjective evaluation of the decision aid, e.g., trustworthiness, but not the objective outcome of the decision process [65,66]. Our study represents the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to understand how output explanation-featured decision aid can impact consumers' online shopping behavior and the outcome. Our approach contrasts sharply with other prior studies such as the work of Huber and Seiser [29], which only examined how different types of justi<sup>fi</sup>cation pressure affect decision process but without considering the use of any decision aid. Similarly, Klayman and his colleagues [34] only investigated decision makers' preference for input-related and outputrelated explanation types from a pure cognition perspective. In this regard, our work has contributed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to the extant literature by synergizing the decision overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence paradigm [21] with the notion of cognitive laziness [2] to demonstrate how providing output explanations to complement the decision aid's recommendations can potentially improve consumer's con<sup>fi</sup>dence and lower decision effort, albeit at the expense of not purchasing the best products available. However, this observed phenomenon is certainly consistent with the proposition of the effort–accuracy tradeoff notion [43]. To the best of our knowledge, our study represents the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to understand consumers' online shopping behavior and outcome from the decision overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence paradigm perspective, responding to prior researchers call for greater research effort towards understanding the output strategy of decision aid [68].

Practically, this research highlights a potential paradoxical problem in shopping websites that used explanation-featured decision aids based on a single form of explanation type. On the one extreme end, TRACE explanation-featured decision aids help shoppers to purchase a product in shorter time and with greater con<sup>fi</sup>dence. But they inevitably lead to lower consumer welfare with respect to the product quality. On the other extreme end, STRG explanation-featured decision aids might be able to help shoppers <sup>fi</sup>nd the best product alternative. Unfortunately, shoppers' purchase propensity could be reduced due to the lower con<sup>fi</sup>dence developed after a longer shopping duration. At <sup>fi</sup>rst glance, JUST explanation-featured decision aids might offer a plausible solution to this paradox. However, we think that providing a pair of balanced explanation-featured decision aids could be more desirable since shoppers could enjoy the best of both worlds. For instance, combining TRACE with STRG could achieve a more balanced decision outcome. A caveat is that the information processing load demanded by the dual explanation-featured decision aids might overwhelm the human shoppers, who are known to be cognitive miser [52].

In a similar fashion, this research also highlights the bene<sup>fi</sup>t and challenge faced by managers of electronic commerce websites when adopting explanation-featured decision aids to create customer loyalty. While increasing customers' purchase propensity is certainly crucial, managers should be careful not to sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce customers' welfare, i.e., the quality of the product purchased. Providing a dual explanationfeatured decision aid with TRACE and STRG appears to be a win–win strategy for managers to enhance their electronic commerce websites.

## 7. Conclusion

In conclusion, the increasingly prevalent use of explanation-featured decision aid in commercial shopping websites might give an intuitive perception to consumers that such decision aids are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to them. But we reason that this may be a fallacy and a closer examination of whether the decision aid is indeed effective in increasing consumer's decision making process and outcome is in order. This paper is one such study that contributes signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to the body of knowledge on online decision aid by highlighting the paradoxical bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of explanation-featured decision aids.
