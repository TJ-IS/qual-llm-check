## Abstract

The literature on strategic uses of information technology suggests that “champions” are often a key to successful implementation. This article describes a field study of 15 information technology (IT) champions and their needs for support from their information systems units. Innovation research is used to hypothesize that IT champions, like other champions, need information, technical resources, and political support from their information systems department units. Interview findings suggest that IT champions strongly value (1) assistance in developing information to help sell their IT vision, (2) a flexible implementation process, and (3) legitimation of their ideas for using information technology. Moreover, IT champions do not want bureaucratic process or arm’s length dealings with information systems departments. The way a firm should conduct its dealings with an IT champion depends in large part on a number of organizational factors, including the IS department’s mission.

Keywords: Championship, IS implementation, IS development, IS management, partnership

ACM Categories: D.2.9, K.6.1, K.6.3.

## Introduction

The literature on strategic uses of information technology (IT) suggests that a very important, and sometimes the most important, antecedent to a successful implementation of a mission-critical information system is a “champion” for the new system (Lockett, 1987; Reich and Benbasat, 1990; Runge, 1988; Vitale and Ives, 1988). According to these researchers, information technology champions are managers who actively and vigorously promote their personal vision for using information technology, pushing the project over or around approval and implementation hurdles. They often risk their reputations in order to ensure the innovation’s success.

The organization behavior and innovation literatures have strongly linked the success of technological innovations to the presence of such champions (e.g., Kanter, 1983; Kimberly and Evanisko, 1981; Pennings and Buitendam, 1987). Champions are more than ordinary leaders; they are more like transformational leaders who inspire others to transcend self-interest for a higher collective purpose (Burns, 1978). When champions are charismatic, inspiring, and intellectually stimulating, they are exhibiting transformational leadership behavior (Bass, 1985; Howell and Higgins, 1990). In the organization literature, champions are also distinguished from sponsors. Sponsors have the funds and authority to accomplish their goals (see Vitale and Ives, 1988), but champions, in spite of having less than the requisite authority or resources, bring about change in their organizations by using a variety of other influence processes (Achilladelis, et al., 1971; Roberts, 1968).

Judging from the current literature on strategic uses of information technology, it seems that IT champions may be important to the implementation of information systems because of their skills in bringing about organizational change. New information systems frequently require significant upheavals in organizational structure and processes, which are often met with some resistance (Markus, 1984). Successful implementation of mission-critical systems often requires adjustments to reward schemes, changes in authority or responsibility patterns, or shifting of power centers (Rockart, 1988). Champions, the innovation literature says, have the organizational clout and savvy needed to overcome resistance to such organizational changes (Kanter, 1983; Maidique, 1980; Rogers, 1983; Schön, 1963). Enthusiastic, visionary champions push their ideas around and through the organization, resolving turf disputes, overriding established norms, and ignoring anguished cries of “It won’t work!” from those who stand to lose with the new system. With competitive advantage at stake, speedy implementation can be critically important. Successful champions can break down bureaucratic barriers and drive change through the organization while the firm’s competitors deliberate over the feasibility of an idea.

Less desirable in the eyes of the IS manager is the IT champion's use of these same influence techniques to cut through the IS bureaucracy. Runge (1988) notes that for 80 percent of the systems he studied, IS's planning and project selection procedures were either "purposely circumvented or simply ignored." The tendency of champions to go it alone, creating new (and often profitable) islands of automation, is also well-documented (Beath and Ives, 1988; Vitale and Ives, 1988). Headstrong, hard-driving IT champions may not be easy to support, but if they are moving full steam ahead on the implementation of systems of strategic importance to the firm, they can be valuable partners for information systems departments.

This research seeks to understand how IS managers should support IT champions in order to harness their energies in support of the firm's goals and objectives for information technology. It asks the following questions: How might the IT champion's organizational savvy, commitment, and energy be brought to bear on the firm's overall information technology agenda? What assistance should be offered to a champion and what hindrances can be avoided? The current literature on championship does not directly address questions about proactive peer support (that is, how one line manager supports another line manager). But propositions can be developed from this literature regarding the kinds of activities and policies IT champions might value from their colleagues in IS departments. Three such propositions are developed in the next section.

# An Information Technology Champion Support Agenda

The literature on organizational innovations and product champions offers a reasonably clear picture of how champions operate and what kinds of organizational structures and environments foster championship. Central to this literature is the notion that champions operate using three resources—information, material resources, and political support (Brown, 1981; Kanter, 1983; Kimberly and Evanisko, 1981; Zaltman, et al., 1973). They need information to evaluate, choose and sell an innovation; material resources to obtain the necessary information and to test and make transitions; and political support to guarantee both the availability of the material resources and, eventually, rewards for successful innovations (or protection from sanctions, in case of failure).

Kanter (1983) summarizes this literature and tells CEOs, in effect, “Design your organizations so that these three valuable resources are available to champions, and your organization will be innovative.” Principal among the innovation-fostering features are those that facilitate change and reduce the risk of negative consequences, such as policies that encourage experimentation and procedures for the review of proposed changes (Hage and Dewar, 1973). Professionalism, the availability of slack resources, and interdependence between units (Pierce and Delbecq, 1977; Zaltman, et al., 1973) are likewise believed to be innovation-fostering because they increase the flow of information, material resources, and political support to champions. Some employment policies are also innovation-fostering. Examples include job mobility, employment security, or opportunities for teamwork, all of which provide opportunities for accumulating information and political support (Kanter, 1983).

Many of the organizational features that are believed to foster innovation are familiar to IS managers. The IS department is, after all, designed to be an agent of change in the organization. The IS planning process, for example, structures the accumulation of information on the costs and benefits of a proposed innovation and then manages the allocation of material resources (programmers and computers, for example) to the most deserving of these innovations. Some of the innovation-fostering features, like employment policies, are not under the control of IS managers; others, like the distribution of slack resources, are not so easily changed in the short term.

Nevertheless, starting from the general notion that champions will “tin cup” for more information, material resources, and political support from anyone, we can infer guidelines from this literature on how a peer or colleague manager, in particular an IS manager, can help another manager champion an organizational innovation. ## Need for information

Roger's (1983) information processing view of innovation lies at the heart of the notion that champions value information. In his view, innovations are preceded by an information-intensive process of learning and persuasion, followed by a commitment to a new way of doing business. Champions can be thought of as accumulators and bearers of the evaluative and persuasive information needed to bring about a possible innovation in which neither the problem nor the solution are well-understood. In the IT context, a champion needs evaluative and persuasive information on the eventual use and behavior of the technology, as well as information on the process of transition. While the areas of relevant information may be endless, the following seem particularly valuable to the evaluation of the IT champion's vision: (1) what the particular information technology can really do, (2) what it is likely to cost, and (3) how a transition can be accomplished.

In addition, because most mission-critical information systems are connected to existing applications or secure their advantage from linking existing applications, IT champions would seem likely to value better information on how to use enterprise data or applications. Finally, if the systems development life cycle is the organization's approved method of facilitating technological change, it would seem that IT champions might appreciate more information on this somewhat arcane process.

## Need for resources

Like other champions, IT champions need slack resources for scanning, experimentation, and developing new uses of information technology (Pierce and Delbecq, 1977; Zaltman, et al., 1973). IT champions need a combination of technology-related resources and other resources. Among the technical resources over which IS departments have discretion are technical specialists, implementation tools and techniques, and computing equipment or services. Free access to these resources, particularly during planning or experimentation phases, would help champions. There are a number of ways IS managers, even those in “full” charge-back environments, can make department resources available at low or no cost to champions. For example, in many organizations users are not charged directly for IS managers’ time, or for information center, data administration, and training services. Fortunately, these are the very units that are potentially of the most use to the IT champions. IS managers might also persuade vendors or suppliers to provide free or nominally priced technical resources to the champion. As the official volume purchaser of information technology supplies or services, the IS department might also help champions husband their limited resources by obtaining discounts for them. According to Kanter (1983), staff time is the resource champions value the most highly. From their IS departments, IT champions want high-quality, professional technical staff equipped with appropriate tools and techniques.

On the other hand, it is apparent from the literature on IT champions that policies and procedures that restrict access to technical resources, such as IS's rational, public project review and approval processes, are regarded as a hindrance by IT champions (e.g., Runge, 1988). IS staff tied to development methods that are short on experimentation and long on bureaucratic processes force IT champions to turn to alternate sources for technical resources, such as consultants or skunkworks (Lockett, 1987).

In summary, the IT champion's desire for easy access to technical resources leads to the following proposition:

Proposition 2: Information technology champions will value IS actions or policies that make staff time and other technical resources available to them in a relatively unconstrained manner.

## Need for political support

Champions operate in a political environment and use a variety of influence strategies, including impression management, rational justification, and repeated informal expression of enthusiasm and confidence about the innovation (Howell and Higgins, 1990). To champions, “neutrality” signals danger and disapproval, particularly when, in most organizations, more than a majority vote is needed to proceed with new and potentially risky ideas (Schön, 1963). For those IT champions whose strategic vision requires cooperation from other functional areas (for applications that link units, for example) even higher levels of agreement must be attained. Moreover, lengthy development efforts may require periodic re-commitment or increased investments. IS department enthusiasm and confidence, in both formal and informal settings, should be valued by IT champions, as will IS efforts to persuade others of the merits of the champion’s vision. This suggests the following proposition:

Proposition 3: Information technology champions will value IS actions or policies that help a champion muster the political support needed to gain approval for or implement a champion's vision.

For an IT champion, it may not be enough for the IS department to provide a professional staff, the right infrastructure, and control methods that enable on-time, within-budget delivery of services. As the above propositions suggest, IT champions may seek a support agenda with a different focus than the one most IS departments are accustomed to delivering. As long as the IT champion's vision includes an information technology component, it would seem that the champion would value a supportive coalition with the IS department. To learn what kinds of support IT champions receive and value from their IS departments, a field study was conducted with a group of IT champions of recently implemented information systems with strategic impact. The goal of this research was to answer the following questions: What kinds of support from their information systems departments did these IT champions value? What did they need but not receive? And, perhaps paradoxically, what did they consider to be hindrances? The next section describes the study. Subsequently, the research and its implications are summarized and discussed.

## The Field Study

## Research design

Four considerations were important in the design of this study. First, because the propositions of this study deal with preferences and attitudes of IT champions, it was critical to use champions themselves as subjects. Most of the prior IS research on championship has relied on IS managers, project participants, or users as informants (e.g., Lockett, 1987; Reich and Benbasat, 1990; Runge, 1988), but IT champions themselves have not been studied. Second, because it was unclear how “champions” or “systems with strategic impact” should be accurately qualified, or what terminology the champions would use to describe the systems development and implementation process, it seemed important to talk directly with the champions. Third, in keeping with the existing literature on IT champions, the subjects should be champions of strategic uses of information technology. Champions of successfully implemented systems with strategic impact were sought (to avoid confusion between evaluations of support and explanations of failure). Finally, for reliability purposes and to lay groundwork for later large-scale survey work, it was desirable to obtain follow-up data from the champion and his or her IT manager or others working with the champion.

A sample was sought that included both champions who had worked closely with IS and champions who had worked around or outside of the IS group, for example with an outside consultant or vendor, or by hiring their own development staff. No particular differences in these two groups were expected, particularly in a small sample, but given Runge's (1988) finding that champions tended to work without IS support, a sample that included both types of champions guarded against potential spurious effects of unmeasured differences.

Most of the champions were initially nominated by the 21 members of the Dean's Council at the Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota. These people, mostly public affairs officers or other senior executives, received a letter from the associate dean for external affairs asking them to assist the author in identifying "mid or senior-level executives, outside of the usual information department, who were responsible for shepherding a potentially strategic use of information technology through the approval, development, or implementation process." A brief attachment repeated this definition and further described the project. Follow-up phone calls to the letter recipient and the nominated champions were used to further screen the sample. By using these non-IS contact points, we hoped to include in the sample some champions who had unsatisfactory relationships with their IS departments. Five additional champions were contacted through IS executives.

Interviews were conducted with 18 nominated IT champions at 10 firms. Of these 18, three were eliminated from the sample either because they did not appear to have taken a championship role in the project or because the application they supported did not appear to have strategic importance. Of the 15 remaining champions, who constitute the sample in this study, seven worked with IS and eight worked around or without IS.

The focus of the interview was to determine the nature of the support provided to the IT champion by his or her IS organization at some critical juncture in the life of the project. The image of a champion as an expediter, cutting through bureaucratic process, as described by Runge (1988), came up in several interviews. One said, “If we’d tried to do this in the normal way, in committee meetings, with reports and decisions, it would have taken three years instead of six months. Overall, the 15 champions on which the study is based embodied the main characteristics of the champion “type,” as described in the innovation literature, and were operating in champion fashion, exhibiting transformative leadership behavior, taking risks, and using a variety of influence techniques to pursue their vision.

Finally, as noted earlier, we attempted to include in the sample both champions who had worked closely with IS as well as champions who had not worked with IS. The interviews revealed that all the champions, even those who mostly had worked without IS, were at least partly supported by IS. Moreover, no systematic differences were observed between the two sub-groups (working with IS/working without IS) in the course of the interviews with either the champions or the IS managers with respect to the types of support received from IS or the types of support valued by the champion. In the post-interview survey, no overall differences were observed between the two subgroups in either the types of support acknowledged as having been received (e.g., "IS group helped us to . . .") or in their overall evaluations of that support. Three of the champions who had worked mostly without IS gave slightly negative ratings, overall, for the support they received from IS (that is, average scores were less than 3 on a scale in which 5 = "helped", 3 = "had no effect" and 1 = "hindered"). But the five others in this "without IS support" group rated IS support activities more positively, so that overall no difference in the two groups could be detected by the survey. $^{2}$

## Interview results

## Proposition 1: IT Champions Value Information for Evaluation and Persuasion

All the subjects talked about their need for information-related help from IS for evaluating technology or persuading others of its merits. As we expected, nearly all mentioned their need in the early stages of the project for additional information about the capabilities of the technology and its likely costs. In the post-interview survey, "providing cost and time estimates" was judged to be the second most helpful form of support received from IS. Also, as expected, the champions, not someone from IS or an IS-sponsored workshop for identifying new ideas for using information technology, were the source of the original concept or idea for the information system. In only one case did the system idea originate in IS, and in that case the champion's interest and commitment were necessary to get the ball rolling. In general, several champions suggested, IS understands what the system will do or what it will cost but not what its business benefits will be.

Frequently the champions needed details on how the target technology operated. As one champion said, "Our IS person told us what would work and what wouldn't." The same IS person told us, "I answered basic questions on what can or cannot be done." For new technologies, vendors are often the most appropriate source for this kind of information. Some of the champions, particularly among those that ultimately worked without IS support, ran into what looked to them like mud-slinging between IS and outside vendors. This made it harder to obtain all the desired information about the new technology. The champions found this very frustrating and interpreted it as self-serving by IS.

Experimentation is a common way in which innovations are tried and proved (or disproved). While none of the champions emphasized the learning benefits of experimentation during the interviews, on the post-interview survey half of the champions who worked with IS said finding “ways to experiment or prototype the target information technology” helped them.

When a new system uses data captured by existing applications, IS can help the champion mine the data. As one champion told us, “The system used a massive amount of data already in the portfolio and manipulated it in a complex way. The key to making it happen was on the IS side.” On the post-interview survey, most of the champions whose systems used significant amounts of enterprise data rated as “helpful” IS efforts to help them learn how the data used in the system related to the firm’s existing databases.

Once the champion has developed the information needed to evaluate the new vision, a persuasive case must be built. Assistance from IS in developing persuasive arguments was mentioned as helpful by some champions. One champion said, “[The CIO] was a translator between a business man’s jargon or mind set and the technicians. He helped me frame questions, helped me understand the answers, and helped me translate the answers back into language that the senior managers were going to accept. This was the most important thing he did for me.” This CIO not only understood the technological issues but also spoke the language of the executive suite.

It was anticipated that IT champions would value IS's help in fathoming the sometimes mysterious systems development life cycle process. None of the champions mentioned this. But some of the champions who had worked with IS appreciated assistance in fathoming the organization's norms for information technology change, in particular in understanding the ins and outs of the project approval process. According to one, "The IS team member on our task force helped us understand their process, who determined priorities, how things got labeled 'important.' They showed us the need for a 'blueprint.'" In this case, the firm's managers put great emphasis on projects that were part of an overall scheme or strategy, known locally as a "blueprint." After the task force produced a document boldy titled "BLUEPRINT," project aproval was obtained. Another champion said, "Our liaison person described how the planning system—the way resources were allocated—worked. I asked him, what are the other users doing? How are they getting so much of the budget? He worked up a process for us, which we followed. We got a nine-fold increase in the money allocated to new systems."

Overall, the evidence is strongly supportive of Proposition 1. IT champions, whether or not they work closely with IS, value assistance in fleshing out their particular vision for using information technology. They are most interested in developing persuasive arguments for proceeding with implementation, particularly with respect to implementation cost and schedule, the use of enterprise data, and appraising the potential of information technology. Where IS might have helped but did not or could not, the champions complained.

## Proposition 2: IT Champions Value Technology Resources

Staff resources, according to Kanter (1983), are the change agent's most important resource. On IT projects, the technical expertise that IS personnel possess makes them a critical resource for IT champions. As we expected, all the champions commented on this. Many of those who had worked around IT did so because IS staff resources were unavailable to them. As one CIO told us, "My philosophy is to keep the staff level stable and just do what can be done with that amount of resource; it cuts down on the less valuable projects or creates pressure to redesign them so they are more valuable." The unsupported champions tended to go it alone by hiring consultants, by pushing for decentralization of IS resources, or by general bootlegging. As one said, "The IS people didn't want this added to their slate. Their slate was already full for the next two years. They didn't have the resources to support it."

Of those who worked with IS, some were grateful for having had high-quality expertise made available to them, while others voiced some disappointment. Sometimes the champions were concerned about the amount of staff they could get assigned to their project. As one champion put it, "Where [the CIO] offered me unprecedented support was in doing things that they said couldn't be done. Sometimes I would go to him and say we need more people on this, and the technicians would say there are only 20 people who have the ability to work on this, and I'd say we need 25, and then somehow [the CIO] would find five more qualified people."

Several of our champions focused on the quality of staff assigned to their projects. “We got better than average people for a project of this size,” said one. Another said, “We kept getting weak people assigned. All the power hitters went to [another project].” Some IT champions felt that IS staff had limited vision. As one put it, “When you want an idea, IS tends to pull out what they know and what they’re comfortable with. IS wasn’t creative about designing the outputs. They were only creative about building the system in the most efficient way. They complained when the specs changed, but the specs had to change if we were going to evolve an improved product. When you’re doing something new, you need flexibility.” Lack of flexibility was also noted by one CIO who said, “IS is more of a hindrance to champions than a help. IS is tradition-bound and turf-oriented, with an attitude that no one knows how to do things besides us.”

As the innovation literature suggests, the IT champions were especially grateful for free IS managerial and staff time. One said, “IS developed the budget that was presented to the steering committee but didn’t charge for the time.” On the post-interview survey, free technical staff time (which 12 of 14 said was available to them) was rated as the most helpful support type. (We also expected that champions would appreciate free hardware and software resources, but no one mentioned this.) In order to deliver free staff resources to IT champions, one CIO said that he sometimes charges staff time for developing proposals to “education.” At other times he splits the costs with champions. He also encourages his contractors, vendors, and consultants to spend free time with IT champions. As he points out, “[The champions] can’t find it in their own budgets. They have less flexibility, and we know how to work the [expense] approval process.”

While we expected that champions would appreciate free, high-quality IS staff resources, we also learned during the interviews that champions want much more control over how those people work, how they make design choices, and what technologies they use (including hardware, languages, development methods, outside services, and so forth). This was a strong and unexpected finding. IT champions who want to change the organization in a hurry put a premium on responsiveness, both with respect to IS process and IS products. As one champion said, "IS does a good job but we would like it quicker." When IS demonstrated flexibility with respect to the process they use or the products and services they offer, the IT champions really noticed it. "Having the mental flexibility to change the way they [IS] do business is very important," one said.

Many standard operating procedures (like the SDLC) restrict the process options available to IT champions. Besides offering little support for experimentation, these procedures also tend to lock onto a solution early. One IT champion argued that IS's ponderous system development life cycle made programmers reluctant to reconsider decisions. Another thought their SDLC discouraged the systems people from raising problems in a timely fashion. Two experienced IT champions bemoaned the formality and rigor they find in their IS organizations today. "I wouldn't be able to do today what I did then. MIS was pretty easy to maneuver and didn't offer resistance; I went directly to the programmers. Today I couldn't get to first base," said one. The other said, "I don't think I could get this project approved today. The system is much more formal now."

Technology standards, such as bans on laser printing, restrictions on fourth generation languages, and reluctance to deal with additional vendors, tend to limit the technology product or service options available to IT champions. Said one, "IS didn't want [alternative vendor] equipment; they didn't want to deal with another vendor. Then they weren't anxious to get involved with an outside software supplier." This IT champion told us that IS's roadblocks had cost the firm more than a million dollars. His CIO agreed that IS had been balky, saying, "There was significant resistance in the early stages to the champion's proposal to use an outside software supplier." Most of the IT champions who worked without IS support were forced to do so because they wanted to use technologies or services—networking services, text manipulators, a special report generator—that were not considered part of the IS product or service list.

Overall, the interviews confirmed that IT champions want quality IT staff to implement their systems. More importantly, the interviews revealed that IT champions want much more control over these resources. It is not enough to have project staff assigned; it is also necessary to be able to exercise sufficient discretion over the staff's work to be able to hold it to the champion's particular vision. The potential conflict that this raises between the firm's long-range information technology goals and the champion's short-range strategic goals needs considerable additional exploration and will be discussed further below.

## Proposition 3: IT Champions Value Political Support.

IT champions frequently commented early in the interview that they either received or didn't receive public backing from IS management. These IT champions were very sensitive about how their claims about technology were treated in public by IS experts. When legitimation was forthcoming, it was highly valued by the champions, particularly if they otherwise worked without IS support. The third highest-ranked item on the post-interview questionnaire was "The IS group 'testified' about the merits of the project to funders, such as senior management or an IS steering committee." Champions said things like, "IS's support gave a validity that made it possible to get business support," and "The CIO said, 'He's right!' It validated the project from a technical standpoint," and "[The CIO] supported my analysis. He wasn't defensive. This was very important in convincing senior management that there was a light at the end of the tunnel. He had the necessary prestige with senior management."

Some CIOs understand this need for legitimation. As one IT champion told us, “In front of the steering committee [the CIO’s] attitude was extremely supportive.” The CIO told us the rest of the story: “They had tried something very similar a few years before and I expected this to be another disaster; but I thought that the downside risk was manageable, and the champion was a loyal friend. I made a pact to support him 100 percent. I expressed my support formally to his boss and the executive VPs up the chain. I attended a presentation that the champion made from which IS support was inferred.” The project was approved and was ultimately successful, much to the CIO’s happy surprise.

One CIO noted that it is particularly important to distinguish between the private elaboration of the project concept and plans (relating to Proposition 1) and the public position taken on the project (relating to Proposition 3). It is possible to talk about cost and schedule privately with the IT champions while focusing all public presentations on the impact of the idea, preferably its strategic impact.

The same CIO summarizes the managerial support concept eloquently: "Sell the idea. Say, 'Here's an idea worth pursuing' to everybody, every chance you get. Give it relevance; give it momentum. The first thing is to get people interested. The second thing is to ask whether or not it's feasible. Some IS managers get those two reversed. Negative arguments are meaningless until you know what the idea is. IS should be there when the idea is presented—you don't have to say anything, just look positive."

It was also anticipated that IS managers might help IT champions by engendering support for the champions' projects with other managers in their organizations. While most of the champions did not seem to expect this from IS, a few did mention it. One champion appreciated that his IS liaison manager, who had credibility with a department whose cooperation was needed, had helped the champion deal with that department. As the CIO told us, "Behind the scenes another IS manager was very effective in talking to other players, to people who had some voice in the funding decision. The IS manager went around and said, 'Here's what we're doing,' communicating the vision, keeping the folks informed, building the case." By contrast, in a case where data from several other departments were needed to make the champion's system work, IS management did not pitch in. The champion said, "The people working on this [were so junior in the organization that they] could do very little to help us gain and maintain commitment from our coalition of users."

Overall, the interviews support the proposition that IT champions value actions by IS managers that help them gain or maintain a coalition of support within the firm. Fortunately, most CIOs recognize the value of support and know how to be supportive. As one CIO put it, "IS should put its weight behind a champion. IS gains clout by doing this. Pitch the champion's ideas up, down, and around." Interestingly, none of the CIOs we talked to seemed to be concerned about accidentally giving political support to the wrong person or the wrong project. Apparently they trusted their judgment on selecting which champions to support.

## Discussion

The sample for this research is small and mainly representative of line managers in large firms with relatively institutionalized or mature information systems departments. Comparison groups of non-champion user managers were not studied, nor were champions of unimplemented systems. The particular findings should therefore be treated with some caution. A larger sample would surely reveal some differences between champions working with IS and those working around IS (for example, with respect to their attitudes toward the current architecture). And most line managers, not just IT champions, probably have a strong preference for an ample supply of high-quality technical staff. Overall, these champions' opinions are the consequence of many factors besides just the presence or absence of support by IS, and their views are no doubt partially shaped by success.

Nevertheless, because the propositions are rooted in the championship literature and were strongly supported by both qualitative and quantitative data, it can be concluded that information technology champions value assistance from information systems departments in:

\- developing persuasive evidence to support the champion's (not the IS department's) vision for using IT (Proposition 1),

\- obtaining flexible, appropriate implementation resources (Proposition 2),

\- mustering political support for the champion's IT vision (Proposition 3), and

\- fathoming the politics of the systems approval and development process (Propositions 1, 2, and 3).

It is also apparent that many CIOs currently deliver on this innovation support agenda despite the fact that this requires them to juggle resources and bend policies.

This research also finds that peer managers do support champions by providing information, material resources, and political support. Also, IT champions operate much like other champions. Additionally, in contrast to what one might conclude from Runge (1988) or Lockett (1987), these results show that some IT champions are able to work well with their IS partners. The research confirms that IT champions pursue their own vision and get impatient with the bureaucratic processes common to IS departments.

The research reinforces the notion suggested elsewhere (Vitale, et al., 1986) that IS departments should offer “greenhouse” or “incubator” services where potential champions (and others) can experiment with new IT ideas or develop persuasive arguments for large innovation investments. In these units, resources could be allocated to ongoing new technology evaluation or information technology research and development. This group might also be effective at facilitating and monitoring vendor-champion interactions. Data administrators, the research suggests, may also need to be given some slack so they can help potential champions get a better grasp on existing data.

## Thoughts for practitioners

Because of the limitations of this study—in particular that the sample includes only successfully implemented projects—no action recommendations are made here for managers beyond noting that this research indicates that IT champions operate much like other champions, and hence the more general literature on product champions should be useful to them in dealing with IT champions.

More significant for managers, perhaps, is that this study shows that the real “champion problem” that IS managers face is not figuring out what information or resources they should make available to carefully selected champions. The real problem is that IT champions usually want IS managers to give up something. That is, they want IS managers to postpone other projects in favor of theirs. They want IS managers to sacrifice department efficiencies. They want IS managers to waive standards and policies. In essence, IT champions want IS managers to alter the firm’s long-range information technology plans. The problem IS managers confront is how to manage the constant realignment of goal sets perturbed by a zealous champion.

For example, the champions we talked to often placed their CIOs in the position of having to trade off long-range IT goals in favor of short-range IT goals. As a result, champions of the future who want to mine enterprise data may find themselves thwarted by the champions of yesterday who insisted that data integration standards be set aside in order to speed an implementation. Infrastructure plans, integration targets, and efficient service goals, which are often dependent on adherence to equipment and software standards or architectures, were pitted by champions against first mover competitive advantages. Particularly at risk were those standards that mainly help IS work efficiently; these are the most vulnerable to challenge by a champion.

At issue here is the question of whose interests are served by these standards, architectures, policies, or procedures. The tendency of staff departments to exhibit a lack of goal congruence with the firm is well-documented in the management control literature (Anthony, et al., 1984). While staff unit managers may benefit from having an “excellent” department, the cost of doing this may exceed the value created for the firm. IS managers are reputed to have tendencies to build technological empires or to overinvest in reliability without regard to the value created for the firm. For those IS managers who are evaluated on the basis of the quality of service provided and whose market value is in part based on knowledge of new technologies, this can be argued to be rational behavior (Mendelson, forthcoming). But it is vulnerable behavior. Champions were quick to point out policies or activities that struck them as self-serving. “Their [project control] reports did nothing for me,” one said, “but they made [IS] look like they were on top of things.”

A related, but separate, issue raised by this research is that of goal conflicts between IS (acting as a staff unit) and line sub-units of the firm. Staff-line conflicts arise where both staff and line units want to have complete authority over the resources needed to fulfill their responsibilities. As McGregor (1960) points out, the notion that line managers have all the authority and that staff are there simply to support is an illusion. Champions, perhaps, exploit that illusion when they successfully demand and get the concessions they need to pursue their particular business vision. The rationale for giving IS legitimate authority over certain technical decisions is apparently unclear or unstable in many firms.

In summary, some organizations apparently find it difficult to answer these two questions: (1) To what extent should the IS department be allowed to restrict technology choices in order to pursue its own efficiency and service goals? and (2) What is the legitimate authority domain of the IS department? Where there is lack of clarity in the IS mission, it is particularly difficult for IS managers to support champions and still maintain a delicate balance of local and global technology objectives. If champions are key to the implementation of highly innovative, organization-transforming applications of information technology, then supporting them is desirable. But supporting champions is exceedingly difficult if the IS mission is indistinct.

A simple solution might be to redefine the IS mission to specifically exclude support for some information technologies—point-of-sale terminals in the firm's retail outlets may be part of the IS mission, but point-of-sale terminals in customers' retail outlets may need to be a marketing responsibility. The IS steering committee may need to get involved in decisions to set standards, particularly those standards that will ultimately affect integration of information or technology. In any case, the CIO can take a lesson from the champion for making changes to the IS mission and making short/long and firm/unit goal tradeoffs—CIOs should put together a solid, persuasive case, muster peer-level managerial support, and sell the decision to everyone who will be affected.

## Suggestions for future research

This study opens several avenues for future research. First, how good are the champions' applications, really? Answering this question is a knotty problem because researchers have not been able to establish convincingly the long-range value of information technology. But some approximations might be achieved for a set of particular applications if the cooperation of a consortium of IS practitioners can be obtained. It is very important that the champion's applications be evaluated in the context of the firm's entire information technology architecture.

IS managers might be particularly interested in knowing whether champions are really very different from other users. It seems conceivable that many user managers would value champion support; but some initial evidence in the organization literature (Howell and Higgins, 1990) suggests otherwise. Transactional leaders (as opposed to transformative ones, like champions) use different influence techniques and behaviors and thus may have different information, material resource, and political support needs. Large-scale survey research, based in part on the work reported here, might be used to pursue this question.

Considering the apparent conflict between a champion's vision for IT and traditional application planning, we might ask by what process the strategic vision for information technology arises in organizations. How do the strategic visions of champions, information specialists, and others become integrated into a collective vision? Field-based process research is needed to answer this question. Given that champions are so often associated with innovative, organization-transforming applications of information technology, should the vision for information technology perhaps be constructed of portfolios of champions instead of portfolios of applications? If IS managers wanted to promote champions, how would they find them? The extensive literature on championship can be of some use in pursuing this question (see Beath and Ives, 1989).

Some of the champions studied in this research worked around IS to avoid the opportunity costs of working with too-slow IS departments. Some of those who insisted on technologies that were not supported by their IS departments now find themselves running department-level computing operations. Are these dysfunctional choices? Without an understanding of the IS mission at these firms, we cannot know. IS managers saddled with unclear or broadly inclusive missions might find it difficult to support champions. This raises questions such as: What are the consequences, for champions, of very broad or very narrow IS missions? What role do IS evaluation or reward schemes play in shaping the relationships between champions and the IS department? One way of studying questions related to the design of the IS department is to use an agency theory framework (Fama and Jensen, 1983; Jensen and Meckling, 1976). The agency approach acknowledges that the individual actors within the firm have conflicting objectives. It proposes that difficulties experienced by conflicting but interdependent actors, such as champions and IS departments, are intensified by some organization designs or reward schemes (Eisenhardt, 1985; 1989). This suggests that agency reasoning might be used to study the impact of organizational context, including factors such as the nature of the IS mission, the distribution of IT resources within the firm, and the IS reward scheme, on the IT championship process.

In summary, this article, drawing on innovation research, hypothesizes that IT champions want information, technical resources, and endorsement from their information systems departments. The research results confirm that IT champions strongly value assistance in developing their information technology ideas, a flexible implementation process, and legitimation of their ideas about using information technology. But the research also highlights dilemmas information systems managers face in establishing and pursuing long-range goals for information technology. A broader view is needed of the champion-IS department relationship, one that takes into account the mission of the IS department in the firm.
