# Business Models for Monetizing Internet Applications and Web Sites: Experience, Theory, and Predictions

Eric K. Clemons

Eric K. Clemons is Professor of Operations and Information Management at the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania. His education includes an S.B. in Physics from MIT, and an M.S. and Ph.D. in Operations Research from Cornell University. He has been a pioneer in the systematic study of the transformational effects of information on the strategy and practice of business. His research and teaching interests include strategic uses of information systems, the changes that IT enables in the competitive balance between new entrants and established industry participants, transformation of distribution channels, the structure and governance of the IT functional area, and the impact of IT on the risks and benefits of outsourcing and strategic alliances. Industries of focus include international securities markets and financial services firms, consumer packaged goods retailing, and travel. He specializes in assessing the competitive implications of IT, and in managing the risks of large-scale implementation efforts. Dr. Clemons is the founder and project director for the Wharton School’s Sponsored Research Project on Information: Strategy and Economics Within the Program for Global Strategy and Knowledge Intensive Organizations. He recently participated in the World Economic Forum in Davos, Switzerland (February 2009). He is currently a member of the editorial boards of the Journal of Management Information Systems and International Journal of Electronic Commerce. Dr. Clemons has 35 years’ experience on the faculties of Wharton, Cornell, and Harvard, and consulting experience in the private and public sectors both domestically and abroad.

Abstra ct: Almost all attempts to date to monetize Internet applications targeted at individuals have focused on natural extensions of traditional media or traditional retailing. Most are either some form of consumer-focused advertising or of consumerfocused e-commerce. And yet the Net is far more powerful than traditional media on one hand, and far more liberating and thus inappropriate as an alternative to traditional media on the other. There are numerous potential online business models that are not based on advertising. This paper explores several such areas and divides them into two basic categories, those that sell some product, experience, content, or service and earn revenues from the sale, and those that provide access to consumers and charge for access. It further divides each of these major categories into subcategories, and for each subcategory reviews current experience, places it in the context of the relevant literature in competitive strategy, and uses that theory to make predictions. The paper concludes with strategic recommendations for corporations as well as with suggestions for further research.

This pa pe r is not principa lly a bout online a dve rtising but about alternatives to online advertising, and more specifically about the use of business models for online applications that do not rely on advertising. However, it does appear useful to motivate the interest in nonadvertising Internet revenues, if only to suggest why the topic of alternative models should be seen as important, and an explanation of the limitations of online advertising provides the necessary motivation.

Internet advertising revenues are dropping, as noted by Lacy [30]. Contrary to the thoughts on TechCrunch, however, this decline was neither unpredictable nor unpredicted [12], and it was not caused solely by the general recession and the decline in retail sales. Internet advertising will rapidly lose its impact on consumers and therefore its power as a revenue source, for reasons that can easily be understood. It is necessary for Internet businesses to be designed around different revenue sources and different business models. Discussion of this is extremely difficult at present, and a single recent online post on the subject was both the most widely cited piece in the technological blogosphere and the most reviled [15, 49]. Still, it appears obvious that even the mainstream business press has noticed the need to pay for content and stop trying to support the full cost of Internet applications through advertising [24].

The traditional advertising model simply cannot be carried over to the Internet, replacing full-page ads on the back of the New York Times or 30-second spots on the Super Bowl broadcast with pop-ups, banners, and click-throughs on side bars. This may be a subject where considerable disagreement is possible, if indeed pushed ads were still working in traditional media. Mostly they have failed. One newspaper after another is going out of business across the United States [40, 56], and the ad revenues of traditional print media, even of highly respected magazines, is declining. The ultimate failure of broadcast media advertising is likewise becoming clear. Both of these are explored below.

Pushing a message at a potential customer when it has not been requested, and when the consumer is in the midst of something else on the Net, will fail as a major revenue source for most Internet sites. This is particularly true when the consumer knows that the sponsor of the ad has paid to have this information, which was verified by no one, thrust at him or her. The Net will find monetization models, and these will be different from the advertising models used by mass media, just as the models used by mass media were different from the monetization models of theater and sporting events before them. Indeed, there has to be some way to create Web sites that do other than provide free access to content, some of it proprietary, some of it licensed, and some of it misappropriated without the author’s consent, and funded by advertising. Most striking is perhaps the draft settlement of Google’s class action lawsuit with librarians, which would give it the right to offer some authors a onetime payment of \$60 for the right to distribute their works online without charge, the cite to be funded solely by advertising [45, 54].

The idea that content has a price and Net applications should find ways to earn a profit without providing free access to other people’s content gets explosive reactions;

when virtual reality pioneer and tech guru Jaron Lanier suggested in an op-ed in the New York Times that authors deserved to be paid for their content [31], he actually received death threats, and the response to the TechCrunch posting was nearly as virulent [15]. But alternative models are possible and several suggestions for alternative forms of monetization are offered below. The next three sections support the idea that Internet advertising has been overvalued; although useful but controversial, they are not entirely essential to the more important argument, which is that other business models will be necessary.

## Advertising Will Fail as the Sole Support of the Net

The Inte rne t is the most libe ra ting of a ll ma ss me dia developed to date. It is participatory, like swapping stories around a campfire or attending a Renaissance fair. It is not meant solely to push content, in one direction, to a captive audience, the way movies or traditional network television have done. It provides the greatest array of entertainment and information, on any subject, with any degree of formality, on demand. And it is the best and the most trusted source of commercial product information on cost, selection, availability, and suitability, using community content, professional reviews, and peer reviews.

The basic premise of this paper is that the Internet is not replacing advertising but shattering it, and all the king’s horses, all the king’s men, and all the creative talent of Madison Avenue cannot put it together again. To analyze this statement, we need a working definition of advertising, and the following is proposed, which is as general as could be made:

Advertising is using sponsored commercial messages to build a brand and paying to locate these messages where they will be observed by potential customers performing other activities; these messages describe a product or service, its price or fundamental attributes, where it can be found, its explicit advantages, or the implicit benefits from its use.

This is not a binary all-or-nothing industry, and advertising will not vanish. Some ads are not only successful but are an essential part of the success of the publication. The Men’s Style and Women’s Style magazines in the Sunday New York Times include a significant volume of ads that are as informative as the stories, and the ads in Vogue are as professionally photographed and as interesting to many readers as much of the content of the magazine. But the majority of ads will be seen to be ineffective, and advertising will be a smaller percentage of Internet revenues and no longer able to support the wide range of Internet businesses we will have.

It is frequently argued that the advertising industry will provide sufficient innovation to allow online ads to fully replace the loss of traditional ads on traditional mass media. Again, our basic premise rejects this, suggesting that simple commercial messages, pushed through whatever medium, in order to reach a potential customer who is in the middle of doing something else, will often fail. It is not that we no longer need information to initiate or to complete a transaction; rather, we will no longer need advertising to obtain that information. We will see the information we want, when we want it, from sources that we trust more than paid advertising. We will find out what we need to know, when we want to make a commercial transaction of any kind. The conventional wisdom is that this is exactly what paid searches help us to do, but all too often they are nothing more than a form of misdirection, as is explained further below. Instead of relying on advertising, consumers will use information that they trust, obtained at the time that they want to see it.

Better targeting of ads using individual interests and individual behaviors will ensure that advertisers do not bore or annoy as many people with each ad, but cannot address the issue of loss of consumers’ trust in paid sponsored messages. As for paid search, it is closer to other mechanisms that allow a Web site to sell access to potential customers. It works effectively as a revenue source for Google, of course. But it surely is not replicable for the average content Web site.

Other than the announcement of the launch of new products and services not yet widely reviewed on community content Web sites, or a significant change in price, there would appear to be little need for advertising. Even here there is reason to doubt; a potential film viewer can learn far more about a new release by reading reviews on the Web than from the company’s own Web site (e.g., [46]).

## Online Advertising Will Fall Short of Current Expectations, for Three Reasons

The re a re thre proble ms with a dve rtising in any form, whether broadcast or online:

1. Consumers frequently do not trust advertising. Ariely has demonstrated that messages attributed to a commercial source have much lower credibility and much lower impact on the perception of product quality than the same message attributed to a rating service [3]. Forrester Research has completed studies that show that advertising and company-sponsored blogs are among the least-trusted sources of information on products and services, while recommendations from friends and online reviews from customers are the highest [32].

2. Consumers often do not want to view advertising. Think of watching network television news and remember that the commercials on all the major networks are as closely synchronized as possible. Why was this done? If network executives believed we all wanted to see the ads, they would be staggered so that users could channel surf to view the ads; ads are synchronized so that users cannot channel surf to avoid the ads.

3. And mostly, consumers do not need advertising. My own research [11, 12, 13, 16, 17] and the research of others [21, 22, 27, 41, 47] suggest that consumers behave as if they get much of their information about product offerings from the Internet, through independent professional rating sites such as reference Digital Photography Review (www.dpreview.com) or community content rating services such as RateBeer (http://ratebeer.com) or TripAdvisor (www.

tripadvisor.com). Theoretical underpinnings for this come from diverse sources, principally from the large literature on the reduction of search costs and from the literature on transparency in markets.

Yes, both network executives and their ad agencies have noted that we are not watching traditional ads, and they attribute this to the fact that we have moved beyond newspapers, televised network news, and broadcast movies to video games, iPods, and the Internet. Porting ads to a new medium will not solve the three problems noted above. The problem is not the medium; the problem is the message and the fact that it is not trusted, not wanted, and not needed.

## Alternative Models for Monetization Are Available

Aga in, my re sea rch sugge sts that there are three general categories for creating value that can be monetized, including selling real things, selling virtual things, and selling access. This, of course, is the central thesis of this paper. The declining impact of advertising creates an opportunity to fill the revenue gap; this paper is not about attacking advertising or advertisers, but about suggesting alternative business models that will fill that gap.

Some Web sites exist solely to sell real things. Many of the best-known perform aggregation of demand, so that there will be enough customers to justify stocking and selling items for which there is only limited demand. Amazon.com is merely the best-known example. Sites such as Amazon.com and Zapatos.com are especially good for long tail items; where else do you go for a copy of the Green Sea of Heaven, Elizabeth T. Gray’s magnificent translation of the Ghazals of Hafiz, or for a pair of size 20 basketball shoes? Selling real things online has been studied since the advent of interest in e-commerce and will not be discussed further here.

Other Web sites sell virtual things. These activities fall into five categories:

1. Selling content and information, from digital music to news and information. Some of these sites are funded by subscriptions, such as Gartner Research; some are by direct micropayments for purchases, such as iTunes; and some currently attempt to fund themselves through advertising, such as BusinessWeek or the New York Times, while still searching for a more effective business model.

2. Selling experience and participation in a virtual community, including Second Life and World of Warcraft, Facebook and MySpace, Flickr and YouTube, and LinkedIn. Not all of these have found a way to charge for participation.

3. Selling information gathered from online experience, such as using aggregate information from Second Life to make predictions on consumer trends, or using information from individual Facebook members available for commercial use in background checks, consumer-targeted advertising, or trend evaluation.

4. Selling accessories for virtual communities, such as completed homes and stores, furnishings, clothing, and pets in Second Life or characters and accessories that would be difficult to earn in World of Warcraft (although serious World of Warcraft players generally despise this behavior).

5. Selling content extracted from virtual communities, such as the recent Facebook alliances to provide information to traditional businesses [35].

Finally, some Web sites create and sell access to customers. Again, this can be divided into multiple categories:

•	 Misdirection, or sending customers to Web locations other than the ones for which they are searching. Monetization of misdirection frequently takes the form of charging companies for key words and threatening to divert their customers to a competitor if they fail to pay adequately for key words that the customer is likely to use in searches for the companies’ products; that is, misdirection works best when it is threatened rather than actually imposed, and when companies actually do pay the fees demanded for their key words. Misdirection most frequently takes the form of diverting customers to companies that they do not wish, simply because the customer’s preferred company underbid. Misdirection also includes misinformation, such as telling a customer that a hotel is sold out when indeed it is still available, if the hotel has chosen not to pay a promotional fee, and then allowing the guest to choose an alternative property. Misdirection is, regrettably, still a popular business model on the Net, although, for reasons explored on TechCrunch [14, 15], strategic misrepresentation online seems ultimately to be unsustainable; either users or governmental agencies will demand correction. More significantly from the perspective of this paper, it is not scalable; it is not possible for every Web site to earn its revenue from sponsored search and ultimately at least some of them will need to find an alternative revenue model.

•	 Evaluation, assessment, and validation. The opposite of sending a customer someplace other than what he or she wants is providing the customer enough information for him or her to make an informed choice on his or her own. Recommendations on TripAdvisor allow potential guests to evaluate and validate recommendations provided by Hotels.com; not surprisingly, Hotels.com originally owned TripAdvisor, and benefited greatly from it.

•	 Social search. Social search is a way of tailoring search based on the user’s network of friends. Rather than searching for any hotel in Chicago, or for any hotel that paid for the key words “hotel” and “Chicago,” users would like to be able to ask for the hotel where their friends stay when they are in Chicago.

•	 Contextual mobile ads. At present, contextual mobile ads delivered by text messaging appear to offer much promise. Imagine a hypothetical all-knowing information-based firm that (1) knows your location because you have registered to have the information from your in-phone GPS shared with your friends, (2) knows that you like Thai restaurants because it monitors the content of your e-mail and your online restaurant searches, and (3) knows that you are hungry because you just said so in a text message or Twitter post sent from your phone. This could be a great time to text you an advertisement for a nearby Thai restaurant, sent directly to your phone.

## Consumers’ Loss of Trust in Sponsored and Commercial Messages

Pe rha ps the grea te st proble m fa cing tra ditiona l a dve rtising is the loss of credibility experienced by most commercial messages. Consumers no longer trust messages that they believe come from sources with a vested interest in consumers’ accepting them. Most striking, individuals no longer accept as true statements coming from sources that they feel may have had vested interests or may have misrepresented facts to them in the past, even if their current statements should appear entirely neutral and unbiased now. Perhaps the best authority on this at present is the work of experimental economist Dan Ariely [3], who has explored consumers’ acceptance of the same statement when the claimed source of the statement is varied.

In one of Ariely’s experiments, subjects were asked to assess the truth value of a large collection of statements; half were indeed true, but consistent with a slight bias toward trust, subjects accepted about 55 percent of the statements. The subjects’ willingness to accept the statements dropped significantly when the statements were attributed to the Democratic Party, the Republican Party, or to Procter & Gamble (P&G). Interestingly, the largest drop in willingness to pay, to about onethird, occurred when the statements were attributed to a major political party, and the willingness to accept statements from either party was not significantly better among members of that party than among members of the opposite party. Equally interesting, this lack of trust was evidenced in the rejection of statements that had nothing to do with candidates, with the policy of either party, or with products from P&G or its competitors.

In a second experiment, subjects read a review of a stereo system and then listened to the stereo and were asked to rate it. In one case, subjects were told that the review was from a highly regarded stereo store close to MIT and in another case they were told that the review was from Consumer Reports. The wording of the review and the listening conditions for the stereo were identical in both cases. Subjects were asked to evaluate and rate the stereo after listening to it. Those who believed the favorable review was from Consumer Reports actually gave the stereo significantly higher assessments after listening.

Ariely describes the collapse of credibility of paid messages as an example of the “Tragedy of the Commons” [26]. Ariely argues that as long as any single advertiser is tempted to overclaim, overgrazing in the commons of consumers’ trust, consumers trust will be weakened, additional advertisers will be tempted to make exaggerated claims in order to have an impact on consumers’ perceptions, and ultimately each advertiser will be forced to overpromise and to overclaim. This will effectively overgraze the commons of consumer trust.

This is not a loss of trust in television ads or print ads, but a loss of trust in ads. It carries over to Internet ads, and even more strongly to company blogs and company Web sites [32]. This cannot be dismissed as a problem only of radio, television, newspapers, or magazines.

## The Rise and Future Fall of Internet Advertising

## The Promise of Internet Advertising

De spite ongoing e nthusia sm for Inte rne t a dve rtising, and despite initially attractive sales figures reported by firms that earn their revenues through Internet advertising, we believe that using the Net as if it were no more than an alternative for other entertainment media will ultimately be unsuccessful. Yes, radio replaced traveling musicians, network television replaced television, and premium channels and pay per view have captured much of the television audience. Yes, the Internet has now captured much of the audience that formerly watched television. But the nature of the Internet experience is fundamentally different from previous networks and far more liberating; a liberated Net surfer will view ads only when he or she wants, and can readily find more reliable information than ads when he or she is ready to purchase.

Based on this premise, we do not consider the large literature on the effectiveness of different approaches to targeting advertising, the literature on analyzing click-stream data, or the emerging literature on pricing key words for sponsored search, nor the relative merits of pay per impression, pay per click, or pay per sale pricing in sponsored search. The premise of this paper suggests that current forms of advertising will not transfer over to the use of the Internet to replace traditional media-based forms of advertising. If this premise is true, then the large and interesting literature on online advertising has little to tell us about the monetization of online applications. The paper focuses instead on other, nonadvertising, sources of revenue.

This is not to suggest that the literature on Internet marketing [57], Internet advertising, and personalization [1, 33, 55], or viral marketing and influentials [27, 28],<sup>2</sup> or social network marketing [18, 41, 47] is not helpful in understanding emerging trends in advertising and marketing. Rather, we suggest that it is orthogonal to understanding the role of online social networks, which we argue are difficult to harness or to convert to commercial objectives.

The growth rate, both of Internet advertising and of the share price or acquisition price of companies believed to have the prospect of Internet advertising, has been staggering. Still, the premise of this paper is that growth of Internet advertising and the prospects for firms that rely on Internet advertising are both far more limited than currently believed.

## The Backlash Against Internet Advertising

Objections to Internet advertising are becoming more clear, and they are not very different from the well-known objections to more traditional advertising. The problems faced by users are the same, whatever the media:

•	 Spam is spam, whatever its form. Just as we have learned to ignore commercials and channel surf whenever we see an unhappy caveman or a cheerful gecko selling insurance on broadcast television, we have learned to resent and to ignore a prerecorded telemarketing offer during dinner, an offer to engage in a business deal with the orphan son of the former defense minister of Nigeria, or a misdirecting URL in response to a Google search. Spam—unsolicited, poorly targeted, and irrelevant online communication—is losing its impact. The problems with advertising simply port over to problems with Internet and Second Life advertising when advertising is transferred to a new medium.

•	 Likewise, fraud is fraud, whatever its form. The principal forms of online fraud involve misdirection on search (attempting to exploit the consumer) or click fraud and overstatement of impressions and click-throughs (attempting to exploit the advertiser). While perhaps alarmist, articles have been claiming that online advertising fraud is potentially catastrophic to the future of the Internet (see, e.g., [20, 36, 39]).

•	 Invasion of privacy is abusive, regardless of who is doing it. Even a favorite (and until recently) counterculture Web site such as Facebook can be guilty of invasion of privacy and can provoke an immediate and furious backlash from consumers who feel that private information is being disclosed in ways that can injure or embarrass them. A recent search for online postings with the three words “Facebook,” “Beacon,” and “Arrogance” returns 44,500 hits (as of February 22, 2009).<sup>3</sup>

And the intensity of consumers’ objections is becoming clear:

•	 Internet advertising has already led to a general backlash against banners and popups, of course, against e-mail spam, of course, and against Beacon. We believe that this will also begin to show up as consumer resistance to or resentment of cell phone–based advertising, and even potentially against anything that can be viewed as a form of mobile spam.

•	 Resentment of Google misdirection is on the rise. Google’s sponsored search, sponsored Web site piggybacking, and misdirection of consumers’ search to the firms that have been the highest bidders for key words and trademarks is increasingly seen as inferior search [50]. Hotels.com was the subject of investigations by numerous states’ attorneys general.

•	 Consumers, privacy advocates, social network Web site users [9], and large advertisers [44] all made their objections to Facebook’s Beacon program quite clear, leading to its redesign. Some forms of advertising are clearly objectionable to consumers.

## Consumers’ Continued Need for Information

The pote ntia l re je ction of Inte rne t a dve rtising does not mean that consumers do not need, or do not value, information. Indeed, a large body of research suggests that consumers are better informed than ever before, and that they are using this information in a variety of ways.

One of the most striking changes in consumer behavior and in marketing strategy has been the dramatic impact of the Internet on the sale of long tail [2] items, things for which the demand is too small, and too fragmented, to justify a physically based retailer with inventory on hand. While the “geographic catchment area” of any physical store may be too small to justify stocking these items, the national footprint of an online store may be large enough to generate sufficient demand, and thus to make online selling of these products both possible and profitable. This is not trading up; many of these products are not luxuries [48]. This is trading out, or moving away from the mass market to smaller, finely tuned sweet spots that form the basis of resonance marketing strategies [11, 16, 17]. The variety of categories affected appears limitless. AsiaMex.com offers 51 different hot sauces and 27 different soy sauces; Shizuokatea.com offers 73 varieties of green tea; and Penzeys Spices offers 4 different kinds of paprika, 12 different forms of peppercorns, and 13 different kinds of hot chili peppers. Barbecuewood.com has perhaps the largest variety of flavored woods for barbecue smoke, with 97 offerings under wood chunks and 92 offerings under wood chips. Surely the choice of pecan wood or peach wood for smoking instead of hickory or mesquite is one of personal taste; one is not better than the other in any absolute sense, although the demand for hickory and for mesquite is much higher than for most other woods. Likewise, the demand for chipotle (with a heat rating of 5,000 to 10,000 Scoville heat units, the standard rating for heat or intensity in chili peppers) is much higher than the demand for piquins (at about 140,000 Scovilles) or habaneros (at about 350,000). The demand for the more extreme products is much less, making them much harder to find, but that does not imply that one chili pepper is better than another in any absolute sense.

We have shown that consumers frequently engage in trading out, finding the products that best match their individual wants and needs, cravings, and longings. In particular, we studied the craft brewing industry. While the relationship is not necessarily causal, we have found that this behavior emerged at the time of widespread Internet usage, and that online reviews can be a powerful predictor of consumer behavior. Consumers willing to pay a premium price, and seeking delight and perfect fit with their preferences, behave as if their purchasing decisions are influenced by the most positive reviews online [11, 16, 17].

In contrast, another well-documented form of behavior is trading down. Consumers seeking to avoid disappointment when shopping for online hotel bargains behave as if their decisions are influenced by the presence or absence of the most negative online hotel reviews [16]. Finally, consumers seeking a social experience, in a category where price is uniform and not a basis for decision, behave as if the means of other customers’ reviews are the best predictor [22]. While this does not prove that customers are informed by consumer-generated online content, they certainly behave as if they are.

Consumers appear to be better informed than ever before, and they appear to use their information even when shopping for traditional goods in traditional settings. If, as our prior research [11, 12, 16, 17] shows, consumers are becoming better informed through mechanisms other than advertising, then the need for advertising to inform them and the ability of advertising to manipulate their beliefs will both decline.

## Selling Content, Experience, and Virtual Things

Cha rging for conte nt ha s not be n ne ce ssa ry for most of the period of mass media; the cost of a daily newspaper was sufficient only to cover the wood pulp and printing, and broadcast radio and television were both entirely free. The price of ads was set high enough to provide cross-subsidies that funded the development of news stories and broadcast programming, or simply content. We know from work by Baumol and others [6, 7, 8, 38] that this form of cross-subsidy is the hallmark of a noncontestable market; that is, content providers enjoyed a near monopoly on the distribution of advertising and were able to set the prices high enough to provide the development of content. This may no longer be possible; there being no shortage of places to stick ads, and at best limited interest in ads, the price of advertising is going to be driven low enough to make it extremely difficult to fund content delivery. The online version of the Seattle Post-Intelligencer (www.seattlepi.com) is going to be a stripped-down version of its former self, and as long as there is no reasonable monetization model for content providers, content choice is going to be diminished. The alternative, of course, is the necessity to charge for content.

As noted in the Economist, in “The End of the Free Lunch—Again,” eventually consumers are going to have to pay for content, or in their words: “Ultimately, though, every business needs revenues—and advertising, it transpires, is not going to provide enough. Free content and services were a beguiling idea. But the lesson of two Internet bubbles is that somebody somewhere is going to have to pick up the tab for lunch” [24].

## Selling Content

We expect that just as participants pay to attend seminars and executive education programs, or pay annual membership fees to join professional and academic associations or user groups, there will be an emerging market for online content and online user groups. However, to date, consumers have shown enormous resistance to paying for content. Both the New York Times and the Wall Street Journal have recently dropped fees for viewing much of their content online. Indeed, as long as almost everything is available without charge, and subsidized through advertising, charging for content will remain extremely difficult.

We know from the work by Teece [52] on cospecialized assets and appropriability regimes that there are two conditions needed to profit from an idea: (1) a unique asset needed to extract value from the idea and (2) the ability to protect from easy duplication. Legitimate newspapers and magazines do indeed have a unique asset: they have entire departments dedicated to fact-checking, and their reputation is based on ensuring the accuracy of their reporting. At present, they lack the ability to profit from this, because anyone can copy material from published stories. If stronger shortterm copyright were available—if the Wall Street Journal or the Financial Times had unique access, perhaps even something as limited as 24 hours of protected use for their stories—that would be sufficient for them to create enough value to charge for stories. Admittedly, they would need to be more efficient, and they may need to move to micropayments, allowing subscribers to pay for individual stories rather than for access to the entire bundle that constitutes the daily “paper,” but they would survive as purveyors of “all the news that’s fit to print” (New York Times) and would do so “without fear and without favor” (Financial Times).

## Selling Virtual Experience

Charging for participation in a virtual electronic social network or group, or for access to content that has been made available online, will increasingly become a revenue source, even though it is difficult at present. Currently, only a few Web sites have succeeded in capturing enough loyal interest to induce a significant number of users to pay for access. The most visible and the most successful are massively multiplayer online games (MMO games) or massively multiparticipant online virtual world electronic social networks (MMO virtual worlds), where a large number of users pay for gaming software, monthly access, or both. Second Life and Blizzard/World of Warcraft are the two best-known examples. MMO Web sites have indeed found a way to charge for participation, either for the initial software, the monthly access, or both. Revenues from the online gaming segment of social networking are growing quickly, now exceed those of Hollywood, and are half those of cable television, so the prospects for usage fees from successful social networking Web sites would appear to be considerable.

Again, we know from Teece [52] that it may be difficult to protect the revenues from an idea (the World of Warcraft game) that can be replicated. Here, work on switching costs, market participation externalities, and network externalities more generally provide the protection that World of Warcraft needs. If a player quits World of Warcraft for a competitor, he or she loses his or her World of Warcraft status, his or her player’s level, and the accessories won through numerous quests; the player also loses access to his or her guild, the players with whom he or she has cooperated for months or years. The player experiences switching costs. He or she moves to an MMO domain with fewer participants and fewer opportunities for interaction. He or she has reduced benefits from network externalities. This may be sufficient to provide the necessary defense for World of Warcraft and its revenues from virtual experience.

## Selling Online Services

As cloud computing and software as a service gain acceptance, selling online services may gain in acceptance. At present, Google is providing software services that compete effectively with Microsoft Office; although they are currently available without charge, charges could later be imposed. SurveyMonkey.com (for online survey data collection, summary, and storage), MobileMe (Apple’s cloud computing service for synchronization of an iPhone address book and calendar with one or more desktop and laptop systems), and premium Flickr service (for the storage and sharing of a large number of digital images) are but a few early examples of consumer-oriented subscription-based Internet software services.

## Selling Content Gained from Social Networks

The social networks have been difficult to monetize for a variety of reasons [12]. Facebook is currently exploring the use of its content to provide insight to traditional companies, and, less benignly, perhaps to permit background checks on potential employees. If Facebook Connect can avoid the invasion-of-privacy concerns and the backlash associated with Beacon (its heavy-handed foray into online advertising), this may prove valuable.

## Selling the Accessories for Virtual Experience

Users will often buy virtual accessories or additional capabilities for their icons, paying for “professional” quality, rather than taking the time to earn their own accessories through the game. This includes paying for avatar design, paying for avatar clothing and accessories and pets (www.sldogs.com), and paying for capabilities such as dance synchronization in Second Life. The online economies have indeed become economically significant [4, 53].

Although it is strongly discouraged, and has a certain social stigma attached to it, it is even possible to buy an experienced character for World of Warcraft, one with a high competitive level and with a portfolio of power-granting accessories, with prices ranging from an average of \$400 to more than \$10,000 for top-end characters. While it is not yet fully understood, there is an emerging market that involves paying real money for virtual goods and services [25].

Obviously, this does not generalize to a large number of companies that provide content or services over the Internet; I cannot see the New York Times, TripAdvisor, or even TechCrunch earning significant revenues selling dresses for avatars.

## Selling Access to Customers

In a ddition to se lling conte nt dire ctly, some Web sites may be able to charge for acting as honest brokers, providing trusted and trustworthy recommendations to customers shopping for goods and services. Alternatively, and less benign, some Web sites may be able to charge for acting as if they were honest brokers, and charging for trusted but not entirely trustworthy recommendations. We call this second category misdirection, and start with it.

## Selling Misdirection

Misdirection misinforms the consumer, and online misdirection presently comes in two principal forms—misdirection through search engines and misdirection through comparison-shopping Web sites.

Sponsored search is a means of misdirecting the consumer, in which Google, for example, sells search terms to the highest bidder; the consumer is then directed to the

Web site of the highest bidder for the search terms, and not to the Web site that the consumer might otherwise have found through organic search. More egregious forms of search engine misdirection exist, such as Google’s online piggybacking [50], in which the user is offered a URL that truly appears to be taking the user to the Web site he or she desires, but which then takes the user to an unrelated Web site that has purchased (some might say hijacked) the trademark of another seller from Google.<sup>4</sup> The example of Cornish “wreckers” is instructive and suggests that misdirection has been a commercial problem for centuries. These were, in essence, pirates who operated from onshore and who would place lights, false lights, along the British coast, impersonating local lighthouses. These lights were placed not to mark safe fairways for sailors and navigators but to lure ships to their destruction. The pirates could then harvest (or as they preferred to describe it, salvage) the cargoes for their own profit [5].

The previously noted backlash against Google suggests that it will be difficult to sustain a business model based on online misrepresentation in an environment as transparent as the Internet, and, indeed, awareness of and resistance to Google’s practices is growing among computer professionals [42] and is now being noted in the popular press [29]. More recently, there have been discussions in the high-tech blogosphere about whether the Obama administration will regulate or break up Google. Christine A. Varney, nominated by President Barack Obama to be the next U.S. antitrust chief, has described Google Inc. as a monopolist that will dominate online computing services the way Microsoft Corporation ruled software. “For me, Microsoft is so last century. They are not the problem,” Varney said at a June 19 panel discussion sponsored by the American Antitrust Institute. The U.S. economy will “continually see a problem— potentially with Google” because it already “has acquired a monopoly in Internet online advertising” [43]. Some states are not waiting for federal action; although it ultimately was not enacted, Utah House Bill 450, in 2009, would have rendered illegal the sale of key words derived from corporate trademarks and the use of trademarks in sponsored search.<sup>5</sup>

The Utah bill, in particular, seems clearly aimed at making the distinction between advertising and misappropriation of a trademark for the misdirection of consumers, for commercial gain either directly by the online “misdirector” or for other parties who gain from the “misdirector’s” actions. It appears that at least in some American legislatures demanding payment from trademark holders to avoid misdirection of their customers is no more seen as a form of legitimate advertising than demanding protection money to prevent arson is seen as new commercial construction. Indeed, even at Google, concern about regulation, once unimaginable, is now very real [51]. A recent (April 3, 2009) appellate court decision<sup>6</sup> has chipped away at Google’s business model for sponsored search, remanding back to the lower court a decision that found in favor of Google; when the user specifically requests one firm in a Google search, returning results that send the user to direct competitors may be declared illegal misdirection, although even this remains unclear until the lower court decision is reached after a new trial and, no doubt, the next round of appeals.

One would surely expect that, over time, misrepresentation would be unsustainable as a business model, especially in an environment as transparent and as interactive as the Internet. And yet, for years, misrepresentation survived as at least a part of the business model of Hotels.com. Hotel executives have claimed that Hotels.com did not in some cases represent properly room quality and varied the price charged for the same hotel to suit the quality level requested by the guest. Additionally, a class-action lawsuit maintained that Hotels.com on occasion reported properties as sold out when rooms were available, but not at a preferred rate for Hotels.com [10].

## Selling Validation and Assessment Through Community Content

There is considerable value that can be obtained by harnessing social content by linking user-generated content and reviews to add value to a retailing Web site. Obtaining value from user-generated online reviews is a direct consequence of the relationship between resonance marketing and consumer informedness [11, 16, 17, 21, 41, 47].

The Beeryard (www.beeryard.com), in Wayne, Pennsylvania, provides a great example. Its Web site is remarkably well designed to promote user informedness:

•	 One click on the upper right allows you to determine what is new and which beers have arrived within the last week, two weeks, month, or two months.

•	 A second click allows you to get a description of the beer, provided by the brewer.

•	 Another click takes you to RateBeer, where you can have access to hundreds of thousands of beer reviews.

•	 And yet another click enables you to check the reviewing profile and history of a reviewer to see if his or her tastes are similar to yours and if his or her review should influence your purchasing decisions.

Before its Web site was developed, 90 percent of the Beeryard’s business was from within a five-mile radius of the shop; now, after the Web site was launched, half of the business is from outside this distance and 30 percent is from out of state.

Moreover, the nonlocal business is overwhelmingly either American craft brews or imports, at \$25 to \$160 a case. Significantly, the margin percentages are higher on the higher-priced beers, the absolute margins are better, and the labor involved is the same. These are beers that the Beeryard is delighted to sell, and the business generated by its Web site is volume that the Beeryard is delighted to have captured. Unfortunately, RateBeer has not figured out how to capture any of the value generated by its Web site when used by the Beeryard or by other retailers.

TripAdvisor provides a better-known example of gaining value through communitygenerated content, although the example is not widely known or well understood. A decade ago, if travelers were interested in a hotel in a city they did not know well, they would seek recommendations from full-service travel agents. The agents’ experience with numerous hotels was the travelers’ best guarantee of satisfaction.

Now most consumers search for discount hotels online and get recommendations from sites such as Hotels.com. TripAdvisor provides access to hundreds of thousands of online reviews, posted by individual travelers reporting on their experiences, both good and bad. TripAdvisor provides the detail needed to assess recommendations from Hotels.com, much as a trusted agent’s experience provides credibility to a traditional recommendation. Since Hotels.com did not attempt to influence or censor TripAdvisor content, the Web site was (and is) trusted and helped put recommendations from Hotels.com at a level of trust comparable to those from an experienced travel agent.

Not surprisingly, TripAdvisor was owned by the same company, Interactive Corporation, that owned Hotels.com. The success of Hotels.com was in some ways made possible by the success of TripAdvisor. It was not necessary that TripAdvisor be directly profitable, as long as TripAdvisor contributed to the profitability of Hotels.com. Although Interactive was careful to maintain the independence of reviews on TripAdvisor, it was essential to its success that the relationship between the two was not widely known. This is explored in more detail, and additional references are provided, in previous research [12].

## Social Search

There is also considerable value that can be obtained by harnessing social content by using social networking content to improve search results. Once again, obtaining value from user-generated online reviews is a direct consequence of the relationship between resonance marketing and consumer informedness [11, 16, 17, 21, 41, 47], combined with the well-known phenomenon of homophily [37]: individuals in social networks, online or off, tend to befriend others with similar tastes and preferences. In the presence of homophily, the results from searches that ask, “Where do my friends eat in Chicago?” or “Where do my friends stay in Paris?” are likely to outperform the natural searches available today, and greatly outperform sponsored searches.

There are many problems with this, including the fact that many of a user’s friends may not be on Facebook or other networks yet and those that are may not post their hotel or automobile or restaurant preferences. Most seriously, while it is clear how Microsoft may benefit from this, using its Facebook connection to undercut Googlesponsored search, it is not clear how Microsoft or any other firm could monetize this directly. More recently, Twitter has also begun exploring social search, and, again, it is unclear how this will be monetized.

## Contextual Mobile Advertising

Increasingly, social networking is based on smart phones and mobile computing technology. Commercial context-specific text messaging using mobile devices will emerge either as the most effective form of targeted messaging or as the most invasive and expensive form of spam.<sup>7</sup> The logic behind investing in mobile commercial text messaging is simple:

•	 The market for mobile messaging is becoming large enough to interest advertisers and entrepreneurs. Cell phone usage is becoming nearly universal, smart phone usage (phones with messaging and Internet capability) is increasing, and an increasing number of phone subscribers have unlimited text messaging plans, unlimited Internet access from their smart phones, or both.

•	 Mobile messaging based on both the recipient’s social networking and the recipient’s current location has the potential to be relevant and effective.

This last point can be amplified as follows [12]:

•	 targeted mobile messages can be relevant to individual recipients, based on their interests and their current location;

•	 targeted mobile messages can allow the user to act; and

•	 targeted mobile messages can allow the user to act on opportunities that are both relevant and time sensitive.

At a minimum, these messages would not be seen as intrusive, and at best the recipient might respond to opportunities that would otherwise be missed. The viability of this business is as yet uncertain. Our preliminary research suggests that the reactions of individuals to online social networks vary greatly by age [18, 19] and presumably reactions to online messaging derived from social networks would also vary.

Much of Google’s future business model appears to be based on targeting advertising using increasingly strong forms of contextual mobile marketing. The contents of users’ sent and received Gmail messages, Web searches, and even Google Docs word processing documents can be scanned by Google and used as the basis of highly tailored and individualized advertising. This can be combined with GPS data from Latitude (www.google.com/latitude) to allow Google to direct finely tailored, contextually sensitive messages directly to the user’s phone. In the limit, a user texting to a friend that he or she is hungry might immediately receive an ad from his or her favorite style of restaurant, located within a block or two of the user’s current location.

This has not yet been widely understood, and it is not yet clear how the user community will react. Concerns with privacy continue to surface on the Net. Google is, nonetheless, proceeding full speed ahead with a number of applications that may raise further privacy concerns. Consumer and regulatory reaction needs to be monitored before the effectiveness of this can be accurately assessed. The collection of information sources from various applications and the use of the phone both for GPS data and for message delivery would appear to give Google a strong competitive advantage in this domain.

It is, however, not yet clear how customers will view contextual ads, either as helpful recommendations or as pushed paid content from an agent (Google) that represents a seller rather than the customer’s own best interests, or even from an agent who is profiting from invasion of privacy. Alternatives, such as opt-in services whereby the user subscribes to information feeds that match recommendations to his or her preferences and serve his or her needs, rather than those of a service provider paying for access, might work better.

Vodafone has begun to implement some context-specific mobile ads using the most basic forms of contextual and nonpersonal information for targeting. This specifically includes “non-personal information such as time, the web page visited and the functionality of the phone” and specifically excludes personal information such as “personal demographic information such as age, gender, address and personal behaviour information such as download history, and webpage history or physical location” unless customer consent has been obtained.<sup>8</sup> This is, of course, far less than the full functionality we have assumed for contextual mobile advertising.

A few commercial context-specific and time-sensitive text messaging services may benefit everyone, such as letting the most loyal guests of a restaurant know when it is still possible to get a reservation if they act immediately, eliminating the inefficiency of empty tables, but the restaurant will do this itself, using its e-mail or cell phone contact lists. This is different from advertising, and may not be monetized by any intermediary. Of course, in an age before texting and e-mail, restaurants would have welcomed the all-knowing intermediary as the only mechanism available for communicating quickly with its most loyal customers. Now, restaurants have lists of their most loyal customers and can send out real-time messages of interest. If the Blue Note, an internationally famous jazz club in Greenwich Village, were to text loyal customers on a night that they are in New York, telling them that it is still possible to get a table for two for the great trumpeter Clark Terry, this might be valuable to the customer. Of course, there is no intermediary for this interaction, and this is more like direct communication than paid advertising.

We simply do not have the experience needed to assess this yet.

The findings so far are decidedly mixed. The most powerful revenue source on the Net so far is paying for key words, which Google calls adwords auctions and this paper calls misdirection. Community content recommendations, social search, and contextual mobile advertising all seem to offer the possibility of providing value for users, as long as they are trusted; the greater the monetization of each, the less trusted it may become, and the more subject it may be to deliberate manipulation.

Paying for content and services may be the area that experiences the most growth. For now, this is problematic; as soon as a story is posted on one site, it is immediately picked up on others, making monetization difficult. Apple’s iTunes is the most successful at charging for content, perhaps because the price for an individual song is low enough and the prospects of litigation are now daunting enough to discourage piracy. For this to generalize, publishers such as the New York Times and the Wall Street Journal would need to find some way to protect their content, at least for a short period of time. Similarly, World of Warcraft appears to be most successful in charging for online experiences, perhaps because switching costs and the positive participation externalities of its large user base allows it to charge customers who are reluctant to switch to competing games.

## Predictions

The following represent informed assessments of the prospects for successfully monetizing the Net in the future:

•	 Selling virtual things. People will pay for superior, timely, original content and for superior online experiences. Presently, some readers willingly pay for the Financial Times, Economist, and Foreign Affairs because they value the content; they will pay for the most current stories online. Perhaps archive material will quickly become free, because it is likely to be available elsewhere. Similarly, some people willingly pay the cover charge for a favorite jazz club in New York or the cost of theater tickets, and they will continue to pay to participate in Second Life or World of Warcraft if that is their preferred pastime. Because we continue to compete for status through our purchases of accessories, clothes, and homes in the physical world, it appears at least possible that some people will purchase virtual accessories in Second Life.

•	 Selling access. Misdirection requires that the deceived party be unaware of the deception or that someone else bears the risk. At present, Google’s business model is based more on the threat of misdirection than on actually misdirecting consumers, and as long as sellers bid enough to ensure that consumers are not misdirected, consumers will not switch search engines. Prospects here depend on three possibilities: (1) legitimate Web sites stop participating in key word auctions, or (2) there is federal intervention to limit the abuse of monopoly power possessed by search engine operators, leading to (3) the cleaning up of search or a switch by consumers from current search engines to those tailored to their needs rather than to the needs of a bidder in a key word auction. In contrast, while people will continue to value community content and social search, these will be difficult to monetize. Finally, contextual mobile ads offer significant opportunities, but they, too, will be difficult to monetize.

The Internet is about freedom, and it appears likely that a truly free population will not be held captive and forced to watch ads. We have always known that freedom comes at a price. Perhaps the price of Internet freedom and the failure of ads will be paying a fair price for the content and the experience and the recommendations that we value; surely, the Economist has come to agree [24].

## Directions for Future Research

The following developments are worthy of future study:

•	 Google. Will Google’s attempt to monetize its knowledge of individuals result in a successful platform envelopment strategy [23], trumping Microsoft, or will consumer awareness lead to rejection of even Google’s core search engine products? Far too much current research in the management information systems (MIS) community deals with optimum pricing for key word auctions, and far too little addresses whether paid search masquerading as an unbiased response to user queries will remain viable.

•	 Social search. Will social networks become the basis of effective search, render ing sponsored search for products irrelevant, changing the balance between the Microsoft/Facebook alliance and Google? Will social search [34] augment or replace recommendation Web sites?

•	 Monetization of social content and the deliberate manipulation of community content. Will the use of online user-generated content to reinforce other online marketing efforts increase? Will more manufacturers and retailers find opportunities to benefit, the way that Beeryard benefits from RateBeer, Hotels.com benefits from TripAdvisor, and OpenTable.com benefits from Zagat? Will deliberate content manipulation become a problem? This is less significant at sites such as RateBeer, where there may be hundreds or even thousands of reviews available for each beer, but sites such as TripAdvisor, where each property may have only a few reviews, may be far more vulnerable to manipulation. If so, how can manipulation be detected, and what social and legal policy implications will there be?
