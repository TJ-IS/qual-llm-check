Keywords: Internet abuses Internet use policy Rational choice Cost bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis

## a b s t r a c t

Current studies on compliance with security policies have largely ignored the impact of the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of deviant behavior, personal norms, and organizational context. Drawing on the literature in criminology, this paper applies rational choice theory to examine how employees' intention to comply with Internet use policy is driven by cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t assessments, personal norms and organizational context factors. The results indicate that employees' compliance intention is the result of competing in<sup>fl</sup>uences of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, formal sanctions, and security risks. Furthermore, the effect of sanction severity is found to be moderated by personal norms.

## 1. Introduction

In recent decades, companies have increasingly leveraged the Internet to enhance their internal and external connectivity. Accompanying the well-known bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the use of Internet technology in the workplace are new threats such as increased security risks and improper use. Internet abuses by employees at the workplace are a real concern to nearly all companies and include various non-workrelated Internet activities such as checking personal e-mails, browsing non-work-related Web sites, chatting online, gaming, investing, shopping, or even getting involved in cybercrimes “The International Data Corp. estimated that 30% to 40% of employee Internet use isn't work related” [39]. Eighty-seven percent of employees have used the Internet for personal purposes while at work and, of this proportion, 53% browse the Internet for personal purposes on a daily basis [44]. It is estimated that companies lose \$54 billion annually as a result of lost productivity from Web sur<sup>fi</sup>ng [1]. These non-work-related Internet activities not only reduce the productivity of employees but also open the door to a wide spectrum of security breaches such as viruses and spywares. According to Sophos, in 2007, the Web became the primary route for the spread of malicious codes [42]. Sophos discovered one new infected webpage every 14 s, or 6000 a day in 2007. It has become critically important for companies to safeguard their Internet communications and regulate the Internet usage of their employees.

An Internet use policy (IUP), an important type of security policy, is adopted by most companies to reduce Internet use for non-workrelated purposes [51]. For example, Websense, Inc., an Internet access management company, surveyed 224 companies in 2001 and found that 83% of them have adopted Internet use policies [51]. Despite the wide deployment of IUP in companies, the scope of Internet misuse is still on the rise [19]; more than 50% of the companies deemed their Internet use policy to be ineffective [51]. Non-compliance is a major issue.

The purpose of the current study is to apply one of the leading sociological explanations of deviant behaviors, namely rational choice theory [3,32], to Internet abuses in the workplace. The current undertaking can make an important contribution to understanding compliance with IUPs. In this study, we employ the rational choice perspective to examine the factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing IUP compliance. In particular, our research questions center on 1) major costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that factor into employees' intention to comply with the IUP (compliance intention), 2) the relationships among these factors, and 3) mechanisms that could facilitate IUP compliance. Part of our motivation is to better understand the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and potential limitations of formal sanctions on IUP compliance. We are particularly interested in whether the effect of formal sanctions depends on personal moral norms about non-work-related Internet usage in organizations.

The results of our study suggest that employees' IUP compliance intention is affected by both the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of Internet abuses and the perceived costs of non-compliance, including formal sanction risks and potential Internet security risks. Despite support for the deterrent effect of those two cost factors, our research shows that their effect is not as strong as the effect of perceived benefits and personal norms against Internet abuses. We also found that the effect of formal sanctions is conditioned upon personal norms. Sanction severity only exhibits a deterrence effect for those with very low personal norms and could erode the compliance intention of those with moderate to high personal norms against Internet abuses at the workplace.

Our study has important implications for organizations that seek to reduce Internet abuses in the workplace. It suggests that companies can rely on three approaches to increase IUP compliance. First, organizations can nurture voluntary compliance through enhancing personal norms against Internet abuses. Second, they can rely on Internet security awareness campaigns and make employees aware of the growing threats from using the Internet. Third, organizations can resort to the deterrence effect of sanction probability by making employees aware of the implementation of surveillance measures for detecting Internet abuses. This study also provides direction for future research by emphasizing the importance of moral factors and the context of the organization in which security policies are enforced.

The remainder of the document is structured as follows: in the next section, we review the literature and use the rational choice framework to understand the IUP compliance decision as a cost– bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis bounded by personal moral factors and organizational context. In the following section, we propose our research model, its theoretical underpinnings and hypotheses. Next, we describe our research methodology, followed by a discussion of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study. Finally, we conclude with major <sup>fi</sup>ndings, limitations, contributions, implications, and future research directions.

## 2. Prior research and theoretical foundation

## 2.1. Prior research on individual security policy compliance

Various theories have been applied to examine individual security policy compliance in the workplace, for example general deterrence theory [31], protection motivation theory [31], and agency theory [22] among others. Table 1 compares existing studies on individual policy compliance. As Table 1 shows, there are a limited number of theorybased empirical studies on security policy compliance by individual employees, suggesting the early research stage of individual security policy compliance [22]. Most of these studies are based on general deterrence theory and/or protection motivation theory, focusing on fear-based mechanisms, i.e. the fear of formal sanctions and threats to the organization's security [22,23,31,40]. General deterrence theory emphasizes the effect of formal sanctions in motivating employees to follow organizational policies. Herath and Rao [22] found that formal sanctions increase security policy compliance. Protection motivation theory focuses on the effect of threat appraisals and coping appraisals. In the context of security policy compliance, threat appraisals are an individual's assessment of the level of security risks and coping appraisals refer to one's assessment as to whether one is capable of complying with security policies and whether such compliance is effective in reducing security risks [40]. Siponen et al. [40] extended protection motivation theory to explain IS security policy compliance and suggested that perceived threats to the organization's security in<sup>fl</sup>uence employees' intention to comply with security policies. However, Pahnila et al. [31] integrated general deterrence theory and protection motivation theory and found that formal sanctions fail to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on security policy compliance intention. Moreover, the effectiveness of formal sanctions is arguable. Studies in criminology have challenged the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of formal sanctions [29,32,43,49]. First, formal sanctions increase the cost of policy enforcement [30,43]. The implementation of formal sanctions requires considerable investment in surveillance technology or personnel to detect deviant behaviors. Second, formal sanctions and monitoring may negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence trust and morale [29]. Third, recent <sup>fi</sup>ndings in criminology suggest that moral norms may more strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uence deviance behavior than formal sanctions and the effect of formal sanctions may be moderated by personal moral norms and social in<sup>fl</sup>uences [32,49].

Formal sanctions and the fear of security risks to the organization have provided only a partial explanation of why non-compliance with security policies occurs. Research that applies various socialorganizational perspectives to this phenomenon is particularly limited [13,22]. Herath and Rao [22] used agency theory to explain compliance with information security policy and considered not only penalties but also the social pressure of relevant others and the perceived effectiveness of one's security behaviors. Chan et al. [7] adopted organizational climate theory and examined the effect of the organization's information security climate on security policy compliance.

From the above, we see that prior studies have provided signi<sup>fi</sup>cant but partial insights as to why non-compliance with security policies occurs. They have largely ignored the effect of the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of deviant behaviors, moral values, and the conditions for formal sanctions to be effective. Potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from deviant behaviors could be the major reason for poor compliance with security policies, as recognized by <sup>fi</sup>ndings in recent research in criminology [32]. In this study we will apply one such theory from criminology, rational choice theory, re<sup>fi</sup>ned by Paternoster and Simpson [32] to deviant behaviors in the workplace. The theory provides a comprehensive perspective on corporate deviant behaviors by considering the cost– bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis of individual offenders, the effect of moral beliefs, and the organizational context.

In the subsections below, we <sup>fi</sup>rst give an overview of the rational choice theory of corporate crime developed by Paternoster and Simpson [32]. Then, we elaborate on the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing security policy compliance and further discuss the impact of personal moral beliefs and organizational context factors on decisions to comply with security policies.

## 2.2. IUP compliance and rational choice theory

Rational choice theory was originally developed by Becker [5] with the central proposition that offenders weigh the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of deviant behaviors in deciding whether to offend. It has been adapted to various contexts to explain deviant behaviors such as income tax evasion, juvenile delinquency, theft, and drunk driving [32]. Paternoster and Simpson [32] further re<sup>fi</sup>ned the theory to explain corporate crimes. The theory has two basic premises: “(1) that decisions to offend are based on a balancing of both the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of offending and (2) that what are important are the decision maker's perceived or subjective expectations reward and cost” [32]. The <sup>fi</sup>rst premise suggests that individuals evaluate the expected consequences of multiple alternatives and choose the one with the best outcome. The second premise emphasizes that deviant behaviors are choices by individuals based on perceived rewards and costs. End users are often the weakest link in information security management. It is the deviant behaviors of individual employees that have the biggest impact on the information security of a company. For example, employees can greatly increase a company's exposure to security threats by visiting non-work-related websites, downloading non-work-related software, etc. In this context, individual assessment of the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of deviant behaviors and their personal moral beliefs are critical determinants of their policy compliance intention. Therefore, in this study, we look at the Internet abuse issue from an end user's perspective, i.e. how costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts perceived by individual employees and their moral beliefs drive their IUP compliance intention.

The rational choice model by Paternoster and Simpson [32] also provides valuable insights into the effect of the organizational context. Internet abuses in the workplace occur in speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational contexts. Until now, only a few studies on computer security and abuses examined the organizational context in which deviant behaviors occur. Chan et al. [8], drawing upon the safety climate literature, found that a strong perception of the organizational security climate helps to curb non-compliant behaviors. Lim [26] focused on organizational justice, suggesting that perceived injustice in the employment relationship will cause employees to rationalize their subsequent engagement in Internet abuses. In this study, we consider two constructs that are tied to the organizational context, including organizational norms and organizational identi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

As shown above, deviant behaviors and security policy compliance are driven by assessments of costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts pertinent to individual employees and are further circumscribed by their personal norms and organizational context factors. A general model is presented in Fig. 1 to illustrate these three types of forces in<sup>fl</sup>uencing individual employees' intention to comply with information security policies. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c form of the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis varies with different types of deviant behaviors [32] and is elaborated in detail in the following subsections in the context of IUP compliance intention.

## 2.3. Cost–benefit analysis

Rational choice theory suggests that cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analyses should inhibit or drive employees' IUP compliance. Employees are likely to abuse the Internet access if the risks can be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the Internet abuses. The risks of Internet abuses to individual employees include the possibility of formal and/or informal sanctions, or social censure from important others such as family members, friends, and colleagues. Also, Internet abuses can expose employees' computers and data to additional security risks. The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of Internet abuses could be the savings in cost and time over using private Internet access, convenience, or emotional bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as making the work life more interesting [35].

## 2.4. Personal norms

In addition to cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analyses such as above, the decision to commit Internet abuses can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by an employee's personal norms regarding the deviant act. Personal norms are not part of the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis and can exert an independent role in eliciting compliant behaviors [32]. At the same time, they may restrict the effect of factors that enter into the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis [32]. Some employees may refrain from Internet abuses simply because they believe it is wrong to use the Internet access provided by the organization for personal purposes. Therefore, under strong personal norms against Internet abuses, the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis will have minimal impact on compliant behavior. Conversely, in the presence of weak personal norms against Internet abuses, sanctions and security threats may need to be emphasized to ensure policy compliance.

## 2.5. Organizational context factors

We also considered two organizational factors that may indirectly or directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence employees' IUP compliance intention: organizational norms and organizational identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Organizational norms and organizational identi<sup>fi</sup>cation may play an important role in formulating the personal norms of individual employees. Organizational norms about Internet abuses are de<sup>fi</sup>ned here as moral standards related to using the organization's Internet access for personal purposes. Organizational norms are shared by most of the people in an organization. Previous studies suggest that internalization of external normative beliefs [24,49] leads to personal moral standards or personal norms. Organizational norms, as external normative beliefs, are likely to become part of an employee's internal personal norms when the employee identi<sup>fi</sup>es himself or herself as a member of the organization.

## 3. Research model

In this study, IUP compliance is examined as a cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t-based behavior in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by personal norms and organizational context factors. The following subsections propose the research model (Fig. 2) and hypothesize the relationships between constructs. The research model depicts how employees' intention to comply with the Internet use policy is driven by a tradeoff assessment of the risks and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of Internet abuses and how the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis competes with and/or is bounded by the effect of personal moral norms and organizational context factors. The model suggests that employees' IUP compliance intention will increase when 1) employees perceive high threats from formal or informal sanctions or high security risks to their computer or data and 2) employees have high personal norms against Internet abuses. IUP compliance intention may be reduced when employees hold strong bene<sup>fi</sup>t beliefs about Internet abuses. The model also suggests that personal norms against Internet abuses can be enhanced by the joint effect of organizational norms and organizational identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. The following subsections illustrate each of the approaches and its impact on behavioral intention to comply with the Internet use policy.

## 3.1. Formal sanctions

The role of formal sanctions has been widely examined using deterrence theory and has received some support in several IS studies. Deterrence has been suggested as the primary mechanism for reducing computer abuses and combating software piracy [34] in organizations. Rational choice theory also af<sup>fi</sup>rms formal sanctions as important instruments for deterring deviant behaviors [32,49]. Deterrence mechanisms consist of two dimensions: detection probability (or sanction certainty) and sanction severity [49]. These two dimensions are both related to an individual's perception rather than the actual detection probability and sanction severity level. In our research context, detection probability is employees' perception of the probability that they will be caught if they use the Internet access provided by the organization for personal purposes. It is a type of perceived risk of being caught committing Internet abuses. Prior studies have found that a low level of detection probability is related to increased frequency of employee theft [25] and software piracy [34]. High perceived detection probability tends to decrease the attractiveness of Internet abuses. Employees are more likely to abide by the IUP when they perceive a high likelihood of being caught for committing Internet abuses. Therefore,

H1. Perceived detection probability has a positive impact on IUP compliance intention

According to rational choice theory, perceived sanction severity also plays an important role in deterring deviant behaviors. A high level of perceived severity of formal sanctions tends to increase the perceived cost of deviant behaviors and counteracts the attractiveness or bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of deviant behaviors. So, a high level of perceived severity of formal sanctions may drive the net effect of the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis in favor of IUP compliance. In other words, when employees perceive a severe level of punishment for Internet abuses, they are likely to have a high IUP compliance intention. Therefore,

H2. Perceived sanction severity has a positive impact on IUP compliance intention.

## 3.2. Informal sanctions

Besides formal sanctions, the rational choice theory of corporate crime also emphasizes the effect of informal sanctions, i.e. social censure from friends, family, and other people who are important to the employee. Informal sanctions re<sup>fl</sup>ect the extent of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence from those who are important to potential offenders. Subjective norms, one form of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence, have received considerable attention in the IS literature on technology acceptance [45]. Subjective norms, as perceived pressure to meet the expectations of important others, were found to have a direct compliance effect on technology acceptance intention when the use of the computer system was mandatory [45]. In the context of this study, subjective norms are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an employee's subjective beliefs about the extent of disapproval for deviant behaviors among those who are important to the employee. These norms are expected to directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence employees' decisions to comply with the IUP, as policy compliance is mandatory. If an employee believes that important others disapprove of Internet abuses and will look down on him or her for abusing the Internet, s/he is more likely to follow the Internet use policy. Therefore,

H3. Subjective norms against Internet abuses have a positive impact on IUP compliance intention.

## 3.3. Perceived security risks

Perceived security risks are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the perceived level of Internet security risks in the workplace. In recent years, the Internet has been embraced as the primary platform for virus attacks. Accessing non-work-related Web sites in the workplace increases the chance of an individual employee's computer or data falling victim to Internet security breaches. So, perceived security risks may act as another fear-based mechanism that increases the cost of committing Internet abuse. Woon et al. [50] found that users of home PC wireless networks tend to turn on security features when they perceive a severe level of security risk. In the context of security policy compliance, employees who perceive high security threats from the Internet to their computer or data should be more likely to take precaution in Internet usage at the workplace and more inclined to follow the Internet use policy. Therefore,

H4. Perceived security risks have a positive impact on IUP compliance intention.

## 3.4. Perceived benefits of Internet abuses

According to rational choice theory, potential offenders perform a cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts act as intrinsic or extrinsic motivation for employees to commit Internet abuse. For example, Websense, Inc.

[47] reported high employee traf<sup>fi</sup>c to Web shopping sites on the Monday after Thanksgiving holiday as it “saves employees the time and hassle of having to visit a shopping mall, while allowing them to make convenient purchases directly from their desktop.” Convenience was perceived to be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bene<sup>fi</sup>t of non-work-related Internet use [35]. Some employees also visit non-work-related Web sites for entertainment purposes such as downloading music and gaming. These perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of Internet abuses may override the effect of sanctions and security threats, and lead to the non-compliance with the Internet use policy. Therefore,

H5. Perceived benefits of Internet abuses have a negative impact on IUP compliance intention.

## 3.5. Personal norms

In addition to the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis, rational choice theory places strong emphasis on the effect of personal norms; that is, decisions to commit deviant behaviors are also subject to moral considerations. Examining policy compliance using only a cost– bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis is unlikely to provide an accurate picture. Criminology research has shown that personal norms or moral standards are strong determinants of an individual's intention to commit deviant behaviors such as corporate crimes [32] and tax evasion [49]. In this study, personal norms refer to an employee's own moral standards about Internet abuse. If an individual believes that it is morally wrong for him or her to commit Internet abuses, s/he tends to form a positive intention toward following the Internet use policy. Therefore,

H6. Personal norms against Internet abuses have a positive impact on IUP compliance intention.

To a certain extent, personal norms de<sup>fi</sup>ne what behaviors are permissible and what behaviors are taboos. Behaviors that are deemed permissible are also subject to further cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t assessments while taboos are “strongly inhibited by notions of morality,” leaving minimal room for cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t factors to take effect [32]. This implies that the effect of formal sanctions is conditioned on personal norms. Strong personal norms against deviant behaviors could make the effect of deterrence super<sup>fl</sup>uous. Formal sanctions are only effective for people who do not have strong ethical objections to deviant behaviors. This potential moderating effect of personal norms on formal sanctions has been supported by the criminology literature [32,41,49]. For example, perceived probability of detection was found to have a stronger inhibitory effect on tax evasion for individuals who regard tax evasion as acceptable and exert a weaker effect for those with strong personal norms against tax evasion [41]. Similar results were found in the context of corporate crimes by Paternoster and Simpson [32]. In this study, we expect that personal norms against Internet abuses delimit or interact with the effect of formal sanctions. Therefore,

H7. Under strong personal norms against Internet abuses, perceived detection probability has no (or weaker) effects on IUP compliance intention; but under weak personal norms, perceived detection probability will strongly increase IUP compliance intention.

H8. Under strong personal norms against Internet abuses, perceived sanction severity has no (or weaker) effects on IUP compliance intention; but under weak personal norms, perceived sanction severity will strongly increase IUP compliance intention.

## 3.6. Organizational norms and organizational identification

Rational choice theory also notes the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of organizational context factors on an employee's intention to commit deviant behaviors. Organizational norms and organizational identi<sup>fi</sup>cation are two such factors. Organizational norms pertain to the moral climate of an organization for approval or disapproval of Internet abuses. Many studies have found that organizational norms in<sup>fl</sup>uence employees' involvement in deviant behaviors such as theft and absenteeism [25]. Organizational norms may have a primarily indirect impact on policy compliance intention through personal norms. Organizational norms, as one type of social norms, can be incorporated into one's own belief structure through the process of internalization [24,48]. That is, organizational norms can be an important information source for individuals in shaping their personal moral views and be internalized as part of an individual's personal norms. If most people in an organization are not tolerant of Internet abuses, an individual employee in the organization is more likely to form personal norms against Internet abuses. Conversely, when most people in the organization approve Internet abuses, an employee tends to have weak personal norms against such abuses. Therefore,

H9. Organizational norms against Internet abuses have a positive impact on personal norms against Internet abuses.

Organizational identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is “one form of psychological attachment that occurs when members adopt the de<sup>fi</sup>ning characteristics of the organization as de<sup>fi</sup>ning characteristics for themselves” [16]. Organizational identi<sup>fi</sup>cation leads to a sense of attachment, belonging, and pride in being an organizational member. Individuals who identify strongly with an organization tend to think from an organization's perspective [16]. To this end, employees with high organizational identi<sup>fi</sup>cation are likely to view Internet abuses as unethical. Therefore,

H10. Organizational identification has a positive impact on personal norms against Internet abuses.

## 3.7. Control variables

In addition to the core constructs mentioned above, we also controlled for four variables that might in<sup>fl</sup>uence employees' intention to comply with Internet use policy: gender, Internet experience, the existence of Internet monitoring practices in the company, and awareness of any employees being disciplined for violating Internet use policy. Internet experience is related to one's ability to use the Internet and may in<sup>fl</sup>uence one's tendency to abuse Internet access in the workplace as well. The existence of Internet monitoring practices and the awareness of disciplined employees are likely to increase one's intention to follow the Internet use policy.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Study design and procedure

The research model was examined using an online survey sent out to an industrial panel consisting of organizational employees. All survey respondents in the panel are anonymous to researchers and were contacted through Zoomerang.com. Subjects were noti<sup>fi</sup>ed of the purpose of the study and the voluntary nature of their participation. As the focus of our study is on IUP compliance we included only employees in organizations with an Internet use policy. So, all participants were required to answer two <sup>fi</sup>lter questions about whether they use the Internet in the workplace and whether they are aware of any IUP in their organization. Only those who answered “Yes” to both <sup>fi</sup>lter questions proceeded to answer the rest of the questions in the survey. There were a total of 246 usable responses.

## 4.2. Variable measurement

Most of the instruments were drawn from previous research and re-worded slightly for our research context, i.e. IUP compliance.

Detection probability, sanction severity, and subjective norms were measured using items from Peace et al. [34]. Perceived security risks from Internet breaches consisted of four items from Rhee et al. [38]. The instrument measuring perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts was adapted from the perceived consequence scale developed by Pee et al. [35]. The perceived consequence scale in Pee et al. [35] consists of seven items measuring perceived negative consequences and <sup>fi</sup>ve items measuring perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (advantages) of using the Internet for non-work-related purposes. Among the <sup>fi</sup>ve perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t items, only convenience was found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the study by Pee et al. [35]. To assess the content validity of items measuring perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, we interviewed seven experienced observers of non-workrelated Internet usage in the workplace. Each of them commented on the appropriateness of the <sup>fi</sup>ve items for measuring perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and answered a belief elicitation question to identify potential missing items to ensure measuring the full domain of the construct. The belief elicitation question was “What do you see as other bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using the Internet access provided by the organization for nonwork-related purposes?” At the end of the interview and belief elicitation process, four perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts items were retained in this study: 1) saving personal time; 2) saving personal expense; 3) convenience; and 4) a more interesting work life. No other bene<sup>fi</sup>ts were suggested and/or agreed upon by two or more interviewees. Personal norms and organizational norms were modi<sup>fi</sup>ed after those developed by Wenzel [49]. Organizational identi<sup>fi</sup>cation was measured using items developed by Gautam et al. [20] and Wenzel [49]. IUP compliance intention was measured using scales developed by Limayem et al. [27] and Peace et al. [34]. The perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t scale was implemented as a formative instrument, as an increase in one item is not necessarily associated with an increase in the rest of items in the instrument. For example, in the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t scale, an increase in convenience does not necessarily go together with an increase in other types of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as more interesting work life and saving money. The other eight scales were operationalized as re<sup>fl</sup>ective ones. All constructs were measured using <sup>fi</sup>ve-point Likert scales. The detailed measures for each construct are listed in the Appendix.

## 5. Data analysis

Partial least squares (PLS) analysis, a component-based structural equation modeling (SEM) technique, was used to analyze our measurement model and test the research hypotheses. There are two reasons for using PLS. First, PLS has minimal demands for sample size and residual distributions [10]. Second, PLS is more amenable for handling formative constructs, such as the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t construct in our model, than covariance-based SEM techniques such as LISREL.

## 5.2. Measurement model

We examined the measurement quality of the two types of constructs, formative and re<sup>fl</sup>ective, separately by following different procedures. In this section, we will <sup>fi</sup>rst report the measurement quality of the formative construct and then the reliability and validity of the other eight re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs.

The measurement quality of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts was assessed following the suggestions by Chin [9] and Diamantopoulos et al. [14]. We <sup>fi</sup>rst examined the correlations among measurement items for this formative construct. The absolute correlations vary from 0.18 to 0.57 among the four items measuring perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. These relatively low correlations suggest that the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>t construct is better represented as a formative construct; a re<sup>fl</sup>ective construct would show extremely high correlations among its measurement items (often above 0.8) [33].

We further assessed the strength of the relationship between the formative construct and its measurement items. For perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, “interesting work life” has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient (or PLS weight) (Fig. 3) while the other three items do not. The variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor (VIF) was then computed to assess multicollinearity among the four measurement items. VIF values above 10 would suggest the existence of excessive multicollinearity and raise doubts about the validity of the formative measurement [14]. The VIF values varied from 1.4 to 2.0 for the four items measuring perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Therefore, multicollinearity is not a concern in this study.

According to Mathieson et al. [28], formative constructs may contain non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant indicators, especially in the absence of multicollinearity. Retaining insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant indicators is also recommended by Bollen et al. [6] to sustain the content validity of constructs. Therefore, to maintain the content domain of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, we decided to keep all items including non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant ones in the following data analysis.

The measurement quality of the eight re<sup>fl</sup>ective scales was assessed based on their convergent validity, reliability, and discriminant validity. Convergent validity is suggested if factor loadings are 0.60 or higher [4] and each item loads signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly on its latent construct [21]. All items loaded with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant t-values on their respective latent constructs and have loading values above 0.60; therefore, all these re<sup>fl</sup>ective scales exhibit sound convergent validity (Table 3). We then examined the reliabilities of these constructs using composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE). A scale is considered reliable if it has CR above 0.7 and AVE above 0.5 [4]. As shown in Table 3, all the re<sup>fl</sup>ective scales were reliable. To establish discriminant validity, we examined both the loading and crossloading matrix (Table 3) and the correlation matrix (Table 4). In the loading and cross-loading matrix, all measurement items should load more strongly on their respective construct than on other constructs. Second, in the correlation matrix, the square root of the AVE of each construct should be higher than the inter-construct correlations, i.e. the correlations between that construct and any other constructs [18]. As shown in Tables 3 and 4, all re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs in our model satisfy these two criteria for discriminant validity. Therefore, our measurement model exhibits the sound reliability and validity necessary for further testing of our research hypotheses.

We performed bootstrapping to compute the t-statistics for each hypothesized relationship and the potential impact of control variables. Among the four control variables, only Internet experience signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increased IUP compliance intention.

Next, we analyzed the interaction effects, Hypotheses 7 and 8, before testing the other hypotheses. We followed the procedures proposed by Chin et al. [10]. The existence of an interaction effect was evaluated based on both effect size and statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. $\mathrm { H } _ { 7 } ,$ relating to the interaction between personal norms and detection probability, is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant with an effect size of interaction $( \mathbf { f } 2 ) ^ { 1 }$ being 0.01. H , relating to the interaction between personal norms and sanction severity, is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 0.01 level and has an effect size of 0.04, which falls between small $( \mathrm { f } 2 = 0 . 0 2 )$ and medium $( \mathrm { f } 2 = 0 . 1 5 )$ effect size cutoffs [12]. We also tested the joint effect size considering both interaction terms, i.e. Personal Norms Detection Probability and Personal Norms Sanction Severity, to check the overall moderation effect of personal norms on formal sanctions. The two interaction terms jointly have an effect size of 0.12. Hence, we conclude that personal norms against Internet abuses do moderate the impact of formal sanctions on IUP compliance intention.

The interaction pattern of $\mathrm { H } _ { 8 } ,$ shown in Fig. 5, consists of two regression lines with one for a high value of personal norms (i.e. one standard deviation above the mean) and one for a low value of personal norms (i.e., one standard deviation below the mean). Sanction severity signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increases the employees' intention to follow Internet use policy (p-value<0.05) when personal norm values are below 1.4. Interestingly, we also found that, for those with personal norm values above 2.9, sanction severity signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduces their intention to follow Internet use policy. Therefore, H was partially supported.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Key findings and limitations

We drew upon rational choice theory to investigate the determinants of employees' IUP compliance intention. Consistent with rational choice theory, our study shows that employees' intention to comply with the IUP involves a cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis. Employees are more likely to comply with the IUP when perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are overridden by potential risks from formal sanctions and security threats. The deterrence effect of formal sanction risks is largely exerted through detection probability rather than sanction severity. As discussed in the next paragraph, sanction severity is not an effective deterrence mechanism for the majority of employees. Also, the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence from important others or subjective norms, is not a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor for the intention to comply with the Internet use policy. Previous studies have suggested some conditions for subjective norms to take effect [45,46]. Besides the mandatory settings discussed earlier, the effect of subjective norms is also contingent upon people's experience. They are more likely to take effect in the early stages of experience when an individual's knowledge and beliefs are relatively ill-formed; the effect of subjective norms erodes with the increase in the individual's experience [45]. Because the majority of the subjects in this study have used the Internet for more than 6 years, they are likely to have already formed strong beliefs about Internet abuses. All these may weaken the effect of the opinions of important others, i.e. subjective norms.

Besides the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis, compliance intention is also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by employees' personal norms or moral standards against Internet abuses. Personal norms moderate the impact of perceived sanction severity on the compliance intention. Perceived sanction severity is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant deterrence mechanism only for employees with very low (<1.4) personal norms against Internet abuses or about 4% of the employees in our sample. For those with moderate to high (>2.9) personal norms (about 43% of employees in our sample), perceiving harsh sanctions not only fails to increase but reduces their compliance intention. This result seems counterintuitive but is not totally unexpected. Deterrence has been found to produce counterproductive effects in some prior studies [11,15,36]. Harsh sanctions may undermine the trust or loyalty toward a <sup>fi</sup>rm and, therefore, generate a counterproductive effect on the compliance intention among those with moderate to high personal norms against Internet abuses.

The interaction effect between personal norms and sanction probability may be overridden by that between personal norms and sanction severity, and is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. So sanction probability exerts a largely direct impact on IUP compliance intention.

Our results also suggest that organizational context factors could indirectly in<sup>fl</sup>uence individual employees' compliance intention. Organizational norms against Internet abuses and organizational identi<sup>fi</sup>cation indirectly in<sup>fl</sup>uence employees' compliance intention through developing and/or strengthening employees' personal norms against Internet abuses.

We further compared the relative ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of our research model and the model relying only on fear-based mechanisms in explaining IUP compliance intention. An alternative fear-based model was built by including the same control variables and the four perceived risk factors (i.e. perceived detection probability, sanction severity, subjective norm, and security risks) to predict IUP compliance intention. The $R ^ { 2 }$ of the alternative model is only 11.2% in comparison to 35% of our research model. The result suggests that perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and personal norms are very important in explaining IUP compliance intention while fear-based mechanisms are relatively less effective.

Before we discuss the implications of our study, we point to some of its limitations. Although we found a counterproductive effect of sanction severity among employees with moderate to high personal norms, we should exercise caution in generalizing this effect to other security policies or speci<sup>fi</sup>c forms of cyber crimes. Severe sanctions against cyber crimes such as pornography and cyberstalking are well accepted among employees and, therefore, are not expected to have a negative impact on security policy compliance. Perceived sanction severity should be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in deterring those cyber crimes. Another limitation is that our study did not measure actual compliance behavior. Instead, behavioral intention is used as the proxy for actual behaviors. Our approach is consistent with the dominant research practice based on the theory of reasoned action (TRA) [17]. According to TRA, behavioral intention is closely related to actual behaviors. But future studies could be conducted by setting up actual compliance behavior through monitoring the actual Internet usage of employees.

## 6.2. Contributions

This paper's primary contribution is to identify cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis, personal norms, and organizational context factors as three important forces that influence emplovees' intention to follow security policies. The proposed model, based on rational choice theory, provides an integrative view of the policy compliance issue and enhances our understanding of the complex process of IUP compliance decisions. Second, such an integrative view provides valuable insights into some of the fundamental constructs that have been overlooked such as the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of deviant behaviors, personal moral beliefs, and organizational context factors. Attempting to explain security policy compliance without considering these factors is likely to result in inconsistent or even potentially misleading empirical results. For example, some studies in criminology have found a very weak or no impact of formal sanctions on deviant behaviors [32], while the real impact may be masked by factors not included in their research models such as the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of deviant behaviors and moral factors. Third, the study contributes to the understanding of traditional deterrence-based mechanisms by viewing them as part of a cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis moderated by personal norms. Such a viewpoint provides two possible explanations for inconsistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding formal sanctions. One is the overriding effect of competing factors in the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis and the other is the moderation effect of personal norms. The cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis consists of contrary factors. One factor may override the effect of another. The effect of formal sanctions may be overridden by the effect of the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of Internet abuses, causing a very weak or no effect of formal sanctions. The introduction of personal norms in the research model also provides valuable insights into understanding the deterrence effect of formal sanctions, as personal norms de<sup>fi</sup>ne when perceived sanction severity is effective. Finally, this study contributes to the literature by considering organizational context factors, which shed light on identifying strategies that companies can implement to enhance the compliance with their security policies.

## 6.3. Implications for research

The results of this study have several important implications for research in this area. First, our study found that IUP compliance intention is subject to a cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis. Perceived risks are evaluated against the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of Internet abuses. When perceived risks are overridden by perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, IUP noncompliance is likely to occur. Studies focusing only on perceived risks may show misleading or inconsistent results. For example, the effect of sanctions or perceived security risks may be offset by that of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and researchers may mistakenly conclude that sanctions do not work.

Although this study is conducted in the context of IUP compliance, the empirical support for the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis sheds important light on understanding employee compliance with security policies regulating other activities such as instant messaging, <sup>fi</sup>le management, and password security. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c forms of cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis will vary depending on which security practice is being examined. For example, with respect to the intention to comply with the <sup>fi</sup>le encryption policy, researchers would need to consider the tradeoff between the security risk and the productivity gain from violating the encryption policy.

Second, the results suggest that personal norms circumscribe the effect of formal sanctions on employees' intention to comply with Internet use policy. Examining the effect of formal sanctions without considering the role of moral factors is insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient. A complete understanding of individual employees' security policy compliance must include personal norms together with the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis.

Third, the results suggest that IUP compliance intention is also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational context. In particular, we found that organizational norms and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation indirectly increase an employee's compliance intention through personal norms. Future studies need to explore the effect of other organizational context factors on security policy compliance, such as organizational justice, the security capability of the IT department, organizational resources, or programs facilitating the enforcement of information security policies. For example, the effect of perceived security risks may be contingent upon employees' perception about the security capability of the IT department. Compliance with information security policies could also be enhanced by implementing resources facilitating policy enforcement such as network technologies for blocking or restricting the access to external personal email websites or other non-workrelated Internet websites, and periodical campaigns to increase awareness of security policies, among others. Future studies are needed to further examine the effectiveness of those facilitating resources on information security compliance.

From the above discussion, we conclude that rational choice theory provides a useful theoretical foundation for understanding security policy compliance. The theory not only encompasses fearbased mechanisms but also juxtaposes them with the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of deviant behaviors. Further, rational choice theory considers the importance of moral factors and organizational context factors.

## 6.4. Implications for practice

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study also have important practical implications for companies deploying Internet use policies. Our study suggests that the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts motivate employees to conduct Internet abuses. A more interesting work life is the primary bene<sup>fi</sup>t driving employees to violate Internet use policies. Further, the average value of personal norms against Internet abuses is 2.8 on a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point Likert scale among subjects of this study, suggesting that a considerable number of organizational employees deem it acceptable to use the Internet access provided by the organization for non-work-related purposes. Therefore, it may not be possible to enforce zero Internet usage for personal purposes in the workplace, as it is very likely to erode the trust and morale of organizational employees and increase the cost of enforcing security policies. A fair IUP with a clause of “reasonable use” may work better for most organizations. When formulating an IUP, companies should seek a balance between fair Internet use and the risks of Internet use for personal purposes. An Internet use policy should specify clearly what constitutes fair Internet use for personal purposes and what disciplinary measures will be taken for violations of the IUP. In addition, prior studies have suggested the linkage between interesting work life and creativity [2]. For companies in highly creative domains such as Google and small startups, restricting Internet access may do more harm than good. Future studies may need to empirically test the effect of IUPs with varying levels of restrictions on personal Internet use in the workplace.

Second, to overcome the effect of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and ensure IUP compliance, companies could take several approaches. Companies could resort to enhancing personal moral norms against Internet abuses to cultivate voluntary compliance with security policies, especially for Internet abuses not in the category of cyber crimes. Organizations could further foster voluntary policy compliance through periodic security training, educating employees about risks from Internet security breaches. In addition, companies need to implement various control mechanisms to monitor the usage of the Internet and make employees aware that they could be caught if they abuse their access to the Internet. This sanction-based mechanism could complement the voluntary compliance approach and be used to regulate the Internet behaviors of employees with weak personal norms against Internet abuses. At the same time, companies should be cautious about the level of sanction severity when making policies. For those with high personal norms against Internet abuses, harsh sanctions could potentially erode their loyalty to their <sup>fi</sup>rms and, therefore, reduce their policy compliance intention. As a result, companies need to recognize the relative ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and cost of the above approaches for overriding the effect of the perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of Internet abuses. As a voluntary compliance approach, personal norm factors may be more cost effective than formal sanctions and help to nurture long-term compliance. Formal sanctions incur additional cost for monitoring the Internet usage and may hurt the moral and trust of employees. Also, the effect of formal sanctions is conditioned upon personal norms. Companies may need to <sup>fi</sup>rst consider personal norms, and then resort to the use of formal sanctions.

The results of this study also suggest that a company could use two mechanisms to in<sup>fl</sup>uence individual employees' personal norms. One is to formulate an organizational climate against Internet abuses through regular employee training. When the moral climate of a company is perceived to be intolerant of Internet abuses, individual employees are more likely to form strong personal norms against Internet abuses and comply with Internet use policy. The other approach is to increase the organizational identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of employees or their sense of belonging to the company. When an employee is proud to be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a member of a company, s/he is more likely to act in the interest of the company and follow the Internet use policy.

## 7. Conclusions

Employees are the weakest link in security management. Noncompliance with Internet use policies exposes companies to additional security risks, as the Internet is one of the primary breakthrough points for external security attacks. This study investigated factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence employees' IUP compliance intention. Previous studies have primarily focused on threat-based mechanisms such as formal sanctions and security threats, and have largely ignored the perceived benefits of deviant behaviors, personal moral beliefs, and organizational context factors. From a rational choice perspective, this paper offers an integrative understanding of intention to comply with security policies. The empirical results of our study suggest the importance of considering compliance decisions as driven by a cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis, bounded by personal norms and organizational context factors.
