# Publication patterns concerning the role of teams/groups in the information systems literature from 1990 to 1999

Sameer Prasad <sup>a,\*</sup>, Jasmine Tata <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Management Department, College of Business and Economics, University of Wisconsin – Whitewater, Whitewater, WI 53190, USA <sup>b</sup> Management Department, Loyola University Chicago, 820 North Michigan Avenue, Chicago, IL 60611, USA

Received 9 September 2002; accepted 23 January 2005 Available online 14 March 2005

## Abstract

By reviewing 28 reputable journals that serve as outlets for information systems and operations management/research, 220 articles relating to information systems and teams/groups were identified over a 10-year period from 1990 through 1999. These articles were classified into 22 topic areas and subsequently cross-tabulated.

The data indicate that the number of articles published in this area peaked in the mid 1990s, and that Communications of the ACM, Decision Support Systems, Information & Management, and Journal of Management Information Systems were the primary outlets. Key topic areas intersecting with teams/groups include: decision making, decision support systems, human factors, intra-organization systems, project management, telecommunications, and software. Also, connections between organizational behavior/social psychology and the current teams/groups research in information systems were discovered. <sup>#</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: DSS; Topic areas; Information systems research; Research methodology

## 1. Introduction

As competition intensifies, companies are adopting new models of organizations to gain an edge over other firms. These models involve the use of information systems (IS) and require ways of organizing that are distinct from traditional hierarchies. Today, aspects of IS such as e-commerce, supply chain management, outsourcing, knowledge management, and globalization require disparate individuals and groups to work together effectively. For example, e-commerce transactions today provide an electronic medium to coordinate activities with buyers, suppliers, and other participants within the organization. These groups of individuals need to carefully coordinate their activities and rely heavily on IS.

Models of organizing emphasize connections and coordination both across and within organizations, resulting in an interest in teaming. Numerous benefits of teams have been reported, including increased performance, increased creativity and commitment, higher quality of products, less absenteeism, and reduced turnover [3,5,6,9,11,15,16,19,24]. Teams allow individuals in locations around the world to interact and solve operational problems; they help firms bridge barriers and combine efforts [31].

Given the changing nature of organizations and the prevalence of teams, it is important to understand the literature on IS and teams/groups. Here, we attempted to lay the groundwork by uncovering patterns in the literature on the role of teams/groups in IS. By sampling key journals, we hoped to uncover interactions between teams/groups and various areas in IS. In addition, we looked at the publication propensity by journal and year. As such, this paper can be of value to researchers, in that it could help them identify areas of the literature that are expected to flourish and journals for specific studies. In addition, practitioners may gain from these findings by recognizing the importance of teams/groups relative to various IS functions. Although our work did not provide a prescriptive model, it did highlight the interactions between teams/ groups and primary and secondary IS areas.

## 2. Research methodology

## 2.1. Sample and procedure

Two hundred and twenty-two articles published between 1990 and 1999 were obtained from a sample of 28 journals selected from a number of relevant areas including Management Information Systems (MIS) and Operations Management (OM); OM journals were included because a significant amount of research in ecommerce and supply chain often occurs in this field. Previous researchers [2,17,18,21,38] have identified a vast range of journals in these fields; we selected a sample of journals from this range to make our task manageable. We included both high quality journals as well as some that are perhaps a little less well known. A list of the journals in the sample is presented in Table 1. Although this data set did not provide an exhaustive coverage of all publications in the area of teams/groups in IS, it constituted a fairly comprehensive basis for assessing patterns in the literature. All articles from this set of journals were identified in the years 1990–1999.

Articles were included in the data set based on the following two criteria: (1) the article should concentrate specifically on teams/groups and (2) it should have an IS focus.

## 2.2. Coding of articles

Each article was coded on the following categories: year of publication, primary topic area of IS and a secondary area. Based on a reading of the articles and the keywords used by the authors, publications were categorized under the following topic areas: Artificial Intelligence (AI), audit (information), database, decision making, decision support systems, design, Expert Systems (ES), human factors in IS, human– machine interface, information resource management, intra-organization systems, inter-organization systems, Knowledge Based Systems (KBS), MIS, networks, Object Oriented Programming (OOPS), project management, programming, systems design, telecommunications, and software development. The categories were obtained by examining a number of classifications in the literature [1,32], and extracting the relevant topic areas. These areas were further modified upon consultation with a number of IS researchers. To ensure reliability in the classification process, the first two raters coded the articles independently. The third rater then compared the two classifications. If the two classifications matched in terms of primary and secondary areas, they were included in the database. However, if discrepancies

## 2.3. Methodology of the analysis

Given the classification, a number of analyses were generated. The articles were first sorted by journal and year. From this, it was possible to sum the number of publications by year and by journal. A Chi-square nonparametric test was computed to determine which journals significantly contributed to the overall teams/ group area. The journals were also sorted by the topic areas (primary and secondary combined) yielding a publication pattern of topic areas by journal. A Chisquare statistic was also computed on the summed publications by topic area. This allowed us to identify areas having significantly greater or less attention than average. In addition, for each article, the primary and secondary topic areas were cross-tabulated in order to discover any unique clustering and a Chi-square test computed to determine whether the clustering was significant. The codings (primary and secondary) were also summed by year, providing a pattern of publication over time for each topic area. Finally, the publications by topic area were split into two sets (1990–1994 and 1995–1999) and normalized for the number of publications appearing in those sets. This controls for the influence of teams/groups and can be used to indicate changes in overall publication patterns in the respective IS topic areas. The nonparametric Chi-square test was employed to ascertain significant differences.

## 3. Results

## 3.1. Publication patterns by journal and year

In Table 2, we note that both MIS and OM journals contribute to the IS and teams/groups literature. However, as expected, the IS journals provided a much heavier contribution. In particular, four journals can be considered significantly high contributors with more than 20 articles over the 10-year period. Specifically, Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, Communications of ACM, and Information & Management have published 30 $( p < . 0 1 )$ 28 $( p < . 0 1 )$ 26 $( p < . 0 1 )$ and 23 $( p < . 0 5 )$ articles, respectively, in this area. In addition, moderate contributors (10+ articles) include European Journal of Operational Research (16),

Information Systems Research (13), Information Systems Management (12) and MIS Quarterly (11).

In Fig. 1, it is apparent that fewer papers were published in the initial years (1990 and 1991). The publications seem to have peaked in 1994 (30) and 1995 (29) and have since leveled off.

## 3.2. Publication patterns by journal and topic area

Table 3 provides a classification of topic areas by journal. This pattern provides valuable information on what is being published in various journals. In this table, it is apparent that some areas have received relatively less attention while interacting with the teams/groups literature. Audit and database issues were each only cited in two articles; Object Oriented Programming and programming were each covered in three articles. Finally, design issues were noted in only four publications. Next, the remaining topic areas are organized into four major categories and examined relative to a group process framework.

## 3.2.1. Intelligent systems and decision making

Publications concerning the use of Artificial Intelligence for teams/groups research have been significantly $( p < . 0 1 )$ limited and cited in only six articles with a concentration in Decision Support Systems and Expert Systems with Applications. In addition, there seems to be no discernable pattern over time for this topic area (see Fig. 1). Similarly, Expert Systems and Knowledge Based Systems occurred significantly less frequently and were cited in only six $( p < . 0 5 )$ and nine $( p < . 1 0 )$ articles, respectively. Expert Systems’ articles were distributed over a number of journals, whereas KBS were concentrated in Decision Support Systems and Information & Management. Over time, it seems that the works on ES were concentrated in 1994 and 1995, while KBS has been studied throughout the 1990s.

In general, the literature suggests that AI has been used as a way to support group processes in terms of group characteristics (e.g. participants being distributed [34]), task characteristics (e.g. help in problem solving [7]), and technological factors. The use of AI as a technological factor has been extensively studied. For example, natural language was employed to process inputs from individuals [8], ES were developed to use rule induction as a mechanism for supporting interaction aid, and finally in integrating, organizing and synthesizing this knowledge intelligent agents [39] and KBS [26] were developed.

Using Benbasat and Lim’s framework as a guide, researchers might perhaps examine how AI could interact with contextual factors such as culture, time pressure and rewards to affect the output variables. In addition, it might be possible to examine the role of AI on specific group characteristics issues such as group size, formal hierarchy, and group history. Finally, a majority of the AI studies did not report on meeting outcome variables such as performance, satisfaction, and structural products.

The number of publications dealing with the teams/ groups literature and intersecting with decision making and decision support systems seems to be significantly high with 37 $( p < . 0 1 )$ and 92 $( p < . 0 1 )$ articles, respectively. This is perhaps because there appears to be a natural fit between teams/groups and these two topic areas. Key journals for the interaction of the teams/group and decision making literature include: Decision Sciences, European Journal of Operational Research, Decision Support Systems, and Omega. For the link between teams/groups and decision support systems, key outlets include: Decision Support Systems, Information & Management, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Omega, and Decision Sciences. Over time, it seems that the decision making and decision support systems literature has been published quite uniformly after 1991.

The literature indicates that the areas of GDSS and DSS have been studied extensively and the findings synthesized [13]. Using meta-analyses and statistics from earlier studies, Benbasat and Lim test various propositions specified in the framework. Outcomes from this study indicate that GDSS is positively related to decision quality, number of alternatives generated, and equality of participation. Negative effects included a longer time to reach a decision and lower levels of consensus and satisfaction. A number of moderator variables were also found to have influence, including the size of the team, hierarchy and the level of GDSS support. In this large array, only a few topics still need to be explored. For example, although the link between rewards and GDSS has been examined, other relevant contextual factors such as organizational culture and time pressure have not.

## 3.2.2. Human and organizational factors

Table 3 indicates that articles relevant to intraorganizational systems are significant $( p < . 0 5 )$ in number (33), whereas inter-organizational systems publications are significantly $( p < . 1 0 )$ fewer (9). Articles on intra-organizational systems and teams/ groups have appeared in Information & Management, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, and Information Systems Research. Research on inter-organizational systems has primarily appeared in Communications of ACM and Expert Systems with Applications. Over a time basis, it seems that articles on intra-organizational systems were published throughout the 1990s, but peaked in 1994 and 1995. Articles on inter-organizational systems presented a bi-modal distribution with publications appearing in the early 1990s and late 1990s.

In general, the literature indicated that group processes (inter-team) influence outcome variables such as performance (e.g. effectiveness), satisfaction (e.g. client [20]), and structural products (e.g. equality of influence [28] and collaboration [27,33]). It would also be interesting to see how intra-team processes could affect similar outcome variables. Benbasat and Lim’s [4] framework suggests that key input variables (e.g. task characteristics, contextual factors, and technological factors) could also be included in future analyses.

Human factors seems to be one of the key areas intersecting with the teams/groups literature; a total of 51 articles examined this issue and are significantly ( p < .01) more than other topic areas on average. In addition, human–machine interface studies were published in 12 articles. The key journals for the human factors research were Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Communications of ACM, Decision Support Systems, and Information & Management. Key outlets for human–machine interface works were Communications of ACM and MIS Quarterly. Over a time basis (see Fig. 2), it seems that the articles on human factors peaked in 1994 and 1995, with a somewhat heavier presence in the late 1990s. On the other hand, research on human–machine factors seems to have been primarily published in 1993, 1994, and 1995.

In general, the output variables identified within the literature with respect to human factors and human– machine factors included satisfaction and structural products, such as democracy, consensus, cooperation, and confidence in relation to usage. Benbasat and Lim’s framework suggested that examining performance measures such as decision quality, number of alternatives, and time to reach decisions might also be useful. The current literature has already investigated input factors, such as technological variables (e.g. software method, task/technology fit, richness of media, focus on technology versus real problem, and reliance on a single versus multi-media), all of which were found to be important.

## 3.2.3. Management of IS and resources

In Table 3, we notice that a number of articles have been published on MIS (15) and information resources (7) intersecting with the teams/groups literature, although there seemed to be significantly ( p < .01) fewer articles in the area of information resources. Articles on MIS appear to be somewhat uniformly distributed among the journals. On the other hand, information resources issues seem to appear quite frequently in Information & Management. Publications on information resources seem to pick up after 1994 (Fig. 3); articles on MIS seem to be published mainly after 1991.

Managing the information resources among group participants is considered an important activity. A number of studies have looked at how resources need to be managed in terms of contextual factors such as acquisition policies (information) [29], exchange systems [12], staffing issues [14], and rewards and recognition [37].

## 3.2.4. Technological systems and methodologies

Articles examining project management (21) and systems design (10) are about average in number. Articles on project management appear in both OM and MIS journals. The main outlet for such works seems to be IEEE Transactions. In contrast, articles on systems design are mostly in Communications of ACM and Journal of Management Information Systems. Over time (see Fig. 4), systems design and project management issues have been quite evenly published throughout the 1990s.

Articles dealing with telecommunications and teams/groups are significantly $( p < . 0 5 )$ plentiful (32). On the other hand, publications dealing with networks and teams/groups are significantly $( p < . 0 5 )$ limited (6). Articles on telecommunications and teams/groups have mostly appeared in Information & Management, Communications of ACM, Journal of Management Information Systems and Information Systems Research, whereas articles on networks have generally appeared in Communications of ACM and Decision Support Systems. There seems to be no discernable pattern in publication propensities over the 1990s for the two areas.

Finally, software development seems to have an important connection to teams/groups (25) but is not significantly different than other topic areas in terms of number of publications. Articles have generally been published in Communications of ACM, Decision Support Systems, Journal of Management Information Systems, and Information Systems Research. Articles on software development seem to be somewhat uniformly distributed since 1992.

Studies relative to software development include aspects of programming methodology, systems design, and programming languages. Issues raised include requirement specifications [25,30], models [23], metrics, R&D [10], and distributed systems [36]. Other articles related to software look at their supporting role for GDSS and describe their impact on outcome variables such as effectiveness and applicability to different uses [22].

## 4. Discussion

A total of 220 articles, averaging 22 publications per year were located from a sample of 28 IS/OM journals. The findings suggest that teams/groups research interacts significantly with several IS areas. Some of the patterns uncovered in our study suggest that the areas of GDSS and DSS have been studied extensively in the literature and the findings synthesized. Studies have also found that AI has been used as a way to support GDSS in terms of group processes, task characteristics, and in integrating this knowledge. Variables identified within the literature in relation to teams/groups, human factors and human–machine factors include satisfaction, and structural output variables.

Our study also found that research relative to teams/groups and software development includes technological factors such as aspects of programming methodology, systems design, and programming languages, and raises issues such as requirement specifications, models, metrics, R&D, and distributed systems. Telecommunications is another well-developed area in the literature and includes articles about task, team composition, technology fit, and connections with outcomes.

Based on Benbasat and Lim’s framework, the influence of several team characteristics including task characteristics (e.g. complexity), group characteristics (e.g. proximity), and technological factors (e.g. facilitation, improvement in design) on performance and satisfaction have been identified in this area. Finally, a number of studies have looked at managing information resources among team/group participants in terms of acquisition policies (information), exchange systems, staffing issues, and rewards and recognition.

## 5. Conclusion

E-commerce and global supply chains are heavily dependent on group processes for their success. By linking this study to an established framework for group processes, it is now possible to specify the effectiveness of global supply chains and e-commerce transactions through performance, satisfaction, and structural outcome metrics. Specific performance outcome metrics include decision quality, number of alternatives generated, and time to reach decisions. Also, critical to global supply chains and e-commerce are satisfaction metrics (e.g. satisfaction with the process and confidence with the outcomes). Finally, with multiple partners associated with such systems, it is important that structural outcome metrics (e.g. consensus and equality of influence) are examined. As the framework suggests, it is important to understand the unique influence of input variables (task characteristics, group characteristics, contextual factors, and technological factors) on outputs given that global supply chains are associated with complexity (scores of buyers and suppliers) and solution multiplicity (varying sourcing patterns). Examining group characteristics suggests that the partners within the supply chains can be distributed all over the world, vary in size, have little history among them and operate without a formal hierarchical structure.
