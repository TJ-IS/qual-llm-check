# Perceptions and use of electronic media: Testing the relationship between organizational interpretation differences and media richness

Mariko Kishi \*

Faculty of Business Administration, Hosei University, 2-17-1 Fujimi, Chiyoda-ku, Tokyo 102-8160 Japan

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 24 September 2006 Received in revised form 9 September 2007 Accepted 31 January 2008 Available online 12 May 2008

Keywords: Media richness Media perception Media use Traditional media Electronic media Organizational interpretation behavior

## A B S T R A C T

How do managers perceive and use media? Media richness theory (MRT) appears to provide persuasive answers to this, but does it work when electronic media is used? Surveys of Japanese managers showed that the perceptions of media, including electronic media, did not contradict MRT. But, even so, the use of rich media was found to be influenced more strongly by whether the media was traditional or electronic, with respect to organizational interpretation of its environment. The perception and use of electronic media may not correspond, though they have generally been assumed to do so.

## 1. Introduction

Media richness theory (MRT) states that the use of communication media in organizations is a rational process that strives to achieve a match between the requirements of communication tasks and the capacity of the media. It has been found that managers perceptions and use of media do not contradict predictions drawn from the theory.

MRT originally examined only traditional communication media, however, as new technologies have been introduced, attempts have been made to include electronic media in MRT.

Using the framework of the original MRT presented by Daft and Lengel [2,3], we focused on how managers in Japanese companies perceived the differences in media and how they used them, in order to consider the extension of MRT to electronic media. It became clear that even when electronic media was perceived as rich, the richness was not necessarily related to its use, and that organizational interpretation of the environment had an important effect on the use of rich media.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Richness as a media attribute

MRT discusses the reduction of equivocality that occurs in organizational communication, from the standpoint of media use. It proposed that the use of media which met IS requirements was essential for ensuring organizational effectiveness. Gradually, however, it has changed to describing the rational process of media choice and use on the individual level [4]. Yet the basis of MRT – achieving a match between the requirements of communication tasks and the capacity of the media – has not changed.

Media richness is defined as the capacity of media to develop shared meaning, overcome different frames of reference, and clarify ambiguous issues in a timely manner; thus can be understood as its ability to: (1) facilitate rapid feedback, (2) handle multiple information cues simultaneously, and (3) establish a personal focus [18]. On this basis, face-to-face interaction is the richest medium. In order of decreasing richness, the traditional media are the telephone, addressed documents, unaddressed documents, and numeric documents. Generally the richness level is higher in oral than in written media and in synchronous rather than in asynchronous media.

## 2.2. Organizational interpretation differences and media use

Daft and Lengel postulated that achieving a match between the information-processing requirements and the capacity of the media was essential for organizational effectiveness. They attempted to understand information-processing requirements by combining the standpoint of equivocality reduction proposed by Weick [31] with the standpoint of uncertainty reduction proposed by Galbraith [10]. Reduction of equivocality is aided by rich media. Uncertainty involves the difference between the amount of information required to perform a task and the amount of information already possessed. Reduction of uncertainty occurs when media are available to process a large amount of data. For example, meetings and personal contact facilitate reduction of equivocality; well-defined rules and computer-based information systems facilitate reduction of uncertainty.

Weick and Daft [32] and Daft and Weick [5] proposed models of organizations as interpretation systems. In their second model, two variables were used to describe organizational interpretation differences: (1) organizational assumptions about the analyzability of the external environment and (2) the extent to which the organization intrudes into the environment to understand it. Organizations can be categorized as assuming the environment to be analyzable or not. When the environment is difficult to penetrate, managers will see it as less analyzable [22]. Organizations can also be categorized as intruding into the environment actively or passively. Daft and Weick felt that understanding the organizational interpretation behavior was important because the interpretation was a critical element distinguishing human organizations from other systems, and that numerous organizational variables (such as strategy formation and decision making) were influenced in some way by the organizational interpretation process.

Using this model, Daft and Lengel pointed out that the information-processing needs and the type of media used changed depending on the organizational interpretation differences: making assumptions about the environment was related to reduction of equivocality and organizational intrusiveness was related to reduction of uncertainty. When the environment could not be analyzed, equivocality was high and managers should discuss, debate, and ultimately agree on a reasonable interpretation. In organizations that assumed a high degree of difficulty in analyzing the environment, rich media was required. In contrast, when the environment was perceived as competitive or rapidly changing and was searched actively for answers, uncertainty was high and managers had to gather a great deal of data about it. In organizations characterized by a high degree of intrusiveness, media that could process a large amount of data were required.

Individuals could not be separated from the organizational context in which they were embedded. Because of their shared context, even different individuals who held different opinions on the same matter and who were motivated by conflicting interests could end up with negotiated meanings for the same action and even choose the same way to act [21]. Hence organizational interpretation differences could explain the media use of individuals in organizations, at least partially.

## 2.3. Management and media use

Generally, higher level managers must deal with tasks possessing greater equivocality than at the operational level; therefore they should use richer media. Managers rationally attempt to match their needs to the equivocality of the communication task.

I found this also in my survey of 1500 managers and other employees of Japanese companies [11]. Managers preferred media with a higher richness level than other employees; the more aggressively organizations used computer-based IS, the stronger this tendency. In empirical research on American companies, the choice of media was also found to be related to the organizational level [24].

On the whole, MRT has developed into an explanation of managers’ rational use of media. Managers, in order to achieve personal and organizational effectiveness, rationally attempt to use media with a richness level appropriate to the equivocality of their communication tasks. The perception and the use of media have been expected to correspond. With respect to traditional media, the empirical research supports this assumption.

## 2.4. Media richness of electronic media

Can electronic media take the place of traditional media such as face-to-face interaction and the telephone? To answer this, the framework of MRT has had to be expanded to include electronic media as the object of MRT research (becoming an ‘‘expanded MRT’’).

Electronic mail, for example, uses mostly text processing to provide high-speed information exchange, but it is both written and asynchronous; which suggests that it is relatively lean media. Therefore e-mail must be rated somewhere between media richness of the telephone and non-electronic written communication [28]. Similarly, voice mail should be rated somewhere between the telephone and e-mail. Teleconferences are capable of transmitting more information than the telephone, and thus should be rated somewhere between face-to-face and the telephone [15]. It has become clear that electronic media are used in different task conditions [23]. It also became obvious that multimedia representation was instrumental in reducing equivocality [19].

Zmud et al. [35] performed a study with the managers and professional staff at a large firm that used 14 types of media. To examine richness, they proposed three criteria: immediate feedback, cue variety, and personalization. Their findings, however, confirmed only immediate feedback as a dimension for differentiating media richness. D’Ambra and Rice [6] distributed, on one plane, managers’ preferences for 11 types of media. They found that voice mail was close to face-to-face and written communication, but was separate from electronic mail and the telephone. They concluded that electronic media cannot be understood solely in terms of richness. Markus [20] suggested that understanding such situations required a new look at the dimensions of richness. If richness is an attribute related to reduction of equivocality in the organization’s information processing, then it was possible to create a new concept that included multiple addressability, external recording, and computer-processable memory, which did not exist in traditional media.

## 2.5. New frameworks for the use of electronic media

MRT proposed that richness was an intrinsic objective property of media and managerial use of media. However, if electronic mail is a lean medium used at the operational level (as predicted by the expanded theory), managers should not like to use it. Yet managers at higher organizational levels do as Markus suggested. And voice mail was not preferred to electronic mail for ambiguous situations [7]. Some research (e.g., [25]) suggested that the emphasis should be placed on the message-level as well as the task-level.

In the search for a new framework, researchers have addressed factors related to contextual constraints (e.g., distance between communication partners and the number of message recipients) (e.g., [30]); social factors and experiences in using the media as follows.

Social factors. Research that has attempted to explain media use through social factors found that the use of a medium is not always determined by media richness. Work group norms, collective rules, social utility, coworker and supervisor attitudes and behaviors, the relationship of the sender and receiver, and the socially created symbolic meaning of the medium itself matter most (e.g., [9,13,27]).

Experience. Experience is important especially for electronic media (e.g., [1,33]). The communicative capability is expanded by the effects of experience in using the media. The experiences shape not only communication effectiveness but also richness perceptions that develop through the learning processes.

These studies were attempts to understand richness not as an inherent attribute of the media but as something created by social factors and experience. Therefore, they did not view richness primarily as the reason for media use but as a product of social contexts due to the communication. For example, Lee [16] viewed electronic mail as a medium that promoted rich communication. Managers should not be considered as passive receptacles, but as intelligent beings deriving meaning from their use of media. The richer e-mail is perceived to be, the more it is preferred and used for any communication task (e.g., [8]).

Other research (e.g., [29]) has suggested that the attributes of media and social factors are complementary rather than competing, and that their relative importance depends on the medium. Therefore, as Markus suggested, when enough time has passed, it may be possible to understand the richness of electronic media as an attribute that reflects converged social definitions. Therefore, the perception and the use of electronic media correspond.

## 3. Survey topics

In MRT, it was assumed that richness was the perceived inherent attribute of the media and that managerial use of these media can be explained by this. Empirical research has shown that managers, in order to achieve personal and organizational effectiveness, rationally attempt to use media with richness level appropriate to their communication need, and that the perception and the use of media should therefore correspond.

Electronic media have become the object of MRT research due to the increase in the use of electronic media. The framework of MRT thus has been expanded. But there has been an increase in research dealing with situations that MRT cannot explain. Thus, it is currently disputable whether an expanded MRT is truly valid.

My study focused on organizational interpretation differences as an important independent variable that could explain media use in an organization. Managers cannot be separated from the interpretation behavior of the organization in which they are employed. Managers in an interpretation system share a perception and a cognitive map and the type of media used changes according to the organizational interpretation behavior.

Though MRT assumed that the perception and use of media corresponded, I examined organizational interpretation behavior to determine whether it was true that ‘‘the richer the medium was perceived to be, the more it would be used’’ when using an electronic medium.

Managers tend to prefer rich media if their environment cannot be analyzed. When organizations actively intrude into the environment, managers’ interest in rich media declines and they prefer media that allow the processing of large quantities of data. To explain the relationship between these two variables of environmental interpretation and the use of rich media, including electronic media, the following hypotheses were proposed:

Hypothesis 1. Managerial use of media in business organizations will be related positively to the likelihood of using rich media if there is an organizational assumption that the external environment is un-analyzable.

Hypothesis 2. Managerial use of media in business organizations will be unrelated to the likelihood of using rich media if there is a high level of organizational intrusiveness into the environment.

## 4. A prior survey

In a prior survey conducted jointly with Prof. Y. Sato at Keio University, Japan and with Dr. M. Chen at the National Sun Yat-Sen University, Taiwan [12]. We attempted to clarify the perceptua dimensions involved when managers distinguish among proposed communication media, and examined whether managers were aware (consciously or unconsciously) of richness as an attribute of the media.

## 4.1. Method

The prior survey was conducted with 2306 managers, taken from a database of managers in Japanese companies, who were aggressively involved in the implementation of IS. In this survey, non-similarity between pairs of media, attributes of individual media, and direct measurement of media richness were examined. The questionnaire developed for this survey was completed by 699 respondents with a response rate of 30.3%.

Using a seven-point Likert scale, respondents were asked to rate their perceptions of their experience with the non-similarity of media–pair combinations. Thirteen types of independent media were proposed: face-to-face, meetings, free discussions, telephone, letter, memo, portable phone, pager, facsimile, electronic mail, electronic conferences, videophone, and teleconferences. As in the method employed by Zmud et al., the respondents were divided into five groups. The 78 [(13 - 12)/2] possible media–pair combinations were randomly divided into five different questionnaires, and after the survey the respective data sets were merged to form complete response matrices.

It can be assumed that the non-similarity of items in the media pairs expresses the psychological distance between media [14,26]. The data on an ordinal scale were measured and distance matrices were constructed. Using UNIX SAS (6.09), multidimensional scaling (MDS) was performed to derive an MDS space for the 13 media [34].

In addition, respondents were asked to rate their perceptions from their communication experiences with respect to attributes of the individual media, again using a seven-point scale. Nonmetric regression analyses were performed in order to fit the media attributes into the MDS space. Ultimately five types of media attributes were selected: immediate feedback, variety of information cues, personalization, reliability, and ease of use. The five attributes of each of the l3 media were randomly divided into five questionnaires. When attempting to fit the media attributes into an MDS space, the vector approach with external unfolding was employed, using UNIX SAS (6.09) PROC TRANSREG to find a direction through the scaling space.

In addition, to investigate the relationship between media richness and the dimensions that make up the MDS space, a direct media richness score for each medium was measured. This was possible because the respondents had been given the definition of the concept of media richness and had rated the richness score of each medium by assigning 100 points to the medium with the highest degree of richness, 0 points to the medium with the lowest, and numbers between 0 and 100 points to those in between, as in the method employed by Lengel [17].

The badness-of-fit criterion of Kruskal’s Stress 1 is approximately 0.41 for one dimension, 0.24 for two dimensions, 0.18 for three dimensions, and 0.14 for four dimensions. To fit media attributes into the MDS space, the vector approach with external unfolding was employed (Table 1). Spearman’s correlation coefficients between each attribute score and the values associated with the three dimensions of the MDS space for each individual medium supported this step (Table 2). The variety of information cues, personalization, and immediate feedback were the direction for one dimension (Dim1), reliability was the direction for another (Dim2), and ease of use was the direction for the third (Dim3). The first dimension (Dim1) displays media richness. For the three attributes included in Dim1, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.93, which is quite high.

In Table 3, face-to-face interaction, meetings, teleconferences, and videophone showed a high degree of media richness, whereas pager, fax, and e-mail registered a low degree. These results match the predictions of the expanded MRT including electronic media. To confirm that Dim1 showed media richness, the Spearman’s correlation coefficient between the value associated with Dim1 for each medium and the media richness score for the same medium was directly measured (though MDS procedures were applied, the interpretation of the MDS dimensions used researcher-specified media attributes). The resulting correlation coefficient was high: 0.86 ( p < 0.05). Thus, as one perceptual dimension, media richness can be confirmed to be an attribute of media.

## 5. The main survey

After examining media richness as an attribute of media, including electronic media, my main survey was initiated to find whether a rich medium was always used at high managerial levels. Using MRT, surveys of managers in Japanese companies was conducted to assess their perception of media attributes in the prior survey and media use in the main survey (including electronic media).

The data in this survey were provided as answers to a postal mail questionnaire sent to 2111 corporate directors in charge of the planning divisions of Japanese companies. With 363 persons responding to the survey, the response rate was 17.2%.

It is difficult to measure either of the two variables that describe differences in the organizational interpretation of the environment: (1) the assumption that the external environment is un-analyzable and (2) the organizational intrusiveness into the environment to help understand it. For the proactive use of electronic media, ANOVA showed that the main effects of intrusiveness were statistically significant, but those of un-analyzability and the interaction terms were not statistically significant. We therefore found that Hypothesis 1 was only true for traditional media but Hypothesis 2 was supported. For traditional media, a positive relationship clearly existed between the organizational assumption that the environment was un-analyzable and needed use of rich media. With traditional media, the perceived media richness was strongly related to media use. But in organizations that actively intruded into the environment, interest in rich media was reduced. Electronic media, which included both rich and lean media, as defined by the expanded MRT, were used. As can be seen, managerial use of the media can vary depending on the way it interprets the environment.

## 6. Discussion

Without doubt, the concept of media richness has been effective in understanding how managers perceive and use media. There is little argument on what constitutes richness in traditional media and the use of such media. My study focused on the different ways that the organization interprets the environment as an important independent variable affecting the use of rich media.

One implication of my study is that organizational interpretation, as well as objective factors, such as task ambiguity or contextual constraints, and social factors, such as social influences or symbolism, can be important in considering the use of rich media. Thus, understanding how organizations interpret their environments can help determine an effective strategy for media usage.

In my main survey, which showed that the variables which express the use of traditional media and the use of electronic media are independent, it was shown that traditional and electronic media are used differently. Organizations that assume that their environments are un-analyzable prefer traditional rich media, but organizations with a high degree of intrusiveness into the environment prefer electronic media.

Organizations that actively intruded into the environment employed electronic media to a greater extent; their interest in rich media is reduced, and properties that allowed for the processing of large quantities of data were more important. Traditional rich media and electronic rich media were used differently. Managers preferred traditional rich media, when they assumed that the environments were un-analyzable. But managers preferred electronic media whether the electronic media were rich or not when they actively intruded into the environment.

Managers from Japanese companies perceive media along the dimension of richness to be essential. With traditional media, the perceived attribute of richness was strongly related to media use. For electronic media, however, the perceived attribute of richness was not necessarily related to media use, even though managers recognized the richness of electronic media.

The research reported here had some limitations. In order to use MDS analysis in the prior survey, the dimensions on the basis of specific media attributes had to be interpreted. This factor must be considered in looking at the results of the study. In addition, the results were for Japan and may not be generalizable.
