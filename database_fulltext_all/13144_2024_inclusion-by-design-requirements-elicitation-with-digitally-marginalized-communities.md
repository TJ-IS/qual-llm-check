# INCLUSION BY DESIGN: REQUIREMENTS ELICITATION WITH DIGITALLY MARGINALIZED COMMUNITIES<sup>1</sup>

Isam Faik Information Systems, Ivey Business School, Western University London, ON, CANADA {ifaik@ivey.ca}

Avijit Sengupta Business Information Systems, Business School, University of Queensland Brisbane, QLD, AUSTRALIA {avijit.sengupta@uq.edu.au}

Yimeng Deng Department of Information Systems and Analytics, School of Computing, National University of Singapore, SINGAPORE {yimeng@comp.nus.edu.sg}

A more equal and sustainable digital future depends on the inclusion of digitally marginalized communities in the socioeconomic opportunities created by digital technologies. Digital inclusion is a complex process that involves all stages of digital innovation, including development, adoption, use, and maintenance. However, past research has largely approached digital inclusion as an adoption and use challenge. In this paper, we develop a view of digital inclusion as a design challenge. We focus on the activities of requirements elicitation (RE) as a critical element of the design process and draw on a design-based interpretive study involving the design of two mobile apps for agricultural communities in India and China. We analyze how the conditions of digital inequality underlying the digital marginalization of these communities affect their sensemaking as they participate in RE activities. We conceptualize these challenges as limitations on the emergence of technology affordances. Our findings reveal various shifts, or translations, in the emerging affordances, which enabled the RE activities to be more generative and consequently more inclusive. These affordance translations manifested along three main dimensions: specificity, temporality, and collectivity. We discuss the implications of these findings for the inclusion of marginalized communities in the design of new technologies.

Keywords: Digital inequality, digital divide, digital inclusion, design process, requirements elicitation, participatory design, affordances, the agricultural field, design science, design-based interpretive research

## Introduction

The inclusion of digitally marginalized communities in the opportunities offered by digital technologies is increasingly seen as a necessary condition for creating stronger economies and more equal societies (Madon et al., 2009; Reisdorf & Rhinesmith, 2020; Warschauer, 2004). As advances in digital technology become integral to most economic and social activities, the ability of individuals and organizations to participate in socioeconomic activities is becoming dependent on their ability to make meaningful use of emerging technologies. Yet, digital inclusion, the process of opening the opportunity space created by digital technologies to currently marginalized communities, remains a challenging process, often leading to failed attempts at transforming the socioeconomic conditions of targeted communities (Hayes & Westrup, 2011; Heeks, 2002).

Underlying the digital marginalization of various communities are broader conditions of digital inequality at the local and global levels (DiMaggio et al., 2004; Hsieh et al.,

2008). Digital inequality reflects differences in access to digital technologies, in the capabilities to use accessible technologies, and in the relevance of digital technologies to local practices (van Dijk, 2020; DiMaggio & Hargittai, 2001; Wei et al., 2011). Digital inclusion requires addressing the various dimensions of digital inequality that manifest across the different stages of digital innovation, including development, adoption, use, and maintenance (DiMaggio et al., 2004). However, past research has largely framed digital inclusion as a challenge of adopting and using existing technologies. There has been limited attention to digital inclusion as a challenge in designing new context-specific technologies. Many contexts require the design of solutions that are specific to their unique conditions. The adoption or adaptation of ready-made digital solutions in such contexts often leads to unintended and undesired consequences that limit the effectiveness of technology use (Heeks, 2002). In such contexts, the inclusion of communities in the opportunities created by digital technologies requires their inclusion in the design of those technologies.

Despite significant advances in developing participatory methodologies that support the inclusion of users in the design process, the modalities of participation remain problematic (Castellanos et al., 2020; Lukyanenko et al., 2016; Appan & Browne, 2012), particularly when involving marginalized communities (Drain et al., 2019; Hussain et al., 2012; Puri & Sahay, 2007). The social, economic, geographic, and linguistic isolation of many communities continues to undermine their participation in the design process. In several interventions, their participation remains nominal, lacking any substantive influence on the design outcomes (Harrington et al., 2019, Heeks, 1999). Past studies have identified various barriers to participation in conditions of marginalization (Drain et al., 2019; Harrington et al., 2019; Hussain et al., 2012; Puri & Sahay, 2007) but there has been little analysis of the social and cognitive processes through which participants from marginalized communities face these barriers as they engage, individually and collectively, with the designers and the design artifacts. Understanding these sociocognitive processes is important for addressing the underlying constraints to participation in conditions of digital marginalization and for generating approaches to design that are truly inclusive.

In this paper, we investigate the sociocognitive processes through which users from marginalized communities participate in the design of technologies that are specifically aimed for use in their context. We focus on their participation in the requirements elicitation (RE) process. RE is one of the most critical elements of the design process as it has a significant influence on the eventual success or failure of the design outcomes (Appan & Browne, 2012; Davidson, 2002; Jayanth et al., 2010). Participation in RE provides an opportunity for the users to influence the designers conceptualization of the problem and solution spaces. However, digitally marginalized communities face significant challenges to participate meaningfully in RE processes because their lack of exposure to digital technologies reduces their ability to make sense of the design artifacts and to relate them to their context.

To investigate the challenges of user participation in RE within the context of digitally marginalized communities, we build on a design-based interpretive field study involving agricultural communities in India and China. The study draws on our own involvement as both researchers and designers in the development of digital solutions for these communities. Our interpretive approach (Van Manen, 2003; Walsham, 2006) enabled us to draw on our design experience to identify various meanings that participants in RE activities associate with the design artifacts and activities and, by extension, the meanings they associate with their practices and conditions. In contrast to design science studies, which aim to develop prescriptive knowledge (Hevner et al., 2004), the objective of our approach was to develop explanatory knowledge of the design process and context. Our study involved the design of two mobile applications: MPest, which was developed to provide farmers with better control over pest infestations, and MFarm, which was developed to support farmers in the management of their farming activities. RE activities in the design of the two applications included interviews, focus groups, population stereotype exercises, and prototyping.

To develop a theoretical understanding of farmers’ sociocognitive processes as they engage in RE activities, we draw on the concept of technology affordances (Gibson, 1977; Majchrzak & Markus, 2012). Affordances represent the possibilities for goal-oriented action that a user or group of users perceive in a technology (Markus & Silver, 2008). To perceive affordances in artifacts, users need to make sense of those artifacts in relation to their goals and environment (Anderson & Robey, 2017; Faik et al., 2020; Fayard & Weeks, 2007). However, when an artifact is unfamiliar, it challenges users’ sensemaking and therefore limits the range of affordances that they can perceive when participating in RE activities.

Our findings suggest that participants managed to overcome these limits by drawing on their peripheral experiences with technology, such as observations of technology use by others and their own experiences with technology in different fields. Importantly, we found that participants often build on the affordances resulting from their initial sensemaking of the technology to shift toward alternative affordances. We call these shifts affordance translations and identify three main dimensions along which they manifested in our RE activities: specificity, temporality, and collectivity. We also identify the focus of the participants’ attention that triggered each type of translation. We discuss the implications of an affordance translation perspective on understanding the sociocognitive processes of user participation in conditions of digital marginalization.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. We begin by developing a perspective of digital inclusion as a design challenge. Next, we focus on RE as a critical part of the design process and present an affordance perspective of RE activities. We then draw on sensemaking theory to conceptualize the challenges of generating affordances in RE activities involving digitally marginalized communities. After that, we present our design-based interpretive research methodology, including a description of the various RE activities that constituted our study and our approach to their analysis. We report our findings by showing how various forms of digital inequality manifested in our study’s context and how they shaped the sensemaking of RE participants. We then present an analysis of the affordance translations observed in our RE activities and discuss their implications for the inclusion of marginalized communities in the design of digital technologies. We end by highlighting our contributions to theory and practice in the areas of digital inclusion, RE, and affordances, and our methodological contribution in the form of the proposed design-based interpretive approach.

## Theoretical Background

## Digital Inclusion as a Design Challenge

Digital technologies have contributed significantly to economic growth and social well-being. However, the socioeconomic advantages offered by digital technologies have not been equally shared across the different segments of society (Madon et al., 2009; Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015). Instead, the increasing ubiquity of digital technologies has, in several cases, exacerbated the marginalization of various communities and social groups (Deng et al., 2016; Tarafdar et al., 2013). This form of marginalization is a manifestation of prevalent digital inequality, which reflects differences in people’s capacity to benefit from the opportunities offered by digital technologies and to overcome the constraints that they create (DiMaggio & Hargittai, 2001; Helsper, 2017; Hsieh et al., 2008).

Digital inequality represents three different but interlinked conditions: (1) inequality in access to digital infrastructure, devices, data, and applications (2) inequality in capabilities to use accessible digital technologies, and (3) inequality in the relevance of accessible and usable technologies to the context of use (DiMaggio & Hargittai, 2001; Warschauer, 2002; Wei et al., 2011). Inequality in access can be the outcome of poverty limiting the affordability of digital technologies for segments of the population, social or geographic exclusion limiting the availability of technological infrastructures, or physical disability limiting the usability of technological features (Dobransky & Hargittai, 2016; Servon, 2008). Inequality in capabilities can be the result of differences in literacy and numeracy, as well as in the ability to search for and manipulate information on digital media (Van Dijk & Hacker, 2003). It can also be the result of social norms or political constraints limiting the autonomy of use (DiMaggio & Hargittai, 2001). Inequality in relevance can be the outcome of disparities in the applicability of available digital artifacts, including data, applications, and devices, to the context of the users (Heeks, 2002). Access to digital tools and the capability to use them do not necessarily translate into tangible outcomes for the users when their features and content are not relevant to local practices.

Thus, digital inequality is based on economic, social, and political conditions that influence the ability of individuals and organizations to access digital technologies and use them in meaningful ways (DiMaggio & Hargittai, 2001). While digital technologies are seen as an opportunity to address various forms of inequality (Unwin, 2009), the persistence of socioeconomic inequalities is, in itself, a major obstacle to any attempts at narrowing the gaps in the adoption and effective use of digital technologies. For example, in the past, enhancing access to feature phones was a means to reducing digital inequality, but more recently, digital inequality has been manifesting more between those who are limited to feature phones and those who have access to smart phones (Mabweazara, 2021). Therefore, as digital technologies continue to advance, digital inequalities will continue to translate into socioeconomic inequalities in a vicious cycle that many communities struggle to escape.

Enabling individuals and communities to escape this vicious cycle requires sociotechnical environments that offer opportunities for meaningful change in the social and economic conditions of the users. It requires digital inclusion—that is, the inclusion of currently marginalized communities in the opportunity spaces created by digital technologies (Haenssgen, 2018; Madon et al., 2009; Meneses & Mominó, 2010; Reisdorf & Rhinesmith, 2020; Unwin, 2009; Upadhyaya et al., 2019; Warschauer, 2004). Digital inclusion is a form of social inclusion in which the boundaries of participation in socioeconomic activities are redefined through the use of digital technologies. It involves addressing the conditions that deprive communities of the freedom to pursue meaningful goals in increasingly digitalized social and economic environments (Zheng & Walsham, 2008).

Past research and professional interventions have largely approached digital inclusion as a challenge of enhancing the adoption of digital technologies by marginalized communities (Hsieh et al., 2008; Kvasny & Keil, 2006; Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015; Venkatesh & Sykes, 2013). However, when adopting technologies developed in and for other contexts, marginalized communities often struggle to make them meaningful on their own (Hayes & Westrup, 2011; Heeks, 2002; Miscione, 2007). Therefore, as digital technologies are becoming integral to most socioeconomic activities, participation in those activities increasingly requires the participation of user communities in the design of the digital technologies that support them.

Thus, digital inclusion requires processes that go beyond enabling marginalized communities to benefit from existing technologies. It requires processes for designing technologies that are specifically aimed for use in their contexts (Miscione, 2007; Walsham & Sahay, 1999). The design of contextually relevant technologies empowers user groups (Unwin, 2009) and avoids the common assimilationist view of inclusion, which reduces it to a process of assimilating the activities of marginalized groups into those of a dominant group (Dudley-Marling & Burns, 2014).

Despite the significance of design as a basis of digital inclusion, there has been little research attention to the opportunities and challenges of designing digital technologies for marginalized communities. Therefore, in this study, we try to go beyond digital inclusion as a problem of adoption and use and approach it instead as a problem of design. We focus on requirements elicitation as one of the most critical elements of the design process. Participation in the identification of design requirements is one of the main ways that users can influence the relevance of designed technologies to their context.

## Challenges of User Participation in Requirements Elicitation

The identification of user requirements that can inform the design of digital technologies depends on multiple sociocognitive processes of engagement between designers, users, and the context of use (Appan & Browne, 2012; Jayanth et al., 2010). Several studies have emphasized the adopted approach to RE as a key factor in influencing the success or failure of a technology to achieve effective use among a community of users (Davidson, 2002; Davis, 1982; Hickey & Davis, 2004; Hofmann & Lehner, 2001; Rosenkranz et al., 2014). Digital technologies often fail to attain the objectives of their design due more to a lack of fit with the needs of the users and the context of use than to issues surrounding the technical functioning of the technology (Hofmann & Lehner, 2001).

Several techniques have been suggested for eliciting requirements varying across a wide spectrum, from the structured to the semi-structured to the unstructured (Hickey & Davis, 2004; Jiang et al., 2008; Pohl, 2010; Sharma & Pandey, 2013). They include interviews (Moody et al., 1998), surveys (Zowghi & Coulin, 2005), domain analysis (Hofmann & Lehner, 2001), prototyping (Jayanth et al., 2010), focus groups (Massey & Wallace, 1991), observations (Tuunanen, 2003), and production of population stereotypes (Howell & Fuchs, 1968; Ng et al., 2012). These various RE techniques offer different approaches to enabling user participation in the design process. However, the modalities of user participation through each of these techniques are complex, which makes them often ineffective in generating requirements that ensure the relevance of the design to the users and their context (Hickey & Davis, 2004; Lukyanenko et al., 2016).

Past studies have investigated the challenges of user participation in the RE process and pointed to different categories of challenges, including those related to the users, the designers, and the RE process. Regarding the users, the effectiveness of their participation in RE can be limited by their lack of motivation to participate or by their cognitive biases in describing tasks and processes, such as the tendency to recall events that are more recent or vivid (Browne & Ramesh, 2002). It can also be limited by the use of parochial or highly technical language in describing their practices (Urquhart, 1997) or by high levels of heterogeneity in the user group producing diverse sets of requirements that are hard to reconcile (Castellanos et al., 2020).

The designers can also limit the effectiveness of user participation when they lack critical reflections on their own biases in interpreting users’ input (Boland, 1979; Klendauer et al., 2012), when they introduce misinformation that leads users to misrepresent their practices (Appan & Browne, 2012), or when they direct the RE process toward requirements that fit more their skill set instead of the needs of the users (Jayanth et al., 2010). User participation can also be undermined by issues related to the RE process, such as frequent changes in requirements over the course of the project, significant changes in implementation technologies, or time constraints limiting the scope and quality of user involvement (Davidson, 2002; Ramesh et al., 2010).

The literature suggests a number of approaches to addressing these issues, such as the use of cognitive interviews that intentionally support the users’ memory recall (Moody et al., 1998), the use of planned procedural prompts to help the designers overcome their limitations in capturing requirements (Pitts & Browne, 2007), and the adoption of agile methods to make the RE process more iterative and consequently more generative (Ramesh et al., 2010). However, much of this research has been conducted in relatively affluent environments, which means that it often takes for granted resources and capabilities that are lacking in marginalized communities.

Users in marginalized communities face many of the above challenges, but they also face unique challenges that reflect their peripheral position in the networks for the development and use of digital technologies. A small stream of research investigated these challenges, pointing to challenges resulting from education and language barriers (Drain et al., 2019; Puri & Sahay, 2007) as well as geographic and social isolation and the dependence on daily wages, which may limit the ability of community members to take the time to participate in RE activities (Hussain et al., 2012). Other studies pointed to challenges in contributing to open ideation processes because the dire circumstances of marginalized communities force individuals to focus on more pragmatic solutions (Harrington et al., 2019).

Despite the insights offered by this literature in understanding the main challenges of user participation in marginalized communities, it does not offer analyses of the sociocognitive processes that constitute user participation and consequently underlie many of these challenges. In particular, little attention has been devoted to the sociocognitive challenges to user participation resulting from the lack of familiarity with digital technologies, which our own research and past studies (Drain et al., 2019; Puri & Sahay, 2007) reveal as critical in limiting the effectiveness of RE activities in conditions of digital marginalization. In this paper, we turn to affordance and sensemaking theories to develop a theoretical basis for understanding these challenges.

## An Affordance Perspective of Requirements Elicitation

While engaging in RE activities, users try to make sense of design artifacts by relating them to their practices. In so doing, they generate affordances that represent the entanglement of the artifacts’ materiality with the users’ goals (Leonardi, 2011). Affordances are the possibilities of action that a user or group of users perceive in a technology within a context of use (Markus & Silver, 2008). The foundational proposition in affordance theory is that in interacting with everyday objects, visible qualities such as color, texture, composition, size, and shape are not the primary focus of our attention; we instead directly perceive “the values and meanings of things in the environment” (Gibson, 1986, p. 127, emphasis in original). That is, we engage with objects in our environment by making sense of them in ways that relate them to our goals and intended actions.

The concept of affordances became influential in technology design, particularly in the field of human-computer interaction (HCI), through the work of Norman (1988), who suggested that designers should develop everyday objects in ways that make it easy for users to perceive their affordances. However, applications of the concept in this field tended to reduce them to features and functions, with limited attention to the role of the users in generating affordances (Norman, 1999, 2015). In organizational studies of technology, these tensions over the ontology of affordances manifested in differences between a view of affordances as a dispositional property of the artifacts and a more common understanding of affordances as a relation between the users and the artifacts (Fayard & Weeks, 2014). We adopt a view of affordances as both dispositional and relational (Seidel et al., 2018), accounting for both the designers’ intentionality (the dispositions they seek to embed in the technology) and the users’ experiences as they engage with the technology.

During RE activities, attention to what and how users perceive affordances reveals the mechanisms through which RE enables or fails to enable convergence between users’ goals and the potential features of the technology. RE can thus be conceptualized as a process of enabling participants to perceive and articulate affordances. In everyday technology use, affordances manifest mostly through the actions of the users in the form of affordance actualizations (Strong et al., 2014), but in RE activities, affordances manifest through both the verbal expressions of the users (e.g., in interviews and focus groups) and their actions (e.g., in observations of prototype use). Past studies have leveraged the concept of affordances to theorize the design process while focusing on the possibilities of action as perceived by the designers (Seidel et al., 2018). In this study, we draw on affordances to develop an understanding of the possibilities perceived by the users as participants in the design process.

## Affordances of Unfamiliar Technologies: The Need for Sensemaking

When a form of technology becomes prevalent in a given context, affordances can become institutionalized (Faik et al., 2020) and therefore generated without the users’ conscious deliberation. For example, students entering a classroom are likely to perceive in the available chairs the possibility of “sitting.” However, when technologies are foreign to a context, generating affordances requires active sensemaking efforts from the users (Griffith, 1999). Students finding a new type of furniture in their classroom would be challenged to deliberately make sense of the unfamiliar artifacts and the actions that they afford. When conducting RE with digitally marginalized communities, users are often challenged to make sense of foreign design artifacts (concepts, models, and objects) by trying to relate them to their practices and context.

Sensemaking is the process through which people try to understand events or environments that are novel, ambiguous, confusing, or in some way violate expectations (Maitlis & Christianson, 2014). People engage in sensemaking when “the current state of the world is perceived to be different from the expected state of the world, or when there is no obvious way to engage the world” (Weick et al., 2005, p. 409). Users often need to make sense of technology because of its equivocal nature, admitting “several possible or plausible interpretations” (Berente et al., 2011; Mesgari & Okoli, 2019; Weick, 1990, p. 2). However, in conditions of digital marginalization, users often need to make sense of technology because they do not have enough viable interpretations.

In these conditions, technology triggers the sensemaking of users because of high levels of novelty or discrepancy (Griffith, 1999; Louis & Sutton, 1991). Digitally marginalized users experience technology as novel when its features are either unknown or unfamiliar to them. Making sense of technology that is experienced as novel requires the users to engage in “deliberate” sensemaking, demanding attention to every aspect of the technology, including its most basic functions, as opposed to “immanent” sensemaking, which draws on embodied routines in using the technology (Sandberg & Tsoukas, 2020). In addition, even when elements of a technology are recognizable, its limited applicability to their context can create a discrepancy between what the users expect the technology to afford and what they actually observe (Heeks, 2002). Such discrepancies make the cognitive schemas on which users draw inappropriate for interacting with the technology. In conditions of digital marginalization, novelty can be an outcome of limited access and capabilities, while discrepancy can be an outcome of limited relevance.

Understanding the processes through which users and designers navigate these sensemaking challenges is important for exploring ways in which communities can transcend their digital marginalization to become active participants in the design of their digital solutions. Developing a theoretical understanding of these processes is the main objective of this study.

## Research Methodology

## A Design-Based Interpretive Study

In order to investigate the challenges that digital inequality represents for the design of new technologies, we tried to combine the strengths of interpretive (Walsham, 2006) and design science research (DSR) (Hevner et al., 2004) as we engaged with farming communities in India and China. As our study evolved, the tensions between the two research traditions challenged us to rethink our approach and we came to see it as fitting neither a typical interpretive study nor a DSR study. It evolved into what we call a design-based interpretive study. Thus, instead of building our interpretive fieldwork on a case study (Walsham, 1995), an ethnography (Schultze, 2000), or online data (Eaton et al., 2015), we built it on a design experience. More specifically, we drew on the layers of meanings generated by both the study participants, as users, and the researchers, as designers, throughout the experience of designing two mobile applications for the agricultural field.

Our design-based interpretive approach shares with design science research the objective of generating new knowledge from the experience of designing technological artifacts (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Hevner et al., 2004). However, they differ in that knowledge generation in our case is not based on the novelty of the designed artifact, but on the privileged access that the designers get to the meanings that design participants associate with the design artifacts and activities. In addition, while design science research primarily aims to generate prescriptive design knowledge (Gregor & Jones, 2007), our interpretive approach is aimed at developing explanatory knowledge that advances our understanding of design processes and contexts.

Our design-based interpretive approach also shares with action research (Baskerville, 1999) the objective of generating knowledge from interventions to address real-world problems. However, the emphasis of knowledge generation in action research is on the outcome of interventions, based on the assumption that “complex social processes can be studied best by introducing changes into these processes and observing the effects of these changes” (Baskerville, 1999, p. 4, emphasis added). Instead, our approach puts more emphasis on the interpretive analysis of the intervention itself rather than its effects. Action design research (ADR) is a methodology that offers a cross-fertilization of DSR and action research by emphasizing the intertwining of development and evaluation in design interventions (Sein et al., 2011). Thus, it also foregrounds the intervention as a subject of analysis and a basis for knowledge generation. However, ADR’s objective remains the development of prescriptive knowledge for addressing a class of problems, in contrast to our design-based interpretive approach, which is proposed as a means to advance our understanding of a phenomenon of interest through design.

An interpretive approach (Van Manen, 2003; Walsham, 2006) is particularly suitable for building knowledge from design activities, particularly RE activities, because they involve a series of interactions between the designers and the users which produce rich meanings around current practices, possible futures, and contextual constraints. In addition, the intentionality of design makes design activities a more generative form of data collection (Carlsen & Dutton, 2011). Having a design purpose makes the interactions with the study participants more engaging and meaningful.

## Data Collection through RE Activities

In this study, we build on the design of two mobile apps that support agricultural practices in India and China. The two apps were developed as part of a large multi-disciplinary and multi-institution research project, which had the broad objective of developing digital solutions for marginalized communities in Asia. The project had multiple components beyond the two apps, but the authors of this paper were mostly focused on the research activities related to the two applications, including RE, analysis, and development. The first app was MPest, which is a pest management system that allows farmers to generate pest reports and obtain information that can help them decide on the appropriate course of action to deal with issues of pest infestation. The second app was MFarm, which is an application that supports the tracking and management of farming activities such as plowing, planting, watering, and fertilizing.

The project started with a broad initial objective of developing digital solutions for marginalized communities. The agricultural field was identified as a domain that is lagging behind in digitalization and therefore one in which many critical needs could be addressed through digital innovation. The process of identifying requirements started with an openended exploration of the agricultural field, with a particular focus on smallholder farmers in order to gain a better understanding of their context and critical needs. We then started a process of iterating between conducting RE activities, analysis, and development using a wide range of RE methods. Overall, the RE activities took place over six visits to agricultural communities in India and China, between July 2011 and August 2014, as detailed in Table 1. Descriptive statistics for each visit are detailed in Appendix A.

In the first visit, we conducted interviews<sup>2</sup> aimed at identifying key challenges faced by smallholder farmers in India. We particularly sought to identify the informational needs of farmers as they engaged in agricultural processes. The interviews, along with our analysis of industry reports and the academic literature, led us to identify pest infestation as one of the most critical challenges facing farmers in this context.

We then developed a first prototype for MPest as shown in Figure 1. In this prototype, we sought to offer key functionalities for pest detection while keeping the design open for other features to be added in later stages. The prototype was designed to help farmers send reports of pest infestations in their crops and receive suggestions from a database on how to control the identified pest. The prototype also provided an online forum for farmers where they could discuss issues related to pest infestations and other agricultural matters.

In the second field visit, we assessed the ability of the farmers to engage with the application. A group of 56 farmers were presented with two different interfaces of the MPest prototype and were asked to perform various tasks with the help of MPest. The tasks were introduced through role-based scenarios. This activity revealed various difficulties that the farmers faced in making sense of symbols that did not have a visual link to their environment, which led us to conduct a population stereotype study (as described in our account of the fifth visit). In the third visit, we engaged multiple farming groups in participatory design workshops to co-design the visual content for instructional materials that would be sent as solutions in pest infestation reports.

After initial developments in India, we took the MPest prototype to China in order to assess its fit with the needs and context of Chinese agriculture and identify any specific needs that might require changes to the app design. In this visit (Field Visit 4), we conducted 10 semi-structured interviews with farmers to identify their informational needs, their current technological practices, and their reaction to the MPest prototype. The interviews, along with the field observations, pointed us toward a different but significant need in these communities—the management of their farming processes. Instead of expanding MPest to include farm management tools, we decided to develop a separate application that addresses this need among the farmers. We then developed a prototype of the MFarm app, as shown in Figure 2.

MFarm was primarily designed to help farmers optimize their production processes by enabling them to track their farming activities for each unit of farmland (e.g., User X watered Field A using N liters of water at 8 a.m. today) and to trace the outcomes of these activities.

On our fifth visit to the field, we conducted a “stereotype production” activity (Ng et al., 2012). Population stereotype production involves asking participants to develop pictorial representations of entities or activities that need to be incorporated into the design, and then choosing the most frequent representations as the “population stereotype.” A total of 92 participants were recruited from five different villages with the help of a nonprofit organization. The participants were asked to draw their own representations for eight generic mobile app functionalities and eight functionalities related to the agricultural context. Their representations were then evaluated by a different group of 88 participants who were asked to rank the icons in order of preference, as shown in Figure 3.

On our sixth visit, we elicited more requirements to further develop the MFarm application using the prototype based on the farmers’ input during our fourth visit. This took place during a field visit to agricultural communities in China, where we conducted a total of 22 interviews with smallholder farmers, farm managers, and seasonal workers. In these interviews, we started by asking participants about their informational needs throughout the farming process as well as their current uses of digital technologies. We then introduced them to the MFarm prototype and elicited their reaction to its present features and their suggestions for other features that would enhance its usefulness in their context.

## Data Analysis

Since we based our study on a design experience, we analyzed the data as we were collecting it for the combined purpose of prototype development and theory generation. The analysis of our design activities for the sake of identifying requirements provided a basis for analyzing the design process and context. Our initial objective from this combined approach was to identify design principles that can guide the development of digital solutions for digitally marginalized communities. However, our attempts to derive such principles revealed to us the importance of first developing a deeper understanding of the design process in conditions of digital marginalization. Thus, following iterations with the existing literature on the topic, we shifted our objective to developing a theoretical understanding of the design process and the conditions of digital marginalization in which it was unfolding.

To do so, we needed to take our analysis beyond the descriptive details of the RE activities to a more conceptual level. For this purpose, we conducted open coding (Holton, 2007) of the textual and visual data emerging from the various RE activities. The open codes helped us identify key concepts that could be inferred from each RE interaction with the farmers. We concurrently explored the theoretical significance of the generated concepts through analytic memos (Corbin & Strauss, 1990). Given our interest in the sociocognitive processes of user participation, our analytic memos were focused on interpreting the farmers’ statements and actions in ways that reveal how they were making sense of the design process and artifacts and how they were rethinking their practices and environment when considering technology applications. In addition to the coding of farmers’ statements, we also generated narratives (Langley, 1999) of our design experience, including accounts of each field visit and key insights from the RE activities. Combining these various approaches to conceptual abstraction allowed us to capitalize on one of the main strengths of our design-based interpretive approach, which is the emic perspective that the designers have in interpreting the events in the design process. In so doing, we followed a hermeneutic circle (Klein & Myers, 1999) in which our conceptual understanding of the design process built on our design-focused interpretation of each RE activity, and vice versa.

As we were identifying a broad range of concepts that were helping us develop viable interpretations of the farmers’ thought processes, actions, and interactions during RE activities, we started exploring the literature for conceptual frameworks that could provide an integrative theoretical basis to our interpretations. We considered various theoretical angles, including cognitive absorption (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000), metaphors (Heracleous & Jacobs, 2008), value co-creation (Sarker et al., 2012), and cognitive flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 1988). However, the concept of affordances emerged as particularly suitable for supporting our theoretical developments. It offered us a perspective and theoretical language that was conducive to conceptualizing one of the main challenges in our RE activities, which was identifying or generating spaces of overlap between the farmer’s goals and the possibilities offered by digital technologies.

After establishing affordances as a core category in our analysis, we recoded our data to identify the various affordances articulated by the study participants. We inferred affordances from any articulations by the farmers of benefits or limitations they perceived in the design artifacts (e.g., “for identification of pest issues this (the app) can be very handy”: identifying pest issues), extensions to what the artifacts currently offer or expectations of what the technology, in general, can or should do for them (e.g., “technology should help us protect our crop”: protecting crop), statements of goals that were made when considering a design artifact (e.g., “we need to save water … we don’t have enough water, particularly in the summer season”: saving water), past observations of technology use by others (e.g., “I have seen small computers (tablets) in my daughter’s school … I heard that you can also access internet in those small computers”: accessing the internet), and personal past uses of digital technology (e.g., “I have this agricultural dictionary in my mobile phone … Whenever I have any doubt, I refer to it”: clarifying knowledge). All these are indicative of possibilities of action that the farmers were directly or indirectly perceiving in the design artifacts. These affordance articulations varied along a spectrum from direct actions on the artifacts (e.g., tapping an icon) to more distant actions that could be considered goals for the more proximal ones (e.g., sharing pest reports → protecting crops → avoiding financial difficulties).

As we engaged in this coding process, we noticed several instances in which multiple affordances were articulated in the same statement, which led us to develop the concept of affordance translation. This prompted another round of coding in which we focused on identifying instances of affordance translations and their triggers as shown in Tables 3, 4, and 5 below. In the later stages of the analysis, we turned to sensemaking theory in order to conceptualize the unique challenges of perceiving and articulating affordances in conditions of digital marginalization.

## Findings and Analysis

## Digital Inequality and Technology Sensemaking in the Agricultural Field

Both India and China are experiencing growing demands for the use of digital technologies in agriculture (Hanna & Qiang, 2010; Pingali, 2012). Farming in both countries is still dominated by a large proportion of smallholder farmers, reflecting old policies of land distribution that generated high levels of fragmentation in land ownership (Zhang & Donaldson, 2010; Taylor, 2011). As a result, the adoption of digital technologies in agriculture has been slow compared to other fields, which has resulted in significant digital inequalities because most smallholder farmers and seasonal workers lack access and capabilities to use the currently prevalent forms of digital technologies. In addition, even when farmers were able to afford and use some of the latest technologies, these were still foreign to their context, as most existing solutions were not applicable to their farming conditions.

The farming communities that took part in our study reflected conditions of digital inequality across the three dimensions of access, capabilities, and relevance. First, inequality in digital access was visible in both the Indian and the Chinese contexts, with access to digital devices varying from low-cost feature phones to medium- or high-cost smart phones (see Appendix A for more details on device access levels). In addition, the level of access to digital applications varied significantly between the participants, ranging from minimal access (e.g., voice calls on feature phones), low access to basic functions (e.g., weather forecast, instant messaging tools), medium access to information processing tools (e.g., search engines, office utilities), and some limited forms of high access to advanced information processing tools (e.g., enterprise systems).

This lack of access meant that most farmers in the participating communities had limited exposure to the latest devices and applications, which affected their ability to make sense of those technologies. This made some farmers wary of engaging with the design artifacts during the RE activities:

For the first few times, I want the phone to tell me what are the meanings of these images [icons], what will happen when I touch them … actually I am afraid of touching them … I am always worried I may damage the phone by clicking on the wrong place (Mayank Surve,<sup>3</sup> Sugarcane Farmer, Ozar Village)

Yet most farmers had a broad understanding of what digital technologies could afford them based on past observations of technology use by others in their social circles. This vicarious learning enabled farmers to perceive some initial affordances in digital technologies even when they could not see themselves actualizing them in practice, making them aware of the consequences of their relative lack of access to the latest technologies on their ability to make substantial changes to their condition. A participating farmer explained:

I only have a feature phone … I can only call and see the messages that come to the phone … I can’t even type text messages … but some of my friends have more expensive phones … bigger screen … camera on the phone … can take photograph of the issue in the crop and send it to the crop doctor … crop doctors then send solution … send messages. … For me, I have to call the crop doctor and describe the issue … if he visits, I have to pay his visiting fee … others can save that. (Atul Shinde, Soybean Farmer, Khed Village)

Inequality in capabilities manifested in a lack of digital literacy and low levels of general literacy, which are typical in rural communities around the world (Fong, 2009). In addition, the high rates of rural-to-urban migration in both India and China means that many young adults, who tend to have higher capabilities in the use of digital technologies, leave in search of better opportunities in large cities. As a result, the communities in our study had a shortage of people having both agricultural experience and digital capabilities. These capability limitations meant that the farmers had to engage in deliberate sensemaking even when dealing with the most basic functions of the design artifacts. They needed to actively search for meanings that they could attribute to technology features in order to support their use. One farmer explained:

I always find difficulties to remember the meaning of these pictures [icons on phone], my son reminds me from time to time, I still forget their meaning … and then I am lost not being able to access the thing that I wanted to access. (Basu Patil, Capsicum, Soybean, Okra Farmer, Sukena Village)

We observed that failure to generate stable meanings limited farmers’ ability to engage with the technology. However, as digital technologies are quickly becoming integral to critical services beyond the agricultural field, many of the farmers in our study were cognizant of the constraints that their lack of digital capabilities was imposing on their ability to improve their socioeconomic conditions. A farmer highlighted this awareness in relation to services from the financial field:

See, I can’t avoid taking loans from the Seth [money lenders] with a very high interest rate due to my ignorance about new information technology … due to my current limitation, I failed to apply for bank loans to raise the capital for farming … not being able to prepare papers on the internet … but other farmers are doing it and getting bank loans more cheaply. (Yatrik Sawant, Maize and Soybean Farmer, Kheranagar Village)

Even when the farmers in our study were not capable of engaging with digital technologies in their field of agriculture, the increasing prevalence of digital technologies in adjacent fields (e.g., finance, healthcare, education) was making them aware of the broader affordances that technology could offer them, allowing them to make inferences for their own field.

Finally, inequality in relevance manifested in the lack of applicability of digital solutions to local farming practices. Much of the online agricultural information that was accessible to the farmers was not directly relevant to their context. A participating farmer emphasized the need for relevance by explaining how differences in language, farming environment, and market conditions were limiting the usability of online information in his context:

I have internet in my phone but all the information that I found is either in English or … not relevant to my farming … the market rates are also for the main cities of India … useless to me … I need the market rates of different produce in the local district market or the market nearest to me … that is more helpful to me. (Sanjay Ingle, Sugarcane and Onion Farmer, Pabal village)

Relevance was critical for the emergence of affordances when farmers engaged with the various design artifacts in the RE activities. This was particularly visible in the population stereotype activity, where many farmers stressed the need for action orientation in the design of the various icons. For example, a farmer expressed this preference regarding the icon for pest control:

Showing pest infestation by just showing pest on leaf is not good enough … more important is … now what … what can I do to get rid of them … that is why I prefer the representation which shows farmer spraying pesticide on plants. (Mohan Jadhav, Ginger Farmer, Pabal village)

Icons that were more easily relatable to their farming practices (e.g., spraying pesticide on plants) supported their sensemaking and enabled them to generate affordances as they engaged in the RE activities. In summary, while participating farmers struggled to perceive affordances in the design artifacts, they managed to generate initial affordances when they drew on their past observations of technology use and their experiences with technology in adjacent fields, or when clear action orientation in the design artifacts facilitated their sensemaking.

## Affordance Translations in RE Activities

The digital marginalization of the farmers challenged their ability to make sense of the design artifacts and consequently limited the range of affordances that could emerge from their participation in the RE activities. However, our analysis of the farmers’ engagement in RE highlighted various shifts, or translations, in the emerging affordances that made the RE activities more generative, and consequently more conducive to accounting for the needs of the more digitally marginalized participants. We use translation here in the sense of a general movement or a displacement, but one that always involves transformation (Latour, 1987, p. 132). As the term is used in reference to language, the translation of any entity (an idea, an object, an institution, etc.) shifts it from one domain to another while transforming it in the process (Czarniawska, 2009).

In various RE activities, farmers shifted from the initial affordances they were able to articulate toward alternative affordances that were substantially different. These shifts expanded the range of affordances that could emerge from an RE activity. We identified three key dimensions along which the emerging affordances were shifting throughout RE interactions: specificity, temporality, and collectivity. In the following subsections, we discuss these affordance translations and their effects on the RE process.

## Translations in the Specificity of Affordances

Our design experience started with efforts to gain familiarity with the agricultural field and to identify the major challenges facing members of the farming communities. Therefore, the RE activities in the early stages of our design process were open-ended and sought to elicit requirements related to any of the wide range of practices and issues in the agricultural field. This open-endedness allowed participating farmers to consider a wide array of challenges and opportunities involving agriculture as well as the various technological possibilities that could be used to address them.

The first dimension along which affordances emerging from RE activities varied was their level of specificity. Some affordances were more generic in nature, such as improving harvest or accessing information. These affordances did not point to specific actions; they were instead articulated in terms of broader goals that the farmers associated with technology use. Other affordances were more specific, such as monitoring soil quality or receiving a specific type of information. However, our analysis revealed that several RE interactions started with generic affordances and then shifted toward more specific ones, while others started with more specific affordances before shifting toward more generic ones. Table 3 provides samples of these affordance translations along with the focus of the farmers’ attention that triggered them.

Several participating farmers started their RE interactions by perceiving and articulating more generic affordances such as minimizing water wastage, before moving to more specific ones, such as obtaining accurate water measurements (left column in Table 3). The articulation of generic affordances did not require high levels of familiarity with existing digital technologies and the type of practices they can support. They consequently allowed many participating farmers to make some initial sense of digital technologies before building on that to articulate more specific affordances.

These translations from generic to specific affordances were triggered by the farmers turning their attention to the value of the initial affordances in their context. Assessing the value of the actions and goals that they perceived in the design artifacts pushed them to relate the artifacts to their context and to consequently attribute new meanings to them within the particularities of their environment. They did so by turning their attention to personal conditions that provided a rationale for their perceived goals and actions or by assessing the significance of the generic affordance in relation to their work and business environment. The meanings they generated in the process brought the artifacts closer to their personal circumstances and thus enabled the farmers to be more specific in their articulation of affordances.

Conversely, other participating farmers started by articulating more specific affordances, such as reporting pest infestation, before shifting to more generic ones, such as protecting their crop (right column in Table 3). These translations were triggered by the participating farmers turning their attention to the boundaries defining the initial specific affordances. Attending to these boundaries included thinking about the limited scope of the specific affordances in enabling them to attain their goals. It also involved considering the value of the specific affordances within the limits of existing conditions, as well as the farmers’ own limits in actualizing the specific affordances. Deliberating on the boundaries of the specific affordances enabled the RE participants to broaden their frame for engaging with the artifacts. It allowed them to go beyond specific actions and to relate the artifacts to broader goals at higher levels of abstraction.

## Translations in the Temporality of Affordances.

The second dimension along which affordances were shifting in our RE activities was temporality. Various RE activities were generating multiple affordances that reflected different time horizons. Many farmers started by articulating affordances that were more immediate, i.e, focused on current needs, and then shifted to articulating affordances that were prospective in nature, i.e., oriented more toward the future. In other instances, the farmers started with prospective affordances and then shifted to more immediate ones. Table 4 provides examples of these affordance translations with indications of the sensemaking processes that triggered the translation in each case.

In several RE interactions, participants started by considering the long-term goals that they could envision the proposed solutions supporting (e.g., receiving information about ideal farming conditions), before shifting toward more immediate goals (e.g., receiving information on how to improve current farming conditions). These translations were triggered by the farmers turning their attention to the challenges of actualizing the prospective affordances, which made them consider more immediate affordances that could help them address those challenges. The challenges included the conditions that needed to be met and the costs that needed to be incurred in order for the prospective affordances to be actualized. They also included farmers’ current circumstances that gave more priority to other goals and actions.

In other RE interactions, participants started by perceiving in the design artifacts more immediate affordances (e.g., tracking pest infestations), before articulating prospective ones (e.g., analyzing the history of pest infestations). These translations were triggered by the farmers elaborating on the consequences of actualizing the immediate affordances. These consequences included new capabilities they might gain from the artifacts, potential patterns of use, and opportunities for changes in practice resulting from the actualization of the immediate affordances. Elaborating on the consequences of actualizing the immediate affordances enabled the participants to go beyond their short-term needs and connect digital technologies to their long-term goals.

## Translations in the Collectivity of Affordances.

The third dimension along which affordance translations manifested in our RE activities was collectivity. Some affordances articulated by the participating farmers emerged from goals and technology uses that were individual in nature, while other affordances reflected collective goals and actions. However, several RE interactions started with articulations of individual affordances before shifting to collective affordances, while other RE interactions started with affordances at the collective level before shifting to the individual level. Table 5 presents examples of these affordance translations with indications of their triggers.

Several RE interactions started with the farmers making sense of the design artifacts in relation to their individual needs, such as the need to book transportation vehicles, find seasonal workers, or plan production. Given the farmers limited familiarity with digital technology, it was easier for them to make sense of its potential in relation to their individual needs, which depended less on understanding the interactional features of the technology. However, in many cases, the participants shifted from individual affordances to perceiving and articulating possibilities of collective technology use in their environment.

These translations were often triggered by the participants considering the limitations of actualizing individual affordances, particularly in contexts that require collective action. The limitations included contextual constraints on making individual use of the technology, such as the high cost of using the app individually to book transportation vehicles or to hire seasonal workers. They also included the potential for negative collective outcomes when technology use is kept at the individual level, such as market losses due to the adoption of similar crops. Making sense of such limitations led the farmers to think beyond individual use to imagine possibilities of use that require the involvement of multiple users.

Other RE interactions started with collective affordances, such as the sharing of pest reports or agricultural information, before shifting to more individual affordances, such as monitoring crops or planning individual production. These translations were triggered by the participants elaborating on the implications of actualizing the collective affordances for their individual capabilities. They considered, for example, what new capabilities they would gain from collective uses of the app, such as its use to report pest infestations, inaccuracies in the archives, or production plans. Attention to these implications triggered the translations from collective to individual affordances by enabling the participants to consider potential individual uses of the technology that only become possible when the collective affordances are actualized.

## Discussion

Our experience designing the MPest and MFarm applications for agricultural communities in India and China enabled us to analyze the sociocognitive processes through which farmers navigate one of the main challenges for digitally marginalized communities to participate effectively in the design process— their lack of familiarity with the prevalent forms of digital technologies. The findings of our design-based interpretive study suggest that this challenge constrains the sensemaking of RE participants as they engage with the design artifacts, which limits the range of affordances that can emerge from their participation in RE activities.

However, our findings also suggest that RE participants often engage in deliberate sensemaking that enables them to attribute meanings, albeit tenuous, to the design artifacts and to consequently perceive in them some initial affordances. In particular, they do so by drawing on their past observations of technology use by others or on their experiences of digital technology in adjacent fields (e.g., finance, healthcare, education) or when a clear action orientation in technology features makes it easier for them to perceive linkages between the technology and their daily practices. More importantly, we found that in several instances, RE participants went beyond these initial affordances by following translations along the dimensions of specificity, temporality, and collectivity. Translations along these dimensions reflected movements around sensemaking circles in which RE participants engaged to overcome their challenges in making sense of the design artifacts. Figure 4 presents these sensemaking circles.

These sensemaking circles are akin to hermeneutic circles (Boland et al., 2010) in which movements between the parts and the whole enable readers to make sense of a text. They enable RE participants to move between the generic and the specific, the immediate and the prospective, and the individual and the collective, as they try to overcome their challenges in making sense of the design artifacts in order to perceive and articulate affordances. These challenges result mostly from the novelty of the technology and the discrepancy between the users’ expectations of the technology and their practices (Griffith, 1999). The affordance translations enable RE participants from digitally marginalized communities to bypass some of these sensemaking challenges, allowing them to participate in the RE process in more effective and meaningful ways. Table 6 presents a summary of the translations, their triggers, the sensemaking challenges that they enable RE participants to overcome, and their effects on RE.

The translations from generic to specific affordances allow RE participants to overcome their difficulties in making sense of specific technology features or in relating them to specific goals, by first articulating affordances that have a low level of specificity and therefore depend less on familiarity with digital technologies. Our findings suggest that RE participants shift to articulating more specific affordances when they elaborate on the value, or lack thereof, of applying the design artifacts in their context. Assessing the value of the initial affordances helps them link features of the technology to elements of their context and thereby generate new meanings that the technology can take in their environment. In so doing, RE participants can give the technology some semblance of familiarity and thereby bypass its novelty (Griffith, 1999). The articulated affordances are consequently more reflective of the participants’ own understanding of the technology, which makes the identified requirements less dependent on significant interpretive leaps by designers. Limiting these interpretive leaps can help reduce the biases that designers bring into the design process (Boland, 1979; Klendauer et al., 2012). It can also make participants’ role in RE more substantial, thereby avoiding the problem of many participative approaches in which participation remains nominal, with no real effect on the design process (Heeks, 1999).

In translations from specific to generic affordances, we found that RE participants move from perceiving specific uses of the technology to articulating broader possibilities of use that reflect more expansive goals. These translations enable the participants to go beyond the specific affordances that their limited experience with the technology suggests. They do so by attending to the boundaries that constrain the actualization of the initial affordances, including limits in the technology features and in their available resources and capabilities. Attention to these boundaries pushes RE participants to transcend the restricted range of practices in which they initially envisage technology use and allows them to consider broader categories of practices as potential areas for the application of technological solutions. These translations broaden RE participants’ technology sensemaking by enabling them to overcome the discrepancy between their limited knowledge of digital technologies and their deep knowledge of their practices and environment. Allowing the two types of knowledge to overlap enables RE participants to identify elements in their practices that are amenable to technological applications (Coughlan & Macredie, 2002). The RE process can thus generate requirements that transcend the limitations of participants’ experiences with technology.

Our findings show that translations from immediate to prospective affordances allow RE participants to move beyond their immediate needs and consider future possibilities of technology use. Given the common intertwining of digital marginalization with socioeconomic marginalization (DiMaggio et al., 2004), RE participants are more likely to make sense of the design artifacts based on their more immediate and pressing needs (Harrington et al., 2019). However, by elaborating on the opportunities and capabilities created by the immediate affordances, RE participants can transcend their initial reactions to the technology and perceive possibilities of action that are oriented toward the future. Given their longer time horizon, prospective affordances offer a basis for identifying more stable requirements. Needs that are significant in the present might not be as pressing in the future, so requirements that build on them can be volatile. While agile methodologies can help mitigate the risk of volatile requirements (Ramesh et al., 2010), the level of repeated engagement they require is not always possible for marginalized communities (Hussain et al., 2012).

We found that translations from prospective to immediate affordances enable RE participants to build on initial affordances that might seem too ambitious for their current conditions to generate affordances that are closer to their immediate needs. RE participants can enact these translations by elaborating on the challenges of actualizing the prospective affordances, including the challenges related to their socioeconomic conditions and the idiosyncrasies of their practices. Such elaborations help them identify intermediate goals that need to be met in order to attain their long-term goals. These more proximal goals enable RE participants to perceive and articulate more immediate affordances, which are more conducive to identifying requirements with a higher likelihood of being directly relevant to the users’ needs (Harrington et al., 2019).

Our findings suggest that translations from individual to collective affordances allow RE participants to start by perceiving simpler individual uses of digital technology, before building on them to consider more complex collective uses. These translations are triggered by participants considering the limitations of individual uses in circumstances that depend on collective action. In most marginalized communities, collective actions are critical for their ability to overcome the constraints of their environment, such as resource scarcity and geographic isolation. Yet the relative complexity of coordinated technology use limits individuals’ ability to make sense of their collective practices as potential areas for the application of digital solutions. However, elaborating on the limitations of individual technology use in attaining collective outcomes can push RE participants to consider the imperative of collective use, allowing them to face the discrepancy between the individualized nature of their experience with technology and the interdependencies of their practices. Translations toward collective affordances reveal requirements that take into account practice conditions in which coordination with others is a matter of necessity rather than choice.

Finally, we found that when RE participants have some understanding of the interactional features of digital technologies, they are able to start their engagement with the design artifacts by relating them to their collective practices, which allows them to conceive of possibilities of collective use. However, the high levels of heterogeneity in digital access and capabilities in their environment make them less likely to perceive what Leonardi (2013) calls shared affordances, reflecting the pursuit of common goals through interdependent patterns of use. They are instead limited to collective affordances, which reflect the pursuit of common goals through diverse patterns of use (Leonardi, 2013). The sharing of pest reports and production plans are examples of such collective affordances, which do not require the participants to make sense of reciprocal coordination features in digital technologies. Translations from these affordances to individual ones are triggered by considering their implications on individual capabilities, which reduces the discrepancy between the need for collective practices and the differences in digital capabilities across the community. These affordance translations help in identifying requirements that account for the heterogeneity in digital capabilities among marginalized communities.

## Theoretical and Practical Contributions

This paper makes three main contributions to the literature. First, it contributes to the literature on digital inequality (DiMaggio & Hargittai, 2001; Hsieh et al., 2008; Kvasny & Keil, 2006; Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015; Wei et al., 2011) and digital inclusion (Madon et al., 2009; Reisdorf & Rhinesmith, 2020; Warschauer, 2004) by theorizing some of the main challenges of including digitally marginalized communities in the design of digital solutions, particularly those related to their lack of familiarity with the prevalent forms of digital technology. In so doing, it helps in moving this literature beyond its current focus on adoption and use (e.g., Hsieh et al., 2008; Kvasny & Keil, 2006; Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015; Venkatesh & Sykes, 2013) to emphasize the need for inclusion in design. In particular, the study shows that inclusion in the design of digital technologies requires a deeper understanding of the sociocognitive processes of user participation in conditions of digital marginalization.

Second, the paper contributes to the requirements elicitation (Davidson, 2002; Jayanth et al., 2010; Rosenkranz et al., 2014) and participatory design (Lukyanenko et al., 2016) literatures by attending to the challenges that RE participants face in making sense of the design artifacts and to relate them to their context. The concept of affordance translation suggests that the intended actions and goals through which users engage with the design artifacts often change as the engagement evolves. By enabling users to perceive and articulate new affordances, affordance translations can make their participation more impactful and broaden the range of requirements that can be identified from their inclusion in the design process.

Past RE research has highlighted the need to attend to similar shifts in technology frame, i.e., the assumptions, expectations, and knowledge through which users understand technology (Davidson, 2002), and in collaborative states—the nature of knowledge transfer and trust between participants and designers (Chakraborty et al., 2010). However, the concept of technology frames focuses on the a priori dispositions of the users, not their direct engagement with the design artifacts. In contrast, the concept of affordances focuses our analytical gaze on the entanglement, in practice, of RE participants’ sensemaking with the design artifacts. It also helps us go beyond the challenges of collaboration and knowledge sharing in RE to theorize the sociocognitive processes that enable collaboration around design artifacts.

Furthermore, in our study, the affordance translations emerged from the post hoc analysis of our RE activities. However, designers who seek to involve digitally marginalized communities in their design process can preplan for and trigger such translations to make user participation more meaningful and RE activities more generative. Past studies have recommended that designers use procedural prompts, which are specifically “intended to overcome cognitive challenges faced by the analyst and user” (Pitts & Browne, 2007, p. 98) (e.g., Can you think of a situation in which you would struggle to use the system?). Procedural prompts have been shown to be more effective in generating wider ranges of requirements compared to substantive prompts, which are aimed at directly eliciting specific requirements (e.g., What would you use the system for?). Consideration of affordance translations suggests that conditions of digital marginalization might require a combination of the two types of prompts; starting with substantive ones to generate initial affordances and following up with procedural prompts to broaden the set of affordances. In general, it suggests the refinement of existing RE methods or the development of new ones in ways that would allow RE participants to move along the sensemaking circles of specificity, temporality, and collectivity, as they seek to perceive and articulate new affordances.

Third, the paper contributes to the affordance literature in three ways. First, it suggests that in the context of IS development, we need to pay as much attention to the articulation of affordances as we do to their actualization, which is typically the focus of analysis in studies of IS use. Second, it extends the stream of research that seeks to theorize the multiplicity of affordances through such concepts as affordance networks (Burton-Jones & Volkoff, 2017) and affordance trajectories (Thapa & Sein, 2017). While these concepts theorize links between distinct affordances, the concept of translation seeks to capture how perceived, articulated, or actualized affordances are transformed throughout the users’ engagement with the technological artifacts (Czarniawska, 2009). It thus presents a view of the emergence of affordances as a dynamic sociocognitive process in which initial affordances can become a basis for the perception of new affordances. Third, while the affordance literature tends to assume the preexistence of the users’ goals as they engage with an artifact, the idea of affordance translations suggests that goals emerge and shift as the user interacts with the artifact.

## Methodological Contributions

The paper makes a methodological contribution by presenting design-based interpretive studies as a novel approach to investigating digital phenomena through design experiences. This approach combines the strengths of interpretive research (Walsham, 2006) and design science research (Hevner et al., 2004) by building on the potential of a design experience to form a basis for an interpretive qualitative study. The immersive and intentional nature of design offers unique opportunities for accessing the meanings through which individuals and groups engage with digital technologies and relate them to their practices and environment. As such, design-based interpretive research enables the development of contextualized theories that account in more depth for the goals, aspirations, and capabilities of technology users. These theories can relate to the process, product, or context of design. In addition, design-based interpretive studies can also use design experiences more broadly as a means to investigate digital phenomena through the participatory engagement of technology users.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we highlight the need for a design approach to digital inclusion and theorize the challenges that the continuing prevalence of digital inequality represents for the design of contextually relevant technologies for and with marginalized communities. We focus on the challenges that users with limited exposure to digital technology face in making sense of design artifacts when participating in RE activities. Our designbased interpretive study with agricultural communities in India and China suggests that affordance translations represent an important means for making RE activities more generative and consequently more inclusive.

## About the Authors

Isam Faik is an assistant professor of information systems at the Ivey Business School, Western University. His research focuses on the relationship between digital transformation and institutional change, including studies on the changing nature of work, digital inequality, and online social movements. His work has appeared in leading information systems journals, including MIS Quarterly, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Information & Organization, and Information Systems Journal. He serves as a senior editor at Information & Organization. He received his Ph.D. in management studies as a Gates scholar from the Judge Business School, University of Cambridge.

Avijit Sengupta is a lecturer of business information systems at the Business School, University of Queensland. His principal research interests include applications of design science research and machine learning in healthcare information technology interventions, digital transformation of higher education, and information communication technology for development (ICT4D). He utilizes randomized controlled trials and a variety of machinelearning and econometric techniques in his research dealing with structured, unstructured, and complex data types. His work has been published in journals including IEEE Transaction of Engineering Management and Journal of Medical Internet Research-Formative Research, and his work won a Best Student Paper award (IS in healthcare track) at ICIS. He earned a Ph.D. in business administration and management from the University of South Florida, Tampa, and a master’s degree from the Indian Institute of Technology, Bombay. He worked as a research assistant at the National University of Singapore, Singapore.

Yimeng Deng is a research engineer at Huawei Technologies. She obtained her Ph.D. from the National University of Singapore. Her research interests include digital transformation, artificial intelligence, digital divide, and value co-creation.
