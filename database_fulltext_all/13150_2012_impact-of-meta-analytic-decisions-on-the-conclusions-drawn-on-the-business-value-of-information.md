## a b s t r a c t

Meta-analysis is a quantitative methodology that allows for summarizing the results of primary research studies in a <sup>fi</sup>eld to provide new insights in terms of the phenomenon observed or the outcomes reported. This paper attempts to answer the fundamental question, “Do methodological decisions in a meta-analytic study affect the conclusions drawn from that study?” Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this paper examines the effects of meta-analytic decisions when applied to the business value of information technology (BVIT) research stream. A closer examination of the variation in the methodological decisions informs us that, with each different decision alternative, we are examining slightly different phenomenon. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal that study outcomes do change, depending on the meta-analytic decisions that are tested. In other words, methodological decisions matter. Based on the data from 99 primary studies and 531 effect sizes, we tested seven hypotheses, in the BVIT research stream, using a comprehensive set of different methodological conditions. We <sup>fi</sup>nd support for two <sup>fi</sup>ndings that are consistent across all the different conditions. First, investing in information technology (IT) is positively associated with the firm's performance We also find that large firms get more benefits from IT than small firms These and the overall <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that researchers should be cognizant of their methodological decisions, as they may be observing the phenomenon under different boundary conditions with different methodologies.

## 1. Introduction

One of the testimonies of progress in an academic <sup>fi</sup>eld is evidenced in the chronicling of achievements that re<sup>fi</sup>ne and accumulate the knowledge, so that we may build on it [21,44]. In the past, researchers employed several methods, including qualitative and quantitative research methods, to accomplish such a mission [17]. Meta-analysis is now one of the most widely used quantitative literature review methods and has drawn the attention of research communities from both the social and the behavioral sciences [18]. The aggregation of the results of individual research studies in a meta-analysis (otherwise known as primary studies) represents an attempt to provide coherent answers to research questions that primary studies in the <sup>fi</sup>eld have sought to address. This is especially important when the nature of a reported relationship between the independent and dependent variables in the primary studies is not consistent. A search of the term “meta analysis” in the Social Science Citation Index (SSCI) reveals 8536 hits, yielding a total 231,631 citations over a period encompassing the last two decades. Most of these studies in it are either meta-analytic studies or cite meta-analytic evidence. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, 37 of these meta-analytic studies address different topics within the information systems (IS) <sup>fi</sup>eld. Further, two-thirds of those studies are recent (post-2000 period), con<sup>fi</sup>rming that meta-analysis is becoming popular in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld [22,25].

Although meta-analysis is of great importance in reviewing the literature, like any research the meta-analyst has to make decisions and judgments that may change the eventual results. These decisions and judgments and their impact on results should be investigated in more detail to have deeper insights. The importance of making a well-informed decision when conducting a meta-analysis includes not only in the process of inclusion and exclusion of primary studies, but also in the aggregation process and the ways in which the statistics (e.g., correlations, beta coef<sup>fi</sup>cients) are used in computing the effect size (a statistic used to standardize individual study <sup>fi</sup>ndings) [17,34]. Such meta-analytic decisions are of critical importance because they may affect the results and conclusions drawn [58]. This may lead researchers to provide empirical support for wrong theories and incorrectly reject theories [9]. In short, given the different choices available to a meta-analyst, it is important to specify these choices and their implications.

The current study has three purposes: First, it shows the impacts of methodological decisions on the results reported and the conclusions drawn from the meta-analysis conducted in this paper. Second, the paper investigates the sensitivity of the hypothesis to variations in methodological decisions and provides research implications for future meta-analytic studies. Third, it tests hypotheses using an updated and comprehensive dataset of studies on business value of information technology (BVIT). Drawing from the BVIT literature, we use meta-analysis procedures to test seven hypotheses regarding (1) the main effect of information technology (IT) investments on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance; (2) the effects of IT investments on different <sup>fi</sup>rm performance measures (productivity and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability); and (3) the moderating effects of <sup>fi</sup>rm size (i.e., small and large <sup>fi</sup>rms), industry type (i.e., service and manufacturing <sup>fi</sup>rms), research design (i.e., longitudinal and cross-sectional), data source (i.e., primary and secondary), and sample size (i.e., large and small) on IT payoffs. The effect size estimates and results are recomputed by using different meta-analytic decisions with regard to (1) inclusion of studies, (2) exclusion of studies, and (3) selection of statistical metaanalysis method. Note that each of the decision alternatives leads to a re-evaluation of the effect size for each of the hypothesis that we examine.

The literature on the impact on outcomes of different decision alternatives while conducting a meta-analysis is sparse and remains an under studied area. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, the contributions by Sharma and Yetton [50,51] and Sharma et al. [52] shed some light on this issue but the issue at large remains an under addressed issue. To our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst paper that is speci<sup>fi</sup>cally devoted to examining the impact of meta-analytic decisions on the strength of the BVIT. Also, the present study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst one in IS <sup>fi</sup>eld to use the same data set to compare the correspondence between theoretically developed constructs and to take an end-to-end process view of the meta-analysis methodology.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The next section provides background information on meta-analysis. Section 3 summarizes the related work in the literature. In Section 4 and Section 5, the basic framework is described in terms of the methodological decisions, study context, BVIT research stream, and hypotheses tested. The subsequent section identi<sup>fi</sup>es the methods and procedures used in the study. After providing information regarding the methods, results and discussions are reported in next section. The <sup>fi</sup>nal section is devoted to the conclusions.

## 2. Background information

Meta-analysis is used to quantitatively aggregate studies [59]. Very simplistically meta-analysis can be interpreted as a kind of survey research in which research reports and papers are surveyed instead of people [34]. Based on prede<sup>fi</sup>ned coding protocols and procedures, a large number of primary studies from a body of literature are “surveyed” (coded) by the coder(s). For a primary study to be eligible for inclusion, it needs to be empirical and have quantitative research <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The studies should examine similar and/or same constructs and relationships and the <sup>fi</sup>ndings should be in comparable or convertible statistical form, called “effect size” [20,34]. A paper may be excluded from a meta-analysis if some form of effect size is not reported in it or is not extractable from the given information [14]. The effect size enables us to statistically combine different primary studies. The correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient (Pearson r‐product moment correlation) is one of the most widely used effect size statistics in research studies [34].

Meta-analysis is carried out for several purposes. First, it is used to integrate knowledge in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c academic <sup>fi</sup>eld. The initial focus in such a case is to evaluate the status of the current knowledge in the <sup>fi</sup>eld [17]. For example, Lin et al. [33] use meta-analysis methodology in order to develop a theoretical framework as part of their multi-method research design. According to Hunter and Schmidt [20], relationships among variables form the “building blocks of a theory.” Meta-analysis provides conclusive empirical evidence for these building blocks.

Second, meta-analysis can capture the variation in procedures used across different primary studies. Such variations stemming from the different decision alternatives encountered during a research study affect the meta-analytic results and the quality of the theories developed from them [3,17,58]. For example, Kohli and Devaraj [27] state that primary studies using primary data source report higher IT payoffs than studies using secondary data source.

## 3. Related work

The role of meta-analytic decisions and their effect on the results and conclusions drawn have been discussed in a few research studies. These studies examined the phenomenon by (1) comparing four pairs of meta-analytic studies [58], (2) comparing three independently and simultaneously conducted meta-analysis studies with respect to certain topics [38,39], (3) examining the impact of meta-analytic decisions and judgments on theory quality and scholarly impact [3,4], and (4) meta-analytically examining the impact of several methodological choices on certain theoretical relationships [15]. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings, which are summarized in Table 1, are con<sup>fl</sup>icting.

Despite not directly examining the impact of methodological deci sions on meta-analytic results, there are several studies that are looselyrelated to the literature. Examples of the topics covered in this literature include but are not limited to the examination of choices of effect sizes [13,41,43], addressing the internal and external threats to validity [50,51], reporting of the methods and results of meta-analysis [11,47], integration and misinterpretation of primary studies with different levels of analysis [40], and detecting and identifying the moderating effects in meta-analysis [48].

## 4. Meta-analytic decisions

The primary purpose of the present study is to examine the impact of meta-analytic decisions on the results and conclusions drawn in the BVIT research stream. The decisions that are examined in the context of the current study include the decisions made during initial (inclusion and exclusion of studies) as well as later (selection of statistical meta-analysis method) stages of the meta-analytic process. The decisions with respect to the inclusion and exclusion of the studies are of vital importance in a meta-analytic study as the sample and number of studies to be used in the data analyses will be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed based on these decisions. The selection of the statistical method to be used in data analysis is also very important as it may cause variations in results.

## 4.1. Inclusion and exclusion of studies

After de<sup>fi</sup>ning the relationship of interest and searching for relevant papers, at the outset, a meta-analyst has to decide which research papers to include in the analysis. Decisions regarding the inclusion of studies available to meta-analysis typically involve either selecting all papers or a subset of them, such as only studies published in journals; selecting studies only from domain (e.g., IS domain journals including MISQ, ISR, JMIS, etc.) journals; selecting studies from top-tier journals (as a measure of rigorous work or as a quality parameter); selecting studies based on a certain time frame (e.g., selecting studies published in the last 15, 10, 5 years, or including all published literature); and selecting studies with certain type of statistics (e.g., correlation, beta coef<sup>fi</sup>cients etc.) [17,34].

Is it appropriate to include all primary studies on the relationship of interest? What is the impact of this decision? Typically, the indoctrination is that a meta-analytic study should include all papers (journal and conference papers, PhD theses, and unpublished manuscripts). Exclusion of papers is often viewed as biasing the results toward the set of papers in the data set [35]. Inadvertently not considering unpublished works, such as theses and conference papers, is commonly referred to as the “<sup>fi</sup>le drawer problem.” [46]. The problem is that by ignoring unpublished studies (that may have non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results), the metaanalyst may inadvertently threaten the internal validity of their study by including a biased sample of primary studies that reported signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results and by excluding studies that reported null results. The metaanalysis may report signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects with the sub-sample of published papers, but when the entire sample (including the unpublished papers) is taken into account the results may not be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant.

An argument can also be made in favor of including only papers published in top-tier journals by questioning the quality of the research and the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of unpublished work. A demand for methodological and theoretical rigor may compel the meta-analyst to analyze only those papers published in top-tier journals to derive conclusions about the phenomenon being examined [8,20,34,58]. Choosing papers published in journals speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the domain of the study has been argued to cause selection bias as it may lead to results different from those in which the study involved all papers, regardless of the domain [17]. Both of these approaches would be forms of the <sup>fi</sup>ledrawer problem mentioned above.

Another criterion for inclusion of papers, especially in a rapidly evolving research stream (e.g., BVIT), could be based on the observation of the phenomenon under different ecological conditions, such as the time frame within which the phenomenon is observed [7,8,17,34,58]. For instance, there are recent primary studies in the BVIT research stream with con<sup>fl</sup>icting <sup>fi</sup>ndings; that is IT has a negative impact on a <sup>fi</sup>rm's performance [6,30,45]. Thus, it is of critical importance to look at the time as an inclusion criterion in a meta-analysis study in order to truly observe the phenomena.

The type of statistic (e.g., correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, beta coef<sup>fi</sup>cients) calculated in the primary studies could also serve as an inclusion criterion, given that aggregation across different types of statistical measures may become problematic. Some meta-analytic studies chose to include beta coef<sup>fi</sup>cients while others did not [54]. Some methods allow researchers to convert t-values of beta coef<sup>fi</sup>cients into correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients [5,46]. These types of conversions have yet to be widely accepted. Therefore, we test whether effect sizes computed by using such different conversion techniques lead us to have divergent or convergent results.

In addition to the conversion techniques, results may also vary based on the outlier analyses used. Outlier analysis typically removes studies that meet the criteria for inclusion, but in which the data characteristics of the paper skew the results and, therefore, obfuscate the understanding of the phenomenon being studied. According to Tukey [55], data sets contain outliers. One of the purposes of a meta-analysis is to reasonably summarize the research outcomes from a body of primary research studies. Extreme values are arguably more likely to distort the results; on this foundation, the virtues of excluding data items (i.e., primary studies) are developed and justi<sup>fi</sup>ed. The type of outlier detection and handling technique may lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variance in the results [15].

From a methodological perspective, the commonly employed mechanisms for outlier detection and handling methods in meta-analysis studies are (1) the sample adjusted meta-analytic deviancy (SAMD)

statistic; (2) Winsorizing; and (3) simple trimming. In recent years, the use of the SAMD statistic,<sup>1</sup> which considers both sample size and effect size, has increased [19]. Winsorizing is another method commonly used to detect outliers, especially when the investigator does not want to drop the studies having an outlier impact. In this technique, the outlier values are simply recoded into more moderate ones (e.g., mean effect size plus or minus two or three standard deviations) [34]. Another approach that is employed by meta-analysts is to simply trim the largest and smallest effect size by some percentage points (typically 5%) [55]. The set of papers included in the meta-analysis may differ depending on the outlier detection method, which is likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the study <sup>fi</sup>ndings to a considerable extent.

## 4.2. Selecting a statistical meta-analysis method

Two types of statistical meta-analysis method are considered in this study: the traditional meta-analysis approach and the logistic regression approach. The most commonly used method, which we refer to as the traditional meta-analysis approach (or alternatively effect size approach), typically follows the guidelines laid out by Hedges and Olkin [18] and Hunter et al. [21]. This method requires the computation of effect size weighted by the sample size for each hypothesis tested [18], and the construction of con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals around these effect size estimates. Moderator analysis is done by splitting the sample into subgroups and computing weighted mean effect sizes [20]. Weighted least squares regression is also used for testing moderator effects [18]. A second option available to researchers is to test hypotheses using some form of regression in conjunction with the vote count approach [16]. Using a logistic regression procedure, the binary dependent variable, which is coded using a vote count approach, is regressed on the categorical and/or continuous independent variables. Such a methodology has been used in meta-analyses of clinical heterogeneity [49], drug effectiveness in cattle [42], and payoffs from IT investments [27]. Given such a wide range of statistical methods used in meta-analytic studies, could the different methods lead to different meta-analytic results?

## 5. Hypotheses tested

The present study focuses on the literature with respect to determining the BVIT while examining the results under the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of different methodological decision alternatives. The BVIT term refers to “the organizational performance impacts of IT, including productivity enhancements, pro<sup>fi</sup>tability improvement, cost reduction, competitive advantage, inventory reduction, and other measures of performance” [36]. The impact of IT on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance and competitive advantage has been extensively examined in the literature in the past three decades [1,2,12,37,53,57]. Such research on BVIT has emphasized the role of IT (e.g., hardware and software components of the system), the management of IT, and the capabilities provided by IT on any type of economic impact it creates in <sup>fi</sup>rms. This issue has been addressed in various industries and in <sup>fi</sup>rms varying in both size and operating environment. Overall, there is a consensus that IT creates business value, whether it is <sup>fi</sup>nancial, intermediate, or affective [29].

Based on reviewing the past literature as well as the review studies on the topic, we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed seven hypotheses. These hypotheses are the most commonly studied issues in the BVIT research stream. In the process of selecting the hypotheses to be examined in this paper, we attached importance to whether the hypotheses can be meta-analytically examined. To do so, we have reviewed the literature with respect to the data required to test the hypotheses. Hypotheses that could be tested with a statistical power have been selected to be included in this study. Table 2 shows the hypotheses and the relevant references.

Hypothesis 1 examines the main effect of IT on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. The remaining hypotheses capture the moderating effects observed in IT payoff literature, such as type of performance measures (Hypothesis 2), <sup>fi</sup>rm size (Hypothesis 3), industry type (Hypothesis 4), research design (Hypothesis 5), data source (Hypothesis 6), and sample size (Hypothesis 7). The table presents each hypothesis as well as the reference studies from the prior literature which talks about and tests the hypothesized relationship.

## 7. Results and discussion

## 7.1. Impact of inclusion and exclusion criteria

When the inclusion criteria is varied based on the type of journal (type also embodies, in a sense, quality attributes), we see that three of the seven hypotheses (Hypotheses 2, 5, and 7) are not consistent across the range of possible decisions that the meta-analyst makes in different studies. The results presented in Table 3 show that (1) Hypothesis 1 and Hypothesis 3 are supported regardless of the choice of journal type and (2) Hypotheses 4 and 6 are not supported in all cases. An interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding is that when the inclusion criterion permits consideration of only IS journals, the outcomes indicate that there is not a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between (a) studies with longitudinal and cross-sectional data, and (b) studies with large and small samples. On the other hand, the results show that there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating impact of study design and sample size on IT–<sup>fi</sup>rm performance relationship, when all other decision alternatives regarding the type of journal are considered.

Primary studies often report correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients between the variables of interest. In the BVIT research stream, however, some primary studies, which use a production function and regression, only report beta coef<sup>fi</sup>cients. Typically, traditional meta-analyses compute an effect size using correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients at the peril of excluding studies that report purely beta coef<sup>fi</sup>cients. The recent advances in this area stem from the work of Aloe [5] and Rosenthal [46]. In contrast, the decision to include or exclude studies reporting beta coef<sup>fi</sup>cients affects Hypotheses 4 and 5, indicating that the mechanics of the conversion of beta coef<sup>fi</sup>cients does in fact matter. The process of beta coef<sup>fi</sup>cient conversions to correlations, therefore, needs to be established more <sup>fi</sup>rmly, and we suggest further research in this area.

In contrast, when SAMD is employed as a mechanism for excluding outliers, conclusions drawn do not support this hypothesis, bringing the validity of the outlier detection into question. The counter-question of the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of using other mechanisms or using no mechanism at all is one for further investigation. Fig. 2 graphically depicts the studies excluded as a consequence of applying the various outlier detection mechanisms. This, in turn, imposes different mathematical <sup>fi</sup>lters based upon the values of the effect and sample sizes. Table 7 provides details about these studies.

SAMD excludes primary studies that typically have both large effect sizes and sample size but, at times, have had a smaller effect size and an extremely large sample size. If, however, it is evident that these excluded primary studies have been conducted in observance of good methodological rigor and have been published in top tier journals, the results published in such studies may be held in higher regard. Excluding such studies by the mechanical application of the SAMD process, therefore, may not re<sup>fl</sup>ect the true phenomenon nor would the results of the meta-analysis capture what is truly happening in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. A careful analysis of the excluded studies, in terms of their attributes, must be conducted prior to any such exclusion. Studies with extreme effect size values may not be excluded by SAMD if the sample sizes are lower. But these studies have a higher probability of exclusion when Winsorizing or simple trimming are used as outlier techniques. Such extreme values, however, may represent special contingent conditions (example, <sup>fi</sup>rms operating in special environments). Exclusion of primary studies by a mechanical application of Winsorizing or simple trimming, therefore, may result in excluding (inadvertently in this case) the impact of the relationships that speak to those contingent conditions. The danger with any exclusion is that the results of meta-analytic study may not re<sup>fl</sup>ect excluded studies that are more rigorous and well founded. Much of the prior meta-analytic studies have not explicitly justi<sup>fi</sup>ed the exclusion of the primary studies beyond the mechanical application of the outlier techniques. We therefore recommend that, as a meta-analytic process, after the application of outlier detection techniques, the attributes of the excluded primary studies should be examined in detail and a decision on their actual exclusion or retention should be made on a more holistic basis instead of a mechanical implementation of the process.

Based on the frequency of the hypotheses being ‘not consistent’ across different decisions shown in Table 9, we created Fig. 3, which calibrates the hypotheses on a continuum to provide a sense of their sensitivity level. This continuum shows that the results of some hypothesis tests may remain consistent no matter what meta-analytic decisions you make (or robust), while the results from some hypotheses tests may differ depending on the meta-analytic decisions made (or sensitive).

Based on the results obtained with the various meta-analytic decisions, it is clear that Hypothesis 5 (moderating effect of study design) is the most sensitive to decisions made by the meta-analyst (inconsistent results in three out of <sup>fi</sup>ve meta-analytic decision criteria). In other words, the meta-analytic result from the moderating effect of study design (longitudinal and cross-sectional) on IT payoffs differed depending on the meta-analytic decision criteria we selected. By comparison, Hypothesis 1 (IT's overall impact on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance) and Hypothesis 3 (moderating effect of <sup>fi</sup>rm size) are far more stable and robust. For example, our results regarding the moderating impact of <sup>fi</sup>rm size (small <sup>fi</sup>rms and large <sup>fi</sup>rms) on IT payoffs did not differ in four out of <sup>fi</sup>ve meta-analytic decision criteria alternatives we investigated (see Table 9). Thus, across all these different conditions, the consistent <sup>fi</sup>nding was that large <sup>fi</sup>rms gain more from IT than small <sup>fi</sup>rms. In a sense, the invariance of the support (or lack thereof) for the hypotheses is indicative of the robustness of the hypotheses to changing conditions. This speaks to the believability and the reliability of the results and their conclusions. Therefore, we suggest that, in the future, analysis on the impact of methodological decisions on results should be done in regard to the hypotheses tested.

## 8. Conclusion

An over-arching conclusion that emerges from all of the results of our study is that meta-analytic decisions do matter—that is, metaanalytic decisions do change outcomes. These outcomes have domain implications in terms of discovery of new relationships and trends. There are, however, some relationships that remain invariant across different decision alternatives. This invariance may be re<sup>fl</sup>ective of the basic nature of the phenomenon (i.e., BVIT) and its robustness.

Although it is not the main focus of the current paper, an initial observation from our research <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveals that, no matter how the BVIT data is sliced and diced, the value from IT investments is ‘positive’ in all cases examined in this study. The positive effect of IT investments on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance has largely been investigated and validated through numerous primary studies and a number of review studies in the past three decades. However, the current study meta-analytically and methodologically shows that IT's impact on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance is positive in various conditions. We also show that <sup>fi</sup>rm size moderates the relationship between IT and <sup>fi</sup>rm-level outcomes, and this result is robust in almost all of the cases. Thus, we conclude that BVIT phenomenon has different implications for small and large <sup>fi</sup>rms. Finally, the results from the hypotheses relating to the moderating effect of type of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance measures (i.e., productivity and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability) and industry type (i.e., service and manufacturing) show certain level of sensitivity to meta-analytic decisions. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are worth to consider in future BVIT research studies.

In terms of the main focus of the current study, the results show that more theoretically founded hypotheses, such as the main effect hypothesis (Hypothesis 1: Investing in IT is positively associated with the <sup>fi</sup>rm's performance) and the moderating effect of <sup>fi</sup>rm size (Hypothesis 3: Firm size has a moderating effect on the IT investments—<sup>fi</sup>rm performance relationship) are highly robust to different methodological decisions. Hypotheses relating to methodological processes in the primary studies, such as the operationalizations of the dependent variables (Hypothesis 2: productivity and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability measures), industry type (Hypothesis 4: service and manufacturing), study design (Hypothesis 5: cross-sectional and longitudinal design), data source (Hypothesis 6: primary and secondary data source), and sample size (Hypothesis 7: small and large samples), show greater sensitivity to the decisions made. Of these, the issues with respect to the study design and data source are of greater signi<sup>fi</sup>cance in BVIT research since (a) recent studies claim the importance of longitudinal data in measuring the true value of IT investments, and (b) the data quality is able to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the IT value measurement in primary studies [27]. Therefore, the issue of what type of study design and what data source is optimum should be further addressed in future research endeavors.

We also conclude that, by selecting one methodological decision alternative over others, researchers are likely to draw different conclusions. The variation in the inclusion criteria may allow us to study the phenomenon under different conditions. Meta-analytic inclusion criteria may be varied so that the data reveal micro-trends and further the analysis which may allow us to observe new phenomena in the domain.

From a more methodological perspective, the technique used to analyze the data may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the results obtained. Our study suggests that meta-analysts should report on all of the steps that are followed in arriving at their conclusions and should explicitly link the goals of the meta-analysis to the meta-analytic decisions made. A detailed examination of the attributes of studies excluded by the mechanical application of outlier detection techniques may inform us of the loss or gain from excluding the individual studies. We therefore recommend that meta-analysis studies should examine the characteristics of primary studies (e.g., effect size, sample size, quality of the paper, etc.), prior to excluding them, subsequent to the application of outlier detection technique. This study also suggests, as a fail-safe mechanism to the meta-analytic process, that justi<sup>fi</sup>cation supporting the exclusion of primary studies be provided which goes beyond the mechanical application of outlier detection techniques in order to improve the con<sup>fi</sup>- dence in the reported results.

Our study also demonstrated that different hypotheses show different levels of sensitivity to variations in methodological decisions. The strength of the relationships can perhaps be used to create a sensitivity index. Further development of the sensitivity index process for use in meta-analysis is beyond the scope of this paper; we leave it as a topic for future research.

Because reporting on all possible combinations of inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as analysis techniques, would be prohibitively expensive in terms of the length and the focus of this paper, we have constrained our reporting to results using all papers (journal, conference papers, and theses), except when the focus of the analysis was to vary the inclusion criteria. The gain derived from controlling for the research stream (i.e., BVIT) allows us to be more focused in our conclusion and results and it also serves as a limitation; future research might potentially test the impact of meta-analytic decisions in other research streams and areas.

Given that meta-analytic decisions do affect outcomes, we recommend that future meta-analysis studies report the decisions that are made at every stage of the process. Such transparency enhances the reproducibility of the results, which in turn provides more con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the reported results. Varying the meta-analytic decisions may, at times, provide greater insight into the phenomenon being examined. This new knowledge may reveal micro-trends or newer trends that help improve our understanding on the subject.

## Appendix A. Meta-analytic procedures

## 1. Paper search and coding process

In order to ensure an exhaustive collection of eligible papers, bibliographies of prior literature reviews have been examined, and an online database and website search have been performed by using combinations of BVIT related keywords. Firm level studies examining the IT investments–Firm performance association have been selected for inclusion in data analyses. Papers with correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients as well as other types of statistics that can be converted into correlations are selected to be a part of the sample. The search process yielded 99 primary studies (101 samples) published between 1983 and 2008. The sample is composed of journal papers (62), dissertations (18), conference papers (9), book chapter (1), and other papers (9).

Two coders cataloged the entire set of papers and their codes (for the variables speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the following table) were compared. Table A-1 provides an exemplar of the variables. The initial level of agreement between the coders was 95%. A subsequent discussion resulted in resolution of the disagreements and a complete agreement (100%) was achieved at the end of the process.

## 2. Meta-analysis methodologies

In the traditional meta-analysis approach, only <sup>fi</sup>rm-level studies reporting the bivariate correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients (Pearson r‐product moment correlation) between IT investment and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance were included. Studies that reported exclusively regression-based outcomes or other statistical and analytical outcomes were excluded. The effect size and the sample size were coded for each paper. For the main effect hypothesis (Hypothesis 1), all of the relevant effect sizes in the given study were averaged. Before taking the average of the effect sizes, the effect sizes were converted into Fisher's z value. After taking the average, resulting z value was converted back into the effect size [46].

After effect size and sample size coding, the weighted effect size for each hypothesis was computed according to <sup>fi</sup>xed effects model, which assumes that all studies in the sample come from the same population. Thus, the effect size in each study is weighted by within-study variance (or inverse variance weight). For the moderating effects (Hypotheses 2 through 7), the sample was split into subgroups and then the weighted mean effect size was computed for each group. Then Z-statistic is computed to test the moderating effect. If the Z-value is greater than 1.96, we concluded that the difference was statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at pb0.05.

In the logistic regression with a vote count approach, the studies reporting statistics other than correlations (e.g., beta coef<sup>fi</sup>cients from the regression analysis) have been included. Coding of the effect sizes was relatively easier in this case because only whether the study reported positive results was coded. If a study reported statistics (e.g., correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients and regression coef<sup>fi</sup>cients) with all positive coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, then it was coded as “1” for the dependent variable (i.e., <sup>fi</sup>rm performance) (or “0” otherwise). As covariates of the logistic regression analysis, subgroups were coded as covariates that are categorical. Finally, logistic regression analysis was conducted. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cant t-value of beta coef<sup>fi</sup>cient leads us to conclude that the hypothesis is supported.
