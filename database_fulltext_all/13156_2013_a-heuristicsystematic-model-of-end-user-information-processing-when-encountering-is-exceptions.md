# A heuristic–systematic model of end-user information processing when encountering IS exceptions

Joshua M. Davis <sup>a,</sup>\*, Brad M. Tuttle <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Marketing and Supply Chain Management, School of Business, College of Charleston, 66 George Street, Charleston, SC 29424, USA <sup>b</sup> Department of Accounting, Moore School of Business, University of South Carolina, 1705 College Street, Columbia, SC 29208, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

## A B S T R A C T

IS exception management often relies on end-users for success. A key aspect of end-user management of IS exceptions is sufficient information processing before responding. However, end-users process information dynamically across different judgmental contexts and sometimes favor effort reduction over accuracy maximization. With today’s IS tightly meshed with underlying organizational business processes, inappropriate end-user responses pose organizational risks. We therefore developed a theorybased research model which was designed to uncover the motivational mechanisms needed to provide effective information processing in this context. Empirical testing using 318 experienced users of Microsoft Excel supported our model.

## 1. Introduction

We know little about how individuals interact with IS [2,3,15]. A primary reason for this is the fact that researchers have placed emphasis on the amount of IS use rather than the nature of its use. Consequently, there has been a suggestion that we need an expanded behavioral view of IS use that considers activities and features commonly encountered during use of the IS: a particularly important facet of this, which has received little attention, is enduser management of IS exceptions. These are extraordinary information processing situations that cannot be handled routinely by the IS [16]; they are an almost-inevitable part of system use [8].

With organizational IS tied to the business processes of the firm [14], IS exceptions can be serious vulnerabilities, to which inappropriate end-user reaction can impede business processes and/or lead to economic loss. An IS exception is generally unique to the application, data, and processing task involved; thus there is no standard response to it. Furthermore, IS exceptions frequently are discovered by the end-user in a manner far removed from the initial cause [10], making their management difficult, even in relatively simple applications. Thus, sound management of IS exceptions requires human judgment, which often requires information processing prior to responding [9].

Researchers and practitioners have worked to establish best practices in software design for alerting users when an exception has occurred and equipping them with diagnostic resources. However, theory and past research on behavioral judgment formation see judgment formation as a dynamic and complex process in which individuals change their information processing strategies to minimize the costs of cognitive effort and judgment inaccuracy. Individuals often attempt to reduce their effort rather than maximize accuracy when processing information. End-user bias toward effort reduction can result in habitual patterns of responding and treating IS exceptions as something to ignore, by clicking ‘OK’ and hoping the situation will disappear [1]. Thus, system alerts and supplementary resources are important but insufficient to promote effective end-user behavior.

Work is therefore needed to determine the motivational mechanisms behind end-user information processing. We decided to investigate the motivational mechanisms that underpin effective information processing when IS exceptions occur. Overall, we addressed the question:

What motivates end-users’ effortful information processing when encountering IS exceptions?

## 2. Theoretical foundation

## 2.1. The heuristic–systematic model of information processing

The heuristic–systematic model (HSM) of information processing is a dual-process theory that addresses information processing during judgment formation [13]. Dual-process theories argue that individuals are often engaged in validity-seeking contexts in which they try to understand the situation [11]; they also assume that judgment formation involves two different types of information processing (implicit and explicit) requiring different degrees of cognitive effort. We chose HSM as the theoretical basis for our study for two reasons. First, it was designed to be applicable to a wider range of validity-seeking contexts and information processing activities than other dual-process theories [17]. Thus studies have used it to understand human information processing in several information adoption situations: online communities, videoconferencing, and inter-organizational collaboration. Second, HSM applies directly to communication contexts in which individuals are told about a situation in which they, colleagues, and events, must make a decision or formulate judgment. Thus HSM is particularly suited to the IS exception context, where task, user, and social factors affect the decision making process.

HSM argues that individuals can use two distinct informationprocessing modes when making judgments: systematic or heuristic processing. Systematic processing is information intensive and analytically oriented. Heuristic processing focuses on easily acquired and processed information with simple decision rules. When systematically processing information, decisionmakers access and scrutinize various information sources for relevance and importance in the decision before using it.

HSM further asserts that decision-makers prefer low-effort processing. They try to strike a balance between minimizing effort and maximizing judgmental confidence. Thus, one of the most important questions for any dual-process model relates to the triggering conditions for a given processing mode. This was termed satisficing by Newell in the work resulting in his Nobel Prize.

Information sufficiency is the difference between the amount of information an individual thinks he or she needs to make an accurate decision and the amount of information currently held. Motivation to obtain additional information increases when information sufficiency is not achieved. Thus, processing effort can be viewed as a function of the amount of discrepancy between actual and desired confidence.

## 2.2. HSM, information sufficiency, and IS exception management

While the sufficiency principle can be used to predict systematic as well as heuristic processing modes, we were mainly concerned with systematic processing in our study. Therefore, our application of HSM centered on the relationship between information sufficiency and systematic processing. In addition to this relationship, HSM suggests that other factors can influence systematic information processing through their direct influence on desired and/or actual confidence. In the IS exception management context, three categories of factors motivate processing: the exception message provided to the user, the computing task being performed at that time, and the user who must respond.

## 3. The conceptual model

Our conceptual model (Fig. 1) shows desired confidence and actual confidence as the direct determinants of systematic information processing. In addition, the model shows message-, task-, and user-related factors as the indirect antecedents of systematic information processing through their direct influence on the confidence variables.

## 3.1. Message-related factors

## 3.1.1. Perceived message severity

Because exception messaging conveys important information about the situation, the information provided should bear important influence on information sufficiency. One primary way users tend to evaluate exception messages relates to the severity of the situation, as communicated by the system [1]. The literature generally agrees that perception of risk in a particular situation directly impacts the individual’s desired level of judgmental confidence in that situation [7]. Translated to the context of an IS exception, when the severity of the exception communicated to the user is high, we expect heightened awareness about the consequences of responding improperly, which in turn increases desired confidence.

H1. Perceived message severity will increase desired confidence.

## 3.1.2. Message ambiguity

Message clarity/ambiguity is identified in the HSM literature as an important factor in information processing. When informing the decision-maker, ambiguous information often leads to greater uncertainty, which creates lack of understanding and ultimately spent time thinking and seeking out additional information. For example, an error message that simply reads ‘‘A critical system error has occurred’’ is likely to result in uncertainty on the part of the end-user, reducing actual confidence about how to recover. On the other hand, when communication about the exception is clear and detailed, the receiver is more likely to view it as sufficient and credible, increasing his/her actual confidence in forming an accurate judgment [12]. Framed in HSM and the IS exception context, message ambiguity will reduce information sufficiency by decreasing actual confidence.

H2. Message ambiguity will decrease actual confidence.

## 3.2. Task-related factors

## 3.2.1. Task importance

Task importance also has been emphasized as an important driver of systematic processing in the HSM literature. In the context of IS exceptions, when the computing task associated with the exception is highly consequential, users should require higher levels of confidence about how to proceed. For example, desired confidence should generally be higher for IS exceptions generated during computing tasks involving critical data processing, as compared to more superficial computing tasks such as general formatting. To the extent that the computing task associated with the exception is considered important, HSM suggests that desired confidence will be heightened.

H3. The perceived importance of the specific task that gave rise to the exception message will increase desired confidence.

## 3.3. User-related factors

## 3.3.1. Subjective norm

With its roots in the theory of planned behavior, subjective norm is the degree to which an individual believes that people who are important to her/him think she/he should perform the behavior in question. In the HSM literature, subjective norm is considered influential in information processing by motivating the individual to adopt attitudes which satisfy interpersonal goals, such as appearing intelligent. Translated to IS exception management, we expect subjective norm to positively influence the standards an individual places on managing the situation, which manifest as increased desired confidence.

H4. Organizational social norms will increase desired confidence.

3.3.2. Computer self-efficacy

The IS literature on human factors generally agrees that technology-specific dispositional differences can play a role in how users engage technology [5]. One factor shown to influence user engagement with technology is computer self-efficacy. Rooted in social cognitive theory, computer self-efficacy is defined as an individual’s perception of efficacy in performing computer-related tasks within the general domain of computing. In the IS exception context, higher levels of computer self-efficacy should increase the user’s actual confidence about how to manage the exception.

H5. Computer self-efficacy will increase actual confidence.

## 3.4. Information sufficiency

## 3.4.1. Desired confidence and actual confidence

When managing IS exceptions, the sufficiency principle suggests that information sufficiency regarding how to proceed will determine subsequent systematic information processing. Motivation to engage in information processing is a function of the discrepancy between the person’s desired confidence and actual confidence for a specific judgment task. As the gap between desired and actual confidence becomes larger, the motivation to systematically process information will become stronger. Taking a decomposed view of this understanding of information sufficiency, desired confidence should have a positive impact on systematic processing as higher levels of desired confidence close the gap, all else equal. Following the same rationale, actual confidence should have a negative impact on systematic processing.

H6. Desired confidence will have a positive impact on systematic information processing.

H7. Actual confidence will have a negative impact on systematic information processing.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Study context and sampling frame

The substantive context we chose to empirically validate the model was using Microsoft’s spreadsheet software: Microsoft Excel<sup>TM</sup>. Spreadsheets are among the most common decisionsupport tools in practice. A survey instrument was generated, focused specifically on the context of exception conditions encountered while using Excel. The sampling frame for our study consisted of practicing management accountants in several industries and organizations. This group was well-versed in Microsoft Excel and its use.

Respondents were recruited via an invitation in a regularly scheduled emailing to members of the Institute of Management Accountants (IMA). We offered a \$10 Exxon Mobile gas card to each of the first 200 participants. Following the 200th response, the survey was augmented with a startup screen informing potential participants that the incentive was no longer available. A total of 409 individuals opened the study by going to an on-line survey service (http://qualtrics.com) and 318 completed the survey for a response rate of 78%. Table 1 shows some of the participants demographic information. Overall, the typical participant was 40 years old, had four or more years of both college and work experience, and considered him or herself to be experienced in using Excel.

First, specific instances of typical exception conditions encountered while using Microsoft Excel were selected from among the comprehensive library of all Excel exceptions, as cataloged in the Microsoft Excel 2007 Solution Center available at the Microsoft online support site. After filtering, a random starting point on the list was chosen and every 20th message was selected, resulting in a list of 10 exceptions for the pretesting phase.

Second, we asked 18 regular users of Microsoft Excel to rate the perceived risk of each exception condition using a seven-point Likert scale anchored by 1 = Very Low Risk and 7 = Very High Risk along five dimensions – inability to recover data changes; inability to execute important actions; possibility of corrupt software; possibility of corrupt hardware; and inability of a user to fix the issue. These dimensions are consistent with Microsoft’s guidance to programmers on how to determine whether an exception condition is sufficiently critical to warrant a message to the user. The results of this pretest indicated that all ten exception conditions were perceived to be at least moderately serious. From the list of ten, four exceptions were chosen to represent typical workplace tasks – starting the application; manipulating data; modifying the application; and saving changes. Our plan was that each participant in the final sample would be randomly assigned one of the four exceptions to reference when responding to the survey; this helped us ensure that the results were not anchored to a single, specific exception. An illustration of the survey design used for each of the four exceptions is provided in Appendix A.

Third, the survey instrument was developed; it incorporated measures for the latent constructs in the research model. Where possible, existing scales were incorporated directly into the instrument or adapted to fit the IS exception context. All other scales were developed and pretested for convergent and discriminant validity prior to administering the study to the accountants. The measurement items associated with each construct are also shown in Appendix A.

## 5. Data analysis and results

Given the theory-building nature of our study, PLS estimation techniques using SmartPLS version 2.0.M3 were used to assess the measurement and structural models. PLS uses regressionbased techniques to analyze structural models with multi-item latent constructs [6] and were appropriate for theory development [4], but it should have at least 10 times as many data points as the maximum number of indicators. Our data met this requirement.

The psychometric properties of the measures were assessed by examining internal consistency reliabilities, and convergent and discriminant validity. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using composite reliability scores. As shown in Table 2, all the constructs in the model exceeded the suggested cutoff of 0.70 for composite reliability, indicating adequate internal consistency reliability.

Analysis of convergent and discriminant validity was assessed by confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Convergent validity was demonstrated because each of the measurement items loaded significantly (p < 0.05) on its intended latent construct. See Table 3.

A stronger test of convergent validity was provided by examining the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE). The AVE values of each of the constructs shown in Table 4 and exceed 0.707. Discriminant validity was assessed by determining whether the AVE value for each latent construct was larger than the inter-construct correlations. As also shown in Table 4, the results indicated good reliability and discriminant validity of the measures. Overall, the results of testing indicated that the psychometric properties of the measurement model were sufficient for valid testing of our structural model.

Assessment of the structural model was accomplished by examining standardized path coefficients and the variance explained in the dependent constructs, as derived from a bootstrapping procedure. As illustrated in Fig. 2, all of the study’s hypotheses were confirmed. The antecedent factors in the model account for 33% of the variance in desired confidence levels and 30% of actual confidence levels and all path coefficients were in the predicted direction. Furthermore, the information sufficiency components – desired confidence and actual confidence – accounted for 22% of the variation in systematic processing. Importantly, analysis using the gap in confidence, as measured by desired confidence less actual confidence, produced qualitatively similar findings with no differences in interpretation. Overall, the results of model testing supported HSM and the sufficiency principle in predicting end-users’ information processing intentions when encountering IS exceptions.<sup>2</sup>

## 6. Discussion

The results of our study provide important implications. For researchers, prior work on IS exception management has suggested that effectively mitigating the risk of IS exceptions on business performance is a matter of technical design principles and providing end-users with supplementary information resources. The findings from our study offer a more nuanced and human-centered view of effective IS exception management which recognizes that human judgment formation is a dynamic and complex process whereby end-users seek to strike a compromise between information processing effort and judgmental accuracy. Our perspective argues that motivated end-user information processing is at the core of effective IS exception management.

Our theoretical model and results highlight the primary role of information sufficiency in determining how an end-user will respond to an IS exception. The effects of desired and actual confidence on systematic processing lend strong support for the efficacy of HSM and the sufficiency principle to explain differences in how end-users react to IS exceptions. The strong effects of perceived severity and message ambiguity on desired and actual confidence point to the important role that messaging plays in shaping end-user behavior. Our findings provide new understanding of the process through which messaging influences user behavior: users establish an initial assessment of exception severity based on immediately available information. This then drives subsequent information processing through its influence on desired confidence about how to proceed. Our results also highlighted message ambiguity, which may influence end-users to process information in a more systematic fashion. Messages that end-users find ambiguous may not always produce negative outcomes, as low levels of ambiguity may actually create an illusion of confidence.

Our results suggest that task importance influences a user’s desired confidence about effectively using an IS to complete the task, leading to more systematic information processing when encountering IS exceptions. Our study also contributes by shedding light on the deeper social and cognitive dimension of IS exception management. Specifically, our results indicated that subjective norms play a significant role in how users respond to IS exceptions, through its influence on desired confidence. Finally, the significant positive effect of computer self-efficacy on actual confidence may actually point to a potentially negative side-effect of increased enduser confidence with an IS. High levels of computer self-efficacy can lead to inflated levels of actual confidence about how to proceed, which ultimately results in less systematic information processing prior to responding to IS exceptions.

For practitioners, our study has implications for IS design and management of end-user computing for mitigating problems rising from misunderstanding IS exceptions. Microsoft’s MSDN library provides only the following guidance to system designers regarding the communication of exception severity:

‘‘Choose standard icons based on the message type, not the severity of the underlying issue. The message types are: (1) Error—an error or problem that has occurred, (2) Warning—a condition that might cause a problem in the future, and (3) Information—useful information. . .While severity isn’t a consideration when choosing among the error, warning, and information icons, severity is a factor in determining if a standard icon should be used at all.’’

A much more explicit set of guidelines for communicating severity through exception messaging is necessary for effectively guiding end-users toward the appropriate response.

In addition, the relationship between task importance and desired confidence highlights a novel conceptual lever available for exception messaging, in order to promote appropriate levels of information processing prior to responding. Given that IS exceptions are frequently reported to the end-user in a manner that is far removed from the initial cause, including specific information related to the criticality of the computing task involved can help convey the necessary level of caution to take prior to responding.

For managers, the influence of task importance on desired confidence suggests that educating the users on the importance of certain computing tasks could promote more cautious end-user behavior when unexpected processing situations arise. By communicating the potentially critical nature of IS exceptions and the importance of effectively managing these situation, managers may improve end-user computing across exceptions and applications. A potentially effective strategy for promoting sound end-user behavior may be to counterbalance growing levels of computer self-efficacy with increased communication about the importance of this aspect of end-user computing, in effect cultivating a social norm of careful exception management as users gain experience and confidence with an IS.

## 7. Limitations

Our study used a small subset of exception conditions tied to a relatively simple IS. While steps were taken to ensure that the subset was representative of exceptions encountered in the workplace, the results must be generalized with this limitation in mind. Other, more complex IS may present more complicated computing tasks and IS exceptions. Also, the sampling frame for our study involved a relatively homogenous group of business professionals who relied heavily on IS and spreadsheets to complete their work. While the demographic characteristics of the sampling frame suggest that this group had experience with the target system, the results may not generalize to the end points of novice or expert users. Also, this study was cross-sectional in nature.

## 8. Conclusion

Our study offers a unique, user-centered perspective of IS exception management. Acknowledging the integral role of the enduser in successfully recovering from IS exceptions, and drawing on the heuristic-systematic model of information processing, we have brought together a variety of factors that may be intuitive but have not previously been incorporated into a single unifying model of end-user information processing in the IS exception management context. Model testing via 318 experienced workplace users of Microsoft Excel provided support for all of our hypotheses.
