## A B S T R A C T

The relationship between a doctor and a patient plays an important role in patient satisfaction with health-care services. It has become an important and contentious issue in China’s health-care reform. This study uses service fairness as a theoretical lens to investigate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence an implementation of a patient-accessible hospital information system (HIS) has on doctor–patient relationships and patient satisfaction. We developed a research model that relates patient-accessible HIS implementation with perception of service fairness, doctor–patient relationships, and patient satisfaction. Data were collected from patients in one of the biggest hospitals in East China that has implemented patient-accessible HIS. Results of the study show that patient-accessible HIS promotes patients’ perception of service fairness, improves doctor–patient relationships, and increases patient satisfaction.

## 1. Introduction

Despite the fast pace of economic development in China, health safety, and national social security systems have developed relatively slowly. The health-care reforms in China were introduced to address long-term problems of low ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and inequity in the health system and to provide “universal healthcare by 2020.” Based on the literature and publicly available data, Wang et al. [1] found that many problems, such as a laggard system of community doctors, poorly designed payment schemes, and lack of patient trust, inhibit health-care reform in China. Based on a 3-year appraisal of China’s health-care reform, launched in 2009, Yip et al. [2] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the need for further reform in incentive structures for providers, improved governance of public hospitals, and a stronger regulatory system. These health-care reforms have certain effects on the quality improvement of health care service, but the situation of tense doctor–patient relationships has not been radically unchanged yet. On the one hand, patients complain that they do not get appropriate services and their interests may be harmed. On the other hand, doctors complain that they have to bear the pressure from various social and economic aspects of health care while providing care. These con<sup>fl</sup>icts might be caused by the lack of information transparency, untimely information transfer, or lack of service awareness of medical staff. From the perspective of information asymmetry, patients may perceive an information disadvantage during the hospital treatment process. Hospitals and medical staff generally have information superiority, which may be detrimental to patient care and may result in patients providing inaccurate and misleading information.

Ma [3], China’s Vice Minister of Health, reported that new health-care reform priorities include improving the quality and safety of care and increasing patient satisfaction. Patient satisfaction has been widely accepted as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant indicator for measuring the quality of health care and a critical component of performance [4]. Due to the important role of patient satisfaction, researchers have studied the impact of information technology (IT) adoption on patient satisfaction and the results show that IT can enhance service quality and increase patient satisfaction [5,6].

Patient satisfaction is an important concern in China. In order to safeguard the vital interests of the patient, hospitals are promoting patient-oriented information systems (ISs) and information disclosure, making medical procedures and fees more transparent [7]. Cheng et al. [8] found that the doctor’s caring attitude, interpersonal skills, explanation of treatment, technical skills, hospital equipment, and clinical competence have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive impact on patient satisfaction and outcome.

Services differ from products in that they are intangible, heterogeneous, often produced and consumed simultaneously, and require customer participation during the service delivery process [9]. In the case of health-care services, even a slight negligence may cause patients to feel that they have been treated unfairly and may cause discontent. Patients’ perceptions of health service quality might shape their con<sup>fi</sup>dence and subsequent behaviors with regard to choice and usage of the available healthcare facilities [10]. Boulding et al. [11] found that after adjusting for clinical quality, patient satisfaction was associated with lower 30- day risk-adjusted hospital readmission rates. Studies have found that the major determinants of patient satisfaction are physical comfort, emotional support, respect for patient preferences [12], and meeting of patient information needs [13]. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that a patient-centered IS has the potential to improve patient satisfaction and outcome and can play an important role in the evaluation and management of hospitals.

Berry [14] considered fairness as a basic human need that affects people’s behavior. Consumers’ perception of fairness in delivery of services depends on service quality, as well as on the staff’s attitude and behavior, which play an important role in determining customer satisfaction [15]. Most previous studies related to equity and fairness focused on the consumer’s satisfaction with relatively well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned usage scenarios, consumption episodes, or transactions such as book purchases or banking services. Fornell et al. [16] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the antecedents of customer satisfaction as expectations, perceived quality, and value. They identi<sup>fi</sup>ed loyalty as the consequence of customer satisfaction. In the context of health care, satisfaction is a multidimensional construct based on expectations demographics, and psychosocial variables [17]. Although both patients and customers have expectations of quality and value, customer satisfaction in a commercial area is not the same as patient satisfaction in the health-care context. Rooted in the consumer movement of the 1960s, which viewed patients as valuable consumers of health-care services, satisfaction in the health-care context has shifted from consumerism to a focus on “patient experience" and the encouragement of patient's involvement in their medical care [18]. Four components of patient satisfaction were presented in a review by Sitzia and Wood [17]: accessibility, interpersonal aspects of care, technical aspects of care, and patient education/information. Few scholars have explored service fairness and its impact on patient satisfaction and doctor–patient relationships in the context of medical care, which is a specialized service industry. Ventres and Frankel [19] recognized patient- and relationship-centered care (PRCC) and electronic health records (EHRs) as the most important developments in ambulatory practice. However, they identify large gaps in PRCC and EHR knowledge and practice. The potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of PRCC and EHRs for patients include better access to health information, increased ability to self-manage chronic health conditions, increased medication tracking, safer prescription renewals, and improved connections between patients and providers [20]. As organizations streamline their processes and ISs to address this change in clinical practice, the potential benefits thev can derive include increased patient satisfaction continuity of care, and improved standardization of care.

According to the DeLone and McLean model of information systems success [21], information quality, system quality, and service quality affect the subsequent use or intention to use ISs and user satisfaction. As a result of using the IS, certain bene<sup>fi</sup>ts will be achieved. The net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts will (positively or negatively) in<sup>fl</sup>uence user satisfaction and the further use of the IS.

A hospital information system (HIS) that generally includes EHRs can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an integrated system that supports the comprehensive information requirements of hospitals, patients, clinical services, ancillary services, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial management. Many newer versions of HIS allow patients to access information through dedicated terminals in hospitals, World Wide Web, and smartphones. Thus, an HIS plays an important role in hospital management and patient care. To our knowledge, there are no studies related to the impact of patient-accessible HIS on patient’s perception of service fairness, doctor–patient relationships, and patient satisfaction. The main purpose of this research is to explore the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of patient-accessible HIS implementation on doctor– patient relationships. This study contributes to the research by providing insight into the impact of HIS use on patients and further develops and improves the service fairness theory.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review theories and research on service fairness and patient satisfaction to provide a foundation for the study. We then develop the research model and propose the research hypotheses in Section 3. Section 4 discusses the research methodology used to test the hypotheses. Section 5 presents the results. Finally, the paper concludes with the discussion of <sup>fi</sup>ndings, implications for theory and practice along with limitations of the study and opportunities for future research.

## 2. Theoretical foundation

To study doctor–patient relationships and patient satisfaction, we use the theoretical lens of service fairness, which originated from equity theory [22]. The equity theory argues that people generally compare their input-to-outcome ratio to the ratios of others, and then determine fairness. Clemmer and Schneider [23] investigated the dimensions of equity in the service environment. They argued that customers evaluate the fairness of transactions from three aspects: distribution, procedure, and interaction. Since then, service fairness-related research started to adopt this threedimensional framework. Service fairness plays an important role in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing customer satisfaction and loyalty [24] and also reduces software piracy [25].

Because of the importance of information fairness in organizational justice research, it was introduced as a factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the service provider. The social determinants of justice include procedural justice, distributive justice, informational justice, and interpersonal justice [26,27]. Colquitt [28] regarded the four justices as four different types of fairness and developed measurement scales for them. Their empirical studies in two separate scenarios successfully tested the validity of the scales; showed that the four-factor fairness model has a good <sup>fi</sup>t; and concluded that fairness should include procedural fairness, distributive fairness, informational fairness, and interpersonal fairness. Carr [29] conducted fairness research in the IT industry and found that at least half of the variance in systemic fairness is explained by the aforementioned four components of fairness; Carr also concluded that informational fairness and interpersonal fairness are two facets of interactional fairness. The four-factor theory has been widely adopted in the literature. Ambrose et al. [30] adopted it to explore the effects of justice on event- and system-related attitudes; Turel et al. [31] adopted it to explore the effects of justice on user acceptance of e-customer services. Based on the allocation model proposed by Leventhal [32] and the

exchange relationship among IS users, Joshi [33] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the three dimensions of fairness that are relevant to the MIS[A2] context: procedural fairness, distributive fairness, and reciprocal fairness. Further, he found that the three dimensions of equality may not be factorial-independent facets of equality. As IT is being increasingly used in the service industry, consumers’ access to information is becoming an important issue. Easy access to useful information and the actual use of information largely affect the perception of fairness and judgment for customers. Thus, researchers agree that in a networked environment the four dimensions of service fairness theory are more suitable than the three dimensions proposed earlier.

Researchers have found that service fairness is also important in the health-care context [34,35]. They believe that improving patient’s perceptions of service fairness is an effective and low-cost way to improve health service results. However, they have not explored the factors that could improve patients’ perceptions of service fairness and how the various dimensions of fairness affect each other. An explicit consideration of the dimensions of service fairness in the HIS context will improve our understanding of this phenomenon, and help develop better HIS management strategies [29,36]. In this paper, we adopt the four-dimension theory of service fairness to explore the effects of patient-accessible HIS on patient satisfaction and doctor–patient relationships. Next, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y describe these four dimensions and adopt them to the health-care context.

## 2.1. Distributive fairness

According to equality theory, in every exchange relationship, people are constantly comparing their inputs and outcomes [37]. People’s perception of fairness is not an absolute scale, but it is measured relative to others. In the early research, many scholars thought that distributive fairness referred to the perceived fairness of actual and tangible outcomes compared with inputs. Typical distributive outcomes include compensation (e.g., gratis, discounts, coupons, free upgrades, and free ancillary services), offers to mend or totally replace product/reperform service, and apologies [38,39]. Due to its focus on outcomes, distributive justice is most closely related to cognitive, affective, and behavioral reactions to a particular outcome. Lin and Huang [40] analyzed the antecedents of knowledge withholding (KW) on project teams and found that KW is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by distributive fairness. Chou et al. [36] examined the impact of organization justice on the citizen behavior of IS personnel and found that organizational distributive fairness positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the IS worker’s job commitment. Consequently, this research suggests that distributive justice may play an important role in patients’ cognitive, affective, and behavioral reactions to the health-care services they received. Thus, following the logic and de<sup>fi</sup>nitions in the previous research [27,37], we extend the concept of distributive fairness to healthcare services. In this paper, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne patients’ perception of distributive fairness as patients’ subjective judgment of the fairness of the health-care services received relative to their cost and outcome.

## 2.2. Procedural fairness

Procedural fairness is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the fairness of the process by which outcomes are determined. Thibaut and Walker [41] proposed the theory of procedural fairness in legal and political settings. They argued that people are not only concerned about outcome fairness but also concerned about procedural fairness. Procedural justice is so important that in some cases, it is the most important determinant of perceived organizational justice [42]. Although equity has been found to have the highest causal in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the overall user information satisfaction (UIS)

compared with the other known factors, procedural fairness is found to be the most important fairness issue for users, followed by the rest of the dimensions of equity. Drawing on fairness and organizational justice literature, Fadel and Durcikova [43] showed that a willingness to contribute knowledge to an electronic knowledge repository is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the perception of procedural fairness. Similarly, we propose that procedural fairness is an important fairness issue for patients. Applying the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of procedural fairness from the previous studies [27–29] to the context of health care, patients’ perception of the procedural fairness is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the delivery of patient-requested medical care in a timely, rational manner by knowledgeable professional staff.

## 2.3. Interactional fairness

Interactional fairness is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the respect and interest shown to the customer by the service provider such that the customer feels treated fairly during consumption interactions”[44]. Although the <sup>fi</sup>rst form of procedural justice focuses on the process of service, the second form of procedural justice focuses on employees’ perception of the quality of the interpersonal relationship, commonly labeled as interactional fairness [45]. Interactional fairness re<sup>fl</sup>ects on various actions displaying social sensitivity, such as a supervisor’s treatment of employees with respect and dignity (e.g., listening to a subordinate’s concerns, providing adequate explanations for decisions, and demonstrating empathy for the other person’s plight) [46]. Clemmer [23] pointed out that customers often assess the fairness of the service according to the following aspects of the service provider: politeness, showing love to the customer, no cheating, and concerns for the customer. Fadel and Durcikova [43] found that the perception of procedural fairness is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by perceptions of interaction. Chou et al. [36] found that interactional justice positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the IS worker’s job commitment. Gefen et al. [47] found that interactional justice increases users’ assessment of the value of their IS. In the context of health care, patients interactions with health-care providers are fundamental in de<sup>fi</sup>ning the health-care experience. Applying the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of interactional fairness from the previous research [44,41] to the context of health care, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne patients’ perception of interactional fairness in the delivery of medical services as delivery of treatment in a courteous, tolerant, respectful, enthusiastic, and friendly manner. It also involves listening to the views and suggestions of the patients.

## 2.4. Informational fairness

Greenberg [26] divided interactional fairness into two parts: informational fairness and interpersonal fairness. Informational fairness is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the ability to provide consumers with information or knowledge about a service procedure. Interpersonal fairness is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as whether a service provider delivers service in a courteous and respectful manner, which may be as easy as smiling and greeting customers with politeness and respect. Some scholars believe that information fairness is part of interactional fairness, but its unique role makes it different from interactional fairness [48]. Fadel and Durcikova [43] found that the perception of procedural fairness is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by perceptions of informational fairness. Patients have the right to be informed, which constitutes one of the most important rationales for patient satisfaction [17]. Applying the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of informational fairness from the previous research [27] to the context of health care, in this study patients perception of informational fairness refers to providing many ways of capturing information from patients and providing useful cost and treatment-related information in accurate and detailed form on a variety of easily accessible platforms.

## 3. Conceptual model and hypotheses development

## 3.1. HIS implementation and informational fairness

In the health-care industry, informational fairness refers to providing patients with adequate information and knowledge about services being provided and procedures being done [49]. However, in a clinical environment, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant information asymmetry exists between doctors and patients [50]. The reasons include differences in professional background, difference in social status between a service provider and patient, and the process of transferring information [51]. Thus, the patients and their family members might feel that they have been treated unfairly. Previous studies have suggested that patients desire to know their personal health information and want to be treated fairly. In case of treatment failure, patients generally cannot ascertain whether the impairment was caused by the underlying disease or by a service failure [52]. However, the use of HIS can provide patients with information about the treatments and procedures being performed, which can make patients feel empowered and can potentially enhance the usage of such ISs [53]. For example, if the patients can access detailed information about the treatment and the associated cost through the HIS, they may be more likely to regard the treatment provided as fair.

IT offers tremendous opportunities to reduce clinical errors, to increase the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of care (e.g., shorter waiting times for patients), and to improve the quality of patient care [54]. The implementation of an HIS makes patients’ records electronically accessible. It captures, stores, processes, and communicates accurate and timely information for better coordination of health care at both the individual and population levels [55].

H1. HIS implementation has a positive effect on patient perception of information fairness.

## 3.2. Informational fairness and procedural fairness

Studies have shown that people are concerned about procedures when they experience high levels of uncertainty [15,58]. In addition, the business value of IT research shows that IT generates improved organizational performance through intermediate business processes [59]. In the context of health care, digitizing patient-accessible information improves the process by making the information more consistent and less subject to human judgment. As mentioned before, in hospital settings, differences in capabilities to access information generate information asymmetry. Information provides people with a reasonable “explanation” of their concerns, which in turn may in<sup>fl</sup>uence their judgment about the procedures [29]. Explanations eliminate the misunderstanding and remove any doubts about motivations or intentions of service providers [60]. On the contrary, if the HIS provides little information on treatment and its cost to patients, they may become doubtful about procedural fairness. In addition, perceived waiting time as well as perceived adequacy of waiting for procedures is positively related to the perception of patients’ procedural fairness [57]. In a hospital setting, the implementation of HIS might reduce the aforementioned waiting times through better utilization of resources.

A number of previous studies have established the relationship between the information people receive and their perceptions of procedural justice [61,62]. We build on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings and apply them to the health-care settings. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2. Patients’ perception of information fairness has a positive effect on their perception of procedural fairness.

Previous studies about justice showed that procedural justice in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the evaluation of outcomes of social exchanges [64,65]. Research demonstrates that people make inferences about distributive justice from procedural justice [66]. IT business value research shows that improved processes and organizational performances are generated when the right IT is applied within the right business process [59]. ## 3.4. Procedural fairness and interactional fairness

The process of organization distribution consists of a series of events [45]. In these events, the procedure causes interaction and <sup>fi</sup>nally leads to distribution. Bies [60] suggested that procedural fairness has an interactional component, which refers to the interpersonal behavior of the exchange partner (e.g., whether the exchange partner provided accounts for his or her actions and, more generally, whether the exchange partner treated others with dignity and respect). Once a process has been implemented, interactional justice becomes important [38]. In addition, perceptions of procedural fairness can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perceptions of interactional fairness in the organization. The implementation of HIS helps enforce and monitor medical personnel’s compliance with established procedures [68]. Thus, when HIS is in place, the patient cannot be deceived by the medical staff, which ensures the honesty of medical personnel. Hence, we hypothesize as follows:

H4. Patients’ perception of procedural fairness has a positive effect on their perception of interactional fairness.

## 3.5. Service fairness and patient satisfaction

Satisfaction is a psychological concept, which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned in different ways. Sometimes satisfaction is considered as a judgment of individuals regarding any object or event after gathering some experience over time [69]. Cronin et al. [70] suggest that satisfaction re<sup>fl</sup>ects the degree to which a consumer believes that the possession and/or use of a service can evoke positive feelings. It is also an attitude construct that affects a customer’s behavioral intention [71]. Furthermore, Amin and Nasharuddin [72] highlight that customer satisfaction is meeting the customer expectations of products and services by comparing them with the perceived performance. If the perceived performance matches customer expectations of services, they are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. If it does not, they are dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed [73].

Patient satisfaction even though analogous to customer satisfaction in some aspects is different from customer satisfaction with a for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t business. Health care differs from other services because it is a rare service that people need but do not necessarily want [74]. Patients expect health-care experiences to be fair, and they engage in negative reactions when they believe that they have been treated unfairly. The organizational processes in hospitals are different from business processes, and the relationships between doctors and patients are different from those for business owners and customers. It is not possible to explain patient satisfaction by using the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of customer satisfaction because of these differences. Pascore [75] de<sup>fi</sup>ned patient satisfaction as “an evaluation of healthcare services experienced by patient, as well as some kind of healthcare service expectations based on health, disease, and other life requirements.” Patients express how much they like or dislike the service after experiencing it [76]. Chakraborty and Majumdar [77] introduced patient satisfaction as a subset of consumer satisfaction. Some consider satisfaction as a cognitive reaction [78], others as an emotional attachment of individuals [79]. As a special form of consumer attitude, satisfaction represents patient ful<sup>fi</sup>llment in regard to cost, accessibility to services and resources, and patient well-being [69]. Patient satisfaction is increasingly seen as a part of health outcome quality, which also encompasses the clinical results, economic measures, and health-related quality of life [80]. A number of studies have concluded that patient satisfaction in<sup>fl</sup>uences health care and have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the factors that affect patient satisfaction [81]. For example, according to Naidu [82], factors affecting patient satisfaction include access, care quality, cost, physician’s role and behavior, tangibles (physical facilities), and other factors. In the context of this study, patient satisfaction refers to an emotional evaluation of a medical service by comparing the patients’ service expectation with the service they actually received.

Different dimensions of perceived justice have been shown to be strong predictors of user reactions such as satisfaction [83]. For example, Hui et al. [15] found that interactional justice in<sup>fl</sup>uences people’s satisfaction by simply changing their level of satisfaction with the exchange process. Some researchers indicated that the delivery of a service requires interaction between service provider and consumer, and thus in addition to the service outcome, the process by which the service is delivered to the consumer also in<sup>fl</sup>uences the consumer’s perception of service quality [56,84,85]. Other researchers indicated that perceived justice (distributive, informational, and interactional justice) of recovery efforts could affect satisfaction with the recovery [85]. Moreover, perceptions of interactional justice lead to increased user assessment of the value of their IS and their satisfaction with it [47].

The implementation of HIS provides patients with suf<sup>fi</sup>cient information, and that information can reduce the degree of patient dissatisfaction even if the result of the treatment is not very satisfactory [86]. In light of the above, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H5a. Patients’ perception of informational fairness has a positive effect on their satisfaction.

H5b. Patients’ perception of interactional fairness has a positive effect on their satisfaction.

H5c. Patients’ perception of distributive fairness has a positive effect on their satisfaction.

Patient satisfaction is seen as a critical factor in the process of building and maintaining relationships with patients while providing medical services [88]. Gill and White [80], showed that compliance with medical advice and treatment is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to health outcomes. Bacon and Mark [89] indicated that as accessibility to services and resources increases, patient satisfaction is expected to increase. Faezipour and Ferreira [69] found that an increase in patient satisfaction can also decrease the level of patient complaints. Furthermore, as patient complaints increase, the level of demand

## 4. Research method

To test the above research model, a study was conducted at AU [A3] hospital, a 3-A hospital and one of the biggest hospitals in East China. The 3-A represents one of the best hospitals in China. ## 4.1. HIS implementation and use at AU hospital

## 4.2. Measures

Measures for most of the variables were adapted from previous studies. Seven constructs were used in this study: HIS implementation, informational fairness, procedural fairness, interactional fairness, distributive fairness, patient satisfaction, and doctor–patient relationships. All the items were measured with a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Participants indicated their degree of agreement using the following interpretation: 1, strongly disagree; 2, disagree; 3, slightly disagree; 4, neither agree nor disagree; 5, slightly agree; 6, agree; and 7, strongly agree. The HIS implementation was measured by a three-item scale adapted from Angst and Agarwal [94] and Tippins and Sohi [95]. The informational fairness was measured by a four-item scale adapted from Colquitt [28]. To measure the procedural fairness, we used a three-item scale adapted from Colquitt [28]. Interactional fairness was measured by a four-item scale adapted from Clemmer [23] and Colquitt [28]. The four-item scale for distributive fairness was adapted from Clemmer [23] and Deutsch [96]. Patient satisfaction was measured by a four-item scale adapted from Diener et al. [97] and Bhattacherjee [98], as well as Olsen and Johnson [99]. Finally, we developed the four-item scale of doctor–patient relationships because we cannot <sup>fi</sup>nd an adequate or appropriate existing scale to measure the construct. To ensure that the measurement instrument is reliable and valid, we consulted with the relevant medical specialists and with experts in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of MIS, based on which we created the scale. Among the items, questions 28, 29, and 31 are related to complaints. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, question 28 measures the patient’s personal complaint about the hospital; question 29 measures the patient’s perception of overall feeling of patients regarding the hospital. They form these feelings by observing other patients and overall environment in the hospital. In China, the hospitals are much more crowded and there is little to no privacy of patients. Patients loudly discuss their frustrations and concerns; thus, it is easy for patients to form a perception of overall environment in the hospital related to patient complaints. Thus, question 31 can measure the patient’s perception of other patients’ complaints about the hospital. All of these measures are used to assess the overall environment in the hospital related to patient complaints after the implementation of HIS.

Based on the above measures, a survey instrument was developed. After compiling the English version of the instrument, the items were <sup>fi</sup>rst translated into Chinese by a bilingual research associate and then veri<sup>fi</sup>ed, re<sup>fi</sup>ned, and back-translated for translation accuracy by one of the MIS professors and two senior doctoral students. The content validity of all scales (both formative and re<sup>fl</sup>ective) was established through both a literature review and a content validity expert panel comprising eight faculty and doctoral students skilled in quantitative analysis and quantitative research methods. The content validity panel review suggested dropping one item of doctor–patient relationships. The rationale for the removal was that the item was redundant and contributed to an unnecessarily lengthy instrument without capturing a unique dimension within the construct. ## 5.2. Common method variance

To test for common method variance (CMV), we conducted Harman’s single-factor test. According to Podsakoff, et al. [108], if a detrimental level of common method bias exits, “(a) a single factor will emerge from exploratory factor analysis (unrotated) or (b) one general factor will account for the majority of the covariance among the measures” (p. 889). In the exploratory factor analysis of this study, more than one factor emerged to explain the variance, and one general factor did not account for the majority of the covariance among the measures. Thus, the common method bias in this study is low.

## 5.3. Multicollinearity

To ensure that there is no threat of multicollinearity, we tested the data and found that none of the bivariate correlations were above 0.90 [109]. Also, tolerance values averaged more than 0.30, which is acceptable. The maximum variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor (VIF) was 7.25, well below the prescriptive diagnostic of 10.0 [110,111].

## 6. Discussion, implications, and conclusions

## 6.1. Discussion of findings

This study uses the theoretical lens of service fairness to explore how HIS implementation affects patient satisfaction and doctor– patient relationships. The empirical results showed that all eight hypotheses in the model (path) were supported.

H1 indicated that the use of IT makes hospital medical services more transparent. Both patients and doctors can easily get the information they want through the system. Information transparency and spread speed improved after HIS implementation in the AU hospital. More precisely, the use of IT can improve the patients’ perception of informational fairness. This is a new and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>nding.

We explored the relationship between the four dimensions of service fairness in the medical service context through H1, H2, H3, and H4. The empirical results show that service fairness consists of four types of fairness. In addition, we found relationships between these dimensions. The results show that patient perception of informational fairness affects patient perception of procedural fairness, and procedural fairness affects the perception of interactional fairness and distributive fairness. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with Xie’s [27] conclusion, which was conducted in a tourism environment. It con<sup>fi</sup>rms that these relationships hold within the health-care environment.

H5a, H5b, and H5c indicated how service fairness affects patient satisfaction. Although procedural fairness does not directly affect patient satisfaction, results show that procedural fairness indirectly affects patient satisfaction through interactional fairness. The other three types of fairness directly affect patient satisfaction. According to Maslow’s hierarchy of needs theory, after satisfaction of material needs, we should pay more attention to the emotions of the patients and respect them, which is called humanistic care. As indicated in Section 3, patient satisfaction is different in some ways from customer satisfaction. Thus, the above relationships (H5a, H5b, and H5c) are a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution to the important issue of patient satisfaction.

H6 investigated whether patient satisfaction has an effect on doctor–patient relationships. The results indicate that the effect is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Patients play a very important role in the medical service industry. There can be no [A4] health-care industry without patients. Patient satisfaction directly affects doctor–patient relationships. The higher the patient satisfaction, the better the doctor–patient relationships. Thus, one way to improve doctor– patient relationships is to enhance patient satisfaction. Furthermore, as discussed in Section 3, patient satisfaction and a better doctor–patient relationship results in better health outcomes. This is a new and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>nding of this research.

## 6.2. Implications for research

This study has several important implications for research. Our study extended the theory of service fairness to the context of patient-accessible HIS. We examined the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using HISs in improving patients’ perception of fairness. As users of HISs, patients are different from employees who use ISs in their daily routine. Thus, our focus is different from the adoption of ISs in the traditional organizational context, which has been examined by other researchers and makes a unique contribution.

We investigated the effect of HIS implementation on patient perception of information fairness (H1). Based on the service fairness theory, we further investigated the source where the perceived informational fairness originated from and found the effect of patient-accessible HIS implementation and use. We indicated which features of HIS typically contribute to which fairness. However, we did not have data to test the impact of individual features. We did report the aggregate HIS use statistics, which are considerably high for most features. This is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution and can guide the future design and use of a patientaccessible health-care IS. Further, we rationalized the hierarchy within various dimensions of fairness and hypothesized their relationships with each other (H2, H3, and H4), as well as patient satisfaction (H5a, H5b, and H5c). Previous literature has examined these relationships in various contexts. However, hospitals are different from for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t businesses and organizational processes in hospitals are different from business processes. The results of our study validate the previously established relationships within the health-care context (H2, H3, and H4). Our results also show that relationships between the dimensions of fairness and patient satisfaction are different from those for customer satisfaction. Most importantly, our study <sup>fi</sup>nds a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between patient satisfaction and the doctor–patient relationship, which is quite different from the relationships between business owners and customers, and that these differences in<sup>fl</sup>uence the outcome.

We explored the relationship between the four dimensions of service fairness in the medical service context through H1, H2, H3, and H4. This is different from the previous studies that examined organizational fairness (among employees and employers) or transactional fairness (between customers and businesses). More signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, our study focused on examining patients’ perception of service fairness after they used patient-accessible HIS, through various perspectives (different from business environment). The use of HIS improves patients’ involvement with the diagnosis process, provides a clearer explanation of patient’s medical conditions, provides better explanations of treatment cost, and

To our knowledge, there are no studies related to the impact of patient-accessible HISs on patients’ perception of service fairness, doctor–patient relationships, and patient satisfaction. Previous studies have already pointed out the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of interactional justice, distributional justice, and procedural justice on satisfaction in the organizational or transactional contexts without indicating their speci<sup>fi</sup>c relationships or effect size. There was another research gap related to the direct or indirect impact each dimension of fairness has on satisfaction. This research helps to clarify those relationships. Focusing on patient satisfaction, this study demonstrates how the use of HIS improves doctor–patient relationships in the hospitals in China, which is of great importance and in<sup>fl</sup>uences health outcomes. As one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst research groups focusing on patient satisfaction and doctor–patient relationships, we applied the service fairness theory to the medical services industry and investigated how the use of patient-accessible HIS affected patient satisfaction and the doctor–patient relationship. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have shed light on the formulation and mechanism for improving doctor–patient relationships, which can serve as a foundation for further research on using patient-accessible HIS.

## 6.3. Implications for practice

Our study found that interactional fairness has a direct effect on patient satisfaction. Interactional fairness can be improved by the effective use of empathic communication skills or having more empathic physicians. That is to say, hospitals with more empathic physicians have an advantage over other hospitals with fewer empathic physicians. Thus, the health-care staff should be friendlier to patients and place the patient at the center of care. This is an important approach to improve the quality of medical care and patient satisfaction.

Besides this, we examined the impact of HIS implementation on doctor–patient relationships. Our study provides a theoretical basis for planning HIS implementation and helps provide an understanding of the mechanism through which HIS implementation can improve patient satisfaction and the doctor–patient relationships. We believe that the use of HIS can yield betterinformed patients with a higher likelihood of compliance with recommended treatments resulting in improved health outcomes. HIS also shows a remarkable potential for improving the doctor– patient relationship. Potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of good doctor–patient relationships include enhanced communication, access to health information, shared decision making, and more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient use of clinical time. Our study offers new ideas for medical reform, especially for the improvement of doctor–patient relationships. Many hospital administrators and health-care providers focus on improving patient care; although desirable, better care does not always mean a higher level of patient satisfaction and outcome. The top health-care concerns in China are the treatment environment, the hospital layout, service attitude, service processes, privacy protection, information transparency, emergency service, and resolution of patient complaints. Chinese citizens have become increasingly dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the health-care system – which suffers from urban and rural inequalities, medical information asymmetry, and overpriced, low-quality products and services. China is improving medical care in its rural areas and calling for increased implementation of HIS. This paper investigated the impacts of using HIS on patient satisfaction. As we can see from the results, providing ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and fair services can generate positive emotions and trust, thus resulting in higher levels of patient retention and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Boosting patient satisfaction is important not only because it determines whether a hospital will retain current patients and attracts new patients, but it is also a big factor that in<sup>fl</sup>uences the relationship between doctors and patients. The increasing tension between doctors and patients has become a serious social problem in China. The implementation of HIS positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences patient perception of service fairness, which correspondingly increases patient satisfaction, and ultimately improves doctor–patient relationships. Thus, our results encourage practitioners to implement HIS in rural areas, thus improving patients’ perception of fairness and satisfaction. More importantly, as more emerging countries develop their economies, the citizen’s expectations for better health care and other government services increases. Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this paper could be applied for health care in other emerging countries.

## 6.4. Limitations and future direction

This paper uses service fairness theory to study the impact of HIS on doctor–patient relationships. Due to time constraints and other factors, our research has some limitations.

First, we studied the use of patient-accessible HIS, which contains various features implemented by different subsystems. Due to the limitations of survey instrument length, we could not collect detailed data on which patients used which features of HIS and link those to the satisfaction of speci<sup>fi</sup>c patients. However, we did obtain data on the aggregate use of various features, which does validate our hypothesis. Future research can investigate the speci<sup>fi</sup>c role of different features of patient-accessible HIS on information fairness.

Second, the data of this study are from one of the biggest hospitals in East China, which has successfully implemented HIS. For many small and medium-sized hospitals, the implementation of HIS may be different, and the contribution of HIS to improving doctor–patient relationships may also differ. Follow-up research can focus on the impact of HIS implementation in small and medium-sized hospitals.

Third, we collected sample data from one hospital and received 229 valid questionnaires. Although bootstrapping and PLS were used to accommodate the sample size in this paper, future research should achieve a larger sample size to facilitate other empirical methods.

Finally, this study is based on China’s speci<sup>fi</sup>c health-care environment, which is called “Chinese characteristics.” The relationships between the four types of fairness might be different in other countries. Future work can explore research questions that build on the results of this study. For example, what types of service fairness (i.e., informational fairness, procedural fairness, interactional fairness, and distributive fairness) are most likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence satisfaction and, consequently, the doctor–patient relationships? What is the best way for hospitals to implement HIS and encourage users to use the system?

## 6.5. Conclusions

China is in the midst of reforming and modernizing its healthcare system. Much has been written about market-driven imbalances and <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives to doctors and hospitals that may be at odds with achieving high-quality, affordable, and accessible care [24,112,113]. New health-care reform priorities include increasing patient satisfaction with health care and improving the quality and safety of care. The doctor–patient relationship is one of the major challenges in Chinese hospitals. Our study found that HIS implementation has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on service fairness, and it improves the doctor–patient relationships by raising patients’ satisfaction. First, the use of HISs can streamline patient treatment by identifying and eliminating many redundant procedures in the process of diagnosing and treating the

patient. Second, the use of HISs makes diagnosis, treatment, and charges more standardized and clear. All of these changes will improve the patient perception of service fairness. Once the patient perception of service fairness has been improved, patients will be more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the hospital. High satisfaction also increases a patient’s trust, which can reduce patient discontent and eventually improve the doctor–patient relationships. Based on the results of this study, hospitals should pay more attention to the following process of diagnosis and treatment. First, good use should be made of an HIS to help patients, and to streamline and remove redundancy in the process of diagnosis and treatment. Real and useful information should be provided to patients in a timely manner, and patients’ awareness of service fairness be improved. Second, hospitals should focus on patient satisfaction. The healthcare provider should actively look for methods, such as IT tools, to improve patient satisfaction and eventually improve doctor– patient relationships. Third, the medical staff should have high service awareness. They should have respect for patients’ personal characteristics and rights, as well as be polite to patients. They need to be fair to the patient and pay more attention to humanistic care.
