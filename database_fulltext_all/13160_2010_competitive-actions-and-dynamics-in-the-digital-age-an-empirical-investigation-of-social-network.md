# Competitive Actions and Dynamics in the Digital Age: An Empirical Investigation of Social Networking Firms

Devi R. Gnyawali, Weiguo Fan, James Penner

R. B. Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University, Blacksburg, Virginia 24061 {devi@vt.edu, wfan@vt.edu, pennerja@vt.edu}

his paper examines two important questions in the context of the social networking services (SNS) firms: Twhat kind of competitive moves do SNS firms undertake and to what extent do the competitive moves impact firm performance? We blend the literature streams on information systems (IS) and strategic management and argue that given the unique characteristics of this nascent industry, SNS firms’ competitive moves are likely to focus on value cocreation, as well as enhancement of the repertoire of their moves. We propose a conceptual model by blending value cocreation perspectives from the IS literature and repertoire of competitive actions from the competitive dynamics literature, and test our hypotheses using archival data. Results show that firms that emphasize value cocreation actions through the engagement of codevelopers in their technology platform and formation of strategic alliances enhance their performance. Furthermore, firms that undertake complex action repertoires achieve better performance. This study provides unique insights about the ways in which firms compete in the industry and has several implications for future research.

Key words: competition strategy; competition dynamics; competitive actions; firm performance; social networking services; page view, business value of information technology; value cocreation; repertoire of actions; network centrality

## 1. Introduction

How do firms in the social networking services (SNS) industry—a vivid example of nascent markets— compete and enhance their performance? We address this issue by blending the literature on information systems (IS) and strategic management. Although research on IS has recognized the importance of firm competitive actions and suggested that information technology (IT) and systems might help firms to undertake competitive actions of greater volume and complexity (Sambamurthy et al. 2003), empirical IS research has not examined competitive actions and their impact on firm performance in IS- and ITdriven industries. Scholars have called for empirical research that promotes better understanding of how IS helps the firm’s strategy and performance (Piccoli and Ives 2005) and how firms might leverage IT to cocreate value with partners (Kohli and Grover 2008). Scholars in strategic management underscore the importance of firm competitive actions because the pattern of competitive actions reflects the firm’s strategy (Mintzberg and Waters 1985), and competitive actions impact firm performance (Smith et al. 2001, Young et al. 1996). In addition, prior research on strategic management has examined the pattern of competitive actions and their implications in more traditional industries such as the airline, computer, steel, and pharmaceutical industries (Ferrier et al. 1999; Miller and Chen 1996; Gnyawali et al. 2006, 2008). However, little empirical research has been conducted to promote understanding of how firms compete in emerging and nascent markets, i.e., contexts characterized by a high degree of ambiguity, the early stage of industry formation, and undefined and fleeting industry structures (Santos and Eisenhardt 2009). Our goal is to examine empirically how firms in the nascent social networking industry compete and how competitive actions contribute to firm performance. Specifically, we examine the following research questions: (1) What kinds of competitive moves do SNS firms undertake to compete against each other? (2) To what extent do the competitive actions undertaken by SNS firms enhance their performance?

We blend cutting-edge research on IS (Kohli and Grover 2008, Piccoli and Ives 2005, Sambamurthy et al. 2003) and strategic management (Ferrier et al. 1999; Miller and Chen 1996; Smith et al. 2001; Santos and Eisenhardt 2009; and Gnyawali et al. 2006, 2008) and investigate research questions and related hypotheses, using data on SNS firms’ competitive actions, and examine the impact of such actions on user page views—a unique measure of firm performance in the industry—for the period of 2005–2007. We address the first research question by developing a conceptual model that is based on the unique characteristics of the nascent SNS industry and by examining the patterns of competitive actions in the industry. We address the second question by empirically examining the performance implications of the competitive actions. To discern and assess competitive actions and their patterns, we followed the structural content analysis methods used in prior strategy research (Ferrier et al. 1999; Miller and Chen 1996; Smith et al. 2001; Chen et al. 1992; and Gnyawali et al. 2006, 2008) and coded competitive actions of SNS firms based on published news reports using the Factiva database. Results show that SNS firms’ competitive actions are focused mainly on product or service innovations, pursuit of strategic alliances, and codevelopment of the technology based on the core technological platform. In terms of the performance effects, results show that SNS firms that effectively leverage the expertise of program developers and users through the Web 2.0 technology and collaborate with other firms to gain access to external resources and expand the markets enhance their performance. Moreover, firms that undertake a more complex repertoire of competitive actions perform better.

We contribute to the literature in several important ways. First, this is the first systematic empirical study of SNS firms and how firms in this nascent industry compete and create competitive advantages. Our study shows the unique nature of competitive actions in this emerging industry: codevelopment with third-party developers and relational capability building through collaborative relationships or networks. Understanding the nature of competition in this industry is critical because the industry has a tremendous impact on our lives, yet the industry is nascent and the models of competition are still emerging. Second, we provide evidence that the nature of competition and performance implications of competitive actions in this nascent industry are similar in some ways and different in other ways, when compared to more traditional industries in which competitive actions are examined by prior research. Uniqueness of our findings pertains to the role of both IT-based and non-IT-based value cocreation actions in firm performance. We also confirm prior findings (based on more traditional industries) of competitive dynamics research that indicate that SNS firms that undertake complex repertoire of actions enhance their performance (Ferrier et al. 1999). This study provides a strong foundation for future research on competitive dynamics in nascent markets and might help to promote understanding of how the nature of competition might evolve in nascent markets. Finally, and more broadly, we blend the literature on IS and strategy to examine research questions that are of interest to scholars in both areas. Our study contributes to the literature by addressing how firms in this technology intensive, digital technology-based, emerging industry effectively use competitive moves to create value and enhance their performance. Our study suggests that IS researchers may be better off examining how IS and IT contribute to firm competitive actions first in their quest for developing a link between IS and IT capabilities and firm performance (Sambamurthy et al. 2003).

## 2. Conceptual Background

In this section, we first discuss why the SNS industry is an example of nascent markets characterized by a high degree of ambiguity. We then discuss how key unique characteristics of the industry led to our conceptual framework that helps to promote understanding of and empirically examine competitive actions and their implications in this nascent industry context.

## 2.1. SNS as an Example of Nascent Markets

An SNS is defined as a Web-based software application that helps users connect and socialize with friends, family members, business partners, or other individuals. Unlike many previous computer-mediated communication systems such as e-mail, listserv, and

online forums, SNSs are primarily Web based and provide a collection of means—text chat, messaging, e-mail, video, voice, file sharing, blogging, discussion groups, etc.—for users to interact and socialize with one another. Notable examples in this digital industry include MySpace, Facebook, Bebo, Linkedin, and Hi5. SNS websites have gained tremendous popularity in recent years and have impacted the way people socialize and communicate with each other. A recent report by Morgan Stanley in 2008 shows that page views of Facebook and YouTube are greater than those of Google or Yahoo; 6 of the top 10 Internet sites are social, none of which were on the list in 2005; users spend on average, more than 30 minutes a day on Facebook, and more than 14 million photos are uploaded daily on Facebook. Facebook is now growing at an astonishing rate of 5 million new users per week, and more than 24 billion pieces of information are uploaded to Facebook each month (Hempel 2009b). As of March 2009, both Facebook and MySpace had more than 175 million registered users (Hempel 2009a, b). Although the SNS industry is very popular and has experienced rapid growth, the players in the industry are very new. SNS firms have attempted many competitive moves to establish their foothold in the market, and they continue to struggle to develop competitive approaches and business models that might help firms create and maintain competitive advantages. Indeed, the industry has several characteristics of nascent markets, as described by Santos and Eisenhardt (2009). The industry is at an early stage of formation with undefined or fleeting industry structure. Firms’ boundaries and scope of operations are unclear, and firms have experimented with various ways to attract and retain users. In addition, the industry context is quite unstructured, with a high degree of ambiguity. Executives lack a clear view of industry structure, and the industry lacks legitimized industry recipe and logic to guide competitive actions. Entry of new firms with unique focus and business models confounds the problems of nascent markets (Santos and Eisenhardt 2009). New SNS firms have been entering with a focus on niche customers, and firms have competed vigorously to attract and retain users, but the identity and strategies of various SNS firms are quite fluid. The rules of the game have continued to change and evolve, as firms are continuously responding to actions taken by competitors and taking actions to surpass their competitors.

Santos and Eisenhardt (2009) discuss various ways in which firms in nascent markets deal with ambiguity by constructing markets and shaping firm boundaries. Their discussion of how firms claim and demarcate markets is particularly relevant in the context of SNS firms. They suggest three mechanisms firms pursue in “claiming the market”: adoption of templates from other domains; signaling leadership to convey superior expertise and power; and disseminating stories by spreading symbolic narratives about the company. SNS firms have adopted the physical world interaction template and adapted it in the context of online communication. Major SNS firms such as Facebook signal leadership by creating the largest platform to leverage the strong network effects. Smaller firms signal leadership through niche markets either geographically, such as Orkut does in Brazil, or demographically, such as LinkedIn does for professionals. Major SNS firms raise awareness and reinforce their identity by emphasizing their distinctiveness and by allying with reputed firms in related industries. As Santos and Eisenhardt (2009) suggest, these efforts by firms to “claim” the market help to establish their cognitive dominance in the market. In terms of “demarcating the market,” Santos and Eisenhardt (2009) discuss how firms in nascent markets entice powerful firms in related industries to work with them through equity investments and revenue sharing arrangements. Prominent examples of equity investments in the SNS industry include Microsoft’s stake in Facebook and the purchase of MySpace by News Corp. Revenue-sharing agreements are contracts with players in nearby markets for advertising, distributions, or sharing of online interface, which help to deter participants from the nearby markets entering into the SNS market. An example of revenue-sharing agreement in the SNS industry is MySpace with Google. These efforts to “demarcate the market” help to establish competitive dominance in the market (Santos and Eisenhardt 2009). Thus, a high degree of ambiguity, caused by factors such as unstructured industry context, lack of legitimized industry recipe and logic, and many attempts by firms to claim and demarcate the market, is indicative of the nascent nature of the SNS industry.

## 2.2. Web as a Platform and Ongoing Development and Release of Products or Services

Because the development and testing of new products often takes a significant amount of time and resources, release of products in traditional industries often takes a long time and requires longer cycle time. SNS firms, however, engage in continuous development of new product or service features anytime, anywhere (not constrained by firm boundaries and regular work hours) and release them continuously through the website. For example, Facebook platform is used to engage users worldwide to codevelop and continuously release new applications for Facebook users. Flickr, as another example, recently revealed that they release new product features to their website every 30 minutes. The continuous innovation, enhancement, and release of new products and services on the Web platform are unprecedented, and much of the new product or technology release is done through the help of third parties that develop the applications for the firm.

## 2.3. Extensive Value Cocreation with Vendors and Users

The importance of partnering proposed by Santos and Eisenhardt (2009) in nascent markets is evident in the SNS context. Because many of the SNS firms are young and fast growing and are privately held, they have to work with many third-party vendors in expanding their firm capabilities and service offerings. Many SNS firms actively work with different vendors to create rich applications or services on their Web-based platforms. By 2007, Facebook, for example, had more than 10,000 applications produced by third-party developers (Rumford 2007). Such applications are valuable strategic assets for the firm because they help enrich customers’ experiences and retain existing customers. SNS firms also heavily depend on their registered members in creating rich contents. Users create their own profiles, post contents on their friends’ profiles, and engage in many other social interactions to create enormous amounts of contents for the SNS provider (Hempel 2009b). Because SNS firms’ main source of revenue thus far is through online advertising and strong network effects exist in the industry, the SNS providers aggressively try to grow their user base and user traffic, and their actions are aimed at attracting and retaining the users.

## 3. Conceptual Development and Hypotheses

The above-mentioned unique characteristics of the nascent SNS industry, coupled with its rapid growth and impact on our daily lives, suggest it as an intriguing context to study how firms compete and whether firm competitive actions enhance firm performance. Our conceptual framework is driven by the conceptual underpinnings of nascent markets, as discussed by Santos and Eisenhardt (2009), and the unique nature of the SNS industry discussed earlier.

By building on prior research on IS and strategic management, we suggest that SNS firms pursue two distinct avenues to address ambiguity and to compete against other players in the industry. First, they build and leverage relationships, which is akin to Santos and Eisenhardt’s (2009) discussion of alliance mechanisms. Specifically within the context of the SNS industry, that could include both IT-based and non-IT-based mechanisms. We label this set of moves as “value cocreation actions.” We selected two aspects of value cocreation: codevelopment of the technology by using the expertise and passion of third-party developers and development of relational capability by forming alliances with other firms. Thus, in value cocreation, a firm’s competitive actions are focused on working together with developers to enhance the technology behind the SNS service and on working with major players in adjacent markets and industries to reach out and expand the applicability of the SNS service. Working with other major players in adjacent markets is very critical for reputation, legitimacy, and access to markets. Second, SNS firms attempt to reduce ambiguity by undertaking aggressive competitive actions in many fronts, so that they can shape the industry, signal their dominance, and create their legitimacy in the market. Because executives lack a clear view of industry structure and industry lacks legitimized industry recipe and logic to guide competitive actions, firms try to be competitively very aggressive (or initiate a large number of actions) and undertake many types of actions in the hope that at least a few of their actions will be effective in attracting and retaining users and in creating their dominance in the market. We label this construct as repertoire of competitive actions.

Thus our focus on the constructs of value cocreation and action repertoire is based on two key considerations. Second, because our conceptual logics are derived from the IS and strategy literatures, the constructs should be based on these literature streams and help inform researchers from both camps and allow integration of the two literature streams. ## 3.1. Value Cocreation

Value cocreation actions refer to competitive moves firms undertake to create perceived and real benefits for their users by engaging other parties as partners and contributors to the SNS services. The importance of IT platform, central role of users, and strong network effects suggest that SNS firms’ competitive actions are likely to be focused on ways in which firms can create value by leveraging the knowledge and expertise of third-party developers so that meaningful SNS applications can be developed on a regular basis that will help entice, engage, and delight users. Equally important for SNS firms is the need to work jointly (or collaborate) with partners who can help the firms to extend the usefulness of the SNS site. For instance, Match.com and Facebook have partnered to allow Match.com subscribers to connect with Facebook users (Nicole 2007). Similarly, Skype partners with MySpace to provide MySpace users the opportunity for live conversations between members (McCarthy 2007).

Value cocreation is happening in many industries (Prahalad and Ramaswamy 2004, Prahalad and Krishnan 2008). It is much more critical in the Web 2.0-based SNS industry. Yet, as Kohli and Grover (2008) argued, “the issue of value cocreation has been relatively understudied” (p. 28).

Kohli and Grover (2008) suggest that value cocreation can be accomplished through two distinctive mechanisms: IT-based value cocreation and non-ITbased value cocreation. In IT-based value cocreation, IT is used as the main tool or driver for creating value with other parties. In the case of non-IT-based value cocreation, firms collaborate in creating business value with less explicit attention to IT. Examples could be partnerships for product development, new market penetration, and pursuit of new uses of existing products or services. We follow the discussion of IT-based and non-IT-based value cocreation from Kohli and Grover (2008) and examine firms competitive actions in these areas. IT-based value creation involves actions firms undertake to engage third-party developers for the development of various applications based on the core platform (such as the Facebook platform). We call this codevelopment actions. Non-IT-based cocreation refers to actions undertaken to form alliances and networks with other firms to expand the breadth and depth of SNS service offerings to the users. We term this relational capability actions.

3.1.1. Codevelopment Actions and Firm Performance. Codevelopment actions refer to the competitive moves that involve partnerships with third-party vendors and users to create additional functionalities, services, and applications that enhance the quality and versatility of the IT-based system. The focal firm provides the platform infrastructure and application programming interface, which the third-party vendors and users use to create novel and creative applications that can be potentially used by the users of the focal firm’s SNS service. Codevelopment actions thus reflect the firm’s efforts to reach out and leverage the IT expertise of the vendors and the passion of the expert users to enhance the overall quality of the system and services provided by the firm. The idea of codevelopment is similar to coproduction (Skaggs and Huffman 2003), essentially enabling the firm to combine the strengths and IS and IT expertise of the vendors and developers with that of the firm. The vendors are motivated to cocreate with the focal firm because they can use their expertise and generate revenue through the usage of their applications. Besides third-party vendors, some savvy users with strong IT skills are also motivated to create applications because they see a need that is not addressed by existing applications and desire to enrich their own experience and possibly create business opportunities if their applications become popular. All of this allows the focal firm to leverage the collective intelligence and expertise of codevelopers to serve demanding and ever-changing user needs.

Research on customer engagement in service-based industries (Bateson 2002, Mills and Morris 1986) suggests the need to examine the interaction of the firm with users and customers to better understand value creation by service firms. As noted earlier, the customer coproduction literature (Skaggs and Huffman 2003) also suggests that firms engage in coproduction because they have uncertainty about the needs of their clients. SNS firms face high levels of ambiguity in nascent markets because of limited knowledge on the demanding and changing needs of users. Because SNS service involves millions of users but the firms have limited resources, it is challenging for the firms to address the heterogeneous needs of the users. As noted earlier, the concept of value cocreation has become more critical across industries. Cook (2008) suggests that customers are interested in contributing because they can get immediate rewards, serve common interest of the community, get rewards from their community of users, and can express creative thoughts and expertise. Vendors are willing to participate in the codevelopment because of the expected business potential in the long run. In the context of SNS firms, codevelopment increases the focal firm’s service quality with little or no additional tangible costs for the focal firm. It also significantly increases the number of quality applications available. Thus our examination of codevelopment addresses an emerging trend, which is quite important for SNS firms.

We suggest that codevelopment actions enhance firm performance in the SNS industry for several reasons. First, the improvement of service quality and increased service offerings through new applications will have a paramount impact on user adoption and user retention. Prior research (Fornell et al. 1996, Anderson et al. 2004, Cronin et al. 2000) suggests that an increase of service quality will improve the perceived usefulness of the service or perceived value by potential users, which, in turn, will increase the chances of adoption by new users (Bhattacherjee 2001, Davis 1989, Venkatesh et al. 2003, Wixom and Todd 2005). Second, the enhanced service quality through codevelopment will have a positive impact on user retention. Service quality has been found to be a strong factor in affecting user satisfaction and user loyalty (Anderson et al. 2004, Cronin et al. 2000, Fornell et al. 1996). Enhanced services will create a rich environment to entertain and please current users and accordingly create a favorable user experience. Such pleasant user experiences will motivate users to stay with the current service provider, build up users sense of belonging or commitment to the site, and increase continued use of the site to fulfill their needs (Gilliland and Bello 2002, Li et al. 2006, Pritchard et al. 1999). Third, codevelopment will create significant network effect. Enhanced service quality through codevelopment will increase the user base and also increase the amount of resources available to all users because of increase user participation and contribution. Because SNS is a community-driven Web service, the value of the network will increase as the user base increases. Thus the increased user base and resources will create a strong network effect which, in turn, will increase returns for the focal firm that can expand the size of its customer base (Sambamurthy et al. 2003, Piccoli and Ives 2005). Fourth, the arguments made in the customer coproduction literature noted earlier (Skaggs and Huffman 2003) are relevant for the SNS firms, i.e., firms that engage in codevelopment actions will better address client needs and increase their competitive advantage and performance. Based on the above arguments, we hypothesize as follows.

Hypothesis 1. A positive relationship exists between codevelopment actions and firm performance.

3.1.2. Relational Capability Actions and Firm Performance. Relational capability actions refer to firms’ moves aimed at establishing and leveraging partnerships with other firms to enhance the usefulness of the SNS site. Unlike codevelopment actions that are focused on IT, primarily aimed at leveraging technological expertise of third parties, and take place in the upstream and infrastructure of aspects of the value chain, relational capability actions are mainly non-IT, are formalized relationships with firms in adjacent markets, and are largely focused on the downstream aspects of the value chain. The logic put forth by Santos and Eisenhardt (2009) that firms establish alliances with powerful players in the adjacent markets is central to our concept of relational capability actions. As noted earlier, collaboration with other major players in adjacent markets is very important for gaining reputation and legitimacy and for accessing the markets.

Scholars in competitive dynamics research (Smith et al. 2001) suggest the need to study the interface of alliances and network and competitive dynamics because a firm’s network structure impacts the likelihood of competitive actions and competitor response (Gnyawali and Madhavan 2001). Because SNS firms continue to explore ways to expand the usefulness of their services by building collaborative business relationships with various parties, it is logical to examine the extent to which firms’ actions are focused on building network of alliances and whether such actions enhance firm performance. Collaborations are very important to address uncertainties and risks, facilitate entry into new markets, and provide access to external resources (Dyer and Singh 1998, BarNir and Smith 2002). Because of ambiguity in the industry and lack of resources, SNS firms often need to access resources of other firms in adjacent markets to seize and expand the market and to gain legitimacy. Strategic management literature has long emphasized the importance of building relational capability through alliances (Dyer 1997, Dyer and Singh 1998, Kogut 1988, Gulati 1998). Prior research on competitive dynamics suggests that collaborative networks help firms to strengthen their competitive capability by providing access to partners’ information, resources, and status (Gnyawali and Madhavan

2001), which, in turn, enables the firm to more effectively compete in the industry. In the context of IS research, Sambamurthy et al. (2003) suggest that partnerships help the firm to leverage assets, knowledge, and competencies of multiple partners, such as suppliers, distributors, manufacturers, and logistics providers, and, in turn, increase a firm’s ability to pursue innovative opportunities.

We argue that SNS firms that undertake actions to enhance their relational capability are likely to improve their performance for several reasons. First, alliances provide access to various kinds of information and opportunities (Gulati 1998) that the firm can pursue to attract, retain, and delight the users. Identification and pursuit of opportunities to expand the scope and usefulness of an SNS site are very critical to keeping users involved with the site and engaging them over a period of time. For example, MySpace has formed alliances with various cell phone companies, TV broadcasters, movietickets.com, and Warner Music for various advertising promotions to attract user traffic to their site. These alliances make it easier for users to access information and use the MySpace site. As a result, MySpace retains users and increases their use of the site. Second, alliances provide access to resources of their partners (Gnyawali and Madhavan 2001) and enable the SNS firm to work together with the partners to create relational competitive advantage. Relational competitive advantage is generated by the development of capabilities across organizational boundaries and can be achieved by the creation of complementary and distinctive resources, which help generate rents higher than the sum of those generated independently by the resources of each organization (Dyer and Singh 1998). Thus, critical resources can be expanded or built up beyond the confines of the firm and can be integrated into interfirm routines and processes. Firms that emphasize relational building actions are likely to access and acquire relational assets and then combine them with their internal resources to create competitive advantages for themselves. Emphasis on relation-building actions facilitates long-term cooperation, joint planning and pursuit of opportunities, shared information and knowledge, and continuous improvement to consolidate the market position (Lorenzoni and Lipparini 1999). The alliance or interfirm networks provide an effective way for firms to engage in knowledge sharing and transfer activities in dynamically complex domains and in contexts where knowledge is scattered or specialized (Lorenzoni and Lipparini 1999). Through effective organizational learning from one another and asset accumulation from complementary sources, a firm will build up its knowledge capacity and agility to compete (Piccoli and Ives 2005). Zaheer and Zaheer (1997) show that firms with a wide-ranging network exhibit superior responsiveness and performance in turbulent business environments.

Overall, we suggest that SNS firms that emphasize relational capability actions are likely to reduce ambiguity through the exposure to and pursuit of multiple market opportunities, acquisition of partners’ resources, and use of such resources to effectively pursue market opportunities. The goal is to attract new users and retain existing users by offering a diverse range of services. Firms with stronger relational capability actions will, in turn, find more resourceful partners (Ahuja 2000), and further leverage the partners’ knowledge and expertise to pursue competitive advantages (Gnyawali and Madhavan 2001). We therefore hypothesize as follows.

Hypothesis 2. A positive relationship exists between relational capability actions and firm performance.

## 3.2. Repertoire of Actions

Repertoire refers to the set of actions pursued by an organization to attract, serve, and keep customers and is a novel way to examine a firm’s strategy (Miller and Chen 1996). Although competitive dynamics research has examined the repertoire of competitive actions in more traditional industries (such as airlines, computer, steel, and pharmaceutical) (Smith et al. 2001; Ferrier et al. 1999; Gnyawali et al. 2006, 2008), competitive action repertoire and its implications have not been studied in emerging industries such as SNS. Our focus on the construct of action repertoire is based on this important gap. Moreover, as noted earlier, firms in this nascent SNS industry with an unclear pattern of competition try out multiple actions and discern the type of actions that provide better outcomes. Thus the examination of action repertoire and its impact in the nascent SNS industry helps us understand the extent to which established findings from other industries hold in the nascent context of the digital industry. In the IS literature, Sambamurthy et al. (2003) have conceptually suggested the importance of competitive action repertoire in understanding firm agility and performance. They argue that as firms undertake more volume and complex competitive action repertoires, firms further develop their agility and entrepreneur alertness and, therefore, enhance firm performance. Based on these literature streams, we focus on two key aspects of competitive action repertoire—complexity and volume (Ferrier et al. 1999, Sambamurthy et al. 2003, Gnyawali et al. 2006)—and their performance implications.

3.2.1. Complexity of Action Repertoire and Firm Performance. Complexity of action repertoire refers to the range or diversity of moves undertaken by a firm (Ferrier et al. 1999, Nayyar and Bantel 1994) and reflects the scope of competitive behavior (Gnyawali et al. 2006). Complexity is high when a firm undertakes actions across many dimensions, such as research and development, marketing, manufacturing, and distribution (Nayyar and Bantel 1994).

Our conceptual arguments regarding the importance of complexity of action repertoire and its impact on firm performance are based on multiple literature streams. In terms of strategic management, researchers have suggested that competitive advantage based on multiple sources is more sustainable (Porter 1980, Ghemawat 1999). The views of Austrian economics suggest that firms that undertake a broader scope of competitive actions can impact competitors in multiple ways and make it difficult for competitors to understand the complex set of actions and respond to them (Ferrier et al. 1999, Smith et al. 2001, Young et al. 1996). Moreover, firms that implement a variety of competitive actions are often perceived as less predictable, and their actions are more difficult to interpret, thus reducing the likelihood of competitor response and increasing the first-mover advantage (Gnyawali et al. 2006). Finally, Miller and Chen (1996) suggest that firms facing a wide range of issues and a variety of challenges may need to undertake more complex action repertoire to enhance their performance. Firms may be willing to try numerous different actions because this nascent market does not indicate which actions will improve firm performance. The unknown cause effect relationship from actions taken in the nascent market will favor firms that attempt many actions. Multidimensional changes in industry and general environmental conditions and ambiguity caused by such changes require firms to undertake simultaneous competitive actions on many fronts to be successful (Smith et al. 2001).

In terms of IS, scholars suggest that firms that undertake a complex set of initiatives will create barriers to erosion and competitive advantages. Undertaking a diversified set of actions could help a firm develop capabilities on multiple fronts such as IT assets, IT capabilities, and complementary resources, which will create IT resources barriers, complementary resources barriers, IT project barriers, and possible preemption barriers (Piccoli and Ives 2005). Complex and diversified action repertoire will help firms to develop and increase firm agility on multiple fronts, such as customer agility, partnering agility, and operational agility, which will help firms to grow and create competitive advantage (Sambamurthy et al. 2003). In terms of the literature on service adaptability (the degree to which a firm alters its service output according to the desire of customers), scholars suggest that service adaptability influences the level of complexity a firm needs to deal with as it conducts business. Skaggs and Huffman (2003) hypothesized and found a positive relationship between service adaptability and the level of service production complexity (a large number of different processes to be performed for service production). The higher the service adaptability, the higher the variance in customer demands and the need to customize the product or service will be. Compared to others, firms that emphasize service adaptability are likely to attract and retain customers. Firms need to undertake many different types of actions to adapt a product or service to specific customer needs. Because SNS providers need to attract and retain a large number of users, we expect them to be strong in service adaptability. Accordingly, we expect that firms that undertake a more a complex action repertoire will perform better. For example, the surge of Facebook relative to the market dominant player MySpace in recent years highlights the impact of complexity on firm market performance. Facebook undertook competitive moves on multiple fronts such as being more inclusive in membership, being the first to open the platform to allow codevelopment with third-party vendors, active pursuit of alliances, and continuous creation and enhancement of services.

Overall, these multiple streams of literature suggest a positive relationship between action repertoire complexity and firm performance. Firms that undertake a complex range of actions are likely to make it difficult for competitors to understand the intent and consequences of the various actions. Moreover, because each type of action requires commitment of different types of resources, rivals find it very difficult to match a focal firm’s complex set of actions. This inability of rivals to match actions provides a window of advantage to the focal firm. Also, a firm that undertakes many types of actions is likely to have developed the capability in multiple areas and addressed industry and market ambiguity through these various actions. Thus, we hypothesize as follows.

Hypothesis 3. A positive relationship exists between complexity of action repertoire and firm performance.

3.2.2. Volume of Actions and Firm Performance. Volume of actions refers to the total number of competitive actions undertaken by a firm (Ferrier et al. 1999, Smith et al. 2001, Young et al. 1996) and reflects the scale of competitive behavior (Gnyawali et al. 2006). Volume of actions is one of the most commonly studied aspects of competitive behavior (Ferrier et al. 1999, Gnyawali et al. 2006) and has been shown to be critical for firms to enhance their performance in more traditional industries (Ferrier et al. 1999, Smith et al. 2001, Young et al. 1996).

We expect a positive relationship between the volume of competitive actions and firm performance for several reasons. First, the more competitive actions a firm undertakes or the more competitively aggressive the firm is, the more likely it is that it will disrupt the status quo of the industry and create competitive advantages for itself (Ferrier et al. 1999, Smith et al. 2001, Young et al. 1996). Firms that are aggressive are likely to pursue opportunities that are available for only limited windows of time (D’Aveni 1994). Research suggests that these proactive firms can enjoy enhanced market share and improved financial performance when compared to their less active rivals (Chen and MacMillan 1992, Ferrier et al. 1999, Young et al. 1996). Second, volume of actions may also be an indicator of how resourceful the firm is.

Firms that possess the resources and expertise to launch and implement a large number of competitive actions are likely to be competitively strong and, thus, gain the trust of the customer or user and, therefore, will be able to recruit and retain more users. Third, volume of actions will also influence users’ perception about the popularity and usefulness of a firm. Firms with a larger number of competitive actions will be on the news media more often than firms with a smaller number of actions. The stronger publicity about the firms are often associated with better customer image and favorable user recommendations, which have been found to have positive impacts on users’ adoption and continuance decisions (Venkatesh et al. 2003). Finally, the greater the number of competitive actions undertaken by a firm, the more likely that it can disrupt the status quo of the industry and make it harder for competitors to respond to the numerous actions (Gnyawali et al. 2006), thus providing the focal firm the window of time and opportunity to attract new customers and solidify the customer base. We believe that the findings from these prior studies will also hold in the SNS context, as higher volume of actions often suggest higher firm capabilities and resources in meeting user demands and addressing market uncertainty, which ultimately creates significant competitive barriers for rival firms. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 4. A positive relationship exists between volume of actions and firm performance.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Sample

Single-industry samples are common in competitive dynamics research because each industry is believed to have its own norms and idiosyncratic manners of competing (Smith et al. 2001). Our empirical investigation focuses on the SNS industry because of its uniqueness, as described earlier, and the growing popularity of SNS. Also, the explosive growth of the SNS segment in the last three years makes it one of the fastest growing and dynamic segments of the Web 2.0 industry. Since the first SNS site, classmates.com, began operations around 1995, the industry has grown tremendously. For example, Facebook alone currently has more than 200 million users (Hempel 2009a, b). Much of the growth occurred during the last four to five years. Because of their popularity, SNS firms and their competitive moves are often noticed and reported in the news media. Because our data collection is focused on published news reports, media attention received by the SNS firms will help to collect reports on all aspects of firm competitive actions.

## 4.2. Data Collection

Following the methods used by prior researchers (Miller and Chen 1996, Ferrier et al. 1999, Gnyawali et al. 2008), data on firm competitive actions was collected using the Factiva database with a focus on English language newspapers. The Factiva database is the premier database that indexes all the major news reports from all major news outlets, including the Wall Street Journal, The New York Times, Associated Press, as well as hundreds of other sources. All noteworthy actions of a firm are likely to be covered by at least one news outlet. We focused on the 52 largest SNS firms (in terms of member size, as of 2007) and collected news reports on all these firms for a period of three years (2005–2007), resulting in a sample size of 156 firm years. We used page views as the measure of firm performance and collected this data using the Alexa.com site (explained later). Our sample was limited by both the availability of news reports and the page views data from Alexa.com.

To code firm competitive actions, we downloaded all of the SNS news items reported in the Factiva database during the years 2005–2007. Our search criteria required the company name to appear in the headline of the article. Overall, 15,000 articles were analyzed to identify and code relevant competitive actions during the three-year period. It should be noted that many of the articles published are stories from the Associated Press that are republished in local newspapers. These exact duplicates of articles are only counted once in our coding. For instance, the sale of a small portion of Facebook to Microsoft in 2007 resulted in hundreds of duplicate articles. Reporting of these high-profile actions contributed significantly to the number of total articles. However, each competitive action was only recorded once regardless of the number of articles. Using the list of actions, their definitions, and the keywords in the definitions, we prepared a computerized text analysis software program that would detect and flag key action words in the news reports. The software program also recorded the date, source, and number of words in the article. Using this process, we eliminated reports that did not contain purposeful competitive intent (such as an announcement of an annual report filing).

## 4.3. Coding of Competitive Actions

We started with a coding approach used by prior researchers (Miller and Chen 1996, Ferrier et al. 1999, Gnyawali et al. 2008) and refined and expanded the list of actions and categories to ensure their comprehensiveness and relevance to the SNS industry. Because competitive actions are largely industry specific, we took extensive steps to ensure that our list of actions was highly relevant to the SNS industry. We carefully examined business press and industry-specific research to better understand the industry and the nature of actions and refined the list of actions. We also sought feedback from industry experts about the relevance of the list of competitive actions. Based on this process, we identified a total of 47 different types of actions and prepared definitions of those actions for our coding purpose. We made sure that our list of actions and categories is both comprehensive and relevant to the industry context, so that future researchers could use the list to conduct further research on the industry. Using the list and definitions of actions, we pilot coded 100 news reports to ensure that the list of actions and definitions was appropriate for coding the news reports and made minor refinements on the definitions. Finally, a detailed set of coding rules was developed to ensure accuracy and consistency throughout the coding process.

Using the list of competitive actions and the procedures discussed above, we analyzed the articles by looking at the headline (and, if necessary, reading the entire article) to determine whether a competitive action was taken by a company. Using the coding process, we identified a total of 651 competitive actions (43 for 2005, 179 for 2006, and 429 for 2007). This approach of structured content analysis is in line with that of almost all prior competitive dynamics research (Miller and Chen 1996; Ferrier et al. 1999; Hambrick et al. 1996; Smith et al. 1991, 2001).

## 4.4. Measures

The dependent variable used in our study is page views (integer count), which was collected from the Alexa.com website. The SNS firms rely on the sale of advertisements, which is based on the number of users who see the advertisement. Although it would be ideal to collect firm financial performance measures, such as revenues or net income as the dependent variable, the private nature of the industry makes this option impossible. The use of a market-based performance metric such as user traffic becomes the best alternative for this industry because the industry has strong network effects and advertisement-based revenue generation that increases with the increase in page views. Researchers also suggest that Web traffic is an important nonfinancial indicator of Internet firms’ performance (Rajgopal et al. 2000, Trueman et al. 2000). Page views reflect the daily use of the website. It is reasonable to assume that the higher the page views, the more advertising revenue will be generated through user viewing and clicks.

The independent variable, codevelopment actions, is based on competitive actions related to the development of applications or services by third parties. Codevelopment actions help to increase the usefulness and value of the site to the users. The measure of codevelopment is a count of competitive actions that are focused on the use of the platform to develop new applications. If codevelopment actions occurred through alliances with technology partners, we coded such actions only as codevelopment actions, not as relational capability actions, to avoid confounding effects between the two. Thus, measure of codevelopment actions is based on the leveraging of the expertise of the codevelopers using the IT platform.

Our second independent variable is relational capability actions. To derive this measure, we took the following steps. First, we identified all competitive actions that were focused on strategic alliances with other firms (such actions excluded those focused on IT-based technological development with thirdparty developers, as noted above). Using the alliance actions, we created a list of partners of each focal firm and then converted them into a matrix. As noted earlier, prominence of alliance partners is conceptually critical, as discussed by Santos and Eisenhard (2009). We therefore sought to identify an appropriate network measure that incorporates strengths of various nodes in the network. Accordingly, we used the measure eigenvector centrality, which takes into consideration the relative strength of various nodes of the network (Kane and Alavi 2008). It is critical to consider the importance of the nodes, because in a large network, some high-degree positions are connected to many low-degree others, or some lowdegree positions are connected to a few high-degree others (Bonacich 2007). Thus, eigenvector centrality has advantages over other measures of centrality such as degree and betweenness (Bonacich 2007).

The third independent variable used is the complexity of action repertoire, which refers to the range or diversity of competitive actions (Ferrier et al. 1999, Miller and Chen 1996) undertaken by a firm. Following prior research, we calculated complexity of actions as

$$
c o m p l e x i t y = 1 - \sum (N _ {a} / \mathrm{NT} _ {L}) ^ {2},
$$

where $N _ { a } / \Nu T _ { L }$ is the proportion of competitive actions in the ath action category (see Table 1 for a description of the categories of actions).

The fourth independent variable used is volume of actions, which reflects the scale of competitive behavior. Following prior research (Ferrier et al. 1999, Gnyawali et al. 2006), we measured it as the sum of all competitive actions undertaken by a firm during the study period.

As noted earlier, the criteria of comprehensiveness and relevance to the SNS context led to the identification of 47 different types of competitive actions. We categorize these actions into 9 categories, as described in Table 1. The table also provides a brief description of each action category. These action categories were used to compute complexity of actions as described above.

We used two important control variables, firm age and prior year page views. We control for these variables because older firms might have the resources and expertise that newer firms lack. Also, future page views may be dependent on or highly influenced by prior year page views. So, we controlled for these variables. Firm age is operationalized as the number of years a firm has been offering the SNS. Prior year page views is used as a control variable to account for prior firm performance, which may impact firm competitive actions (Ferrier et al. 1999) and firm performance in subsequent periods.

As discussed above, our dependent variable is a count variable, which makes a normal ordinary least squares (OLS) regression inappropriate, as it would result in biased estimates. Because our dependent variable is an integer overdispersed and our data is a longitudinal panel data, we used population averaged generalized estimating equation (GEE) as an estimation procedure for our negative binomial panel regression. We chose GEE for the following reasons. First, the GEE provides an improvement over the generalized linear model (Liang and Zeger 1986). Hardin and Hilbe (2003) define two types of classifications of models: (1) a population-averaged model and (2) a subject-specific model. The population-averaged model is obtained by introducing a parameterization for a panel-level covariance, which is estimated across information from all panels. The population average corrects for the assumption that unobserved fixed effects are uncorrelated. Second, GEE has been discussed and recommended for use in management and organizational research (Ballinger 2004). Ballinger (2004) states that “GEEs offer researchers and managers the opportunity to use longitudinal designs with organizational topics that do not lend themselves to normally distributed responses. Management researchers are frequently faced with dependent variables that do not follow normal distributions    greater analytic precision can be gained by instead applying GEEs to the data” (p. 147). An additional benefit to using the GEE is the ability to comment on the results of a change in the independent variable on the increase in the dependent variable. Third, the population averaged GEE has been used in prior management literature (Hillman et al. 2007) in a similar context. We used lag of one quarter for the effect of competitive actions on page views. This time lag was chosen based on the rapidly changing nature of the industry, where firm competitive actions are likely to affect firm outcomes within a few months.

## 5.1. Robustness Checks

We performed several robustness checks to ensure confidence in our results. First, we performed an OLS regression with log transformation of the dependent variable and got similar results. We also ran Poisson panel regressions and got qualitatively similar results. Prior researchers have used negative binomial regression (Gnyawali et al. 2008), so we performed a negative binomial regression (cross-sectional regression) and got similar results. Moreover, because we have a few zero values in the dependent variable, we performed zero-truncated negative binomial regression and got similar results. Overall, our robustness checks suggest that the findings from our panel regression are very robust and hold well using different regression estimators.

## 6. Discussion and Implications

Firms in the digital age are blending unique technologies and business models to build competitive advantages. Unlike traditional industries in which firms try to build walls and protect advantages, firms in nascent digital industries reach out to third-party developers and other partners and undertake a wide variety of actions with these outside parties to cocreate value so that they can attract, retain, and satisfy their users or customers. Because of complexities of technologies and rapid changes in business environments, firms are increasingly reaching out for sources of advantage. The rules of the game could be changed by a major competitive action by a firm, and firms need to keep innovating their products and services to engage and lock in customers. The SNS industry is just one such example of competing in a nascent market. For example, MySpace was a first mover in music downloading and has continued to expand on its music, photo, and video capabilities. As a result, MySpace grew more than 300% from November 2005 until November 2006 (Jesdanum 2006). However, the tremendous growth did not last. Facebook, a key competitor to MySpace, experienced major growth because of several significant competitive actions it undertook in 2007, including the opening of the platform that provided development of third-party applications designed to enhance user experience and loyalty (Hempel 2009a, b).

Our study aimed to understand the nature of competitive actions and their impact on firm performance in this unique and increasingly important nascent digital industry. The high levels of ambiguity create challenges and opportunities for firms when making decisions about which competitive actions to undertake. Based on the unique nature of the SNS industry, we expected certain patterns of actions to emerge and predicted that the competitive actions would impact firm performance. Accordingly, we conceptualized that SNS firms address ambiguity in this nascent market in two major ways: (1) cocreation of value by developing and leveraging technological capabilities through third-party developers, as well as by building alliances and networks with other firms to expand the scope of the product or market, and increasing the use of the site and (2) being aggressive in the marketplace through a greater volume and complexity of competitive action repertoire so that they could discern the type of actions that provide better outcomes. Moreover, competitive actions in many fronts might enable the firms to shape the industry, signal their dominance, and enhance legitimacy. Our results showed that SNS firms enhance their performance when they undertake more codevelopment actions with third-party developers, when they collaborate with other firms to develop relational capability, and when their competitive action repertoire is complex. Although the volume of action repertoire by itself does seem to help improve firm performance, it may be hurtful when considered together with other aspects of competitive actions. Thus the results seem to indicate that higher volume of actions may not resolve ambiguity in nascent markets, but a broader range of actions, more codevelopment actions, and higher relational capability actions appear to help resolve ambiguity and enhance firm performance. The current nascent SNS industry context will change over time as the players becomes more established, dominant logic and approaches to competition emerge, and the industry becomes more mature. As the industry evolves and matures over time, it is possible that the volume of actions becomes an important determinant of firm performance as prior research has demonstrated in other mature industry contexts (Ferrier et al. 1999, Young et al. 1996).

## 6.1. Managerial and Research Implications

Our findings have important managerial implications. An understanding of the nature of competitive actions in the industry and the types of actions undertaken by dominant and successful players will help managers to better understand industry dynamics and to develop appropriate strategies to compete in the industry. Our research shows that firms leverage external resources and expertise to create value for users and that such value cocreation actions help them achieve better performance. A better understanding of the nature of competition and implications of competitive actions will allow managers to think in nuanced ways about how to successfully compete in this rapidly changing industry.

The blending of IS and IT and strategic management perspectives, as we have done in this paper, offers several intriguing research implications in both areas. We highlight a few of them here. First, we believe that the concept of value cocreation is the junction where IT and IS and strategic management fields converge. Although the bulk of strategic management research has long focused on identifying sources of competitive advantage, little attention has been paid to understanding how IS and IT-based competitive actions might drive firm competitive advantage. On the other hand, IS scholars have, for more than a decade, examined whether IT helps create value for the firm and is a source of competitive advantage (see Kohli and Grover 2008 for a succinct review on this area), yet these scholars have not empirically examined how IS and IT might help firms to undertake competitive actions. Our research is based on the central argument in the IS literature that competitive actions might serve as a missing link between IS and IT and firm performance (Sambamurthy et al. 2003). In a related manner, while strategic management research has examined the contributions of alliances and networks on firm performance, it has not been the focus of IS researchers. Our conceptualization of value cocreation through both IT and non-IT competitive actions provides a solid basis for future researchers to go deeper into the interface of IS and competitive dynamics. Instead of examining whether the amount of IT investment might help firm performance, we believe that a more interesting research direction is to investigate the extent to which a firm acts on the IT or leverages IT to undertake competitive actions.

Similarly, we explicitly show that collaborative actions improve firm performance. While some IS scholars have started to examine the impact of ITbased collaborative systems, such as supply chain management systems and CRM systems, on firm capabilities and firm performance (Rai et al. 2006, Hendricks et al. 2007), we believe that IS scholars need to be more comprehensive in examining the nature and implication of collaboration. Firms engage in a much more diversified set of collaborative actions with their developers, suppliers, other industry players, and even competitors. We believe that examination of all types of collaborative actions is important to advance research on collaboration and firm performance in digital and IT-based industries. It would be equally interesting to examine the role of IT and IS in enabling various types of collaborative actions.

For strategic management scholars, our research underscores the importance of studying competitive dynamics in technology-intensive and emerging industries and provides a conceptual basis for doing so. We have conceptually argued and empirically demonstrated that the pattern of competitive actions is different in industries based on Web 2.0 technology, because the technology allows firms to work together with developers, users, and other third parties to enhance the quality of the system and increase the breadth of service offerings. Our integration of IS research and development of the concept of value cocreation through the involvement of multiple parties provides a fertile ground for future research. While strategic management scholars have discussed the role of engaging customers and users (or cocreating value with them) for competitive advantage (Prahalad and Krishnan 2008; Cook 2008; Prahalad and Ramaswamy 2000, 2004), empirical research has not yet systematically addressed this issue. Because of data limitations, we addressed value cocreation from the standpoint of third-party developers and other alliance partners only. Future research could build on our ideas and explicitly address the role of multiple parties, including users and advertisers, and then develop a more comprehensive framework and empirically test various ways in which value cocreation occurs. Unlike in traditional industries where users are often viewed as passive consumers, users in the Web 2.0 industry are actually in charge and play very active and important roles for the Web 2.0 firms. They are codevelopers and coproducers. Our research provides a useful basis for strategic management scholars in understanding and examining value cocreation.

We also illustrate a couple of other ways in which future research could be enhanced in this area. First, progressing from the firm-level analysis of our study, future research could undertake pairlevel analyses of competitive actions by examining dyadic interaction and implications of such interactions by matching firm profiles and pair of players and competitive interactions between them (Chen 1996). Second, as more data become available, future research could examine how various moderator variables, such as a firm’s resource profiles and industry dynamism, moderate the relationship between firm competitive actions and performance. It would be interesting to examine the moderating role of the industry context using multi-industry samples. Finally, a study of coopetition in the SNS industry would be very intriguing. As we found, SNS firms are very aggressive in forming strategic alliances. In this paper, we examined all alliance actions and did not distinguish between competitor, supplier, and cross-industry alliance. As the industry evolves and alliances become more pronounced, it would be interesting to examine the extent to which alliances exist with direct competitors and what implications alliances with competitors (coopetition) will have on firm strategy and performance (Gnyawali et al. 2006). This question is especially interesting given the recent popularity of the topic of coopetition in both managerial and academic press (Brandenburger and Nalebuff 1996, Bengtsson and Kock 2000, Ketchen et al. 2004, Gnyawali et al. 2006).

Our paper has some limitations. The main limitation is the private nature of this SNS industry, which prevented us from collecting any meaningful financial measures on firm performance. Also, we could not use common firm-level control variables such as the number of employees, firm asset base, or market capitalization. Additionally, we were unable to obtain information on advertisers and the amount paid to

SNS firms. Lack of data at the user level is another important limitation. Although experienced and passionate users play an important role in improving the quality of the SNS service, we lacked user-level data on SNS firms. User-level data is unavailable, and access restrictions and privacy concerns limit researchers’ ability to gather such data. Having data on how users are engaged in the value cocreation process would have produced even more interesting findings. These limitations notwithstanding, we believe that this study generates interesting insights about the pattern of firm competitive actions in the emerging and rapidly growing SNS industry and has important implications for future research and management practice in the digital age.

In conclusion, this study is the first of its kind to specifically delve into the nature of competitive actions and their performance implications in the nascent context of SNS. We identified the pattern of competitive actions and examined the question of which types of actions provide the most benefit to firms in the nascent SNS industry. By blending the IS and strategic management literatures, we developed the constructs of value cocreation actions that reflects both IT-based and non-IT-based value cocreation actions and discussed how such actions enhance firm performance in the nascent SNS context. We further explored why and how complexity of action repertoire is critical in the SNS industry. It is our hope that our findings, together with the conceptual and empirical foundations laid by this research will be instrumental in stimulating future research, on how firms in nascent digital industries compete and create competitive advantages.
