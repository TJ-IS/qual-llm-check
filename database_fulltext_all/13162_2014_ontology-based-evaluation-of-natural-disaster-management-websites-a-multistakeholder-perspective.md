# ONTOLOGY-BASED EVALUATION OF NATURAL DISASTER MANAGEMENT WEBSITES: A MULTISTAKEHOLDER PERSPECTIVE<sup>1</sup>

Chen-Huei Chou School of Business, College of Charleston, Charleston, SC 29424 U.S.A. {ChouC@cofc.edu}

Fatemeh Mariam Zahedi and Huimin Zhao Sheldon B. Lubar School of Business, University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee, Milwaukee, WI 53201 U.S.A. {zahedi@uwm.edu} {hzhao@uwm.edu}

In recent years, the world has witnessed a number of severe natural disasters, causing heavy losses to families, communities, and even nations. Natural disaster management (NDM) websites play an important role in assisting people through various disaster stages. However, such websites are complex and there is little research on standards and guidelines for their development and evaluation. In this paper, we develop an ontology-based evaluation tool to assess the utility of NDM websites. Two main groups of stakeholders— experts who are in charge of NDM websites and potential users of such websites—contributed to the process. A total of 73 experts validated the ontology developed for NDM web elements through a Delphi study. These experts also provided importance ratings for web elements in the ontology. In a survey of the second major group of stakeholders—potential users—818 participants provided another set of importance ratings for web elements in the ontology. The design theory in this work is based on utility theory. The metrics for the evaluation of websites are relative utility and absolute utility. Using the evaluation tool, we evaluated the NDM websites of the 50 U.S. states from the perspectives of the two groups of stakeholders. The results indicate a lack of readiness in most of these websites.

Keywords: Ontology, utility theory, natural disaster management, Delphi method, website evaluation, web elements, analytic hierarchy process

## Introduction

Catastrophic natural disasters have caused devastating losses in recent years. In the 2005 hurricane season, the largest three hurricanes in the United States—Katrina, Rita, and Wilma

impacted millions of lives (American Red Cross 2005b). In August 2005, Katrina caused 90,000 square-miles of devastation and, within a month, Rita damaged and destroyed more than 350,000 homes (American Red Cross 2005a). In May 2008, the Sichuan earthquake in China killed at least 69,000 people, injured more than 374,000 (Sina.com 2008), and left about 4.8 million homeless (Relief Web 2008). Another earthquake occurred in Haiti in January 2010, causing over 200,000 deaths, 300,000 injuries, and leaving over one million people homeless (Yates and Paquette 2011). Australia’s 2010–2011 Queensland floods starting in December 2010 caused more than A\$1 billion damage, affecting more than 200,000 people.<sup>2</sup> The Pacific Ocean earthquake and subsequent tsunami in Japan in March 2011 cost the Japanese economy more than \$300 billion and caused unprecedented loss to the Japanese people, their environment, and the global economy.<sup>3</sup> Superstorm Sandy hit the United States in October 2012, impacting 24 states and affecting close to 9 million people.<sup>4</sup> Typhoon Haiyan hit the Philippines with the strongest recorded landfall in November 2013, causing more than 6,000 direct deaths and affecting close to 13 million people.<sup>5</sup>

Websites are important tools in natural disaster management (NDM). In the aftermath of the 2008 Sichuan earthquake, official government websites in China, such as China Earthquake Administration<sup>6</sup> and Sichuan Provincial Seismological Bureau,<sup>7</sup> served as disaster response and recovery systems, broadcasting the latest information related to the event. Webbased social media technologies, such as wikis and Facebook, were of value in the aftermath of the 2010 Haiti earthquake (Yates and Paquette 2011), as well as in New Zealand’s 2011 flood. In dealing with Japan’s 2011 disaster, Google created a crisis response site, which aggregated information about the disaster, including emergency shelter, water, and food locations.<sup>8</sup> A report by the U.S. Department of Commerce about Superstrom Sandy calls for increased web presence and use of social media for communications about disasters.<sup>9</sup>

Many government agencies rely on NDM websites to assist citizens through various stages of a natural disaster. In the United States, the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) and the 50 states have official NDM websites. Moreover, governmental agencies have made a case for 800- numbers and specialized channels for information dissemination between responding agencies. They also use National

Information Exchange Model (NIEM)<sup>10</sup> compliant systems for communication during emergency management.

While many NDM websites are being developed and improved as natural disasters gain prominence around the globe, there is currently no guideline for developing and evaluating such websites. NDM websites are normally complex since they need to deal with multiple types and multiple stages of disasters, various stakeholders, and numerous issues. At present, there is little published research on identifying the large and complex set of web elements needed to create comprehensive NDM websites. Once created, there is no method for evaluating the strengths and weaknesses of such websites.

These gaps motivate the research questions we address in this paper, where we focus on the evaluation of the utility of NDM websites.

• How can one validate an ontology of NDM web elements?

• Are the web elements equally important?

• How can one evaluate the utility of NDM websites?

How can one develop an evaluation tool to identify the strengths and weaknesses of NDM websites?

In answering these questions, taking the design science approach (Peffers et al. 2007), we develop an ontology-based evaluation tool and use it to evaluate the utility of the NDM websites of the 50 U.S. states in terms of comprehensiveness. The design and evaluation of NDM websites are challenging. A comprehensive NDM website contains many concepts related to different disasters and in different stages of NDM, leading to the need for thousands of web elements. The enormity of design elements has led us to take an ontology approach. The ontology approach establishes a basis for how to design, evaluate, and enhance NDM websites. In a recent study, Chou et al. (2011) developed an ontology containing more than 2,000 NDM web elements. In this paper, we validate this ontology using a Delphi study (Lindstone and Turoff 1975) involving domain experts—U.S. state government officials who manage NDM websites. Hence, the first step in this research is the development of a validated ontology of NDM web elements, which can serve as a template for designing and evaluating NDM websites and for making specific suggestions for improvement.

Web elements vary in importance. For example, web elements that identify shelters and food locations when a disaster is in progress are far more important than, say, pictures of heroes after a disaster. Therefore, the utility of an NDM website in terms of comprehensiveness is not necessarily determined by the number of web elements. The importance of web elements should also be taken into account. Both Hevner et al. (2004) and Gill and Hevner (2011) recommend examining the comprehensiveness of the design. We use the utility theory (McFadden 1986, 2001) to develop metrics for the utility of NDM websites. We need importance ratings of web elements so that we can measure a website’s utility. Hence, the second step in this research involves the elicitation of importance ratings of the web elements in the validated ontology. We do so with two major groups of stakeholders— domain experts who are in charge of NDM websites and potential users of the websites—through the Delphi study and a survey, respectively.

It has been shown that managing disaster data is a complex process and requires careful modeling (Chen et al. 2008b). This complexity is even more extensive due to the qualitative nature of NDM website contents, which have a complicated hierarchical structure. Evaluating the comprehensiveness of NDM websites requires the development of a tool that can incorporate this hierarchical structure in computing websites’ utilities at different stages of disasters, as well as in assessing their overall utilities. Such a tool should provide concrete metrics that reveal websites’ areas of strengths and weaknesses. Hence, the third step of this research is the development of an evaluation tool for NDM websites. The design theory in this step is the utility theory. To operationalize the tool, we synthesize the utility theory with the structure and algorithms of the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) (Saaty 1980, 1986; Zahedi 1985). We use the tool to evaluate the relative and absolute utilities of the official NDM websites of the 50 states in the United States. The results not only provide an overall assessment of these official websites, but also reveal shortcomings and recommend directions for improvement. Our evaluation focuses on the comprehensiveness of NDM website contents, which are domain-specific and have a complex and multistage structure. Other website attributes should also be taken into account when system-specific design elements, such as interface layout, navigation, and multidevice access, are included in the assessment (Agarwal and Venkatesh 2002; DeLone and McLean 2004; Deng and Poole 2010; Fang and Holsapple 2007; Gehrke and Turban 1999; Lee and Kozar 2006; Palmer 2002; Song and Zahedi 2005). Our results should be interpreted within a broader assessment that includes such additional elements.

This study makes several novel and significant contributions to research and practice. First, it provides a validated ontology of NDM web elements. The validated ontology contains a hierarchy of more than 2,000 elements, making this artifact a valuable source for developing NDM websites. This artifact lays a foundation for standardizing design elements for NDM websites and can be used to automate the collection and dissemination of web-based information about natural disasters. Second, the importance ratings of NDM web elements elicited from two major groups of stakeholders provide guidelines for prioritizing NDM improvements. Third, our evaluation tool provides a theoretically sound methodology for assessing the relative or absolute utilities of NDM websites. It constitutes a decision support system for the assessment of critical websites and entails metrics needed for monitoring and enhancing their comprehensiveness. Fourth, the evaluation tool reveals specific weaknesses of NDM websites and guides officials in their enhancement efforts. Finally, our work opens up a new area of research in NDM that is critical in dealing with life-threatening events at the global level, with the potential for standardization and movement toward globally managed NDM websites.

## Stages of Natural Disaster Management

Disasters may be categorized into natural disasters and manmade disasters. A natural disaster is the result of a natural hazard, such as a hurricane, tornado, or earthquake, whereas a man-made disaster is caused by humans. A natural disaster happens “when an extreme geological, meteorological, or hydrological event exceeds the ability of a community to cope with that event” (Lindell and Prater 2003, p. 176). Existing research distinguishes between disaster and crisis. Following definitions from the organizational literature and the Merriam-Webster dictionary, a crisis can be defined as an unstable condition caused by a low-probability and high-impact event at a turning point requiring a swift action for its resolution (Pearson and Clair 2008; www.merriam-webster.com). A natural disaster may have many crisis points, and natural disaster management (NDM) involves multiple stages.

There are two commonly used disaster management frameworks in the literature. (Appendix A reports a summary of the salient literature.) One focuses on crisis management and involves five stages: detection, preparation/prevention, containment/damage limitation, recovery, and learning (Hensgen et al. 2003; Pearson and Mitroff 1993). The other focuses on disaster management and involves four stages: mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery (Bakir 2004; Henderson 2004; Hwacha 2005; Mileti 1999; O’Brien et al. 2010). In this framework, the mitigation stage is intended to minimize the potential damage of disasters in a proactive manner, especially at the infrastructure level, such as regulating building codes. The preparedness stage is intended to reduce the consequences of disasters and involves community education and supplies preparation. The response stage takes place when a disaster is in progress and involves addressing immediate and short-term needs, including evacuation and rescue management. The recovery stage involves rebuilding and restoring operations after a disaster.

While these two frameworks have similar stages, they apply to different contexts and operations. The signal detection stage in crisis management is more important in organizational disasters. For natural disasters, signal detection and preparation must take place almost simultaneously. The preparation stages in disaster management take place before a disaster occurs. However, preparation for different disasters may follow different guidelines and procedures. We further divide the preparedness stage into preparation for a coming/ predicted disaster and general preparation, which is not intended for a particular disaster occurrence but for information and public education regarding general preparation and proactive mitigations for various types of potential natural disasters. Thus, we synthesize the signal detection and preparation/prevention stages from crisis management studies with the mitigation and preparation stage from disaster management studies into the general preparation stage. In addition, we add preparation for a coming/predicted disaster as a separate stage since preparation for a looming natural disaster has more urgency, specificity, and time-sensitivity than general preparation.

Thus, we propose that NDM involves five stages: general preparation, preparation for a coming/predicted disaster, disaster in progress, recovery, and learning.<sup>11</sup> Stage 1 involves general preparation for various types of potential natural disasters. The goal of this stage is to prepare and plan for future occurrences of disasters and educate stakeholders about the course of action in such circumstances. Stage 2 deals with a specific looming disaster and the plans for dealing with the coming disaster. Stage 3 deals with the disaster in progress, with the goal of assisting those in immediate danger and containing the damage. Stage 4 deals with recovery, which begins immediately after a disaster and includes a wide range of activities, such as restoration and individuals’ health recovery. Stage 5 is the learning stage, where lessons learned from the current disaster are assimilated for the refinement of general preparation.

## Ontology of NDM Web Elements

A number of data models related to emergency response have been proposed by researchers (e.g., Chen et al. 2008a; Chen et al. 2008b; Chen et al. 2013), organizational coalitions (e.g., ComCARE Alliance 2002), standardization bodies (e.g., IEEE 2000), and governmental organizations, such as the Department of Homeland Security (DHS 2008, 2012, and NIEM).<sup>12</sup> Most of these data models have been developed for data collection, recording, reporting, transmission, communication, and exchange during emergency response. Some studies have targeted a specific type of emergency, such as a fire-related incident (Chen et al. 2013) and a chemical incident (Chen et al. 2008a), or a specific aspect, such as vehicle data (ComCARE Alliance 2002).

However, Chou et al. (2011) was the first to develop an ontology specifically for NDM website elements, which was developed based on 6,032 webpages from 100 NDM websites in the United States consisting of official state websites, the FEMA website, and top search results from Google search. The resulting ontology consisted of 2,094 web elements organized into 11 hierarchical levels, covering the 5 stages of NDM. This ontology is validated in the present study and used to develop the evaluation tool.

## Ontology Validation and Rating Elicitation

The ontology of NDM web elements needed validation to serve as a basis for the evaluation tool. Furthermore, the NDM stages do not have the same hierarchical structure or number of elements, and some categories of web elements may be more important than others. Hence, we needed to validate and rate the web elements in the ontology.

## Methods

Stakeholders for NDM websites consist of two major groups: those who develop and manage NDM websites (domain experts) and those who may use the websites (potential users). The expert group is smaller, more familiar with NDM websites and elements, and more homogeneous in terms of domain competency than the potential user group. Hence, we used experts to validate the ontology. On the other hand, these two groups of stakeholders may have different utilities for web elements in the ontology. We therefore elicited importance ratings of elements from both experts and potential users. We used the two sets of importance ratings to develop the evaluation tool, thus infusing multiple perspectives.

We validated the ontology using a Delphi study (Lindstone and Turoff 1975; Marakas 2003). In order to increase the efficiency of the process, we also elicited importance ratings of elements in the same Delphi study. Access to domain experts is extremely scant, ruling out methods (such as interview) that require intensive direct interaction. Validating a complex ontology is nontrivial even for experts, prompting us to adopt a method that gives the experts a chance to reconsider and possibly revise their opinions in light of the collective opinion of their cohorts, rather than a single-round method. Furthermore, for economy, we would achieve both objectives (ontology validation and rating elicitation) through a single well-designed study, rather than multiple studies. A Delphi study allowed us to engage a relatively small panel of high-competency members and to reach some level of agreement on a complex problem through repeated opinion giving and revising without requiring direct interactions among the members.

For eliciting importance ratings from potential users, we used a survey since a Delphi study is not suitable for such a large, low-competency, and heterogeneous group. It is infeasible to go through multiple rounds with a large number of potential users. On the other hand, the survey allowed us to engage a much larger sample of subjects, reaching some level of reliability through the availability of a critical mass.

## Validation and Rating by Experts through the Delphi Study

We used a panel of experts to validate the ontology of web elements and elicit importance ratings of the elements. We targeted U.S. state officials who were in charge of their states’ NDM websites. The demand for a significant commitment from the experts during the study and the scarce availability of these officials were constraints in this process. The Delphi process had to be managed economically to accommodate the constraints. To this end, we prepared a questionnaire based on the ontology. We asked the experts to assess if they agreed with the categorization and structure of web elements in the ontology and provided space for changing the structure and adding elements. In the same questionnaire, we asked the experts to rate the importance of the web elements in the questionnaire.

The ontology contains 2,094 web elements. Web elements on levels 5 to 11 are too detailed for the experts. Elements below level 4 are mostly details that have roughly equal importance for creating a comprehensive element at levels 4 and above. For example, the subelements below the fourth level element “Warning method” consist of media, sirens, phone, and e-mail notification, which are of roughly equal importance. Moreover, moving from level 4 to level 5 would triple the number of elements to be assessed, and a pretest showed that 199 elements were about the upper limit to sustain experts’ attention and focus to careful evaluation. Therefore, the Delphi questionnaire consisted of the 199 web elements of the first four levels of the ontology.<sup>13</sup> To help the experts understand and visualize the structure of the ontology, we created a website, which provided detailed instructions for the questionnaire, an ontology browser allowing easy navigation, and an electronic version of the questionnaire. (Appendix B provides a partial snapshot.)

We pretested the instrument using two MIS faculty members and four Ph.D. students. Based on the feedback, the format of instructions was modified. The experts were identified from state government databases. (Appendix C reports the details of expert identification.) In all, 73 qualified experts working for state government emergency/disaster management departments participated in the Delphi study. (The geographical distribution and the job titles of the experts are reported in Tables C1 and C2, Appendix C.) In the first round, we mailed the questionnaire to the experts and provided access to the website. We sent e-mail follow-up in the third week after sending out the questionnaire. We used the inputs from all 73 experts for revising the ontology. However, of the 73 experts, 12 gave equal ratings to the web elements. Since the majority believed that the web elements should have different ratings, we relied on the ratings assigned by the other 61 experts for further validity checking.

We refined the ontology according to the experts’ feedback. Table 1 lists the new elements suggested by the respondents.

Our five-stage NDM framework was the result of synthesizing prior studies. However, some experts who worked at state emergency management agencies had used a four-stage framework (preparedness, response, recovery, and mitigation). To create consensus, we renamed the third and fifth stages per their suggestions, by changing “disaster in progress” to “disaster in progress and response” and “learning” to “learning and mitigation.” Mitigation at this stage involves improvements after experiencing a disaster. Thus the new elements for level 2 were incorporated in names of stages. The new level 4 elements were added appropriately to their level 4 siblings (Table E1, Appendix E).

We revised the questionnaire based on the revised ontology and sent the revised questionnaire to the 73 experts for another round. In order to carry out the Delphi study on the ratings of web elements, we also added the importance ratings expressed by the experts in the first round. The ratings were the averages of experts’ ratings, except for the web elements for “preparation for specific disasters.” For such web elements, we needed to take into account the fact that the likelihood, and therefore the rating, of preparation for specific natural disasters may vary across U.S. regions. For example, preparation for winter snow storms is more important for colder regions. Therefore, in computing the averages for ratings for specific disaster preparedness, we used the U.S. geographic divisions and regions defined by the U.S. Census Bureau (Appendix D).<sup>14</sup>

In the second round, we received 29 responses with rating revision.<sup>15</sup> The experts did not suggest any changes to the ontology. In this round, the experts showed consensus, hence providing support for the validity of the ontology. (The 209 web elements at the first four levels of the validated ontology are reported in Table E1, Appendix E.)

## Validity of the Ontology and Importance Ratings

In the second round, the experts approved most of the ratings and provided ratings for the newly-added elements. Another round of Delphi process was not promising since we expected few experts would be willing to go through a third round and the differences in ratings were not substantial. We further assessed the validity of the importance ratings in two ways: (1) agreement or lack of disagreement among the experts on their ratings of non-disaster-type-specific elements and (2) correlations between expert ratings of disaster-typespecific elements and related historical weather data.

To examine the degree of agreement among the experts, we aggregated their ratings by the four geographic regions. This was done by the state in which each expert was located. Table 2 summarizes the ratings that were similar (i.e., within a point above or below the element’s overall average) across regions, showing that experts from different regions rated the importance of non-disaster-type-specific elements similarly. The variations were due to the generally lower ratings from the experts in the Northeast region. The lower values account for roughly 9 percent of lower (different) ratings on the evaluated elements, compared to the ratings from the other three regions. In addition, there were only 10 differences in the other three regions. Among these 10 differences, 2 were nondisaster-type-specific elements at the third level of the ontology and 8 were disaster-type-specific elements at the fourth level. Overall, we found that a sufficient degree of agreement among the experts had been reached.

Furthermore, to assess the reliability of the ratings, we used Cronbach’s Alpha and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which have been used in previous Delphi studies (e.g., Ferri et al. 2005; Graham et al. 2003; Kadouri et al. 2007; Kastein et al. 1993; Scavone et al. 2006). Alpha provides a summarizing measure of internal consistency of the ratings by the experts (Graham et al. 2003). ICC provides a measure of inter-rater agreement (Kadouri et al. 2007). Conbach’s Alpha was 0.95 and ICC was 0.93, both strongly supporting the reliability of the ratings.

We needed to validate disaster-type-specific elements separately since regions vary in disaster risks. For these elements, we validated the expert ratings by examining the correlations between experts’ ratings of disaster-type-specific elements and related historical weather data. Such correlations provide a validity check on experts’ ratings. If the weather events of a geographic region make it more (or less) susceptible to a certain type of disaster, an expert from that region should have rated the preparation for that disaster type as more (or less) important. Experts’ ratings of “winter storm preparation,” “extreme heat preparation,” and “flood preparation” were validated by historical average January minimum temperature, average July maximum temperature, and annual precipitation, respectively. The rationale for comparing the rating of “winter storm preparation” to the average January lowest temperature is that winter storms usually happen in cold weather and January is historically the coldest month. Similarly, extreme heat most often occurs in July, the hottest month of the year. Regions with higher annual precipitation tend to be more susceptible to floods.

In this validation, we used the weather data from the U.S. Historical Climatology Network (USHCN) Version 2 Serial Monthly Dataset provided by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA).<sup>16</sup> The dataset includes measurements of monthly maximum and minimum temperature and monthly precipitation from many stations in 48 U.S. states, excluding Alaska and Hawaii. The weather data for Alaska and Hawaii were from the Western Regional Climate Center.<sup>17</sup> We used weather data from 1951 to 2009 (the year we finished data collection from the experts) for the correlation analysis. We used the average expert importance ratings for 31 states, where the experts resided.

Table 3 reports the correlation analysis results. The expert ratings of the “winter storm preparation” web element are significantly negatively correlated to the January lowest temperatures of the states of the experts. The expert ratings of the “extreme heat preparation” web element are significantly positively correlated with the states’ July highest temperatures. The expert ratings of the “flood preparation” web element are significantly positively correlated with the states annual precipitations. Appendix F reports the correlations for more than 50 years on the annual basis, strongly supporting the significance of correlations between the expert ratings of web elements and the related weather events. Thus, we concluded that the participants indeed had expertise on the natural disaster events and provided ratings carefully and rationally. Furthermore, the variation in ratings of the disaster-typespecific preparations correctly reflected the likelihood of the natural disasters in the geographical locations. This provided further support for the validity of the elicited importance ratings of the web elements.

## Importance Rating Elicitation from Potential Users

We also elicited importance ratings from potential users based on the validated ontology. This was needed in order to evaluate NDM websites from potential users’ perspectives. A web-based survey was developed for collecting data from users. The instrument included all five stages of NDM with elements at the first four levels of the validated ontology.

Evaluation of a complex artifact requires consideration of context. In the Delphi study, the experts implicitly derived the context from their jobs. In this survey of potential users with low domain familiarity/competency, we explicitly instructed the respondents of the context. For each stage, the stage was described and users were asked to imagine themselves being in the context of the given stage.<sup>18</sup> This was done to condition respondents’ mindset as experiencing the stage, establishing the context for evaluation. To help the respondents understand the ontology, items were placed in the instrument in such a way that it started from the leaf level with specific elements and then gradually moved up to more abstract categories.<sup>19</sup> The instrument was pilot-tested with nine respondents and their comments were used to make minor modifications.

Participants were recruited from students in undergraduate and graduate classes in two U.S. universities, in the Midwest and Southeast, respectively. Each student was given the task of recruiting at least three respondents from family, friends, and acquaintances to participate in the online survey. The incentive for recruitment was a small extra credit in course grade. Recruiters were given instructions to ensure the quality of data collection and to reach a broad population. Respondents had the option to participate in four random drawings of \$100 cash prizes.

The participating students sent out 1,167 survey requests, which resulted in 1,026 responses. In order to ensure the validity of responses, we examined and cleansed the data in three rounds. In the first round, we removed the records with many unanswered questions. In the second round, we examined the time each respondent spent in completing the survey. Our pretests had shown that to answer the survey carefully, a minimum of six minutes would be needed. We therefore removed the observations where the respondents had taken less than six minutes to complete the survey. In the third round, we removed the responses that had the same rating for all elements. This was done to ensure that the data reflected thoughtful and careful responses to each question. The final dataset had a total of 818 usable observations, of which 46.5 percent were male and 53.5 percent female. (Appendix G reports the age distribution and experiences with different disaster types.)

The data showed a strong support for reliability (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.99, ICC = 0.99). Furthermore, expert and user ratings were overall compatible. T-tests on the importance ratings between experts and users did not show significant differences $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ in 84.1 percent of the elements. The correlation of average rating between experts and users was 0.69 (p < 0.0001.)

## Ontology-Based Evaluation Tool

The development of the evaluation tool involved a theorybased design, a method-driven architecture, and an implementation that relied on web-based Java technologies.

## Theoretical Basis

Gill and Hevner (2011) suggest that the usefulness of design must be established rigorously using a utility or fitness approach. Our evaluation tool for NDM websites was built based on the multiattribute utility theory. Since the validated ontology had a complex hierarchical structure, we used the structure and algorithms of AHP for computing the relative and absolute utilities of web elements to arrive at metrics for website comprehensiveness. The utility theory has its origin in economics and seeks to quantify the utility of various attributes (Von Neumann and Morgenstern 1944). It is based on human rationality, constitutes the foundation for rational decision making, and is used to determine the preferences for various alternatives or choices based on the utility of their attributes (Keeney and Raiffa 1976). Utility functions quantify preferences for alternatives that have multiple attributes.

A utility function is formulated as $U = f ( \{ ( w _ { i } , u _ { i } ) \vert i = I , 2 ,$ $M \Game$ , where f is the functional form of the utility, M is the number of attributes of the alternative, u is the utility of attribute i, and $w _ { i }$ reflects the weight or importance of the attribute<sup>20</sup> (Keeney and Raiffa 1976). Utility functions can take different functional forms, such as linear or multiplicative (Keeney and Raiffa 1976; Zahedi 1986b). When attribute utilities (u ) are additive, the utility function takes a linear form. In the absence of a compelling reason, the linear utility function is the most commonly used functional form. Applied to the case of NDM websites, the utility of an alternative a (in our case, a website) may be computed as $\begin{array} { r } { U _ { a } = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { M } w _ { i } u _ { a i } , } \end{array}$ where $U _ { a }$ is the utility of website $^ { a , }$ M is the number of web elements, and $w _ { i }$ and $u _ { a i }$ are the weight and utility of each web element—the website’s attribute.

Were the structure of NDM websites so simple that the ontology of web elements had only one level, the linear utility function would have sufficed in the quantification of the websites’ utilities. That is not the case for the NDM ontology, which has a hierarchy of up to 11 levels (see Figure 1). This hierarchy of web elements should be accounted for in the computation of the websites’ utilities. We used AHP algorithms in the computation of utility values.

AHP allows the decomposition of a decision problem into a hierarchy of multiple attributes (or criteria) in which the elements at a higher level contain more detailed elements with increased granularity (Saaty 1980, 1986; Zahedi 1985). The top level of an AHP hierarchy is the goal of the decisionmaking problem (in our case, the utility of a website), while the bottom level consists of the decision alternatives (in our case, the websites being evaluated). AHP examines the goal of a decision-making problem by aggregating the relative importance of elements at the various levels of a hierarchical structure in a bottom-up manner (Figure 1).

AHP has been successfully applied to many diverse and complex decision problems (Zahedi 1986a). Several prior studies have used AHP in ranking for different purposes. For instance, Anane et al. (2008) applied it to locate and rank suitable sites for waste water treatment. Babic and Plazibat (1998) used it to rank enterprises based on their business efficiency. Sinuany-Stern (1988) predicted the ranking of soccer teams based on selected criteria. While the original development of AHP relied on the relative comparisons of attribute utilities, it is possible to use absolute rating of attributes in AHP, particularly when the attribute set is large.

Hence, we compute the utilities of websites in an additive form using the AHP method from two perspectives: relative and absolute. In the relative term, we assume that the set of websites under evaluation is fixed, and compute the relative utility of each website as compared to others in the set. This corresponds to the “closed system” axiom in AHP (Forman and Gass 2001). In the absolute term, we make no assumption regarding the number of websites under evaluation and compute the utility of each website regardless of other websites. This corresponds to the “open system” assumption in AHP (Forman and Gass 2001). The absolute computation is in line with the utility theory, which does not assume that the alternative set is fixed. The two perspectives reveal different aspects of NDM websites. The relative utility shows the standing of a website compared to others. This comparison reveals the weaknesses or strengths of a website when compared to its cohorts, allowing for rankings in relative terms. For example, if all websites have the same element, none would be rated above the others. Conversely, if all websites are missing the same web element, none would be rated below the others. This metric is useful for relative standing of NDM websites.

Figure 1. AHP Structure for Computing the Utilities of NDM Websites

The absolute utility of an NDM website shows its performance as compared to an ideal website that has all elements of the ontology. The strength of this metric is that it is not affected by other websites in the alternative pool. If a website is missing an element, the website’s utility would be lower, regardless whether other websites have the element. The absolute rating is useful for revealing the weaknesses of a website.

## Architecture of the Ontology-Based Evaluation Tool

Figure 2 illustrates the architecture of the evaluation tool. The key components of the tool include (1) the validated ontology of web elements, (2) importance ratings of web elements, (3) the hierarchy structure of the evaluation, (4) the utilities of web elements using the validated ontology, (5) the weight assessments, and (6) the computation algorithms. The first two components have already been discussed. The rest are covered in this section.

Hierarchy Structure. The ontology for NDM web elements has a hierarchical structure, which fits the hierarchy structure in AHP. Using the AHP approach, the root of the hierarchy is the utility of a website. Web elements are considered as attributes in the hierarchy. At the second level, the five stages of NDM are used as broad attributes. Each attribute may be further decomposed into subattributes according to the validated ontology. An element that is not further decomposed is referred to as an atomic element. Finally, the set of websites to be evaluated constitute the decision alternatives, which are connected to all atomic elements.

Element Utility Evaluation. The utility function in the utility theory has a flat attribute structure. For each alternative a (each website in our case), the utility of each attribute i (each website element), $u _ { a i } ,$ is assessed and used in $\begin{array} { r } { U _ { a } = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { M } w _ { i } u _ { a i } . } \end{array}$

![](/api/attachments/W9ARTJ8Y/fulltext/images/c38696f14aabd359102ca39af9d8719c5f9f0d35c9b98731ee7efb976a93dd9d.jpg)  
Figure 2. Architecture of the Ontology-Based NDM Evaluation Tool

In a hierarchical attribute structure, we observe that $u _ { a i }$ is the utility of atomic element i. Each alternative a $( a = I , 2 , . . . , N )$ has M atomic elements. Based on AHP, we can assess $u _ { a i }$ in one of two ways: pairwise comparison or comparison with an ideal. In pairwise comparison, the utility of atomic element $i ( i = I , 2 , . . . , M )$ in alternative a is compared with that of other alternatives, generating $M N \times N$ matrices. We refer to this approach as relative utility assessment. The advantage of the pairwise comparison is that the utilities are elicited relative to utilities of other alternatives (websites). The pairwise comparison approach compares two items at a time, thus alleviating the cognitive load required in comparing multiple items simultaneously. In comparison with an ideal, the utility of $u _ { a i } \left( a = I , 2 , . . . , N \right)$ is elicited by comparing element i of alternative a with an ideal $U _ { i } { \mathrm { - i n } }$ our case, the element i on the ontology. The ideal comparison results in M N-dimension vectors. Since this comparison is not dependent on other alternatives, we call it absolute utility assessment.

The element evaluation can be subjective or objective. Subjective evaluation is performed by an individual, identifying the value of $\dot { u } _ { a i } .$ In subjective evaluation, the evaluator needs to judge the qualities of the implementations of an atomic element by the alternatives. In objective evaluation, an objective measure is used. For example, the utility of a website with regard to an atomic web element may be measured by a binary variable, indicating the presence or absence of the web element on the website.

Weight Assessment. In the utility theory, the weights of ${ u _ { a i } } ^ { \prime } s \mathrm { - } { w _ { i } } ^ { \prime } s .$ —are assessed directly and used in the utility formula. However, in complex hierarchical structures, these weights have a hierarchical structure, and should be assessed within the hierarchy of attributes. Level 1 of the hierarchy has one element representing the overall utility of an alternative. At the lower levels of the hierarchy $( k = 2 , 3 , . . . , K )$ attributes are structured in increasing granularity. For example, at level 2, there are five stages of NDM. These stages do not have equal importance weight/rating. The importance weights of attributes at each level can be assessed in one of two ways: pairwise comparison or direct assessment. In pairwise comparison, the attributes are compared pairwise. In direct assessment, the weights are directly rated. When the number of attributes is large, direct assessment by experts is shown to be an efficient approach (Ramadhan et al. 1999).

Utility Computation Algorithms. We apply the AHP algorithms to compute the relative and absolute utilities of NDM websites. Each level k of the hierarchy $( k = 2 , 3 , . . . , K )$ has local assessments for weights. For example, for level 2, there is a five-dimension vector of weights for the five stages of NDM, if direct weight assessment is used. Should the weights of the five stages be assessed using the pairwise comparison approach, there would be a $5 \times 5$ matrix of pairwise comparisons. The five-dimension vector of weights would be estimated using the Saaty’s eigenvalue algorithm: $\hat { A } \hat { W } = \lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } } \hat { W }$ , where is the pairwise comparison matrix,A $\hat { W }$ is the estimated five-dimension vector of weights for the five stages of NDM, and $\lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } }$ is the largest eigenvalue. The latter is used to measure the consistency of pairwise comparisons by $\frac { \lambda _ { \operatorname* { m a x } - n } } { n - 1 }$ , where n is the number of elements (5 in this example) (Saaty 1980, 1986). The same algorithm is applied whenever pairwise comparison is used to elicit utilities or weights.

The weight vector at each level of the hierarchy is labeled “local” since it is local to that level (Saaty 1990). The local weight of an element reflects its importance relative to its siblings (i.e., children of the same parent), whereas the global weight reflects the overall importance of the element relative to all elements in the hierarchy. The local weights are normalized such that those of siblings sum to one, that is, $\Sigma _ { c \in C h i l d r e n ( i ) } l w ( c ) = 1$ for i  E\Atom, where E denotes the set of elements, \Atom denotes the exclusion of the atomic elements, lw(c) denotes the local weight of element $c ,$ and Children(i) denotes the set of children of element i.

The global weight of each atomic element is then computed based on its local weight as well as the local weights of its ancestors. $\begin{array} { r } { w _ { i } = \prod _ { p \in p a t h ( i ) } l w ( p ) } \end{array}$ for i 0 Atom, where w denotes the global weight of element i, and path(i) denotes the set of elements along the path from the root to element i. It can be easily shown that the global weights of all atomic elements sum to one, that is, $\Sigma _ { i = 1 } ^ { M } w _ { i } = 1$

In the last step, utilities of NDM websites are generated based on the global weights of the atomic elements computed in the previous step. The overall utility of an alternative a is computed by aggregating its $u _ { a i }$ of the atomic elements weighted by the global weights of the atomic elements, that is, $U _ { a } =$ $\Sigma _ { i = 1 } ^ { M } w _ { i } u _ { a i } .$ At this step, one of two synthesis modes, distributive or ideal (Forman and Gass 2001), may be used in deriving $u _ { a i } ,$ the utility of the atomic element i in alternative a. In the distributive mode, the utility of an atomic element i is normalized such that the shares of all alternatives sum to one, that is, $\begin{array} { r } { u _ { a i } ^ { \prime } = \frac { u _ { a i } } { \sum _ { a = 1 } ^ { N } u _ { a i } } } \end{array}$ and $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { a = 1 } ^ { N } u _ { a i } ^ { \prime } = 1 } \end{array}$ , and $\begin{array} { r } { U _ { a } = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { M } w _ { i } u _ { a i } ^ { \prime } } \end{array}$ The distributive mode accounts for the abundance or dilution effect (Forman and Gass 2001), that is, if a website has an element which others do not, the element becomes more valuable and is rewarded more—relative utility. We refer to this approach as relative synthesis. If the distributive mode is used, the overall utilities of all alternatives sum to one, that is, $\textstyle \sum _ { a = 1 } ^ { N } U _ { a } = 1$

In the ideal mode, the best alternative for an atomic element receives the maximum utility of the element while each of the other alternatives gets a share proportionate to its utility with regard to the element, that is, $\begin{array} { r } { u _ { a i } ^ { \prime } = \frac { u _ { a i } } { M a x _ { a = 1 } ^ { N } u _ { a i } } } \end{array}$ . Adopting a hypothetical “ideal” alternative such that $M a x _ { a = 1 } ^ { N } u _ { a i } = \mathrm { U } _ { i } ,$ the element utility of this ideal alternative, for all i 0 Atom, we refer to this approach as absolute synthesis as U is independent of any particular alternative. The rationale for the ideal mode is that an ideal alternative serves as a reference while each of the other alternatives receives a utility proportionately less (Forman and Gass 2001).

The use of relative utility assessment and/or relative synthesis leads to relative utilities, which are dependent on the set of alternatives under evaluation. The use of absolute utility assessment and absolute synthesis corresponds with the utility theory and leads to the absolute utility of each website independent of other websites. The absolute utility of an NDM website shows how good the website is regardless of what other websites contain.

While we have presented the methods as a means to evaluate NDM websites with regard to overall utility, the methods can also be used to evaluate particular areas and subareas. This can be achieved by restricting the evaluation to a subhierarchy of the design hierarchy, making it possible to evaluate websites at various levels of granularity.

## Implementation

We implemented the tool in Java. The major components of the tool include the ontology, relative importance ratings of web elements, and the algorithms for utility computations. We represented the ontology using XML DOM (document object model). We used Xerces,<sup>21</sup> a Java implementation of DOM, to traverse the ontology. Xerces provides a modular framework through its native interface. We used its API to create an application for storing and traversing the ontology specified in XML. The information and structure of elements in the XML file are parsed and kept in a DOM representation, which maintains the structure of NDM web elements and keeps such values as relative importance ratings and the utilities of the implementations of elements by each NDM website under evaluation. This DOM tree maps its element nodes to the corresponding nodes in the AHP hierarchy. We validated the accuracy of the AHP computations using test cases against the commercial AHP software Expert Choice™.<sup>22</sup>

The buttons located in the center right provide functionalities for different purposes. The subwindow on the bottom displays the output based on the operation performed, such as node statistics and AHP computation result. The tool can be used to evaluate and rank the utilities of NDM websites in relative or absolute terms. All options of element utility evaluation, weight assessment, and utility computation discussed earlier are supported in this tool.

## Evaluation of the NDM Websites of the 50 U.S. States

We applied the evaluation tool to assess the utilities of the official NDM websites of the 50 U.S. states (Appendix H lists the URLs.) To ensure fair comparison, we took a snapshot of these websites collected within a two-week period. We used the objective approach in assessing element utilities, emphasizing the presence or absence of each element as its utility measure. Our evaluation focused on the utility of a website in terms of the comprehensiveness of its elements.

We argue that for such critical websites as NDM websites, which are used by a variety of stakeholders in different types and stages of disasters, website utility should be first assessed in terms of comprehensiveness (i.e., containing needed elements).

We used direct assessment of element weights—considering the large number of elements—by using the ratings elicited from the experts in the Delphi study and from potential users in the survey study. For non-disaster-type-specific elements, we used the overall average ratings. For disaster-typespecific elements, we used the regional average ratings (Table E2, Appendix E). Below Level 4 of the ontology, the subelements of an element have more or less equal importance. For example, the subelements of “Training for preparation” include various methods of training, such as courses, seminars, workshops, and conferences, which have more or less equal weights. Differentiation at this level of granularity is difficult and may be useful for personalization of websites. However, the presence or absence of each subelement is important for assessing the utility of a website in terms of comprehensiveness. We used both relative (with the distributive synthesis mode) and absolute utility computation algorithms.

The evaluation tool allows for comparison at different levels of granularity. Therefore, our analysis results in a wealth of information regarding the performance of the 50 states in terms of their relative and absolute utilities at the five stages of disasters and various levels of the ontology hierarchy from the perspectives of experts and potential users.

## Discussion of Results

The relative and absolute utilities allow website evaluation from different perspectives. Relative utilities make it possible to compare a state’s website relative to others. Relative utilities single out websites that contain more important web elements, since elements that are commonly available in all websites do not distinguish one state over others. At the same time, relative utilities single out those websites lacking elements that are common in most other websites. Absolute utility reveals a state’s performance in having all web elements in the ontology regardless of other states, indicating the weaknesses of the website compared to the ontology. The rankings based on relative and absolute utilities do not always agree since relative utilities compare a website against others in containing the elements of the ontology, whereas absolute utilities directly compare the website against the ontology such that if a website contains all the elements in the ontology, its utility should be 100 percent. We will focus on presenting and discussing absolute utility results. Appendices I and J report the relative utility results.

## Evaluation of Overall Utilities

Figure 4 reports the distributions of overall absolute utilities of the 50 states for experts and users. The distributions are skewed with a long right-tail. Only five states had more than 35 percent of absolute utilities; the rest were below 35 percent with the mode hovering around 25 percent. Similar results for relative utilities are reported in Appendix I. Table 4 reports overall absolute utilities of the 50 websites based on ratings from experts and potential users.

Experts’ and users’ evaluations were quite similar. The correlation of experts’ and users’ absolute utilities was quite high (Pearson’s r = 0.999), so was the correlation of experts’ ranks and users’ ranks (Kendall’s τ = 0.970, Spearman’s ρ = 0.997). The uniformity of results from two distinct groups of stakeholders with different methods of data collection supports the robustness of the results. North Carolina, Mississippi, Hawaii, and Virginia were the best, with absolute utility values in the 35–40 percent range, whereas Utah, Idaho, West Virginia, Wyoming, and New Hampshire were the worst, with absolute utility values below 24 percent, according to both the experts and users. The low absolute utilities (below 40 percent) show that all states still had a long way to go toward building comprehensive websites for managing natural disasters.

## Evaluation of Websites at the Five Stages of Natural Disaster Management

The analysis resulted in the assessment of the websites for their utilities in the five stages of NDM. The results for the 50 U.S. states’ websites for each of the five NDM stages are reported in Appendix K (Tables K1 to K5). Like in the overall utilities, experts’ and users’ utility assessments were highly correlated for all five stages (Pearson’s r > 0.997), so were experts’ and users’ ranks (over 0.96 Kendall’s τ and Spearman’s ρ values), thus providing further support for the robustness of the evaluation results.

The results show that the states were quite different in terms of the utilities of their website contents for various stages of NDM and can learn from the best performers at each stage. There were larger differences across states in some stages of disaster management, and most states had low utility values for those stages. For stages 1, 3, 4, and 5, more than 14, 27, 32, and 39, states had absolute utilities below 25 percent, respectively. This finding shows an urgent need for improvement, particularly in the states (such as South Carolina and Texas) with high risk of natural disasters.

## Post Hoc Analysis

In a post hoc analysis of the association of users’ profiles with their ratings of preparation for various disaster types, we explored the association of age, experience, and gender in users’ evaluations of disaster types by regressing disaster preparation ratings on gender, age, and experience with the disaster type. Regression results are reported in Appendix L. The post hoc analysis indicated that female users have rated disaster preparation higher than male users in all 14 disaster types. Regarding the influence of age, the results showed that age has no influence of ratings for flood, winter storm, wildfire, and hail. It has a significant coefficient for tornado preparation, indicating that older users assigned higher ratings to tornado preparation, whereas younger users assigned higher ratings to preparation for the remaining disaster types.

## Implications

In this paper, we developed a web-based tool for evaluating the comprehensiveness of NDM websites and used the tool to assess the relative and absolute utilities of the NDM websites of the 50 U.S. states from the perspectives of two major groups of stakeholders: experts and potential users. This work has both theoretical and empirical implications.

## Theoretical Implications

This study has major theoretical implications. First, it opens up a new research stream in an important domain, NDM. NDM websites are complex and must address the needs of multiple stakeholders going through stages of NDM with varying time lines, some immediate, urgent, and lifethreatening (e.g., disaster in progress) and some with a longterm scope (e.g., learning and mitigation). The complexity and criticality of NDM websites make it difficult to build such websites and evaluate their comprehensiveness. This research makes a novel and significant contribution by proposing validated tools to assist in the design and building of such websites and for the assessment of their comprehensiveness.

The second major contribution of this work is the development of theory-based metrics, in terms of relative utility and absolute utility, to compare the comprehensiveness of websites as well as to evaluate their comprehensiveness against an ideal ontology. This development is in line with Gill and Hevner’s (2011) guidelines that the usefulness of design must be established rigorously by using a utility or fitness approach. In this work, we show that synthesizing the utility theory with the structure and algorithms of AHP makes it possible to develop an evaluation tool that can manage the complex hierarchical structure of NDM web elements. This work shows that theory-based metrics provide the rigor and relevance needed in dealing with complex design issues in real-world settings.

This work also shows the critical role of ontology as a foundation for creating and assessing a complex design. The synthesis of ontology with the design theory is a powerful combination, as illustrated in this work. We used the Delphi method and weather data to validate our ontology and the importance ratings of web-design elements. This is another novel contribution, illustrating that combining design, behavior, and statistical methods increases the validity and reliability of the artifact. This work established a rated, validated ontology for the design of NDM websites, which can be expanded to man-made disasters, to a global ontology, and to the standardization of NDM websites.

The similarity of results from more than 800 users with those of experts is another interesting finding in this work. It indicates that natural disasters are of real concern to all people, experts and users alike. The wisdom of crowd has been the topic of many studies and applications (see, for example, Kittur et al. 2007; Kittur and Kraut 2008). In a recent study, it has been reported that with careful structuring of the process, the crowd could perform an under-water fish recognition task that “typically requires profound domain knowledge in marine biology” at the level of experts (He et al. 2013, p. 57). Our results confirm these findings, indicating that with a suitably designed instrument, the crowd could provide a reasonable approximation of expert opinions, since many users have had experience with natural disasters, are aware of the dangers such events pose to their personal safety, and could accurately judge the importance of various elements through their first-hand knowledge.

Finally, the results of post hoc analysis indicate that users differ in ratings of preparation for different disaster types, indicating a need for personalization of disaster management websites based on users’ profiles. This is an area of future research.

## Empirical Implications

This work also has a number of empirical implications. As our risk for natural disasters is increasing, so is the urgency of getting ready for them. Web readiness is increasingly recognized as an important aspect of natural disaster readiness. Knowledge, methodologies, and tools for developing and evaluating NDM websites have real value and could save lives and assets. Our work shows that online disasterreadiness can be evaluated objectively and rigorously from the perspectives of multiple stakeholders.

The practical usefulness of our work has been demonstrated in its use to assess the online readiness of the 50 U.S. states. Our analysis revealed a lack of preparation for NDM using websites by most states. This is stunning given the fact that websites have become a common channel of communication to reach the public asynchronously. Our work identified the areas of weakness for each state in the five stages of NDM. This work can be used to enhance the websites of the states and assist in prioritizing the areas that need improvement.

Our evaluation results provide pertinent government officials with valuable insights and recommendations. Government officials in charge of NDM have a critical role in dealing with disasters. Our results allow the federal government, states, and municipalities to measure their online readiness and compare it with others. This comparison is an important learning approach since following best practices, once identified, is a strong motivator for improvement and enhancement for disaster management.

The validated ontology developed in this work can also be used by governments and private organizations as a guideline for deciding on the content that should be part of NDM websites. The elements at the lower levels of the ontology could be modified to fit the needs of private organizations. At the top level, the ontology can serve both public and private organizations in enhancing their disaster readiness. As the ontology is used by governments and private organizations, concerns may arise for gaps and overlaps as well as redundant and conflicting information. The ontology can form a basis of negotiations as well as for establishing principles and standards that can dictate the distribution of rights and issues across groups in public–private partnerships.

The evaluation tool can be used to identify critical weaknesses in the websites of other governmental entities in the United States and in other countries. As such, the tool can serve as a diagnostic or auditing tool for regions that have a high risk of exposure to natural disasters.

## Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Directions

In this paper, we address an important problem of evaluating NDM websites in terms of their utilities for various stages of NDM. To that end, we presented an ontology-based evaluation tool for the evaluation of the comprehensiveness of NDM websites in terms of their relative and absolute utilities. We applied the evaluation tool to assess the utilities of the official NDM websites of the 50 states in the United States. The design theory in this work was based on the ontology approach and the utility theory, which we synthesized with the structure and algorithms of AHP to develop the evaluation tool.

Our current work has a number of limitations, which provide opportunities for future extensions. Our ontology validation involved two of the key stakeholder groups. One extension of this study could be examining the utility measures associated with those persons outside the direct disaster zone who are trying to seek information about persons in the zone. The better the website at handling this group of users, the more resources that can be directly deployed to the disaster zone. Currently, many of the telecommunication systems and other resources get overloaded by persons outside the zone trying to gather information about what is happening within the zone, thus detracting from the task at ground zero.

The data for website contents were collected at one point in time for the analysis reported in this paper. Since the contents of websites change on a regular basis, it is important to reassess these websites to identify their improvements and areas of possible integration. Furthermore, in this paper, we focused on the evaluation of comprehensiveness of NDM website informational contents, which are domain-specific with a multistage structure and extensive contents. The extension of the research on the evaluation of NDM websites should include features that are more system-specific, such as layout, presentation, navigation, performance, access on multiple devices, and customization. Such an extension could benefit from a large body of literature on websites’ general evaluation and success of websites (e.g., Agarwal and Venkatesh 2002; DeLone and McLean 2004; Deng and Poole 2010; Fang and Holsapple 2007; Gehrke and Turban 1999; Lee and Kozar 2006; Palmer 2002; Song and Zahedi 2005). The evaluation results presented in this paper must be interpreted in the broader context of an overall NDM website assessment.

This work focused on managing people in the process and stages of natural disasters. This focus needs to be extended to include detailed elements for NDM for businesses, as well as for man-made (accidental or intentional) disaster management. Among man-made disasters are terrorist actions. The ontology and the evaluation tool could be extended to accommodate special features of these domains.

We validated the top four levels of the ontology and elicited importance ratings of the elements at these levels. An even larger study is needed to validate the entire ontology and collect importance rating of all elements.

The evaluation of the states’ NDM websites was based on objective element evaluation (i.e., whether an element is present on a website). This approach should be extended to include the quality of web elements through subjective evaluation of web elements by experts and users. This makes it possible to distinguish the ease of use and comprehensiveness of web elements for users. This is critical since, in some stages of disasters, people act under time constraints and with high anxiety. They require clarity and ease of use. Even for the general preparation and learning/mitigation stages, usability of web elements and the website is of critical importance since people could easily get confused and lose interest if the website lacks usability.

A challenge for future extensions of this work is in the fact that websites are regularly being updated. Therefore, there is a need for an automatic or semi-automatic method of collecting web elements and updating the ontology in order to keep it up-to-date. This requires an intelligent system that can identify elements and discover new elements on NDM websites. Another possible future research direction is the extension of this ontology to include international elements and use it for the creation of standards for NDM web elements.

## Identification Process and Geographical Distribution of the Experts in the Delphi Study

In order to validate and rate the elements of our developed ontology structure, we needed to assemble experts in the domain of natural disaster management from all regions of the United States. Our goal was to have a good geographic coverage of experts in the 50 U.S. states. We made every effort to have a large pool from the 50 states in order to achieve adequate geographical coverage. That is, a total of 50 respondents, one from each state, would give us full geographic coverage of the 50 states, while a total of 50 respondents from one state would be very poor geographic coverage.

State government officers who worked in the department of disaster/emergency management were considered potential experts knowledgeable in this domain. Based on a list of 50 official state emergency/disaster management websites, we identified over 1,500 potential experts who were working for state governments in the United States. The contact data were assembled from the websites listed in Appendix H. The data were located on various pages on the websites, including About Us, Contact Us, Functional Divisions, Organization Charts, and Staff List, or by a search in the database of the state government. We further screened the pool of potential participants based on their functions and job titles that could be potentially related to the domain of disaster management. For example, those who were responsible for financial analysis, accounting, law enforcement, and so on were excluded from the pool. We kept contact data for those whose job titles were, for example, director of emergency management agency, director of functional divisions such as preparedness, training, planning, response, recovery, and mitigation. The final pool consisted of 1,485 names of individuals whose jobs and expertise could be potentially suitable for our purpose.

Letters for invitation for expert participation were sent to the pool. After three weeks, 718 reminder e-mails were sent to potential experts for whom we had identifiable e-mail addresses. We received 73 responses from experts whose jobs were related to disaster management and who were willing to serve as experts in our Delphi process. This gave us 100% coverage of four regions (north, south, east, and west), 100% coverage of divisions (New England, Middle Atlantic, East North Central, West North Central, South Atlantic, East South Central, West South Central, Mountain, and Pacific), and 78% coverage of states. Appendix D provides a map of regions and divisions.

In Round 2, only those who needed to provide further updates provided second responses, which included 29 experts from 18 states. For the subsequent analysis, we cleansed the data by removing ratings of experts whose responses were all 10, which indicated a lack of attention and commitment to the study. The final pool consisted of 61 experts in Round 1 and 29 in Round 2, all of whom were active in managing websites of 31 states, providing us with 100% region and division coverage and 62% of states.
