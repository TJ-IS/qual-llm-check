# Speed Matters: The Role of Free Software Offer in Software Diffusion

Zhen grui Jian g and Sumi t Sarkar

Zhengrui Jiang is an Assistant Professor of Management Information Systems at the College of Business, Iowa State University. He received his Ph.D. in information systems from the University of Texas at Dallas. His research interests include the impact of data quality on decision making, software economics, and software project management. His research has appeared in journals, including Management Science, Information Systems Research, and International Journal of Research in Marketing, and in conferences such as the International Conference of Information Systems, Workshop on Information Technologies and Systems, and Pacific Asia Conference on Information Systems.

Sumit Sarkar is an Ashbel Smith Professor of Information Systems at the School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas. His received his Ph.D. from the University of Rochester. His research focuses on the management of data and knowledge in business environments. He has published in leading journals, including Management Science, Information Systems Research, INFORMS Journal on Computing, ACM Transactions on Database Systems, IEEE Transactions on Data and Knowledge Engineering, Transportation Science, and Decision Sciences. He has served as a program cochair for the International Conference of Information Systems and Workshop on Information Technologies and Systems and serves on the editorial board of Management Science, Information Systems Research, and Information Technology and Management, among other journals.

Abs tract: Many software products are available free of charge. While the benefits resulting from network externality have been examined in the related literature, the effect of free offer on the diffusion of new software has not been formally analyzed. We show in this study that even if other benefits do not exist, a software firm can still benefit from giving away fully functioning software. This is due to the accelerated diffusion process and subsequently the increased net present value of future sales. By adapting the Bass diffusion model to capture the impact of free software offer, we provide a methodology to determine the optimal number of free adopters. We show that the optimal free offer solution depends on the discount rate, the length of the demand window, and the ratio of low-valuation to high-valuation free adopters. Our methodol ogy is shown to be applicable for both fixed and dynamic pricing strategies.

Key words and phras es : Bass model, diffusion of innovations, dynamic pricing, free software.

An interes ting phenomenon in today’s market is the availability of a wide variety of free software, ranging from business and professional software such as Web servers, operating systems, and programming languages, to consumer software such as word processors, Web browsers, and multimedia software. Based on their commercial objective, we classify free software into three broad categories. The first category is open source software, which is developed and maintained by volunteer contributors; the two most well-known examples in this category are Linux and Apache. The second category includes proprietary software products that will never generate any direct revenue; they are offered free because firms are seeking other economic benefits, such as boosting sales of complementary goods. Examples include Netscape, Internet Explorer, and Java. In the third category, offering free software is employed as a marketing technique to accelerate the sale of proprietary software, which will generate direct revenue after the promotional period. In this paper, we study free software in the third category.

Free software offer as a marketing technique is being employed by a growing number of software firms. Their free offer practices vary. Some firms offer versions with limited functionality or time limitation; according to prior research [15, 17], firms usually do this to exploit the network externality<sup>1</sup> generated by introducing a limited version to go with fully functioning commercial versions. On the other hand, some firms give away software without functionality or time limitations, although the free offer usually lasts for a relatively short period of time. For example, when Computer Associates International Inc. first introduced its personal finance software Simply Money in June 1993, the company promised to give the software away to the first 1 million customers who agreed to pay the shipping charge [9]. Following Computer Associates, other companies started similar promotions. For instance, shortly thereafter, the president of MicroFrontier Inc. announced that he would be happy to give away 1 million copies of its imaging software in three months. More recently, the owner of an antispyware software distributed more than 5 million free copies of SpyBlocker before charging for it [6], while Sun Microsystems made its office suite StarOffice free for a few years before asking new users to pay for it [8]. For iPhone and iPod users, numerous free applications can still be found at the online Apple Store. Many applications at the Apple Store, such as Speech Cloud Voice Dialer and Drummer, were offered free when they were first released; now new users have to pay for them. Free offers are not limited to consumer software products. In 2001, Santa Rosa–based ACCPAC International gave away its Simply Accounting software to the first 1 million U.S. small businesses that requested the product [11]. Among these free software products, some carry strong network externality (e.g., StarOffice), while others (e.g., SpyBlocker) do not.<sup>2</sup> Thus, network externality cannot be used to explain all free offer practices.

Our study examines the practice of first giving away a software product (for which network externality is insignificant) without either time or content limitation and then charging later adopters for it. Hereafter, we refer to this practice as free offer. Our objective in this research is to explain this free offer phenomenon, provide insights regarding when it is profitable, and present a methodology that firms can use to determine the optimal number of free offers.

In sharp contrast to the rich literature on the open source software phenomenon [19, 30], we have seen only a limited number of formal economic analyses on the practice of free offers for commercial software. Much of that literature focuses on the network externality effect of free software. In addition to the work by Haruvy and Prasad [16, 17] mentioned earlier, Gallaugher and Wang [12] empirically study the impact of free software on commercial software in markets where both freeware and paid software are available. According to all of these studies, free adopters help increase customers valuation of the software product by increasing the size of the network, which enables a firm to impose a higher price on the product.

Studies focusing on network externalities ignore the impact of free software adopters on the speed of a commercial software product’s diffusion process. Free adopters can help speed up the adoption of a product by verbally spreading information regarding its availability and quality, and by nonverbal influences such as display effect (potential adopters may observe existing customers’ usage of a product), peer pressure, or social influence. In the diffusion literature, these direct and indirect influences are jointly referred to as a word-of-mouth effect.<sup>3</sup> The word-of-mouth effect is quite different from network externality. For instance, the verbal spreading of product information is not unlike the spreading of news across a population; it does not require the presence of network externality. Other nonverbal influences such as the display effect, pressures from competitors, and social influence from a potential adopter’s surroundings may also be present even if there is no network externality.

In order to understand well the effect of free offer on the speed of paid adoptions after free offer, we ignore factors such as network externalities and other economic benefits in competitive environments (e.g., technology lock-in and increased sales potential from complementary products or services) and focus on just the word-of-mouth effect. The analyses we conduct are therefore suitable for a monopolist software firm with a product for which network externalities are negligible or are not dominant. A software product fits into this category if buyers of the software make their purchase decision primarily based on the quality, service, and cost of the software and are not influenced much by the number of potential users of the software, or by the use of other complementary products. Examples of such software include antispyware software and various utility software products that help efficiently manage computer resources, among others. We examine the impact of several important factors that make free offer beneficial, including the discount factor, a finite demand window, and the existence of low-valuation customers. We attempt to answer, in the context of the above factors, these questions: If free offer is beneficial, when and how many free copies should be distributed? What types of customers should be the target of free offer? Can free offer be beneficial with a dynamic pricing strategy?

The problem we address in this research is an example of a prelaunch and launch strategic decision [25, p. 7]—a problem area that has been well recognized as important in the diffusion literature. Specifically, we examine the free offer strategy under four different cases. In the base case, which we label as Case I, all free software copies go to those customers who would adopt even if they are charged the fixed sales price— that is, all target customers have a high valuation for the product. We show that a free offer is mathematically equivalent to a simple, yet elegant, left-shifting transformation of the Bass diffusion model [1] and demonstrate that even if other economic benefits such as network externality are not significant, a software firm may still benefit from free offer because of the factors mentioned earlier. In Case II, a portion of the free copies go to those customers who would not adopt it at the fixed sales price—that is, to low-valuation customers. We show that a software firm is in a more advantageous position in this case as compared to the base case, because of the word-of-mouth effect from low-valuation customers. In Case III, the free offer strategy under a dynamic pricing policy is examined. We show that even with dynamic pricing, profits can be improved with free offer. Cases I, II, and III all assume that free adoptions cause a negligible delay to the commercial launch of the software product. This assumption is relaxed in Case IV where we analyze the impact of free offer with noninstantaneous adoption. We show that free offer can be beneficial even if free adoptions do not occur instantaneously.

The closest to our work is the research that examines the diffusion effect from pirated software [13, 14, 27]. These studies have concluded that a certain amount of piracy may be beneficial instead of detrimental to software firms. Although both free offer and piracy can help firms benefit from accelerated diffusion at the cost of potential loss of revenue, they have different implications for firms. One of the main differences lies in the degree of control firms can impose on these two practices. Clearly, firms exercise full control on free offer, but have much less control on piracy. Piracy mostly occurs to already popular software products instead of less well-known new products. Therefore, even when a firm does not impose any control on piracy, the diffusion of a new software product may still take an unacceptably long time to reach a desired rate of adoption. Thus, free offer would still be needed to accelerate the diffusion of a new product.

## Diffusion Models

There exis ts a rich body of diffus ion literature in information systems (IS). At a macro level, diffusion models have been proposed to forecast the growth of information technology (IT) spending in a country [15]. Several studies have focused on the adoption of IT products or processes within organizations [10, 28, 33]. Some others have focused on the diffusion of IT innovations across organizations or in certain geographical regions [18, 24, 31]. By examining the effect of free software offer on software diffusion, we are adding another dimension to this literature.

The most widely accepted diffusion model for consumer products is the Bass diffusion model [1], which is often referred to as the mixed influence model in the IS diffusion literature [18, 24, 32]. The Bass model is also the basis for numerous extensions and applications [25]. In this section, we briefly discuss the Bass model and an extension by Mahajan and Peterson [26] that has important bearings on our research. Additional diffusion models that consider dynamic pricing strategies are discussed in the sixth section.

The Bass model [1] is derived from a simple premise that the conditional probability of adoption by a randomly chosen consumer at time T given that adoption has not yet occurred is a linear function of the number of previous adopters. Due to the word-ofmouth effect (which includes influences from display effect, peer pressure, or social influence), as the number of existing adopters increases, potential adopters are more likely to become aware of the availability of or quality information about a product, and be directly or indirectly influenced; therefore, they are more likely to adopt as well. The Bass model is mathematically represented as

$$
\frac {f (t)}{1 - F (t)} = p + \frac {q}{m} Y (t)\tag{1}
$$

or

$$
\frac {d Y (t)}{d t} = \left[ p + \frac {q}{m} Y (t) \right] [ m - Y (t) ],\tag{2}
$$

where $m , p ,$ and $q$ are constant parameters representing the total number of potential adopters, the coefficient of innovation, and the coefficient of imitation, respectively. All three parameters must be positive. $Y ( t )$ is the total number of adopters by time t. $f ( t )$ is the likelihood of adoption at time t and its cumulative form is $F ( t )$ . Bass [1] derived the cumulative number of adoptions by time t and the noncumulative adoption rate at time t, as shown in Equations (3) and (4).

$$
Y (t) = \frac {m (1 - e ^ {- (p + q) t})}{(q / p) e ^ {- (p + q) t} + 1}\tag{3}
$$

$$
S (t) = \frac {d Y (t)}{d t} = \frac {m (p + q) ^ {2}}{p} \frac {e ^ {- (p + q) t}}{\left[ (q / p) e ^ {- (p + q) t} + 1 \right] ^ {2}}.\tag{4}
$$

The diffusion of a product can be completely characterized by the three parameters $p , q ,$ , and $m .$ The diffusion rate at any point in time is proportional to $m .$ . Other factors held constant, a higher p-value represents a higher initial diffusion rate and a higher $q$ value implies a larger word-of-mouth effect of existing adopters on future adopters. For a new product, $m , p ,$ and $q$ can be estimated based on the diffusion history of analogous products [4, 5].

Figures 1a and 1b show the shape of the diffusion rate under two different conditions—namely, $q > p$ and $q \leq p .$ . If $q > p$ , the diffusion rate reaches its peak at time $t ^ { \mathrm { p e a k } } = [ 1 / ( p + q ) ] \ln ( q / p )$ [1]. Under this condition, the diffusion curve is symmetric with respect to the point $t ^ { \mathrm { p e a k } }$ [20]. If $q \leq p$ , the diffusion rate decreases monotonically with time t. Most products, with perhaps a few exceptions such as movies, exhibit the diffusion pattern shown in Figure 1a.

The original Bass model does not take into consideration marketing mix variables such as price, advertisement, and promotions, and assumes that the total number of potential adopters is fixed. As an extension, a dynamic diffusion model proposed by Mahajan and Peterson [26] assumes that the number of potential adopters is a function of some exogenous or endogenous variables, such as price. This extension allows the original parameter m in the Bass model to be set as a nonincreasing function of price, denoted by m(price). As shown in Figure 2, m(0) and m(P) denote the number of potential adopters at price zero and price P, respectively. For expositional convenience, we refer to the group of potential customers who would adopt the software product at price P as high-valuation adopters, and the group of customers who would adopt only if the price is lower than P as low-valuation adopters.

## The Value of a New Software Product

Since the Bas model cons iders only the initial purchas es of a product, for most physical products, the diffusion curve estimated using this model will no longer be valid after a certain period of time because of repurchases. However, the restriction does not extend to software products. Software does not wear out and can generally be consumed over a long period of time. Furthermore, it is nowadays a common practice for software firms to provide free updates or even free upgrades to existing software users. Therefore, for expositional simplicity, we assume that the software under study has one version in its life cycle and hence the software is purchased at most once by a consumer. Under this assumption, since all purchases are initial, the Bass model is valid for the entire duration of the software life cycle.

Unlike physical products, software products are cheap to reproduce and distribute. Therefore, a common assumption made in the literature about software products is that the marginal cost of production is zero. With this assumption, software product development cost can be considered a sunk cost as soon as the software is ready for release. Consequently, maximizing net profit is equivalent to maximizing the net present value (NPV) of the future sales throughout the software life cycle. As mentioned earlier, we examine both constant pricing and dynamic pricing strategies in this research. The constant pricing strategy is important because many software products are sold at a relatively stable price for a long period of time. In the case of constant pricing, we denote the price charged to buyers by P. Without loss of generality, we set $P = 1$ in the following analyses. Based on the diffusion rate in the Bass model, we represent the NPV of future sales of a software product by $V _ { \mathrm { 0 } }$ as shown below:

Figure 2. High-Valuation and Low-Valuation Potential Customers

$$
V _ {0} = \int_ {0} ^ {T} S (t) e ^ {- r t} d t = \frac {m (p + q) ^ {2}}{p} \int_ {0} ^ {T} \frac {e ^ {- (p + q) t}}{\left[ (q / p) e ^ {- (p + q) t} + 1 \right] ^ {2}} e ^ {- r t} d t,\tag{5}
$$

where r is the discount rate, and T represents the time when the software exits the market. For notational simplicity, we use m to denote the number of potential adopters at the fixed price P—that is, $m = m ( P )$ . If only the software diffusion process is considered, the software firm should keep the software in the market until every potential customer has adopted it. Since $Y ( \infty ) = m$ , mathematically this suggests that we should set $T = \infty$ . In practice, due to obsolescence, there typically exists a finite demand window for software products [7]. A software product can become obsolete because of exogenous factors such as changes in hardware technology, changes in other major software such as operation systems, or changes in the business processes that the software supports. In addition, decision makers often only consider the cash flows generated during a predetermined planning horizon [21, 27], because the projected revenues generated beyond a certain time period are too uncertain. For these reasons, the effective demand window (or planning horizon) T is independent of the software diffusion process and can be considered an exogenously provided constant. The differences in the analyses of the infinite time horizon T and diffusion-independent finite T are further discussed in the next section.

In subsequent sections, we show how a software firm can increase its profits by giving away fully functional software for a short period of time. In the next section, we provide the analysis for the base case (constant price and all free adopters are highvaluation customers). In the fifth section, we analyze the case where a portion of the free adopters are low-valuation customers. In the sixth section, we consider dynamic pricing situations. All cases discussed in these sections assume that free copies are given away in a very short period of time (assume instantaneously). This is because, unlike for physical products, software products face no supply constraints and can be quickly distributed to potential adopters through broadband communication channels, or by sophisticated database marketing techniques over the Internet. The instantaneous free adoption assumption is relaxed in the seventh section, where we examine the free offer strategy when the free adoption itself may follow a diffusion process. Since it is very difficult, if not impossible, to identify and target specific categories of adopters (i.e., innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards), we assume that the free adopters represent a random sample of the entire adopter population with respect to adopter categories. Consequently, each adopter has the same word-of-mouth effect in the software diffusion process, regardless of whether the adopter paid for the software or not. We note that incorporating different word-of-mouth effects from the free adopters and paid adopters does not affect the result qualitatively.

In order to demonstrate why a software firm can benefit from free offer and how the optimal number of free adoptions can be determined, we need estimates of the Bass model parameter values for a software product. To ensure that the values used for illustration are from a reasonable range, we estimate exemplar parameter values based on the spreadsheet sales data for the United Kingdom [13]. The Bass model parameters obtained are p = 0.002, $q = 0 . 6 4 8$ , and $m = 1 , 0 2 5 \mathrm { K }$ . We note that any set of parameter values would suffice for our purpose. Qualitatively, our findings do not change for different sets of parameter values.

## Free Offer with High-Valuation Customers Only (Case I)

When a free offer is made, depending on the number of free copies distributed and how the contact information of target customers is obtained, the percentage of highvaluation and low-valuation free adopters may vary. If customers targeted for a free offer are selected from loyal customers of other products of a firm, then most, if not all, free copies may go to high-valuation customers. For convenience, we refer to this case as Case I.

Under Case I, the firm distributes free copies in a short time period and they are adopted by n (n < m) high-valuation potential customers. Since these n customers would buy the software but have received it for free, the firm loses revenue from these customers. After the free offer period, the software firm charges a fixed price P until the end of the software life cycle. The software firm achieves a higher starting sales rate because of the word-of-mouth effect of the n free adopters, at the cost of losing the potential revenue from these adopters.

In the case of complete market penetration, T remains to be positive infinity regardless of the amount of leftshifting. In the case of a finite demand window, T takes on the value $\tau + D ,$ , where D represents the length of such a demand window—that is, the integral in Equation (8) is evaluated over the interval from τ to $\tau + D$ . Obviously, $V _ { \mathrm { 0 } }$ in Equation (5) is a special case of V(n) in Equation (8) with $\tau = 0$ , or equivalently, $n = 0$

Although it is not possible to obtain the closed-form integral of Equation (8), we have derived several interesting properties of the solution to the optimization problem. They are stated in Propositions 1, 2, and 3 (proofs are in the Appendix). The asterisk denotes optimal values in the following discussion.

Proposition 1: The optimal free offer solution for Case I can be characterized as follows:

(a) Both the net revenue (V <sup>\*</sup>) and optimal number of free adopters $( n ^ { * } )$ are proportional to the number of high-valuation potential adopters (m).

(b) With a positive discount rate, the net revenue $( V ^ { * } )$ increases with the coefficient of innovation (p) and the coefficient of imitation (q).

(c) The net revenue $( V ^ { * } )$ increases with the length of the demand window (D).

(d) The net revenue (V<sup>\*</sup>) decreases with the discount rate (r).

Proposition 2: It is never optimal to have the amount of left-shifting (τ ) greater than the initial time to peak $( t ^ { p e a k } )$ . If $q \leq p ,$ the optimal amount of left-shifting (τ <sup>\*</sup>) equals zero.

Proposition 3: If free offer is beneficial to a software firm, the earlier the offer is made, the better; this implies that free offer should be made at the time of release.

In Proposition 1, the conclusion with respect to m is clear if we consider optimizing V with respect to τ . From Equation (7), it is easy to see that $\tau ^ { * }$ is independent of $m .$ Therefore, $V ^ { * }$ is proportional to m. From Equation (6), we conclude that in order to have the same $\tau ^ { \ast }$ for different $m , n ^ { * }$ has to be proportional to $m$ (a rigorous proof is provided in the Appendix). Although the conclusion with $p$ and $q$ may not be easily evident from the mathematical expression in Equation (8), they are quite intuitive. A higher $p$ implies a higher initial diffusion rate. A higher $q$ implies a larger word-ofmouth effect from existing adopters on later adopters. Therefore, other parameters held constant, an increase in $p$ or in $q$ increases the diffusion rate. Due to the discount factor, this leads to a higher $V ( n )$ . The conclusions with parameters D and r are relatively straightforward from examining the expression for $V ( n )$ .

Proposition 2 helps us reduce the size of the feasible range in the numerical search for $\tau ^ { * }$ and frees us from considering the less common case of $q \leq p .$ . Proposition 3 shows that it is not optimal to distribute free software copies throughout the demand window.

A closed-form solution for $V ( \tau )$ can be obtained only when the discount rate r equals zero. In what follows, we first analyze the simplified optimization problem (8) under the condition $r = 0$ , and then examine the general problem with $r > 0 .$ . For each condition, we consider the complete market penetration and the finite demand window situations separately. Therefore, there are four scenarios considered. These different scenarios allow us to demonstrate that free offer can be beneficial when there is a positive discount rate, or a finite demand window, or both. Furthermore, they are used to illustrate how the optimal number of free adoptions can be determined.

## Scenario 2: Zero Discount Rate, Finite Demand Window

With a finite demand window, $T$ equals $( \tau + D )$ . If the discount rate is zero, the optimal $\tau ^ { * }$ specified in Equation $( 8 )$ can be determined by moving a window with length $D$ along the diffusion time line such that the area under the diffusion curve is maximized, which is illustrated in Figure $4 .$ The solution follows from the fact that the Bass diffusion curve is unimodal and symmetric with $t ^ { \mathrm { p e a k } } = [ 1 / ( p + q ) ] \ln ( q / p )$ , as discussed in the second section. Therefore, the optimal amount to shift is

$$
\tau^ {*} = \max \left\{0, \left[ 1 / (p + q) \right] \ln (q / p) - D / 2 \right\}.\tag{9}
$$

Once the optimal amount to shift is obtained, the optimal number of free offers can be calculated by substituting for $\tau ^ { * }$ into Equation (3):

$$
n ^ {*} = \frac {m \left(1 - e ^ {- (p + q) \tau^ {*}}\right)}{(q / p) e ^ {- (p + q) \tau^ {*}} + 1}.\tag{10}
$$

## Free Offer with High-Valuation and Low-Valuation Customers (Case II)

In this s ection, we examine the cas e where a portion of the free adopters are from the group of high-valuation adopters, and the rest are from the group of low-valuation adopters. This is likely when free copies are given to a random selection of prospective customers from existing mailing lists, or when free download is made available via the Web to the first predetermined number of customers who avail the offer. We denote the total number of free adopters by $( 1 + \lambda ) n$ , of which n is high-valuation adopters, and $\lambda n$ is low-valuation adopters. Thus, $\lambda$ is a constant that represents the ratio of low-valuation and high-valuation free adopters, and n is the decision variable to be determined. Similar to the situation in Case I, the firm will benefit from the word-of-mouth effect of these $( 1 + \lambda ) n$ adopters; however, the firm only loses the potential revenue from the n high-valuation free adopters, since the low-valuation adopters will not buy at price $P .$

If we were to neglect the low-valuation adopters, Case II is exactly the same as Case I. Free offer to the high-valuation adopters is equivalent to shifting the diffusion curve to the left by an amount of time τ . Now, taking the low-valuation adopters into account and appropriately modifying the failure rate equation in Equation (1), we find that this is equivalent to increasing $p$ by $( q / m ) \lambda n$ , as shown in Equation (11):

$$
\begin{array}{c} f (t) / \Big [ 1 - F (t) \Big ] = p + (q / m) \Big [ Y (t) + \lambda n \Big ] = p + (q / m) \lambda n + (q / m) Y (t) \\ = p ^ {\prime} + (q / m) Y (t), \end{array}\tag{11}
$$

where $p ^ { \prime } = p + ( q / m ) \lambda n$ . Therefore, we can formulate the optimization problem for Case II as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \underset {n} {\text { Max }} V (n) = \frac {m (p ^ {\prime} + q) ^ {2}}{p ^ {\prime}} \int_ {\tau} ^ {T} \frac {e ^ {- (p ^ {\prime} + q) t}}{\left[ (q / p ^ {\prime}) e ^ {- (p ^ {\prime} + q) t} + 1 \right] ^ {2}} e ^ {- r (t - \tau)} d t \\ \text { s.t. } p ^ {\prime} = p + (q / m) \lambda n \text { and } \tau = \frac {\ln \left[ (p ^ {\prime} m + q n) / (p ^ {\prime} m - p ^ {\prime} n) \right]}{p ^ {\prime} + q}. \end{array}\tag{12}
$$

By comparing the two optimization problems (8) and (12), we find that if we assume the same $n ,$ then the only difference between the two objective functions lies in that $p ^ { \prime }$ in (12) is greater than $p$ in (8). To better understand the implication of this difference, we examine the time of peak sale $t ^ { \mathrm { p e a k } } = [ 1 / ( p + q ) ] \ln ( q / p )$ and the peak diffusion rate $S ( t ^ { \mathrm { p e a k } } ) = m ( p + q ) ^ { 2 } / ( 4 q )$ for the general diffusion process. Clearly, the peak diffusion rate increases with $p$ . We also find that the partial derivative of t <sup>peak</sup> with respect to $p$ is negative with $p < q$ , which implies that the time of peak sale decreases with $p$ until $p = q$

Curve 1 in the figure represents the diffusion process without free offer. Curve 2 shows the diffusion process with the optimal number of free adoptions under Case I. Curve 3 shows the diffusion curve with the same number of high-valuation free adopters as in Case I, but assuming a diffusion process under Case II with $\lambda = 1$ (i.e., equal proportions of low-valuation and high-valuation adopters). We find that under Case I, the impact of free offer on the diffusion process is a pure left-shifting of the diffusion curve. Under Case II, the effects include a leftshifting and a change in the shape of the diffusion curve, manifested by a higher peak diffusion rate. The figure helps to illustrate why Case II is more beneficial than Case I for a software firm.

## Free Offer with Dynamic Pricing (Case III)

So far, we have as umed that a firm always charges a fixed price after free offer. In this section, we demonstrate that even if a firm implements a dynamic (instead of fixed) pricing policy, giving away some free copies before dynamic pricing starts may make the firm still better off. We first assume that all free adopters are from the group of high-valuation potential adopters.

Dynamic pricing is a well-studied topic in the product diffusion literature and a number of models have been proposed [21, 23, 29]. We adopt the model by Krishnan et al. [23] because it is relatively more recent and the authors have shown that its findings are consistent with empirical data.

Krishnan et al.’s dynamic pricing model (DPM), which is based on the generalized Bass model [3], extends Equation (1), the basic premise of the Bass model, to

$$
\frac {f (t)}{1 - F (t)} = \left[ p + \frac {q}{m} Y (t) \right] x (t),\tag{13}
$$

where $x ( t )$ is the current marketing effort that reflects the impact of marketing mix variables such as price and advertising on the likelihood of adoption. The total number of adoptions by time t and the diffusion rate at time t can be derived from Equation (13):

$$
\tilde {Y} (t) = \frac {m (1 - e ^ {- (p + q) X (t)})}{(q / p) e ^ {- (p + q) X (t)} + 1}
$$

and

$$
\tilde {S} (t) = \frac {m (p + q) ^ {2}}{p} x (t) \frac {e ^ {- (p + q) X (t)}}{\left[ (q / p) e ^ {- (p + q) X (t)} + 1 \right] ^ {2}},
$$

where $X ( t ) = \int _ { 0 } ^ { t } x ( \tau ) d \tau$ is the cumulative marketing effort at time t. In the DPM,

$$
x (t) = 1 + \beta \frac {P r ^ {\prime} (t)}{P r (t)}
$$

and

$$
X (t) = t + \beta \left[ \ln \frac {P r (t)}{P r (0)} \right],
$$

where $P r ( t )$ and $P r ^ { \prime } ( t )$ represent the price and the rate of change in price, respectively, at time $t ,$ and $\beta$ is the diffusion price parameter that reflects the effect of price in accelerating and decelerating the rate of diffusion.<sup>4</sup> Krishnan et al. [23] examine three different cases, one of which is appropriate for our analysis. The formulation for this case is

$$
\underset {P r (t)} {\text { Max }} V = \int_ {0} ^ {T} P r (t) \tilde {S} (t) e ^ {- r t} d t.\tag{14}
$$

In their study, Krishnan et al. [23] conclude that the optimal price path for a new product should be either first increasing and then decreasing, or monotonically decreasing throughout. We use the same notation $( t _ { 1 } )$ from the original study to denote the time of the peak price. We find based on the DPM model that an increasing price decreases the speed of diffusion before $t _ { 1 } ,$ and the decreasing price increases the speed of diffusion after $t _ { \scriptscriptstyle 1 } .$ . The optimal $t _ { 1 } ^ { * }$ is the price turning point that leads to the maximum time-discounted revenue.

Analogous to Krishnan et al. [23], we adopt numerical methods to calculate the discounted revenue V. We use the same parameter values estimated from the spreadsheet data $( p = 0 . 0 0 2 , q = 0 . 6 4 8 , m = 1 , 0 2 5 \mathrm { K } )$ , and assume a planning horizon of 10 years and an annual discount rate of 0.1; this is analogous to the most general scenario, Scenario 4, that we considered for Cases I and II. Based on the DPM, we find that the sales price should increase monotonically until $t _ { 1 } ^ { * } { = } 5 . 4 4$ , and then decrease afterward. The optimal price path and the corresponding diffusion curve are shown in Figure 11. From this figure, we can see that under this optimal price path, it still takes several years for the product to reach a substantial level of diffusion. The discounted revenue under this optimal pricing policy is 357.2K. This serves as the benchmark solution in deciding whether free offer is still beneficial under dynamic pricing.

We now apply the left-shifting concept to the diffusion curve and examine the optimal price path. The approach is similar to that discussed for the base case; here, too, n and τ uniquely determine each other. We calculate the net revenue associated with different values of τ . We are able to show that the firm can indeed benefit from free offer at the time of release. As shown in Figure 12, the firm’s profit is maximized with a left-shift equivalent to 5.92 years. At this point, the revenue increases by 102 percent (to 721.3K) relative to the benchmark solution in Figure 11. Similar to the cases with constant pricing, free offer under dynamic pricing can significantly increase the speed of diffusion, leading to peak sale occurring sooner.

The solution obtained by first determining the optimal price path and then searching for the optimal amount of left-shifting, however, may not be the best one when both dynamic pricing and free offer at the time of release are considered—we cannot rule out the possibility that a price path that is suboptimal to the DPM, when combined with a suitable free offer solution, may lead to higher net revenue. Therefore, to obtain the optimal solution, we should consider all combinations of possible price paths and free offer solutions.

Incorporating both dynamic pricing and free offer directly into the problem formulation poses a considerable challenge. However, we are able to develop a procedure that makes the problem tractable, and easy to evaluate numerically. Our procedure takes advantage of the parameter-revision technique proposed by Jiang et al. [20]. According to Jiang et al., the Bass model parameters associated with a left-shifted diffusion curve can be rewritten as functions of the amount of left-shifting (or equivalently, the number of free adoptions) and the parameters associated with the original diffusion curve. For instance, if the original Bass model parameters are m, $p ,$ and $q ,$ and the number of free adopters at the time of release is $n ,$ then the left-shifted diffusion curve can be treated as a new diffusion curve with a revised set of parameters:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} \tilde {m} = m - n, \\ \tilde {q} = q - (q / m) n, \\ \tilde {p} = p + (q / m) n. \end{array} \right.\tag{15}
$$

Incorporating the equations in (15) into the formulation in (14), we obtain the following generalized model that can be used to obtain the optimal solution when both dynamic pricing and free offer at the time of release are considered:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \underset {P r (t), n} {\text { Max }} V = \int_ {0} ^ {T} P r (t) \tilde {S} (t) e ^ {- r t} d t \\ \text { s.t. } \tilde {S} (t) = \frac {\tilde {m} (\tilde {p} + \tilde {q}) ^ {2}}{\tilde {p}} x (t) \frac {e ^ {- (\tilde {p} + \tilde {q}) X (t)}}{\left[ (\tilde {q} / \tilde {p}) e ^ {- (\tilde {p} + \tilde {q}) X (t)} + 1 \right] ^ {2}} \\ x (t) = 1 + \beta \frac {P r ^ {\prime} (t)}{P r (t)} \\ X (t) = t + \beta \left[ \ln \frac {P r (t)}{P r (0)} \right] \\ \tilde {m} = m - n \\ \tilde {q} = q - (q / m) n \\ \tilde {p} = p + (q / m) n. \end{array}\tag{16}
$$

Numerically, for each value of n (where n could be varied at an appropriate level of granularity), we can first calculate the three revised parameters based on (15), and then use the DPM to compute the optimal price path and the corresponding net revenue. The solution that results in the highest net revenue is the global optimal solution.

Following this parameter-revision-based approach, we obtain the best solution for our example: the maximum discounted revenue is 873.8K, which is achieved at $n ^ { * } = 6 3 . 6 5 \mathrm { K }$ and $t _ { 1 } ^ { * } = 8 . 0 6$ . This is a further improvement over the solution shown in Figure 12, representing a 145 percent increase in net revenue as compared to the benchmark solution without free offer. By comparing this with the results for Scenario 4 in Table 1, we conclude that free offer under dynamic pricing may further increase a firm’s net profit. This is expected since dynamic pricing provides a firm with more pricing options to choose from. We would like to point out that, for various reasons, dynamic pricing may not be feasible for every product and may not fit a firm’s overall strategy. Hence, a fixed pricing strategy is often observed in practice.

Similar to Case II, we conduct sensitivity analyses on three of the more important parameters—that is, the coefficient of innovation (p), the coefficient of imitation (q), and the ratio between low-valuation and high-valuation free adopters (λ). Analogous to Case II, the word-of-mouth effect of low-valuation free adopters is assumed to be equivalent to increasing the value of $p$ by $( q / m ) \lambda n$ . The results of the sensitivity analyses are shown in Figures 13, 14, and 15. By comparing these three figures with Figures 8, 9, and 10 for Case II, we find that the effects of $\lambda$ and $q$ under dynamic pricing are very similar to their effects under constant pricing. In both cases, the net revenue increases monotonically with $\lambda$ and $q .$ The impact of $p ,$ on the other hand, is not monotonic when the value of $p$ is very low. This is probably because when the starting diffusion rate is extremely low, other factors such as the pricing path may be more dominant than $p ,$ thus making its impact less predictable. At higher values of $p ,$ the impact is consistent with what we observe in Case II. Once again, the result confirms that when $p$ is large, implying that the diffusion can take off quickly by itself, free offer is not needed.

## Noninstantaneous Adoption (Case IV)

The focus of this res earch is on the s peed of paid adoptions after free offer, which is affected by the number of free adoptions during the free offer period. We have assumed instantaneous free adoption in all the cases discussed so far. This assumption is reasonable if (1) the free offer period is very short (e.g., a few weeks or less), or (2) although it takes a significant amount of time (e.g., several months), free offer causes little or no delay to the commercial launch of the product. An example of the latter case would be that all free adoptions occur after the product is finalized and before all production and presale arrangements (e.g., getting approvals from the corporate hierarchy or government agencies, planning for distribution, and negotiating contract details with vendors and retailers) are complete. In practice, however, there will be situations where the delay to the commercial launch caused by the free offer cannot be ignored. In this section, we examine how the free offer strategy can be formulated with the noninstantaneous adoption assumption.

![](/api/attachments/Z3JNJ77R/fulltext/images/05f3cb798ab1c7e0ad51ef86cf1ba56c4972a67e32de94a5574e597d6bf1f21a.jpg)  
Figure 15. Net Revenue Versus q

We denote the amount of delay to the commercial launch of a software product by G(N), with N representing the total number of free adopters, including both lowvaluation and high-valuation types. As shown in Figure 16a, with the instantaneous free adoption assumption, the commercial launch of the product can be viewed as starting from time zero. When a noninstantaneous free adoption is assumed, the commercial launch will start from G(N), as shown in Figure 16b. By adding a discount factor e<sup>–rG(N)</sup>, the problem formulation derived with the instantaneous free adoption assumption can be easily extended to address the decision problem with noninstantaneous free adoption. For instance, when free offer takes time G(N), the right-hand side of the objective function in Equation (8) changes to

$$
e ^ {- r G (N)} \cdot \frac {m (p + q) ^ {2}}{p} \int_ {\tau} ^ {T} \frac {e ^ {- (p + q) t}}{\left[ (q / p) e ^ {- (p + q) t} + 1 \right] ^ {2}} e ^ {- r (t - \tau)} d t.
$$

Depending on various internal and external factors, G(N) may take different functional forms. For instance, if a firm plans to distribute all free copies to participants at a trade show event, G(N) is a constant equal to the delay caused by waiting for the event. On the other hand, if a firm has the contact information of potential adopters but can only process a fixed number of free offer requests in each time period, G(N) is a linear function of N. In a third scenario, a firm may make the software available for free download, and then rely on the word-of-mouth effect of free adopters to help spread the news about the offer. The first two scenarios are relatively straightforward, and therefore we examine the last one where both the free offer period and the commercial sale period follow a diffusion process. We note that the Bass model parameters for the two different periods are not expected to be the same for several reasons. First, the number of potential adopters at price zero is larger than that at a positive price. Second, the firm’s marketing activities in the free offer period may be different from those in the later sales period. Finally, the existing adopters’ word-of-mouth effect for a product when it is free is not expected to be the same as that when it has a price associated with it. For these reasons, we introduce M, $p _ { f }$ , and $q _ { f }$ to denote the three Bass model parameters for the free offer period.

## Managerial Implications and Directions for Future Research

By examining a variety of market s cenarios and pricing s trategies , we have shown that by giving away fully functioning software at the time of release, a software firm can benefit from the accelerated software diffusion process. Our research provides a formal diffusion-based economic justification for the free offer practice with neither time nor content limitations. We show that important contributing factors that make free offer beneficial include the discount factor, a finite demand window, and the existence of low-valuation customers. Our findings have several important implications for managers in deciding the best promotional strategy when launching new software products.

Our findings suggest that free offer is more likely to be beneficial for a new software product that cannot take off quickly by itself. This provides firms with a low-cost alternative to market a new software product. The free offer strategy would be especially useful for small firms or firms with a limited promotional budget. In addition, our models can help managers determine the optimal number of free adoptions based on various factors. For firms that wish to exploit the word-of-mouth effect from free adopters, our model will enable them to determine an optimal free offer strategy based on factors such as discount rate, demand window, and the proportion of lowvaluation to high-valuation free adopters. Thus, our analysis can help managers better appreciate the benefits from making free offers, and the circumstances under which such offers are beneficial. In addition, we provide a viable methodology to implement such a strategy.

Our models can also help firms make informed decisions when selecting from a number of marketing alternatives. First, we conclude from our analyses that a firm is in a better position when a larger percentage of the free software copies go to low-valuation adopters. If it is easy to identify low-valuation potential adopters, then all of them should be targeted for free offer. On the other hand, if identifying lowvaluation adopters is costly, our model can help a firm decide whether it is worthwhile to identify them for targeting purposes, or to make the free software available to all adopters without explicit targeting. Second, in the diffusion literature, the coefficient of innovation p is also referred to as the coefficient of external influence, and its value can be affected by factors such as advertisements. While low-cost advertising activities, such as posting new product information on a Web site or displaying the product at trade show, may not have much impact on its diffusion rate, a heavy investment on advertisements can significantly speed up the diffusion of a new product. Therefore, other than free offer, an alternative way to increase the initial diffusion rate of a new software product is to increase the value of p through advertisements, or through a combination of free offer and advertisements. In deciding the best strategy, a firm should compare the costs and benefits associated with each approach; our model can be used to calculate the benefit associated with a higher p value. Third, as is the case for p, the parameter q may also be controllable. To increase the value of q (the wordof-mouth effect of existing adopters on potential adopters), firms sometimes provide incentives to existing customers when they refer their products to other potential customers. The benefit of such a promotion can be obtained based on our model, once the change in q is estimated.

The free offer strategy could also be used to help a firm reach a predetermined level of adoption by a specified time T. This is important in situations where such an adoption level is considered necessary for a firm to maintain its monopolist status, to establish a competitive advantage, or to survive in a fiercely competitive market. The analytical models we develop in this paper can help determine the number of free copies needed such that the total number of adoptions can reach the target adoption level by time T.

Furthermore, the cases we analyze in this research can help firms make better decisions in developing their pricing policies for new products. Against conventional wisdom, we find that free offer can further improve the profit derived from established dynamic pricing policies. Therefore, if dynamic pricing is practical for a new product, then the model we develop for Case III can be used to simultaneously determine the best combination of free offer and dynamic pricing policy. In market environments where dynamic pricing is difficult to implement, possibly because of the overhead associated with frequent price updates and other potential contractual, legal, or reputational issues, then the model formulation for Case II may be used to determine the optimal numbers of low-valuation and high-valuation potential adopters.

Several extensions to our research warrant further study. Currently, we assume that free software is offered without any restrictions. Our approach can be extended to study free software offer with time or functionality limitations. For example, the word-ofmouth effect of free software adopters and the likelihood of these adopters purchasing a full-blown commercial version can be modeled as a function of the degree of limitations of the free software. Our approach can be appropriately enhanced to determine both the optimal quantity of free offer and the optimal degree of limitation for the free version. In addition, this research considers only products without significant network externality effect. Intuitively, for products that carry strong network externality, free offer may lead to high speed of diffusion and, simultaneously, an increased product valuation. This will allow a firm to realize its revenue faster, charge a higher price, or sell to a larger number of potential adopters. A model that incorporates both the network externality effect and the word-of-mouth effect may be developed to study this more complex problem. Another possible extension is to examine the implications of free offer in a competitive market. Depending on how compatible the competing products are, and how costly it is to switch between products, free offer of one product may have different effects on the diffusion of other products. A diffusion model incorporating competition or game theory–based models could be used to study this problem. Finally, low-valuation and high-valuation potential adopters are treated as two separate but homogeneous groups in this research. Future studies could examine the heterogeneity in potential adopters’ valuations of a new product and study its impact on their adoption behavior during and after free offer. For instance, it is possible that in some environments potential adopters with lower valuations seek free offer opportunities more actively that other adopters, and they may delay their purchase even longer if they do not receive a free copy and are forced to buy. Alternative free offer strategies could be developed to examine scenarios with such heterogeneous potential adopters.
