# On the Measurement of Ideation Quality

BRUCE A. REINIG, ROBERT O. BRIGGS, AND JAY F. NUNAMAKER JR.

BRUCE A. REINIG is Professor and Chair of the Department of Information and Decision Systems at San Diego State University, where he researches the development and evaluation of technologies and work practices to support decision making. He earned his Ph.D. from the University of Arizona in 1996.

ROBERT O. BRIGGS is the Director of Academic Affairs for the Institute for Collaboration Science and Professor of Marketing and Management at the University of Nebraska. He is also an Associate Professor of Systems Engineering at the Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands. He earned a Ph.D. in Management and Information Systems from the University of Arizona in 1994. He researches the theoretical foundations of collaboration and applies his findings to the design and deployment of new collaboration technologies and work practices. He is a codeveloper of the Collaboration Engineering discipline, and co-inventor of the ThinkLets design pattern language for collaboration processes.

JAY F. NUNAMAKER JR. is Regents and Soldwedel Professor of MIS, Computer Science, and Communication and Director of the Center for the Management of Information at the University of Arizona, Tucson. He received his Ph.D. in operations research and systems engineering from Case Institute of Technology, an M.S. and B.S. in engineering from the University of Pittsburgh, and a B.S. from Carnegie Mellon University. He received his professional engineer’s license in 1965. In a 2005 journal article in Communications of the AIS, he was recognized as the fourth to the sixth most-productive researcher for the period 1991–2003. Dr. Nunamaker received the LEO Award from the Association of Information Systems (AIS) at the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS) in Barcelona, Spain, December 2002, and he was elected a fellow of the AIS in 2000.

ABSTRACT: Ideation is a key step in organizational problem solving, so researchers have developed a variety of technological interventions for improving ideation quality, which we define as the degree to which an ideation activity produces ideas that are helpful in attaining a goal. In this paper, we examine the four measures typically used to evaluate ideation quality, including idea-count, sum-of-quality, average-quality, and good-idea-count, and discuss their validity and potential biases. An experimental study comparing three levels of social comparison was used to illustrate the differences in the ideation quality measures and revealed that research conclusions were dependent on the measure used. Based on our analysis of the measures and experimental results, we recommend that only good-idea-count be used as a measure to evaluate ideation treatments and call into question research that has based its findings on the other measures. Finally, we discuss implications for research and other potential approaches to evaluating ideation quality.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: construct validity, electronic brainstorming, group support systems, idea quality, idea quantity, ideation.

RESEARCHERS AND PRACTITIONERS HAVE LONG SOUGHT to improve the ideas generated by a group facing a problem or opportunity. Ideation, the process of generating or conceiving of new ideas, is identified as a key step in systems development (e.g., [42]), organizational planning (e.g., [1]), decision making (e.g., [6]), and problem solving (e.g., [5]). It is therefore understandable that interventions for improving ideation have been the subject of much research (for surveys of the ideation literature see [2, 14, 16]). Given the importance of ideation, many researchers have derived and tested interventions to improve ideation quality. Ideation quality is the degree to which an ideation activity produces ideas that are helpful in attaining a goal. It pertains to the relative merits of an ideation process or intervention rather than to the value of a particular idea.

Researchers use a variety of technologies to support ideation interventions, ranging from flipcharts and felt-tip pens to sophisticated collaboration software, in their efforts to encourage the production of more good ideas. Dennis et al. [10], for example, used electronic brainstorming technology to implement a task-decomposition strategy to reduce cognitive inertia and improve ideation. Santanen et al. [35] seeded electronic ideation folders with cues designed to stimulate creativity. Other researchers have used technology to create ideation environments that allow anonymous and simultaneous participation (e.g., [8]) to overcome evaluation apprehension and attention blocking. Shepherd et al. [36, 37] implemented real-time feedback graphs that invoked social comparison to mitigate a tendency to free ride during anonymous brainstorming. Thus, researchers have produced a rich variety of interventions in their efforts to improve ideation.

An important consideration for information systems research is determining the basis for assessment; that is, the phenomenon-of-interest (the consequent construct, the dependent variable) that a technological intervention is designed to improve. Ideation researchers typically follow one of four approaches to measuring the relative merits of their ideation interventions. One common measure for the value of an ideation session is simply the number of ideas a group produces without addressing the quality of the individual ideas produced (e.g., [7, 9, 19, 22, 26, 27, 31, 33, 38]). This approach is based on the assumption that interventions that yield more ideas will, of necessity, also yield more ideas of good quality. The other three approaches begin by assigning quality scores to each individual idea and then compute an overall quality metric for the ideation session by either summing the idea quality scores, averaging the quality scores, or counting the number of quality scores exceeding some threshold.

This paper examines the intermeasure reliability and validity of these four approaches to measuring ideation quality. We argue that only one measure, good-idea-count, is a valid measure of ideation quality and call into question research conclusions based on the other measures. With an experiment designed to compare three ideation treatments, we demonstrate that it is possible for the four measures to lack intermeasure reliability and lead to contradictory research conclusions. Recommendations for measuring ideation quality in future research are then presented.

## Evaluating Ideation Quality

ALTHOUGH THERE IS VARIATION WITHIN EACH STEP, researchers have tended to follow a four-step approach for assessing ideation quality. These steps include (1) identifying the set of unique ideas from an ideation session, (2) assigning a quality score to each idea (except when only unique ideas are counted), (3) computing a metric value using one of the four approaches discussed above, and (4) using the metric from each session to make statistical comparisons between treatments. We now consider each of these steps individually and review their treatment in the literature.

## Identifying Unique Ideas

When researchers evaluate the quality of an ideation session, they typically start by identifying the set of unique ideas that were generated during the session. However, the definition of what constitutes an idea varies from context to context. Idea has been variously defined as a contribution that contains task-related information [13], a solution in the form of a verb–object combination [37], and a specific benefit or difficulty related to the task [4].

Researchers have also differed in their methodologies for determining the level of abstraction of the ideas to include in the idea list for a given ideation session. For example, some include only a generalization of more specific instances of a concept (e.g., [4]), while others include both generalizations and specific instances (e.g., [37]). The utility of these approaches could only be determined with respect to the goal of the ideation session. That is, whether general or specific ideas, or both, are included in the idea list depends on what is most useful in the context of a given task. Thus, that which would be counted as an idea in one study might legitimately be excluded as a nonidea in another. As long as the choices are consistent with the goals of the ideation, and as long as the participants are aware of the goals (and so could, if they choose, generate ideas at useful levels of abstraction), the results of studies using different methods to identify unique ideas should be comparable, and their results should be generalizable.

## Evaluating the Quality of an Idea

The next step is typically to evaluate the quality of each individual idea. As with idea content and level-of-abstraction, the quality of a particular idea can only be judged with respect to the specific goals of the ideation session. Researchers have therefore used a variety of criteria for judging the merits of individual ideas. These include, for example, feasibility and originality (e.g., [14, 15]), effectiveness (e.g., [39]), the value ideas could create (e.g., [3]), the importance of an idea within a specific context (e.g., [40]), as well as the magnitude of impact an idea might have (e.g., [11]). Quality measures are typically represented with semantic differentials, Likert scales, or rubrics that evaluate one or more dimensions of quality.

## Calculating Ideation Quality Metrics

Having assigned a quality score to each idea, the next step is to calculate an overall metric for the ideation session. This metric is the dependent variable used to evaluate and compare the efficacy of ideation treatments. Some researchers follow Osborn’s [28] conjecture that an intervention that produces more ideas will, of necessity, produce more good ideas, and, consequently, do not evaluate the quality of the individual ideas generated during an ideation session. In such cases, step two is not completed and they report the idea-count as a measure of ideation quality (e.g., [17, 37]).

Researchers who explicitly measure idea quality (i.e., complete step two above) use one or more of three analytical approaches identified by Diehl and Stroebe [14], which include:

• sum-of-quality, which is calculated by summing the quality scores of the individual ideas in the session (e.g., [18, 23]);

• average-quality, which is calculated by computing the mean of the quality scores of the individual ideas in the session (e.g., [40]); and

• good-idea-count, which is calculated by counting the number of ideas in the session with a quality score that meets or exceeds some quality threshold (e.g., [13]).

These approaches derive a single metric or score to represent the ideation quality for one ideation session.

## Using Measures to Make Statistical Inferences

## About Treatments

The final step to evaluating ideation quality is to make statistical comparisons between treatments. It is important to clarify the unit of analysis for this final step. The above measures produce one metric for an entire ideation session that, with the exception of idea-count, does not indicate the number of ideas generated during the session. None of the metrics indicates the variability of the individual idea-quality scores for an idea set. For example, whether 10 or 100 ideas are generated during an ideation session, a single metric is used to represent the relative quality of that session and any variability among the ideas in the session cannot be deduced from the ideation quality metric. Thus, if a study contains two treatments with 10 groups or ideation sessions per treatment, there will be 10 observations or ideation quality metrics produced for each treatment.

## The Merits and Limitations of the Four Ideation Quality Metrics

THIS SECTION PRESENTS THE LOGIC TO ARGUE that three of the four ideation metrics that are commonly used in the literature have inherent flaws that limit their validity. This may explain why researchers have reported that these measures yield contradictory results within the same study (e.g., [12, 14]). For simplicity, we use the term good idea to refer to an idea that was rated highly, such as receiving the highest possible quality score, and the term bad idea to refer to an idea that was rated the lowest possible quality score by whatever standard researchers happen to use in a given study.

## Idea-Count

The idea-count measure is less labor-intensive than other approaches, and perhaps less subjective, in that raters need not assess the quality of each idea. However, in order for greater idea-count to always indicate greater ideation quality than less idea-count, one must assume that either all ideas are of equal quality, or, as Osborn [28] conjectured, as more ideas are generated, more good ideas will also be generated. However, these assumptions may not always hold. For certain problems, such as identifying a vendor or debugging software, only a limited number of good solutions are possible. Thus, once the limited set of good ideas has been discovered, no more good ideas will be generated, regardless of how many more ideas the group contributes. Further, some ideas may be more useful than others. Therefore, we cannot assume all ideas to be of equal quality. Yet, using the idea-count metric, ideation interventions that gave rise to a few good ideas would score lower than interventions that generate only a large number of bad or mediocre ideas. Thus, idea-count cannot be deemed a reliable way to discern superior ideation interventions from inferior ones.

## Sum-of-Quality

The sum-of-quality has a bias similar to the idea-count measure in that the score of this metric can be increased (indicating higher ideation quality) by the presence of additional bad ideas. The measure does reward good ideas in that they contribute more to the metric than do bad ideas. Nevertheless, interventions that produced many bad ideas would be judged superior to interventions that produced only a few good ideas. This bias could create an artificially positive relationship between idea quantity and idea quality and lead researchers to conclude that ideation interventions that produce many bad ideas are superior to ideation interventions that produce a few good ideas.

Another limitation of the sum-of-quality approach is that it is based on the assumption that the quality scale used to evaluate individual ideas is a ratio scale. A ratio scale is necessary for a researcher to argue that the values are additive, and that sums could be meaningfully compared even when they are generated from idea sets that vary in size. However, this assumption is questionable, given that ordinal scales are used throughout the ideation literature. For example, Dennis et al. use a five-point scale ranging from “1 = very poor quality to 5 = very good quality” [12, p. 100] and

Connolly et al. use a seven-point scale with the anchors “1 (a very poor solution) and 7 (a very good solution)” [8, p. 694]. Thus, if one were to use the Dennis et al. [12] scale to compute the sum-of-quality metric, one “very good quality” idea would be equated with five “very poor quality” ideas.

## Average-Quality

The average-quality measure is unique among the four measures in that it is not monotonically increasing as more ideas are generated. This metric decreases in value as more bad ideas are generated, even if a large number of good ideas are present in the idea set. Interventions that generate only a few ideas, but still had some good ideas in the set, could score higher on this measure than interventions that produced more good ideas but were accompanied with a proportionately higher number of bad ideas. Thus, the average-quality metric could have a negative correlation with the idea-count and sum-of-quality measures, which may help explain why some researchers find that average-quality and sum-of-quality measures produce conflicting results for the same data set (e.g., [12, 14]).

Another limitation of the average-quality measure is that it is based on the assumption that the quality scale used to evaluate individual ideas is an interval scale. An interval scale is necessary for a researcher to compute the mean. This assumption, as discussed above, has not been satisfied for the quality scales used throughout the ideation literature, which are predominantly ordinal. Researchers could legitimately compute the median and mode quality score from an ordinal scale but it is unclear what insight, if any, these measures would provide.

The average-quality measure would be problematic, however, even if an interval scale could be developed and used. In such an event, the average-quality measure would fail to represent the variance of quality within an idea set. For example, assume a five-point interval scale was used. The average-quality measure would lead to the conclusion that an ideation intervention that gave rise to an idea set where all ideas were mediocre, {3, 3, 3, 3, 3, 3}, was as useful as an intervention that gave rise to an equal number of good and bad ideas, {1, 1, 1, 5, 5, 5}; such a conclusion is not reflective of what is desired in practice, which is the generation of good ideas. Thus, again, the average-quality measure, like the idea-count and sum-of-quality measures, cannot reliably discern the most basic intention of ideation—the generation of good ideas.

## Good-Idea-Count

The good-idea-count measure does not have the limitations and biases present in the idea-count, sum-of-quality, and average-quality measures. Unlike idea-count and sumof-quality, the presence of bad ideas does not inflate the measure so it is not biased in favor of large idea sets irrespective of quality. Nor, unlike average-quality, do bad ideas reduce the measure irrespective of the number of good ideas in the idea set.

Further, good-idea-count does not require an assumption about the relationship between idea-quantity and idea-quality, such as Osborn’s [28] conjecture, nor does it assume that a ratio or interval scale was used to evaluate idea quality. Thus, goodidea-count does not rely on questionable assumptions and directly measures the primary purpose of ideation—to generate as many good ideas as possible. The goodidea-count metric is a discrete random variable that lends itself to hypothesis testing about ideation quality.

The good-idea-count measure depends on the setting of a threshold on an ordinal scale. The logical level for setting that threshold must be derived from the task itself. The lowest threshold should be set at the point where ideas can contribute some value toward reaching the goal, which is determined by the context of the larger task in which ideation is subsumed. Whether the threshold is set low or high would depend on the context of the study.

## An Illustrative Example

To clarify the merits and limitations of the four approaches discussed above, in this section, we consider the results each would yield with a simple hypothetical data set (Table 1). Assume that all unique ideas were identified and evaluated on a four-point ordinal quality scale. For purposes of illustration, assume that all ideas were either rated as good (i.e., score = 4) or bad (i.e., score = 1).

If the idea-count or the sum-of-quality measures were applied, idea set A would rank first among the three sets, despite having the fewest good ideas. This illustrates how these two measures can be biased in favor of large idea sets, irrespective of the quality of the ideas in those sets.

If the average-quality measure were used, one would conclude that set B was superior although it contains fewer good ideas than set C. This illustrates how the measure can be biased against idea sets containing bad ideas.

If good-idea-count were used, one would conclude that set C is superior because it yielded more good ideas than either set A or set B. Thus, the four measures lead to different conclusions about the relative quality of the three data sets, which suggests that the measures cannot be not equally valid representations of ideation quality. We argue that only the good-idea-count measure could be considered valid because the other measures are based on unsustainable assumptions and have the potential to produce biased results.

## Experiment

IN THIS SECTION, WE TEST WHETHER the purported biases derived by logic and illustrated by the hypothetical example will manifest when the four measures are applied to the results of an experimental study. The experiment was originally conducted to test whether invocations of social comparison could improve ideation quality among teams brainstorming anonymously [37]. For the current paper, we reanalyzed the session transcripts and reevaluated the quality of the ideas contributed to those sessions.

An extensive literature on social loafing demonstrates that people tend to make less effort for group tasks than they do for individual tasks, unless their contribution can be specifically identified, or unless they believe that their contribution is critical to the success of the task [14, 20, 21, 24, 25, 29, 30, 34].

## Participants

A total of 280 undergraduate students from the college of business in a large western university were randomly assigned in 56 five-person groups working on an ideation task. Each group was then randomly assigned to one of three treatments.

## Treatments

The three treatments were named “control,” “moderate social comparison,” and “high social comparison.” In all treatments, participants contributed ideas with an electronic brainstorming tool and an electronic line graph appeared on the wall of the room in full view of all participants. The graph displayed a cumulative total of the number of comments the participants made over time. In the control treatment, the graph provided no basis for social comparison.

In the “moderate social comparison” intervention, a horizontal line appeared halfway up the graph. Researchers read a script to the participants informing them that the line represented the performance of a mythical “average group,” and that if they pushed the graph above the line, their performance would be above average. The same graph appeared with the same horizontal line for teams in the high social comparison intervention. However, the script for that treatment sought to raise the salience of the social comparison by using a jocular tone to emphasize the group’s performance relative to that of the mythical “average group.” For example, they received evaluative prompts at scripted intervals during the session, such as “You are really smoking!” or “Did your fingers fall asleep?”

## Task

The experiment used a variation of Wheeler and Mennecke’s [41] school of business task, as modified by Shepherd et al. [37]. This hidden-profile task required participants to propose solutions for a set of interrelated problems facing an imaginary school of business. The problems were structured such that solutions that addressed one set of symptoms without addressing underlying causes tended to exacerbate other symptoms. Examples of problems include overcrowded classrooms, burnout faculty, and a declining reputation among employers.

## Procedure

Before ideation began, the session facilitator moderated an oral discussion among the participants to reveal all facets of the problems in the scenario. The oral discussion lasted approximately 20 minutes for each group and was based on scripted prompts to ensure that all relevant information surfaced prior to ideating potential solutions. When the oral discussion was finished, a standard list of issues and information was distributed to all participants, so all groups started generating solutions with an identical problem statement.

Each participant used electronic brainstorming software to generate ideas for 40 minutes in response to the question, “What can be done to resolve the problems of the school of business?” In electronic brainstorming, each participant types an idea onto a different electronic page. When a user submits an idea, the system takes away the user’s page and replaces it with a page containing other people’s ideas.

## Analysis and Results

## Identifying Unique Ideas

TREATMENT-BLIND RATERS ANALYZED THE DISCUSSION transcripts of each session to identify the unique ideas. A unique idea was defined as a unique verb–object pair suggesting a possible solution to one or more of the problems faced by the school of business. Compound ideas were disaggregated into single unique ideas. For instance, a comment suggesting to “advertise in newspapers and on television” would be disaggregated into two unique ideas: “advertise in newspapers” and “advertise on television.” The raters achieved a 97 percent overlap in their lists and then discussed and reconciled any differences.

## Rating the Quality of Each Idea

Two treatment-blind expert raters with a minimum of 15 years’ experience in the task domain independently evaluated the quality of each solution (idea) from the sessions. They were fully briefed on the school of business task and the associated solution space. The raters used a holistic evaluation method ranging from 1 to 4 as follows:

• A solution receives a score of 4 if it is easily implemented and if it solves the problems (eliminates unacceptable symptoms) completely without creating new unacceptable symptoms.

• A solution receives a score of 3 if it is easily implemented and would ease most symptoms considerably, but would not completely eliminate them or if it would be difficult to implement, but would completely solve the problems.

• A solution receives a score of 2 if it would be very difficult to implement and would solve some of the problems considerably but would not completely eliminate them, or if it is easily implemented, but would only have minor, marginal improvement in terms of solving the problems.

• A solution receives a score of 1 if it would be impossible to implement or if it does not solve any of the problems to any degree.

The raters considered the economic, technical, and political feasibility of each idea and gave scores that differed by no more than one point for 99.1 percent of the ideas. The raters then met to discuss and reconcile all discrepancies. Examples of ideas that received top scores were:

“Raise tuition.” This solution would decrease the number of students, which would ease teaching loads and overcrowded classrooms, as well as provide additional funds.

“Raise admission standards.” This would also reduce the number of students while raising their skill levels, which could reduce overcrowding while improving the reputation of the school.

Examples of ideas that received low scores were:

“Build more classrooms.” With no funds and no additional faculty, this solution was infeasible.

“Fire professors who don’t do enough research.” This solution addresses none of the root causes but exacerbates other problems and, given the tenure system, it is also infeasible to implement.

## Computing the Ideation Quality Measures

Having identified each unique idea and assigned a quality score to each, we then used these quality scores to compute the idea-count, sum-of-quality, average-quality, and good-idea-count measures for each treatment.

The good-idea-count metric required that we set a threshold for what constitutes a good idea. Although ideas rated a 4 are clearly preferable, ideas rated a 3 or 2 could also be of use in the task defined for the participants. Thus, the task provided a reasonable basis for testing the sensitivity of the approach to threshold settings. We therefore computed three different good-idea-count measures for each session: number of ideas with a rating of 4, number of ideas rated as either 3 or 4, and number of ideas rated as 2, 3, or 4.

## Correlation Matrix of the Ideation Quality Measures

A correlation matrix for the ideation quality metrics across all treatments appears in Table 2. The idea-count and the sum-of-quality correlated strongly with each other (Table 2). However, the correlation matrix revealed a significant negative linear relationship between average-quality and idea-count, and between average-quality and sum-of-quality. Average-quality did not significantly correlate with good-idea-count for any of the three threshold settings. Good-idea-count correlated to varying degrees with idea-count and sum-of-quality. These findings are consistent with the limitations and biases argued above.

## Analysis of Treatments

We calculated the mean quality rating of each treatment using the four approaches, including the three different thresholds for good-idea count. Summary data and analysis of variance (ANOVA) results from these analyses are reported in Table 3. These findings reveal that the conclusions one would draw about the relative merits of the three interventions would differ, depending on which ideation quality measure was used. The idea-count and sum-of-quality measures both resulted in the high social comparison and moderate social comparison treatments having significantly higher means than did the control treatment. The average-quality measure, however, indicated that the control treatment was superior to the high social comparison treatment. The good-idea-count measure yielded no significant differences between the treatments, regardless of the threshold used for identifying a good idea (see Table 3). ## Discussion

THE BIASES OF THE QUALITY MEASURES ARGUED and illustrated above also manifested in the context of this study, which examined the efficacy of technology-supported social comparison interventions on ideation quality. We now discuss the construct validity issues of these measures and the implications for past and future technology-supported ideation research.

## The Validity of the Four Ideation Quality Measures

The four approaches to measuring ideation quality suggested three different conclusions in the present study. The study demonstrates a lack of intermeasure reliability among the measurement approaches when they are used with the same data set, including two significant negative correlations between the measures (see Table 2). This brings into question the construct validity of these measures, which we now discuss in light of the results of our study and the aforementioned limitations.

The measure idea-count led to virtually identical conclusions as the sum-of-quality measure, with each one accounting for 96.6 percent of the variance in the other (see Table 2) and leading to identical research conclusions (i.e., both of the social comparison treatments were superior to the control treatment). This finding is consistent with the bias argued above with respect to the sum-of-quality approach. In this case, because the social comparison treatments generated significantly more ideas than the control treatment (as shown with the idea-count measure), and did not generate significantly less good ideas (see Table 3), the sum-of-quality measure turned out to be little more than a slight modification of idea-count. The high correlation between these two measures may have created an illusion of a strong relationship between quantity and quality as reported in the literature.

By contrast, idea-count correlated negatively with average-quality $( r = - 0 . 4 2 0 , p <$ 0.001) and only weakly with good-idea-count when the highest threshold for rating an idea as good was used. The correlation between idea-count and good-idea-count steadily increased as the threshold for rating an idea as good was lowered. That is, idea-count accounted for 16.8 percent of the variance in the number of ideas rated a 4, 26.4 percent of the variance in the number of ideas rated a 3 or 4, and 59.9 percent of the variance in the number of ideas rated a 2, 3, or 4 (see Table 2).

## Implications for Past and Future Ideation Research

The idea-count, sum-of-quality, and average-quality measures, which account for many of the findings in the ideation literature, are flawed, and do not appear to provide reliable or valid indications of the relative merits of ideation interventions. An implication of this is that some of the research questions regarding ideation, particularly those examining technology-supported ideation, remain unanswered. The ongoing debate about whether technologies such as group support systems can help groups produce more ideas have been largely off-target (e.g., [10, 31, 32]) as the researchers have focused on the wrong dependent variable (i.e., idea-count rather than good-idea-count).

We argue here that the various measures used to test the efficacy of such interventions are based on insupportable assumptions, and we have demonstrated that when applied to a laboratory experiment, they lack intermeasure reliability. We argue that, among the four approaches, only the good-idea-count measure has sufficient validity to serve as the dependent variable on which to evaluate ideation interventions.

Researchers should therefore exercise caution when interpreting the results of past research. In future research, inferences about ideation quality should not be drawn from the use of the idea-count, sum-of-quality, and average-quality measures, nor should Osborn’s [28] conjecture that quality follows quantity be used as an excuse to avoid the laborious task of rating the quality of individual ideas. The idea-count, sum-of-quality, and average-quality measures are neither necessary nor sufficient to demonstrate the superiority of one ideation intervention over another in terms of ideation quality. Thus, although some researchers have argued that good-idea-count is the most appropriate of the set of measures (e.g., [14]), we would recommend that the other measures are not suitable for assessing ideation quality and should therefore not be used.

Of the current measures used in the literature, we recommend that researchers use the good-idea-count measure. This measure most accurately reflects what is desired in the workplace; teams ideate to generate a set of good ideas and ultimately initiate or implement one or a few of them [2]. We also recommend setting the threshold high for classifying an idea as good as it will help researchers identify those technological interventions that are most useful in practice. However, the threshold should be justified in terms of the goal of the task in which the ideation exercise is subsumed.

## Limitations of Good-Idea-Count

Although good-idea-count is the only valid ideation quality measure of the four examined in this study, it also has limitations. One limitation is that the measure does not reflect any negative consequence caused by the information overload that may occur in the presence of too many ideas. For example, one might prefer a set of ten ideas containing five good and five bad ideas to a set of 200 ideas containing six good and 194 bad ideas, even though the second set contains one more good idea than the first set. Thus, in order to ignore this limitation, one must assume that there exists an efficient method for converging on the best ideas or demonstrate that an intervention that produces more good ideas does not also produce an unwieldy amount of additional bad ideas.

Another limitation of the good-idea-count measure is that it requires the assumption that all good ideas are weighted equally. If a researcher sets the threshold for a good idea as the highest rating on his or her scale, then this limitation becomes a function of his or her quality scale in that the scale is discrete when, in reality, the quality of individual ideas often follows a continuous scale whereby some ideas are only slightly better than others. To illustrate this point, consider an example whereby a venture capitalist ideates and identifies a set of investment opportunities. Assuming he or she has enough capital to pursue all opportunities, he or she might set the threshold for a good idea (i.e., investment opportunity) as one that provides a return on investment (ROI) that exceeds a fixed return rate offered by a bank, such as 5 percent. Obviously, all investment opportunities exceeding 5 percent are not equally good; for example, some good investments might provide greater ROI than others and so would be more preferred. Thus, when the quality of ideas is quantifiable on a ratio scale, one could reasonably sum the value of the ideas exceeding some minimum quality threshold. Other examples of this would include identifying prospects for a new credit card offer (quality could be measured by the response rate exceeding the break-even point of random mailing) and identifying products to offer in a grocery store (measured in profit per space requirement). These examples share the attribute of a quantifiable ratio scale as the appropriate measure of idea quality. All good investments, advertising campaigns, and product offerings are not equal; therefore, assuming that accurate information can be obtained to rate the ideas on the appropriate ratio scale, the ideas exceeding the minimum quality threshold would have to be weighted in order to achieve an appropriate measure of ideation quality.

## Other Approaches to Measuring Ideation Quality

We have examined the four most common approaches to assessing ideation quality in the literature. All of these approaches, however, separate ideation from the larger context of organizational decision making and problem solving, of which ideation is only one component. It is not clear, however, that interventions that produce superior idea sets will lead to better decisions. This is because most researchers, present study included, do not take ideation to the next logical step of isolating a few alternatives from those generated and evaluating which action should be taken. Ideation quality may be better measured by including a convergence phase whereby teams use their idea sets to propose a small number of alternatives for further evaluation. Such research could then be coupled with other studies examining methods for evaluating alternatives.

## Conclusion

WE ARGUED AND SUBSEQUENTLY DEMONSTRATED with a laboratory study that the four ideation quality measures in the literature lack intermeasure reliability and thus may lead to different research conclusions with the same data. Although there are special situations where the four measures may converge (e.g., if all ideas turn out to be good ideas), we cannot assume this will be the case. Therefore, conclusions based on the idea-count, sum-of-quality, and average-quality measures should be called into question. In spite of its aforementioned limitations, we encourage researchers to use the good-idea-count measure in order to avoid drawing unwarranted conclusions about the efficacy of their ideation interventions. It might therefore be useful for the ideation research community to revisit some of its key findings to ensure that the relationships still hold when tested with the good-idea-count measure.
