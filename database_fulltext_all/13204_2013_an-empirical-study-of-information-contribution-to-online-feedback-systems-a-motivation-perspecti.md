# An empirical study of information contribution to online feedback systems: A motivation perspective

Yu Tong <sup>a,1</sup>, Xinwei Wang <sup>b,2</sup>, Chuan-Hoo Tan <sup>a,</sup>\*, Hock-Hai Teo <sup>b,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon Tong, Hong Kong <sup>b</sup> Department of Information Systems, National University of Singapore, 13 Computing Drive, Singapore

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 30 October 2012 Accepted 13 February 2013 Available online 12 August 2013

Keywords: Online feedback system Electronic word-of-mouth Motivation theory Economic rewarding mechanism Product review

## A B S T R A C T

Online feedback systems (OFSs) are increasingly available on online shopping websites; they allow consumers to post their ratings and consumption reviews for products. We employed motivation theory and a goal attainment perspective to model a set of motivating and inhibiting factors that could influence a consumer’s intention to contribute to an OFS. Our experiment, which involved 168 university students, showed that a consumer’s intention to contribute product reviews is influenced by perceived satisfaction gained in helping other consumers, perceived satisfaction gained in influencing the merchant, perceived probability of enhancing self-image, and perceived executional costs. In addition, the presence of an economic rewarding mechanism was found to promote a contribution when a consumer’s perceived probability of enhancing self-image was relatively high or when perceived cognitive cost was relatively low. Implications of our findings are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Today’s consumers are becoming increasingly Internet-knowledgeable, and are thus able to gain product information from others who have bought the product, and share their experience with others on the Internet [1,8]. To leverage people-to-people communication of product information, online shopping organizations have embedded applications of electronic word-of-mouth (EWOM) on their websites. We refer to such applications as the online feedback systems (OFSs), which allow consumers to post their ratings, short comments, and even detailed consumption reviews for products [4].

Today, many people are voluntarily sharing their product experience through OFSs [2]. From a cost-and-benefit analysis, such a trend may not be rational. First, contributing to an OFS could be cognitively and physically demanding. Writing a review, even though it may not be as comprehensive as experts’ reviews in Epinions.com, requires a reviewer to write the experience into a coherent message and put it in the system. Such efforts may dampen the reviewer’s enthusiasm to contribute [15]. Second, information contributed to an OFS may be perceived as a free public good from which others could benefit. These negative aspects led us to ask: What really inhibits or prompts a consumer to share product experiences through an OFS?

To compensate for contribution effort and foster more EWOM, providing contributors with economic rewards has become a trend in OFSs. However, existing rewards are mainly given to those who have provided outstanding reviews. Whether such a mechanism would attract a general consumer to provide EWOM is unclear. To shed light on appropriate and effective use of economic rewarding mechanism, our second research question was: Under what circumstances does a current economic rewarding mechanism help increase consumers’ OFS contributions?

Our review of extant EWOM literature suggests that consider able effort has been expended on understanding the impact of OFS on trust building and income; e.g., [3,10,11,13,14]. Among the relatively few studies investigating the determinants of a consumer’s intention of OFS/EWOM contribution, most have focused on identifying the factors that could motivate the contribution, such as desire for social interaction and overall value of artifacts [17]. However, inhibiting factors have seldom been considered in tandem. We therefore drew on the motivation theory to develop a research model containing both motivating and inhibiting factors that affect a consumer’s contribution to an OFS. Furthermore, based on a goal attainment perspective, we examined the circumstances when an economic rewarding mechanism could increase the likelihood of contribution by a consumer.

## 2. Theoretical foundation and research model

Motivating and inhibiting factors in our study were based on motivation theory, which offers significant insights into attitudes and goals underlying human behavior. These act as psychological forces that make a person try to perform a particular activity [9,16]. Motivation can manifest itself in three ways resulting in different experiences and consequences: amotivation (when he/she shows a lack of purpose or inspiration with respect to the activity and hence is less likely to perform it), intrinsic motivation (when he/she derives satisfaction and psychological well-being from performing the activity), or extrinsic motivation (when an individual performs the activity with a view toward securing certain outcomes as a result of participation). As the outcomes can be out of his/her control, the extrinsically motivated individual may often experience tension and pressure.

In the context of OFSs where large-scale EWOM is promoted and can be controlled by OFS practitioners, a consumer’s review is likely to be fostered or deterred by multiple motivation orientations [5]. These motivations can originate from participating in conventional word-of-mouth (WOM) behavior or be induced by the specific settings in OFSs. Thus motivation theory provides an opportunity to explore both inhibitors and motivators of a consumer’s contribution.

Our analysis leads us to the research model of Fig. 1. Our main proposition was that a consumer’s intention to contribute product reviews to an OFS was negatively associated with inhibiting factors (amotivators) such as perceived cognitive and executional costs, positively related to intrinsic motivating factors, such as perceived satisfaction in helping other consumers and in influencing the product merchant, and positively associated with two extrinsic factors, such as perceived probability of enhancing self-image and presence of economic rewarding mechanism. Furthermore, the effect of the economic rewarding mechanism on intention to contribute may vary with the factors resulting in an expectation of rewards.

## 2.1. Inhibiting factors of information contribution to OFS

Amotivation: This refers to a lack of motivation to engage in an activity; i.e., it inhibits the occurrence of related behavior and can occur when an individual needs to expend excessive effort on an activity or bears large costs in performing it. Some inhibiting factors include loss of power in organizational electronic knowledge sharing and lack of computer efficacy in the general online context. While prior studies have offered valuable insights, the factors they have identified may not have been unique traits of OFS. The potential of losing power can be an important deterrent because knowledge contributors and seekers compete for limited resources. However, this may not be significant in the OFS context where contributors and seekers do not have a competitive relationship.

In our study, we tried to identify the types of amotivation relatively unique to the OFS context. We analyzed the process of providing product reviews to OFSs to identify the perceived efforts and costs that may act to amotivate. First, a consumer who provides a product review to an OFS is likely to undergo intensive mental processing to retrieve past consumption experience and organize it into a coherent and convincing review. Compared to conventional WOM, where contributors’ mental preparation is aided by interpersonal interaction and cues such as specific prompts, this stage is relatively independent and likely to be cognitively taxing. Second, instead of using verbal communication, OFS contributors need to spend effort in composing and submitting the review. This highlights two types of cost that potentially deter a general consumer’s propensity of providing product reviews: (1) perceived cognitive cost, and (2) perceived executional cost, when he/she needs to codify the review.

Hence, a general consumer may perceive the mental process to be cognitively costly and annoying, discouraging the consumer’s propensity to contribute a review. Thus:

H1. Perceived cognitive cost is negatively related to the intention of a consumer to contribute product reviews to an OFS.

Perceived executional cost has been discussed in the context of electronic knowledge sharing behavior, where providing a detailed product review was seen as requiring substantial time. Furthermore, the effort could be tiresome. Thus a general consumer perceives high cost and will be less willing to contribute:

H2. Perceived executional cost is negatively related to the intention of a consumer to contribute product reviews to an OFS.

## 2.2. Motivating factors of information contribution to OFS

Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation: Depending on the source of motivation, an individual may be motivated either intrinsically or extrinsically. An intrinsically motivated individual would derive pleasant psychological feelings, such as accomplishment and satisfaction, from the process of performing an activity. Under such circumstances, the individual’s behavior is volitional and he/she experiences a sense of autonomy when performing the activity [7]. In contrast, an extrinsically motivated individual focuses on the results of the participation and uses it as a way to obtain outcomes (economic rewards or others’ approval).

We conducted a review of the conventional WOM literature and this suggested three types of motivation that promote WOM behavior: (1) involving other people and providing consumption information because of a genuine desire to help others make a better purchase; (2) noting a good or bad aspect of a product and/ or its merchant that creates pressure to communicate it to others, thereby promoting the product and the merchant or, through a negative experience, retaliating against the company; and (3) the consumer seeking the opportunity to improve his or her self-image.

A consumer’s reviews provided to an OFS are persistently stored and can be easily accessed by a global community [6]. Furthermore, when consumers rely on the system as the medium to share information with others, OFS practitioners are able to deploy specific settings to provide attractive outcomes to the contributors.

Perceived satisfaction in helping other consumers is closely related to the concept of ‘‘altruism’’. The thought of helping others is often argued as a self-fulfilling return to the contributor. To the extent that providing product review to an OFS can give other consumers the opportunity to make a better purchase decision, a consumer who feels good when helping other consumers is likely to be motivated to engage in the review contribution behavior. Thus:

H3. Perceived satisfaction in helping other consumers is positively related to the intention of a consumer to contribute product reviews to an OFS.

Fulfilling a sense of accomplishment is also an important type of intrinsic motivation. While an individual performs an influential or a challenging task, he/she can experience satisfaction during the process. Based on the impact of the influence, satisfaction can be derived by offering: (1) positive reviews to help promote a product merchant or improve the product as a return for a satisfactory consumption experience, or (2) negative reviews to exhibit dissatisfaction or exert vengeance after a negative consumption experience. Regardless of the valance of the motive, an individual could anticipate pleasure from being able to influence the product merchants.

H4. Perceived satisfaction in influencing the product merchant, through providing either positive or negative reviews, is positively related to the intention of a consumer to contribute product reviews to an OFS.

Extrinsic motivation could be either internalized or external. An individual who is motivated by internalized extrinsic motivation assimilates external incentives and integrates them with his/her endorsed values. When the individual undergoes such a process, he/she feels less constrained by external contingencies. In the context of our study, an OFS contributor, if able to provide insightful product reviews, could receive respect and recognition from many people. The respect and recognition can result in the achievement of a higher status in the OFS. An individual often relies on others’ appraisal and feedback as a means of improving his/her social status. Thus we posited:

H5. Perceived probability of enhancing self-image is positively related to the intention of a consumer to contribute product reviews to an OFS.

An individual who is driven by external extrinsic motivation performs the activity purely for the purpose of receiving desirable external outcomes, such as financial compensation. Economic rewards are provided to reviewers in some OFSs such as Epinions.com’s economic rewarding mechanism. We expected that the presence of monetary rewards could operate as an external incentive to motivate a consumer to share product consumption experiences to an OFS:

H6. The intention of a consumer to contribute product reviews to an OFS is higher when the economic rewarding mechanism is present, as opposed to when the economic rewarding mechanism is absent.

## 2.3. Moderation effects of the economic rewarding mechanism

While an economic rewarding mechanism is adopted by OFSs, rewarding criteria are usually tied to the review performance, such as its quality and quantity. While the rewarding criteria appear to promote continual long-term EWOM contributions, does compensation effectively encourage consumers to contribution to an OFS?

For the economic rewarding mechanism to motivate a consumer’s review contribution to an OFS, the consumer needs to consider the economic rewards as a goal and engage in an activity that could help attain the goal. Researchers have suggested that expecting to achieve a goal could influence the consumer’s behavior. Specifically, when an individual perceives a high probability of attaining the goal through performing the activity, he/she will be motivated to engage in that activity. However, when an individual perceives that it is difficult to obtain the desirable goal due to high demand on time and effort or inadequate capability, etc., he/she will be less likely to pursue the goal. We therefore proposed that the effectiveness of the economic rewarding mechanism on a consumer’s contribution would hinge on factors that affect his/her perceived probability and difficulty of meeting the rewards criteria (of constantly providing high-quality product information).

A strong belief in one’s ability to perform a goal pursuing activity assures a high probability of success. In the context of our study, this means that if a consumer strongly believes that he/she can give high-quality reviews, the consumer’s expectancy of attaining economic rewards will be heightened. A high probability of enhancing self-image implies that the consumer believes that his/her reviews will be valuable to consumers. To the extent that the quality of the these reviews matches the critical criterion used by the OFS to grant rewards, consumers with a expectation of enhancing self-image are likely to be more responsive to the current economic rewarding mechanism as they are likely to obtain the rewards. Thus:

H7. The positive effect of the economic rewarding mechanism in increasing a consumer’s review contribution intention will be more significant when the consumer perceives a high probability of enhancing self-image than when the consumer perceives a low probability of enhancing self-image.

While the perceived ability of attaining a goal could serve as a positive stimulus, the difficulty of performing the goal pursuing behavior may also be considered as a negative stimulus reducing an individual’s assessment of goal attainment expectancy. Taking the goal attainment perspective, we proposed that the effect of an economic rewarding mechanism on a consumer’s contribution intention is also contingent on the extent of two cost perceptions.

Though perceived cognitive cost and perceived executional cost occur at different times in a review contribution process, both costs may heighten the difficulty of achieving a reward. Specifically, a consumer who perceives that review contribution to an OFS requires less cognitive and executional costs would expect less difficulty and effort when providing helpful reviews. Under such circumstances, it is easier for the consumer to contribute highquality information on a long-term basis, providing him/her with the advantage of acquiring economic rewards under the criteria that require many quality reviews. The consumer’s expectation of gaining economic rewards could thus increase his/her intention to start to contribute reviews. Consequently:

H8. The positive effect of the economic rewarding mechanism in increasing a consumer’s review contribution intention will be more significant when the consumer perceives a low cognitive cost than when the consumer perceives a high cognitive cost.

H9. The positive effect of the economic rewarding mechanism in increasing a consumer’s review contribution intention will be more significant when the consumer perceives a low executional cost than when the consumer perceives a high executional cost.

## 2.4. Control variables

We included two factors that may influence review contribution as control variables in our research model: opinion leadership and Internet experience. Opinion leaders (individuals who are inclined to provide other consumers with consumption advice) may affect the consumer’s intention to participate in an EWOM. Thus, we considered opinion leadership to be a control variable. Furthermore, we considered Internet experience to be a control variable because it is recognized as a good proxy for computer self-efficacy, which can foster Internet-related behaviors.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Research design

A vignette-based experiment was used in our study [12]; it allowed us to use a large population and to execute quasiexperimental manipulation by providing two different scenarios: with presence or absence of an economic rewarding mechanism. This variable was chosen to reflect current OFS operation practices. Today, only some large-scale OFSs (e.g., Epinions.com) offer this benefit to reviewers, since an OFS may not be able to generate profit in its start-up stage. In our study, economic rewards were presented in the form of shopping vouchers that were designed in accordance with Epinions’ income sharing program (accumulation, quality, and demand). Table 1 lists the instructions provided to the respondents about the economic rewarding mechanism.

The OFS gives out economic rewards to all reviewers in the form of ‘‘gift certificate vouchers,’’ which can be used to buy any product in the website. These vouchers are given to reviewers who consistently provide reviews that are deemed helpful for consumer decision-making. Focus on items where there is a need for decision-making information. Be among the first to write about a product.

Write high-quality, original work that details the pros and cons of the item being reviewed.

## 3.2. Instrument development

Measures that had been validated in previous studies were used to ensure their validity; they were adapted to the current topic. All measurements used a seven-point Likert scale, from ‘‘1’’ for strongly disagree, to ‘‘7’’ for strongly agree. Sorting procedures were used to ensure conceptual validation where items at the operational level reflected constructs at the conceptual level. We conducted two rounds of sorting (i.e., unstructured and structured) to ensure the pertinence of each question to its own construct. Both Cohen’s kappa and the item placement hit ratio were selected to assess the results of each round of sorting. For unstructured sorting, the kappa score average was 0.71 and the overall placement ratio of items was 0.84, while for structured sorting, Cohen’s kappa score average was 0.96 and the overall item placement ratio was 0.98, indicating that measures were satisfactory for further analysis. Modifications were made in each round of sorting results and judges’ comments. Twenty-seven undergraduate students voluntarily participated in the pilot test to confirm the nature of the two versions of vignettes. Both Cronbach’s alphas and a factor analysis with varimax rotation were computed to test the reliability of items and instrument validity. Table 2 lists the survey items and related sources.

## 3.3. Experimental task

Undergraduate students, from a large public university and owning cellular phones were recruited as participants in our experiment. This product selection ensured that participants had experience that allowed them to provide good product reviews. The participants were recruited from different faculties to increase the generalizability of their results and minimize selection bias. Experiment booklets containing experiment manipulations and questions were distributed to the students after class and collected by the experiment investigators. Participation was voluntary with a lucky draw incentive.

The completed booklets contained a cover letter stating the purpose of the study, a short introduction to the OFS used in this study, and a description of a sample detailed product review. Additionally, each participant was required to answer a set of questions that provided their opinion on a cell phone that he/she was using. The participants were randomly assigned to one of the two scenarios, presence or absence of an economic rewarding mechanism. At the end of the experiment, demographic information was collected, during which the participants were assured of the confidentiality of their information. Out of 400 questionnaires handed out, 188 responses were received, resulting in a response rate of 47%. Subsequently, we used only 168 entries, as 20 questionnaires were incomplete and had to be discarded.

## 4.1. Measurement model

Convergent and discriminant validities were performed for all multiple-item constructs. Cronbach’s alpha greater than 0.7 shows adequate reliability. Next, convergent validity was assessed through item reliability, composite reliability of constructs, and average variance extracted (AVE). An AVE score of 0.5 indicates an acceptable level of variance extracted. Item and composite reliabilities were expected to be at least 0.70. The results of our tests presented in Table 4 exceeded the acceptable thresholds. Therefore, we achieved good reliability and convergent validity.

Discriminant validity was assessed in two ways: factor analysis and item correlation. The commonly accepted threshold for item loading is 0.5, while loadings above 0.71 are considered excellent. Our results yielded seven components with eigen values greater than 1.0. All loadings of items were higher on their targeted constructs and exceeded the acceptable threshold. The square root of AVE for a construct should be greater than its correlations with other constructs. Our result, shown in Table 5, had data on the diagonal showing the square root of AVE. All constructs fulfilled the requirement for discriminant validity.

## 4.2. Moderated multiple regression and its assumption

Multiple regression analysis (MRA) was used to simultaneously assess the relationship between a single dependent variable and several independent variables. Moreover, the moderating effect can be tested by multiplicative terms using moderated multiple regression (MMR). Normality of a variable can be tested using skewness and kurtosis. A value between -2.5 and 2.5 suggests a normal distribution. Outliers occur when the values are more than 3 standard deviations from the mean value. Multicollinearity can be tested using a tolerance value and the variance inflation factor (VIF). The acceptable range is a tolerance >0.2 and VIF <5. Our test results, recorded in Table 6, showed that all values were within the acceptable thresholds.

## 4.3. The structural model

MMR was used to test both main and moderating effects. The average of each construct’s score was used for both model and hypothesis assessments. Interaction terms were computed by multiplying two independent constructs. All constructs were centered to alleviate possible multicollinearity problems.

Table 7 shows the results of hypothesis testing. The overall significance of our model was assessed by F ratio and R<sup>2</sup>. Model indices in Step 1 which included only independent variables $\textstyle ( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 5 0$ , adjusted $R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 4 8 , F = 2 6 . 5 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and Step 2 that included independent variables and three interaction terms (R<sup>2</sup> = 0.55, adjusted-R<sup>2</sup> = 0.53, change in $F = 6 . 4 8 , p < 0 . 0 1 \rangle$ ) indicated that the overall theoretical model possessed satisfactory explanatory power in explaining the variance in OFS contribution intention. Significance of change in F value indicated that the model with interaction terms had significantly more explanatory power than the model with only main effects.

As shown in Table 7, the main effect of cognitive cost was not significant; thus, H1 was not supported. Perceived executional cost had a significant negative effect on contribution intention; hence H2 was supported. Three motivating factors (i.e., perceived satisfaction in helping other consumers, perceived satisfaction in influencing the product merchant and perceived probability of enhancing self-image) were positively and significantly related to a consumer’s contribution intention to the OFS, indicating that H3, H4, and H5 were supported. We failed to validate H6. We observed that the economic rewarding mechanism interacted with the perceived probability of enhancing self-image (positively) and perceived cognitive cost (negatively), but not with perceived executional cost, to affect a consumer’s contribution propensity.

## 5. Discussion and implications

## 5.1. Discussion of the results

EWOM is a prominent Internet phenomenon that has a profound impact on both online and offline businesses. However, the mechanism for identifying factors that influence a general consumer to initiate EWOM contribution is under-researched. To address this gap, our work focused on OFS and identified the factors motivating a general consumer, who may not have prior experience, to provide product reviews.

Results of our study revealed that motivating and inhibiting antecedents accounted for a considerable proportion of variance (approximately 49.7%) of a consumer’s intention to contribute product reviews to an OFS after controlling for opinion leadership and Internet experience. Our observations shed light on the ongoing inquiry into what motivates a general consumer to spend time and effort in contributing product consumption reviews. OFS contribution behavior may be suppressed by one’s cost concerns and driven by motivation induced by the OFS settings.

Specifically, our study found that if one perceives the effort of codifying one’s reviews to be too demanding and onerous, one may decide not to write a review. In terms of motivating factors, results of our study shed light on how motivations can influence a consumer’s review contribution. As hypothesized, when a consumer perceived the review contribution could bring satisfaction associated with helping other consumers, satisfaction from influencing the merchant, or gaining an enhanced image in the OFS community, he/she would be motivated to engage in an OFS.

We found that the presence of the economic rewarding mechanism did not show a significant main effect on a consumer’s OFS contribution intention. Hence, the current rewarding schema may not be effective. Nonetheless, we found two factors that significantly moderated this relationship: perceived probability of enhancing self-image and perceived cognitive cost. The economic rewarding mechanism may be more effective in encouraging contribution from consumers who felt that recalling and organizing product information was effortless. Perceived executional cost did not show a significant moderating effect.

## 5.2. Limitations

Our study had several limitations. First, our measurement of the variables was based on several sets of scales. Second, generalizability of the results may be limited since participants were undergraduate students and only one product category was involved in the study. Third, our study focused on general consumers, the majority of whom may not have prior experience in OFS contribution. It should be noted that their perceptions on the contribution benefits/costs may differ from those of actual contributors. Fourth, while our study only examined the performance-based rewarding mechanism. Fifth, we used Internet experience as a proxy for self-efficacy.

## 5.3. Theoretical contributions

Our study explored various reasons why a consumer intends to participate or not participate in sharing their product consumption experience at an OFS. Such knowledge is critical to its sustainability. Beyond OFSs, adequate voluntary content provision is also crucial for the success of other user-generated content applications such as social networking sites.

We have also provided one of the first empirical studies on inhibiting factors of EWOM contribution, identifying two amotivations that affect a consumer’s contribution propensity in OFSs: a consumer’s perception of the excessive effort of inputting the review and the perceived effort of cognitively recalling the consumption experiences and organizing the information, which impacts contribution propensity indirectly by moderating the influence of the economic rewarding mechanism for a consumer’s contribution.

Also, our study adds to knowledge about testing the effectiveness and boundary conditions of economic rewarding mechanisms. Our results imply that the effectiveness of such a mechanism is contingent on the probability of enhancing selfimage and the extent of cognitive cost perceived by the consumer.

## 5.4. Practical contributions

Our findings should encourage more consumers to engage in EWOM-related activities using an OFS. Specifically, since perceived satisfaction in helping other consumers and in influencing the merchant appear to increase a consumer’s contribution intention, OFS practitioners may need to develop some new features in the system to encourage such perceptions and feelings. Products with high review demand may be emphasized and highlighted with animation. Practitioners can also create a top review demand list and present the list on the homepage of the website. In addition, since perceived probability of enhancing self-image serves as a significant benefit as well, an OFS can offer various ways to help potential contributors increase their reputation. OFS practitioners can further extend the scope of status recognition. For example, a single review, if insightful, may be selected and highlighted in an OFS.

Our study also cautions practitioners to be careful when using the economic rewarding mechanism to motivate participation in OFSs; current economic rewarding mechanism emphasizing on accumulative outstanding performance may not be effective in attracting potential EWOM participants. OFS practitioners need to understand potential contributors’ desire for online image enhancement to identify whether they would be responsive to a rewarding mechanism. The significant interaction between perceived cognitive cost and the economic rewarding mechanism suggests that OFS practitioners should attempt to reduce the perceived cognitive cost associated with information contribution.

Besides proposing strategies for segmenting potential contributors and indicating the segments that the current rewarding mechanism should target, we also offer some possible changes to the rewarding mechanism to attract potential contributors who perceive low probability of enhancing self-image and high cognitive cost. OFS practitioners could consider compensating each comprehensive review by a small amount, in the form of a small monetary reward or future rebate.

## 6. Conclusion

As computer and Internet penetration increase, individuals will have more opportunities to share their experience. Without understanding why consumers are willing to contribute to an OFS, it is possible that electronic commerce may not expand. Our study developed a better knowledge of the factors influencing a consumer’s decision to contribute reviews to an OFS.
