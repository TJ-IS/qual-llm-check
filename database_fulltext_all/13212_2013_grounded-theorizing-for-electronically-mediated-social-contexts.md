# Grounded theorizing for electronically mediated social contexts

Emmanuelle Vaast<sup>1</sup> and Geoff Walsham<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, Montreal, Canada.<sup>2</sup>Judge Business School, University of Cambridge, U.K.

## Abstract

Electronically mediated social contexts (EMSCs), in which interactions and activities are largely or completely computer-mediated, have become important settings for investigation by Information Systems (IS) scholars. Owing to the relative novelty and originality of EMSCs, IS researchers often lack existing theories to make sense of the processes that emerge in them. Therefore, many IS researchers have relied upon grounded theory in order to develop new theory based on empirical observations from EMSCs. This article reviews a selected set of papers concerned with grounded IS research on EMSCs. It examines how the authors of these papers handled the characteristics of EMSCs and, in particular, addresses the topics of data collection, data analysis, and theory building. The paper also draws implications and recommendations for grounded researchers interested in investigating these original and fascinating environments in their future work. For example, it calls for grounded researchers on EMSCs to reflect upon the characteristics of their domains of inquiry, to respect the logic of discovery of grounded methods, and to articulate more clearly their theoretical ambitions along the induction/ abduction continuum. The paper closes by suggesting promising areas for future grounded research on EMSCs, including taking advantage of the potential for combining qualitative and quantitative analytical methods. ## Introduction

Over the last decade or so, electronically mediated social contexts (EMSCs) have become important and intriguing new settings for Information Systems (IS) research. In EMSCs, interactions and activities take place in an environment that is mostly or completely electronically mediated. Participants in EMSCs use computer-supported networks in order to communicate and may not have much opportunity for face-to-face encounters. EMSCs have recently become more and more prevalent in various social and business environments with the widespread development of Web-based networks. For instance, many people have joined a variety of online forums, open-source software communities, online communities, social networks, online knowledge networks, and virtual teams in order to work and collaborate, but also to find support and develop new relationships (e.g. Galagher et al., 1998; Rheingold, 2000; Cummings et al., 2002; Finholt et al., 2002; Moon & Sproull, 2002; Cross & Sproull, 2004; McLure Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Vaast, 2007).

EMSCs are important emerging domains of inquiry for IS researchers because the IS discipline deals directly with the interactions between new computer-based systems and networks and human and social behaviors. IS researchers in this area explore and theorize the complexities of the socio-technical processes emerging from the development of EMSCs (Orlikowski, 2007). They try to make sense of and conceptualize new developments that have emerged as more and more people have joined EMSCs. EMSCs constitute research settings that are especially important and intriguing for IS researchers because they offer the opportunity to extend analyses of existing areas that have been at the core of the IS discipline, such as computer-supported cooperative work or electronically mediated interactions.

At the same time as offering interesting new opportunities for research, though, EMSCs pose certain challenges to IS researchers. Making sense of these emergent dynamics requires new theories that can fit new observations. In this regard, grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Strauss & Corbin, 1998) appears to be an especially well-suited methodology to develop new, substantial theories that are grounded in empirical observations of EMSCs.

Glaser & Strauss (1967) originally developed grounded theory as an alternative to positivism and to the idea of a ‘grand theory’ that would encompass all behavior (Suddaby, 2006). They founded grounded theory on the basis of early symbolic interactionism (from George Mead) and of pragmatism (from John Peirce) that considered that scientific ‘truth’ could not be understood as reflecting an independent reality but, rather, as emerging from observations, and from the building of a consensus making sense of these observations (Pidgeon, 1996; Bryant & Charmaz, 2007). Grounded theory has come to encompass a set of methods that help researchers gradually build theorizations that are deeply grounded in the empirical settings they have investigated (Gasson, 2003; Charmaz, 2006; Suddaby, 2006). The grounded theory method benefits from a strong tradition of research in social, mostly (but not exclusively) qualitative, research. It has been applied fruitfully in the IS field (see, for examples of applications of the method, Orlikowski, 1993; Urquhart, 1999; Galal, 2001, and for reviews of this literature, Gasson, 2003, forthcoming).

Grounded theory can help understand processes and dynamics related to EMSCs since these contexts are usually relatively new and their participants develop and learn original behaviors. Hence observations are changing rapidly and the phenomena under study may not yet be stabilized, forcing researchers to acknowledge explicitly the temporal and processual dimension of investigations of EMSCs as consequential and delicate (Orlikowski & Yates, 2002). Grounded theory is especially well-suited to the development of such process theories that conceptualize what happens over time in certain socio-technical conditions (Corbin & Strauss, 1990).

Moreover, EMSCs constitute new and challenging research settings for IS researchers to explore due to the potential to collect and analyze data that have only recently become part of the social researcher’s toolbox. These electronic data are fast becoming part of the data corpus for researchers interested in EMSCs (e.g. online discussions taking place through email or in an online forum, and the contents of electronic messages as in Orlikowski & Yates, 1994; McLure Wasko & Faraj, 2005). They require rigor and creativity to become part of a meaningful data corpus from which a theory can emerge (Glaser, 1992). These data pose specific challenges and opportunities to grounded researchers. Moreover, given the importance of data to build grounded theory, being able to deal with these new data appropriately is critical for grounded theorists.

The purpose of this paper is to review published exemplars of research on EMSCs that followed grounded theory methods in order to characterize how such methods were applied in these contexts and to provide recommendations that can help future researchers on EMSCs deal with opportunities and challenges arising from grounded methods. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. First, it presents the articles that were selected for review. It then details key issues and approaches for three important and inter-related dimensions of grounded theory research: data collection, data analysis, and theory building. For each of these dimensions, the paper analyzes how the reviewed articles tackled issues arising from the use of grounded methods, and draws implications for future research on EMSCs based on the grounded theory approach. The paper ends with a brief conclusion on contributions and limitations of our study, and outlines some future research opportunities, including a discussion of the combination of qualitative and quantitative analysis for grounded theorizing on EMSCs.

## A review of published research on EMSCs relying upon grounded theory methods

We reviewed published research related to EMSCs – defined here as social settings in which participants interact mostly or only through electronically mediated means – that used grounded theory. We then aimed at selecting papers published from reputable sources of relevance to IS researchers and that seemed to best exemplify the application of grounded theory methods to the investigation of EMSCs and their dynamics.

This sample was purposely theoretical rather than systematic and exhaustive. We aimed at selecting a sample of grounded research on EMSCs that we thought constituted exemplars of application of the grounded methods to these new contexts. In order to choose the papers included in our review, we applied two main criteria: (1) most if not all interactions that took place in the investigated settings were electronically mediated; and (2) the reported research methods explicitly applied key principles of grounded theorizing. We therefore excluded from our review research dealing with EMSCs but whose presentation of the method followed a hypothetico-deductive template (theory-derived hypotheses that are then tested in an electronically mediated social setting, e.g. Constant et al., 1996; McLure Wasko & Faraj, 2005). It is known that the academic peer-reviewed process sometimes makes researchers present the methodology of their research in a neater and more linear manner than how it actually unfolded (Suddaby, 2006; Walsham, 2006). It is therefore quite possible that some apparently deductive research on EMSCs may have been more grounded than presented in the published work. Yet, since we had no way to gauge the potential grounded process that was followed in such research, we focused our analysis on research that explicitly derived theory from data and that followed grounded theory guidelines of analysis and coding. Our selection includes seven papers. Table 1 details the research objectives, investigated EMSCs and main findings of each of these papers.

Overall, through their grounded theory positioning, the papers in our sample shared an effort to let the data speak before imposing a conceptual framework upon it, and to let the theory emerge from the data and become gradually refined through constant comparison of the data among themselves and with the emergent coding and existing theories (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). This was visible, for instance, in O’Mahony & Ferraro’s (2007) research on governance in open-source projects. The paper is built on a series of back-and-forth iterations between existing theories of governance in various organizational forms and the gradually more pointed grounded analysis of the processes of governance within a specific open-source project.

The papers from our review vary in the ways they applied grounded theory procedures, which reflects the variety of grounded theory research (Dey, 1999; Bryant & Charmaz, 2007). From Glaser & Strauss’ (1967) seminal text stemmed various interpretations and 'schools' of grounded theory research, each recommending several variants of grounded procedures (Charmaz, 2006; Morse et al., 2009). In particular, the two founders of the method developed divergent interpretations over time of what grounded theory aims to accomplish and of how it should go about achieving it. Glaser promoted a less structured approach to the development of grounded theory, leaving room for what he called ‘theoretical sensitivity’ (Glaser, 1978, 1992; Suddaby, 2006). Strauss, on the other hand, supported the application of relatively systematic approaches of data collection and analysis in order to rigorously ground the emerging theory on the data (Corbin & Strauss, 1990; Strauss & Corbin, 1998). While the articles from our sample differed regarding how they applied grounded theory methods and the degree of detail with which they articulated their methods, they nonetheless all emphasized the adoption of a relatively structured methodology, making them de facto closer to Strauss’ position than to Glaser’s. This observation is not highly surprising in itself as it echoes the IS discipline’s attempt to develop new theory while also reaching relatively high standards of rigor, including, in particular, for qualitative research (Klein & Myers, 1999).

Grounded theory methods involve a highly recursive process whereby researchers gradually refine their analyses and budding theory by going back and forth between their empirical observations, the emerging conceptualization, and possible existing theory (Dey, 1999; Gasson, 2003). In what follows, for purposes of clarification, while acknowledging the recursive character of grounded theory methods, we analyze how the reviewed papers tackled three essential aspects of such research: data collection, data analysis, and theory building. In each section, we end by highlighting implications that can help future researchers working on EMSCs to deal with opportunities and challenges arising from grounded methods.

## EMSCs as research settings for grounded theorists

Grounded theorists respect the characteristics of their phenomenon of interest when investigating it empirically. In this regard, EMSCs as research settings present certain characteristics that can affect the data that are collected. Indeed, activities taking place in these EMSCs occur partly or completely through electronically mediated means. The sample of reviewed research in this paper (see Tables 1 and 2) confirms both how widespread these contexts have become as well as how diverse they can be (e.g. virtual teams, open-source software communities. inter- and intra-organizational online forums). The diversity of EMSCs is striking with regard to their virtuality, that is to the degree to which participants in the setting have the ability to meet face-to-face or not, or have been able to develop a history of past common experiences. For instance, the developers who participated in the open-source projects investigated by O’Mahony & Ferraro’s (2007) and Lee & Cole (2003) interacted entirely through electronically mediated means. These fully distributed contexts differ greatly from the situation of the teams in Sarker & Sahay (2004) that operated in two different countries and that met both face-to-face (among team members in the same country) and virtually (among team members who were geographically distant).

## Electronic data and ethical challenges for grounded researchers

Even though various EMSCs differ in their degree of virtuality, they nonetheless pose several key shared challenges and opportunities for grounded researchers’ data collection. In particular, grounded researchers interested in EMSCs have the ability to collect data that have been automatically produced in the course of communications and interactions among participants in the EMSCs. Some of these data include message threads in online forums (e.g. Da Cunha & Orlikowski, 2008), emails among participants in virtual teams (e.g. Sarker & Sahay, 2004; Yoo & Alavi, 2004), among members of open-source software development teams (e.g. Lee & Cole, 2003; O’Mahony & Ferraro, 2007) as well as recipients of a list serve emailing list (e.g. Hara & Hew, 2007). For the purpose of simplicity, we label all such data as ‘electronic data’ in what follows.

Electronic data provide new opportunities but also ethical challenges to researchers (Vaast, 2003; Allen et al., 2006) and, in particular, to grounded theorists. Grounded theorists normally aim to develop an in-depth understanding of their research object, and, to do so, often rely on participant observation. In traditional participant observation contexts, where the researcher who is granted access to the setting commits to sustained field visits, access can be broad and deep. However, because of material constraints among others, researchers cannot transparently observe all that is going on in their research setting. By contrast, grounded researchers of EMSCs can potentially access all previous and ongoing electronically mediated communications (e.g. Lee & Cole, 2003; Da Cunha & Orlikowski, 2008).

Such wide access to online interactions can blur the perception of what is private and what is public information (Buchanan & Ess, 2008). Some EMSCs are only accessible to participants with certain credentials or affiliations (e.g. Da Cunha & Orlikowski, 2008). Many EMSCS however are publicly open for anyone to browse and participate. Their administrators usually post a disclaimer acknowledging that the discussions can be accessed by anyone and should not be considered protected from outsiders. For instance, most information exchanged in open-source communities becomes public knowledge available to new members (as in Lee & Cole, 2003 and O’Mahony & Ferraro, 2007). In other cases, non-restricted EMSCs are dedicated to focused topics and therefore are expected to attract particular target participants. For instance, the online community that Hara & Hew (2007) investigated was dedicated to issues faced by nurses, but non-nurses could also access it. Many of the discussions concerned delicate aspects of nursing practice, aspects that not all nursing participants were likely to be willing to share with non-members of the profession. Hara & Hew (2007) respected the privacy of the contributors to the online forum by ensuring their anonymity, keeping the exact name of the online forum and of its participants unknown.

## Collecting multiple sources of data for triangulation of the emergent theory

Electronic data do not constitute the exclusive source of observations that grounded researchers can collect when investigating EMSCs. They can complement electronic data with other data that are often part of the researcher’s more traditional toolbox. Some of these other sources of data are: semi-directed interviews (e.g. Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Hara & Hew, 2007; Da Cunha & Orlikowski, 2008), participant observation taking place in a physical context, if relevant (e.g. Sarker & Sahay, 2004), or questionnaires (e.g. Lee & Cole, 2003; Yoo & Alavi, 2004).

Triangulation helps grounded researchers gradually and rigorously build a conceptual framework that encompasses various sources of evidence (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). The researchers in our sample relied upon triangulation in various ways according to their research objectives and to the specifics of their research context. Triangulation was partly a testament of the different degrees of virtuality and the different levels of access to their EMSC of interest that the researchers enjoyed. For instance, it made sense for Sarker & Sahay (2004) to complement the analysis of electronic activities of the virtual teams they studied with the results of their own direct participant observation because they had direct (and physical) access to these teams. For Orlikowski & Yates (1994), on the other hand, direct participant observation would have yielded few results since the events they investigated were long past. They therefore complemented electronic archives of the forum with qualitative interviews of key forum participants.

Through triangulation, grounded researchers make the different sources of data complement one another. Da Cunha & Orlikowski (2008) for instance relied upon electronic archives to get a sense of the collective response to an event (the restructuring of an organization) and to assess how quickly responses were produced and by whom. Semi-directed interviews helped the researchers get a sense of respondents’ motivations and interpretations of these responses. The forum threads also provided a complete archive of responses to the reorganization while the interview data yielded insights to contextualize and interpret this archive.

Triangulation is also used to help researchers build elements of an answer to different questions. For instance, in Hara & Hew (2007), electronic data (forum threads) helped researchers build a typology of the categories of knowledge shared among participants in the forums; semi-directed interviews with key informants led them to theorize the related question of what sustained knowledge sharing among nurses in the forum.

Overall, finally, triangulation allows grounded researchers to corroborate working propositions and relations among emerging categories and to build their grounded theory. Yoo & Alavi (2004) for instance relied upon the quantitative analysis of the number and length of electronic messages by different virtual team members in order to identify who the emergent leaders of these virtual teams were. They observed that virtual leaders sent many long electronic messages. They then complemented this preliminary finding with a qualitative analysis of some of these messages in order to identify what types of roles these emergent leaders took upon in their virtual team.

## Implications

Overall, grounded theorists interested in EMSCs encounter new opportunities (in terms of depth and breadth of access and of ability to triangulate data) but also face ethical dilemmas that are heightened by these very opportunities. We therefore contend that grounded researchers who investigate specific EMSCs should reflect upon the characteristics of their research setting from which they collect data. Such consideration has long been an important aspect of the grounded theory method. Strauss & Corbin (1998) for instance recommended that a researcher directly involved in a setting reflect upon his/her participation in the investigated process and make this reflection a step in the theory building. We believe that the need for such thinking is especially heightened for research on EMSCs because of the original and diverse character of these contexts. In particular, researchers should pay special attention to the degree of virtuality in the communications among participants in the research setting and think about what their data can and cannot tell them. Grounded researchers should also decide what degree of anonymity to provide to participants as well as to the setting itself. Beyond the technically open character of interactions in many EMSCs, grounded researchers should also consider whether their participants expect privacy from their exchanges (Sveningsson, 2004). If they do, then the researcher should, at the very least, ensure their anonymity (Bromseth, 2002; Lawson, 2004).

Ironically, as grounded researchers respond to new ethical challenges posed by EMSCs as research settings, they may make decisions that make their data collection and analysis process a little less traceable to the readers of their research. Yet, providing tools for readers to retrace the different steps followed by researchers is an important guideline of grounded theorizing (Corbin & Strauss, 1990; Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Therefore, as researchers on EMSCs apply grounded methods in their projects, they should be aware of a potential trade-off in their research between the traceability of the data and the protection of the privacy of the people involved and articulate the decisions they made in their methodology with regard to this trade-off.

The significance of data triangulation in grounded research revealed the tight relationship between the issues of data collection and analysis. We now turn our attention more specifically to key data analysis processes that help grounded researchers elaborate their data set and gradually and systematically analyze it.

## Data analysis

This section discusses how researchers working on EMSCs have applied key analytical principles of the grounded theory method (theoretical sampling, coding sequence, constant comparison). Table 3 describes how the reviewed articles applied these principles in their investigation of the dynamics of their EMSC of interest.

Based on this review, this section discusses how characteristics of EMSCs affect the application and reporting of these principles. It suggests that some characteristics of EMSCs can prove helpful to researchers willing to apply grounded theory methods. It closes by discussing opportunities and challenges of combining qualitative and quantitative methods to discover and build grounded theory of EMSCs.

## Theoretical sampling

Glaser & Strauss (1967) define theoretical sampling as ‘the process of data collection for generating theory whereby the analyst jointly collects, codes, and analyzes his data and decides what data to collect next and where to find them, in order to develop his theory as it emerges. This process of data collection is controlled by the emerging theory’ (Glaser & Strauss, 1967, p. 45, emphasis in original).

Theoretical sampling is important for grounded researchers and shows the intricacies of data collection and analysis. As they tackle their data analysis, grounded researchers continuously refine their observations and attune their data collection to their emerging conceptualization. For research on EMSCs, researchers can carry out the principle of theoretical sampling by taking advantage of the archive of electronic communications and of the likely availability of a search function in these data. For instance, findings from interview data helped Da Cunha & Orlikowski (2008) identify a poster in the organizational online forum who had held a critical role in expressing employees’ reactions to threats to their organizational identity. The researchers then searched through the entire archive of the online forum in order to access all posts authored by this person. This new search allowed them to further their conceptualization of the cathartic role of participation in an online forum.

## Coding sequence

The coding process is crucial to the grounded theory method (Corbin & Strauss, 1990; Strauss & Corbin, 1998; Gasson, forthcoming). Through coding, the researcher is able to identify concepts and relationships among them that help establish a theorization that fits his or her empirical subject and questions. Part of its appeal beyond the application of the grounded theory method is that reporting various stages of the coding process helps the researcher establish the credibility and validity of the analyses (Miles & Huberman, 1994; Strauss & Corbin, 1998; Denzin & Lincoln, 2000).

For grounded researchers interested in EMSCs, reporting on this coding sequence seems especially important. Indeed, as noted in most papers of our sample, EMSCs are changing and still under-explored contexts. Grounded researchers therefore claim a lack of familiarity with the investigated contexts and consider that already established categories should not be automatically assumed to fit observations. Reporting the coding process as involving subsequent stages through which themes and concepts are gradually identified and their relationships tested against each other, and with new data, is therefore of crucial importance. It provides clues for readers as to how the researchers gained a deep understanding of this new context and were able to establish conceptual relationships among constructs observed from the interpretation of the data.

For instance, Da Cunha & Orlikowski (2008) reported their coding process as aiming first at identifying themes about the content and contexts of the message (open coding). Then, through multiple iterations of axial coding, they identified sets of discursive practices through which employees coped with reorganization. Finally, their presentation of the results of their interpretive process provided evidence of the selective coding that supported an emergent overall conceptualization of their research topic (i.e. coping with identity threats through an online forum).

## Constant comparison

Through constant comparison, grounded researchers continuously contrast new, notable observations with previous ones for similarities and differences (Corbin & Strauss, 1990, p. 9). Constant comparison allows researchers to elaborate, label, and then refine over time the concepts that turn out to be of relevance to their research subject. Constant comparison also warrants a certain degree of precision and consistency in the emerging concepts, as only observations that are similar on key, relevant, dimensions become associated with a single concept, and observations that differ along these dimensions get contrasted and explained in the emergent theoretical relationship among concepts. Lee & Cole (2003), for instance, investigated the norms of knowledge sharing in an open-source community and, from their constant comparison of the contents of electronic communications among participant developers, recognized that these norms were related to four distinct conceptual domains.

Grounded research can benefit from features of EMSCs that make it more convenient than in other, more traditional, research settings, to carry out constant comparisons. As noted above, grounded researchers working on EMSCs often enjoy a wide and long-standing access to the activities taking place in their EMSC of interest through electronic data in particular. This continued access can prove illuminating for the researcher as he or she can apply the constant comparison principle by coming back to the data that have already been collected and interpreting them in the light of new emerging concepts. In grounded research taking place in a more traditional setting (say a corporation), such constant comparison takes place usually with a constant set of data, as it is difficult (though not impossible) for researchers to come back frequently to their research setting to collect new data and compare them to emerging conceptualizations.

Moreover, O’Mahony & Ferraro’s (2007) research showed how the application of constant comparison can help add rigor and reliability to an emerging conceptualization. Their analysis of the evolution of governance in an open-source community led them to theorize that governance followed subsequent stages characterized by different levels of concern for technical, organizational, or charismatic issues. Having put forward this working hypothesis, they tested it against data constituted by the discourses of would-be leaders in the open-source community under study.

## Implications

Overall, with regard to grounded theory analytical processes, EMSCs provide some unusual benefits to the researcher, making it relatively more convenient to collect new, pointed, data and to contrast previous observations to an emerging conceptualization. Theoretical sampling and constant comparison, in particular, can happen at later stages of data collection/analysis. Once initial conceptual categories have emerged, the researcher can clarify the properties of these categories and their relationships as well as build a more economical theorization by going back, with a sharper conceptual focus, to the research setting. This ability to perform grounded analytical procedures at relatively late stages of the research process is not usually easily done in more traditional field studies, even those whose access has been very wide and deep.

Moreover, this paper argues that these new opportunities for grounded research on EMSCs can be complemented by carrying out qualitative and quantitative data analysis processes. Most of the papers of our sample relied mostly on qualitative data analysis, but they also supplemented qualitative analysis with quantitative insights in some cases (e.g Yoo & Alavi, 2004; Hara & Hew, 2007). Because of the availability of electronic archives, EMSCs as research settings represent unprecedented opportunities for grounded researchers to use quantitative data analysis. When dealing with the grounded theorization of EMSCs. the sheer volume of data that may be collected demands creativity on researchers’ part. It requires that grounded researchers who have so far mostly relied upon qualitative data analysis find ways to extract meaning from huge amounts of data. In these situations, qualitative methods of analysis have their limits and human cognitive abilities can usefully be supplemented by computer-based quantitative methods (Arriaga & Levina, 2010).

Such promising prospects come with a caveat. Indeed, as recognized by the earliest proponents of the methods, the temptation with quantitative data analysis can be to switch from the logic of discovery of grounded theory to the logic of verification that is inconsistent with grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967, chs. 7 and 8). The goal of the grounded researcher interested in EMSCs, who has a wide access to electronic archives and decides to rely at least partly on quantitative analysis, is thus not to search for statistical significance in volumes of data per se, but rather to establish more modest, partial relationships that make deeper sense of parts of the data. Quantitative analytical methods can prove highly beneficial to guide researchers toward the discovery of non-obvious relationships among categories in the data (e.g. Cheng et al., 2010; Tsui et al., 2010). Examples of such quantitative methods that are consistent with the principles of grounded theorizing include, among others, instances of cluster analysis, factor analysis, or text mining. The logic of discovery rather than that of verification in data analysis is essential to the delicate question of theory building in grounded research, to which we now turn.

## What is ‘theory’ in grounded research?

Ultimately, the purpose of grounded theorizing is to develop new theory grounded in the analysis of actual settings and processes (Urquhart et al., 2009). The papers of our sample exhibited striking differences in their avowed theoretical ambitions. These ambitions, which reflected the variety of grounded theory research, ranged from the mostly descriptive (i.e. providing an in-depth view of various electronically mediated situations and of the interactions of their participants) to the development of more elaborate and original conceptualizations. For instance, Hara & Hew (2007) were interested in the dynamics of knowledge sharing in an online community of nurses. Their main explicit theoretical ambition was to 'categorize and describe the different types of activities and knowledge shared’ within the online community (Hara & Hew, 2007, p. 236). By contrast, Lee & Cole (2003, p. 633) aimed to ‘develop a new model of knowledge creation in a distributed system, in contrast to a firm-based model’ from their empirical work in an open-source software community.

Glaser and Strauss’ and Strauss and Corbin’s definitions of theory are useful to make sense of the wide range of theoretical ambitions of grounded theory research on EMSCs. According to Glaser & Strauss (1967, p. 22), ‘the form in which a theory is presented does not make it a theory; it is a theory because it explains or predicts something’. More recently, for Strauss & Corbin (1998, p. 22), ‘theory denotes a set of well-developed categories (e.g. themes, concepts) that are systematically interrelated through statements of relationship to form a theoretical framework that explains some relevant social (y) phenomenon’. These two formulations are consistent in that they posit that a theory aims to explain empirical phenomena rather than merely to describe observed realities. Therefore, grounded researchers go beyond the observation of the particular in order to provide some kind of theoretical explanation of a certain phenomenon of interest. This definition differentiates pure ethnographies from grounded theory-based research (Gasson, forthcoming; Wacquant, 2002). For instance, while Hara & Hew’s (2007) study was explicitly less theoretically ambitious than Lee & Cole’s (2003) one, Hara & Hew (2007) did not merely report on their observations of the online community of nurses, but they also developed, from these observations and their grounded analysis of them, a typology of the types of knowledge and behaviors most often exhibited in the online forum.

Moreover, grounded theory logically departs from deductive research in that its process ends up in the building of theory, rather than starting with a theory that is then tested against empirical observations. Some research on EMSCs has adopted deductive approaches. For instance, Constant et al. (1996) and McLure Wasko & Faraj (2005) investigated knowledge contributions to EMSCs. They built upon existing theories (e.g. motivation to contribute, usefulness of knowledge, social capital) and applied them to make predictions on what would happen in an electronically mediated context. Researchers who adopt a grounded perspective and are interested in similar topics (e.g. Hara & Hew, 2007) start with the specifics of their EMSC of interest and then build a conceptual understanding of it.

Beyond these clear characteristics of the theoretical ambitions of grounded theory research, and the distinction between grounded theory, ethnography, and deductive research, though, it is important to acknowledge subtler distinctions in the theory development process of grounded theory research in EMSCs.

Induction and abduction in grounded theory on EMSCs As shown in Table 4, grounded theorists often explicitly present their research as following an inductive approach through which observations lead to generalized classifications (e.g. Hara & Hew, 2007; Da Cunha & Orlikowski, 2008). Rigorously following Glaser and Strauss’ or Strauss and Corbin’s aforementioned definitions of theory, generalized classifications are not equivalent to explanations, that is to conceptual ordering and relating of the different phenomena that have been observed and classified. Yet, research that explicitly delimits its theoretical ambition in such a way very often contains rich conceptualizations embedded within their descriptions/discussions of the context (Wacquant, 2002). For instance, in its empirical sections, Orlikowski & Yates’ (1994) paper on genres and repertoires of genres of communication in the context of email use goes well beyond the mere description of the investigated setting. The discussion of this paper relies upon a nuanced conceptualization of genres of electronic communications that cannot be generalized carelessly to other settings, but that provides an original theoretical lens to interpret genres of electronic communications in other contexts.

Other grounded theory studies on EMSCs explicitly posit their goal as that of advancing theory by building conceptual explanations of the phenomena and processes they investigate (Lee & Cole, 2003; O’Mahony & Ferraro, 2007). Peirce (1960–1966) called this process of building explanations from empirical observations ‘abduction’. This diversity in grounded research on EMSCs echoes similarly diverse patterns of grounded theory research in IS (see Gasson’s (forthcoming) review, in particular) as well as in other fields in social sciences. This also reflects the more general continuum in scientific inquiry between induction and abduction. Table 5 contrasts the two extremes of this continuum between abduction and induction on the basis of work in the discipline of logic (Peirce, 1960–1966; Fann, 1970; Eco & Sebeok, 1983; Sebeok & Umiker-Sebeok, 1983; Flachs & Kakas. 2000). Grounded research on EMSCs falls within this continuum.

Very often, grounded theory research is associated with the inductive end of the continuum, that is with findings that tend to describe and classify more than to conceptualize and explain (as, in our sample, in Hara & Hew, 2007). It is however important to acknowledge the importance of abductive logic as well in grounded research. Glaser & Strauss (1967) and later Strauss & Corbin (1998) did not refer directly to the term ‘abduction’. Yet, Strauss & Corbin called for ‘moving between’ induction and deduction (1998, pp. 136–137), which seems directly related to the idea of abduction. Most tellingly, perhaps, is the observation that the intellectual origins of grounded theory (Strauss & Corbin, 1998, pp. 9–10) rely among others on pragmatists’ thinking and, especially, on Peirce’s work. This rediscovery of the influence of Peirce’s pragmatism on grounded theory has led some methodologists to consider abduction as a key logical inference for grounded theory (Kelle, 2005; Richardson & Kramer, 2006; Thomas & James, 2006).

## Implications

Given the relatively novel character of EMSCs and the originality of the observations that researchers therefore often make of them, grounded research on EMSCs needs to be able to describe/classify and to explain/predict. The need to classify and describe comes from the unfamiliar character of many of these new, electronically mediated contexts and, therefore, from the call to explore and discover these new settings. The need to explain and predict naturally derives from the lack of, or the relative unsuitability of, existing theories to make sense of the phenomena under study. In such situations, it is opportune to build new theories on EMSCs from abduction by ordering and suggesting causation processes among the various observed categories in the data.

Overall, this paper calls for making the positioning of grounded theory research on EMSCs on the induction/abduction continuum more explicit. This would clarify the ambitions of the research and the expectations of its readers. This would also illuminate the important issue of the ‘creative leap’ that grounded researchers make as they develop theorization based on their empirical analyses. The data collection and analysis procedures advocated by proponents of grounded methods (in particular from the Straussian side of the method) indeed aim at helping researchers build a theory that derives organically yet as rigorously and systematically as possible from the setting under investigation. However, as acknowledged by many researchers including, notably, Langley (1999, p. 691):

No analysis strategy will produce theory without an uncodifiable creative leap, however small.

The strict application of grounded theory procedures does not replace but is meant to assist this creative leap by helping researchers discover relationships and patterns in their observations. Making sense of these analyses requires that grounded theorists go beyond their data to provide a conceptualization that encompasses them and gives them a relevant meaning. Arguably, it is impossible for researchers to fully articulate the 'how' of their creative jump. Table 4, for instance, reveals that authors of the reviewed papers went from analyzing their EMSCs under investigation into theorizing, but that they did not/could not entirely explain how they did so.

Grounded researchers of EMSCs should be aware of the need for this creative jump and acknowledge it. Positioning more clearly their research along the induction/abduction continuum can help grounded researchers of EMSCs manage the expectations of their readers regarding the size of this jump. Research on the more inductive side of the continuum makes a relatively modest creative jump while categorizing observations and building some relationships among these observations. Research at the abductive end of the continuum relies on a more formidable creative jump that demands that researchers specify the relationships among categories and develop propositions that together explain a new phenomenon.

Incidentally, a more explicit recognition of the need for such a ‘creative jump’, no matter how small, would bring some grounded theory research on EMSCs (as well as on other types of social environments of interest to IS scholars) a little closer to Glaser’s aforementioned advocacy of ‘theoretical sensitivity’ (Glaser, 1978, 1992). We noted earlier that all articles from our sample took pains to articulate as far as possible the procedures they followed in their research to provide evidence of the rigor of their process. Such articulation is admirable, and required of academic publications. At the same time, however, acknowledging the inevitability of the creative jump also involves paying tribute to Glaser’s claim that theoretical sensitivity goes beyond the application of the canonical procedures of data handling and analysis.

## Conclusion

This paper reviewed published exemplars of grounded theory research on EMSCs and discussed key opportunities that this method offers in contexts characterized by many relatively new processes and a lack of existing theoretical frameworks that fit them. This paper discussed the specifics of EMSCs as research settings for grounded IS researchers and examined three important and interrelated aspects of the grounded theorizing method: data collection, data analysis, and theory building.

The paper focused on the methodological aspects of grounded theory and therefore analyzed the methods presented in the sampled papers and not the theoretical frameworks they relied upon and built. In this regard, the paper did not tackle an issue that has significantly divided proponents of the grounded method (Strauss & Corbin, 1998; Glaser, 2004; Bryant & Charmaz, 2007): that of the role of and reliance upon existing theories when undertaking grounded research projects. Another limitation in the scope of this paper is that it relied upon a theoretical, rather than a random or exhaustive, sample of published grounded research on EMSCs. We believe that the papers that we selected constituted strong examples of existing grounded research on EMSCs. However, we do not claim that these were the only instances of such research, nor do we contend that future grounded research on EMSCs should merely attempt to emulate them.

In this regard, upon closing this paper, we speculate on promising areas for future grounded theorizing focusing on EMSCs. In particular, we would like to call attention to the potential to combine qualitative and computer-based quantitative methods when discovering grounded theories of contexts in which most if not all interactions take place electronically. Grounded theorists interested in EMSCs can have access to unprecedented volumes of data. To tap into the potential of these data and to conceptualize their phenomena of interest, researchers can compound the organizing and sorting abilities of quantitative techniques with more qualitative approaches that give meaning and build causations among variables.
