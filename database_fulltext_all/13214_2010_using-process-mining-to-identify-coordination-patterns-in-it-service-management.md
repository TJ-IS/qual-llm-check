## a b s t r a c t

We empirically analyze the database used in the help desk process between a national US public agency and its global outsourcing provider. We considered the question of whether the database might reveal a deeper level of knowledge than was apparent from direct inspection. Our results reveal that four constructs underlie this process. Three are con<sup>fi</sup>rmed through covariance-based structural equation modeling and a fourth is implied through existing data. Our results suggest re<sup>fi</sup>nement in service level agreements to create a different type of governance coordination to assist in aligning the outsourcing provider's execution more closely with the client's needs.

## 1. Introduction

IT services have become critical and essential to business operations [1]. The continuing quest for cost ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness has pushed even large organizations to consider outsourcing as a strategic business decision. The global market for IT services was estimated at \$796 billion in 2009, a 1.7% decline over 2008 attributed to the economic recession rather than declining interest [15]. In a recent Gartner survey, 80% of US <sup>fi</sup>rms have considered outsourcing IT services [39]. Given the increasing interest in outsourcing IT services, understanding and governance by the client over outsourced IT services is critical to its success [9].

When IT services are outsourced, the process goals can fundamentally be altered based on the contractual structure entered into by the two organizations. While anecdotal evidence for such changes in process governance is becoming more frequent, the way process priorities get altered has not been systematically examined. The main objective of this research is to develop and test an approach to empirically examine the underlying coordination structure within IT service processes in outsourced arrangements. Using data from a large outsourcing relationship, we analyze an IT services database to identify the constructs that in<sup>fl</sup>uence process coordination within the IT services organization. The results provide interesting and valuable insights by isolating the underlying coordination constructs emphasized in the relationship from the explicit coordination structures put in place through the data model. The driving research question focuses on whether the database may reveal a deeper level of knowledge than is apparent from direct inspection, and if so, how might this knowledge aid coordination between outsourcing customer and the provider.

Using coordination theory as a basis for analysis, we identify the constructs and associated structure driving coordination (Observe, Discovery, Produce, and Context), and we gain insights into how these constructs are in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the overall coordination process. Building on constructs proposed by [47,48] we develop and evaluate a coordination focused model for an outsourcing provider's organization by taking a process monitoring perspective. There is only limited evidence and illustration of the provider's interest in root cause analysis. This skewed coordination toward process monitoring reveals the source of con<sup>fl</sup>ict between the outsourcer's and client's assessment of the outsourcing relationship. The results and their revelation of construct coordination con<sup>fl</sup>ict also point to where metrics need to be applied within the service level-agreement (SLA) in order to help align the outsourcer's actual process execution with the client's actual interests.

## 2. Literature review

Technological support of business processes has resulted in increasing demand for IT services and the associated need for active management. In one study [38], 64% of IT management and C-level respondents indicated that the management of IT services is more important now than ever; only 8% responded that it was less important. Reasons include the perceived strategic role of IT in contributing to business success, more sophisticated end user expectations of IT, the trend toward delivering IT functions as services, the need to control costs, and competitive pressure to optimize resources. Calls to IT services support are increasing, from an average of 1 call per employee to 1.36 calls per employee from 1995 to 2001 [6]. The average cost per call to IT services support is estimated at \$22 [19]. Considering the direct expense, not to mention opportunity costs due to interrupted business process(es), an organization is obliged to understand much about IT services and IT service management, whether the services are provided internally or outsourced. Previous research has explored outsourcing cost reduction [2,3,20,25,29,49] where executive management regards in-house support largely as a cost burden.

In addition to a cost reduction perspective, improving business performance has also been noted as a strategic motivation for outsourcing [14]. The business performance motivation focuses on the knowledge intensive nature of IT services. IT professionals who are knowledgeable in latest technologies can facilitate improvements in <sup>fi</sup>rm's business performance while allowing the <sup>fi</sup>rm to concentrate on its core competencies. From this perspective, IT professionals with in-depth knowledge of the client were attributed as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor in the success of an outsourced relationship [20]. Positive perceptions of vendor performance were in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the client's belief in the deep knowledge possessed by the provider's staff of the client's operations. This shared knowledge enhances a complementary and positive relationship with the outsourcer [14,29]. In this respect a shared knowledge model relating to the outsourced business process provides the client with an understanding of how the outsourcer executes the service. Lee, Miranda, and Kim suggested that strategic competence and/or technology catalysis arising from an outsourced relationship requires a more cognizant relationship [28].

IT services contractual terms, however, primarily address performance related concerns and drive the information collected during process execution. Most contracts include expectations on services requested (“service level agreements” or SLAs), metrics for setting the threshold goals for these SLAs, standard methods to measure each SLA, and indicators for deviations from actual to agreed-upon SLAs [18, p72–81]. Incentives or penalties may be included to encourage superior performance or to discourage (or respond to) poor performance. Table 1 provides a summary of typical SLA services. The SLA metrics for IT can be categorized as those that attend to process execution where the goal is a positive integration of technology into the client's business process and process interruption where the technology negatively impacts the client's business process(es). Process execution metrics include those such as availability, accuracy, currency, quality, capacity, and system performance. The goal of these metrics can be viewed as desiring a seamless integration of technology into the client's business process. Not all aspects of IT are totally seamless; there are some such as installing upgrades and user training that, while interrupting the client's general business, are intended to provide improvements and are also included into the process execution orientation. Process interruption activities are often the focus of the IT Services help desk. Process interruption metrics include metrics such as problem resolution, recovery time, repeat call incidences, repair time, percentage of abandoned calls, and security incidents. These activities negatively impact the client's general business process and once addressed merely allow the interrupted process to resume.

For each SLA category element, metrics are established by negotiation between provider and client. Fig. 1 depicts the process relationship between client and provider; at the center is the IT service request or problem. The provider implements a process it believes will satisfy a client's need and focuses on monitoring process ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. The client appreciates process ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, yet also wants to understand the nature of process interruptions, including both their immediate cause and resolution, and often some level of root cause analysis to avoid future occurrences. The client may not recognize fully the necessity to ensure the process that is monitored includes the scope and depth of service required, or the client may recognize this need, but not have the tools to analyze the process in a manner that enhances process coordination alignment. The SLA plays an integral role coordinating intentional agreements of the contract into actual process execution outcomes.

The client may accept metric conformance performance as a matter of faith (via provider reporting with no veri<sup>fi</sup>cation directly possible by the client), or by veri<sup>fi</sup>cation directly by the client. The ability of the client to directly monitor IT services process detail can substantiate metric performance and reinforce trust and satisfaction. Customer satisfaction with regard to SLA agreements requires three conditions: (1) that the service provider meet or exceed the metrics established for each SLA, (2) that each SLA is well understood by the parties, and (3) that the set of SLAs accomplishes the totality of service actually required by the client. As expectations can evolve over time, an IT service agreement acceptable at initial approval might no longer address the interests of either provider or client. Thus, the validation of a shared coordination model between provider and client with regard to IT services can help the collaboration necessary to maintain a successful service level relationship.

This research examines the data and its implied knowledge model relating to process interruptions as reported via the help desk process. The help desk can be viewed as a speci<sup>fi</sup>c process provided within IT services to address issues of process interruption when clients discover that their IT resources are not performing, or underperforming. The client's intended business process is temporarily interrupted while the client is required to submit an incident<sup>1</sup> report or request. To address the client's needs, the help desk staff analysts rely on a database of previous incidents. The database possesses data attributes that allow the analysts to report speci<sup>fi</sup>c detail on critical data elements to be provided. The data attributes are speculative acknowledging the reality of the ambiguity associated with anticipating problems and their characterization. However, over time, analysts come to develop a set of skills that allow them some level of standardization in how they resolve anomalies within their domain.

## 3. Model development

Our research orientation builds on coordination theory. Underlying coordination is the theory that multiple actors or components are necessary to produce a process outcome unable to be achieved by a single actor or element [30,31]. The theory seeks to identify the actual dependencies which lead to coordination [11]. While [10] and [11] applied coordination theory in examining the tasks performed in bug analysis, [30] and [34] apply coordination theory to explain how parts of the brain act in coordination to achieve cognitive activity.

Unlike the work of [23] or [32], we contribute to coordination theory not through a detailed analysis of the event sequences, but of the outcomes resulting from those sequences stored in the IT services database. This process mining orientation is similar to earlier process oriented research on knowledge representation developed from casebased reasoning [44] and dynamic memory systems [45,46]; however, unlike this earlier research we are not trying to subsume the analysts knowledge completely into a computational model.

The model reported in this work also contributes to the growing body of literature on process mining. The goal of process mining is to produce an explicit model based on actual process execution such that the model corresponds to observed behavior, rather than from intentional design [52]. Typical process mining has addressed process <sup>fl</sup>ow with the intent on improving process ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency within operational processes such as ERP [21]. In more knowledge-intensive processes where human intervention involves a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of cognitive effort, rigid task coordination is secondary to cognitive coordination, such as in the Help Desk activity, and little investigation has occurred in this area. Recently, the concept of process mining has been extended into organizational analysis “to discover organizational models and social networks and show how these models can assist in improving the underlying processes” [50, p. 300].

The methodology we apply [16] connects the information captured to its underlying process motivation. These underlying motivations not only can help in the understanding of a particular organization, but may provide insights at the domain level. The model we identify moves us into the knowledge-level by revealing tacit knowledge [40], in the form of latent constructs and their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the data elements, not explicitly expressed prior to this study. We describe the operationalization of each construct below, providing an extension to and formalization for the model discussed in [46]<sup>2</sup>. Senge's model synthesizes the theory of re<sup>fl</sup>ective thought from Dewey and the principles of quality assurance noted by Deming to describe business processes that contribute new knowledge back to the organization.

The macro-process stage of Senge's integration of re<sup>fl</sup>ective thought and quality assurance is Observe, where the actions implemented as a process are observed and evaluated. In an IT services context, this translates to an interest in certain process monitoring aspects. Observe includes ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency indicators, such as when the incident is reported, the number of open incidents at a particular time, the severity of the reported incident metrics, outstanding incidents, and the number of similar incidents within a timeframe.

The micro-process stage which initiates a help desk request focuses on Discovery, which is a collection activity to gather new information and insights. In a typical IT services support scenario, a client experiences a problem or otherwise needs assistance and contacts the IT services support group; (we consider the request an anomaly instance). The contact typically occurs by phone or email. An analyst responds to the request by noting customer and other associative information relating to the anomaly. The discovery activity collects data surrounding the request or problem but is not considered the source of the problem. This data has associative, rather than causal, value.

The next stage is Produce, where new actions are executed. In an IT services context, the analyst, alone or with others, initiates a number of resolution actions speci<sup>fi</sup>cally targeted to analyzing the anomalous condition and targeted to either respond merely to the immediate manifestation or toward understanding the overall nature of the anomalous condition (i.e., with respect to either a product formation error and/or a process execution omission). The speci<sup>fi</sup>c resolution is implemented and the client's business processes no longer are impeded.

We expand this theoretical model with the construct of Context, which declares the subject of interest for the business process. This construct brings the process to a systems level of understanding [53] by characterizing the goal state. Within the IT services domain, after the analyst collects the necessary input, other individuals may become involved, but most often the analyst will rely directly on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c database, which may be called a problem-, incident-, failure-, bug-, database. The database is speci<sup>fi</sup>cally constructed for the context of interest and contains a set of declared data items captured relating to past incidents, including both details relating to the execution of the overall process but also speci<sup>fi</sup>c details characterizing the anomaly itself. The Context construct would show an interest in characterizing the nature of reported problems and their <sup>fi</sup>nal resolution. Context data are necessary when an organization seeks to engage in root cause analysis to identify and correct not just the reported incident, but the systematic cause that led to the occurrence of the incident.

A synthesis of the above discussion allows us to develop the model in Fig. 2. In this paper, we take the orientation of [8] and [33] that the model should be treated as a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed, set-oriented hypothesis, rather than isolating each path as an independent hypothesis. The model is a set of equations that frame a conceptual reality, a complex composite. What we are testing is that in this database, we will <sup>fi</sup>nd four, <sup>fi</sup>rstorder constructs (which we can identify as Observe, Discovery, Produce, and Context) that directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence (i.e., motivate or cause) the data to be declared. These four constructs are emergent properties of a coordination model for the IT Services help desk process. We acknowledge that because we utilize empirical process data from practice, it is likely that the results will not correspond identically to this conceptual model. Our conceptual model becomes a normative template in which to assess the coordination alignment against the actual process execution. We predict that deviations of the empirical model from the conceptual model will produce statistical evidence of the coordination or a lack of coordination within this process.

## 4. Research methodology

We observe that the <sup>fi</sup>elds in some databases, such as those used in Help Desk analysis, are not always supplied with data. We also observe that the missing data does not appear to occur at random, but from intention. A common approach in literature and practice is to omit missing values from further analysis or substitute or impute the missing value in the analysis. In contrast, we contend that the missing value patterns themselves are valuable in the analysis. The purpose of the methodology is to help analyze if the data patterns that arise result from a predictable intention.

The unit of analysis for this research is the incident record, or case, recorded in the organization's database. Each row in the database includes attributes describing the data reported involving the incident through <sup>fi</sup>nal resolution and characterization. Our focus is not on the speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of the data, but in its perceived relevance to the case. We apply a text analysis technique of regularization on the attribute-type to note if data was supplied or not.

The empirical database was provided by a large outsourcing client whose provider is a leading, global outsourcing provider. The provider employs two databases: one for open incidents (those whose characterization and resolution are still in process or have only been recently documented) and another as an archival database of closed incidents, no longer being actively processed. The open incident database provides both the contextual (it is the database actively used during the process execution) and the quantitative (missing data appears to be intentional rather than from random error) characteristics needed. A longitudinal sample of this database encompassing 23 months was extracted; providing 12,062 incidents to ensure suf<sup>fi</sup>cient power for CFA in the study. The data model included 39 data elements. The sample includes all the incidents recorded in this period of time by the outsourcing provider's 494 analysts.

## 5. Data gathering

Data preparation is targeted so that the same sample can be analyzed by both PCA and CFA. Data is supplied from the empirical process database utilized during the help desk process execution. The data is initially coded using a regularization technique based on group membership with respect to domain knowledge model, with 1 = data was supplied and 0 = no data was supplied. Supplying data provides evidence of relevance to the actual process. Next, coded elements are separated that have zero variance from the others. Zero variance occurs by one of two conditions: the data for an element is always entered or it is never entered. Each of these conditions provides a theoretically supported orientation. When data is always supplied by all the analysts in this organization, we observe invariant agreement that this element is always useful (we call these elements invariantpositive); they can be immediately included as data elements within the coordination model. Similarly, when all of the analysts never enter data for the element for any occurrence in the sample; it is invariant with complete agreement that the data element is not needed for some reason. The invariant-negative elements are only identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in a larger set known as information-only elements. To this larger set, we also include elements that duplicate other elements semantically and those which are entered so infrequently (i.e., extreme kurtosis) that they have poor data quality for CFA analysis. These elements are not used in the business process with any consistency and so do not justify being included within the coordination model.

The remaining group we will call candidate or partial data elements for the organization-level model. It is this group for which we can apply CFA. The detail related to these elements and how they contribute to the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed model is described in the next section. To this remaining group, we apply the CFA model, randomly grouping<sup>4</sup> incidents in the data set, a technique similar to that validated by [35,36] for categorical analysis.

## 6. Data analysis

This study employs triangulation to address distinct aspects of empirical model identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. The <sup>fi</sup>rst, using PCA, is an exploratory analysis to aid data reduction. The next, using CFA, identi<sup>fi</sup>es a speci<sup>fi</sup>c theory-driven factor structure that parsimoniously explains the IT services support process. Because some of the structure is invariant (i.e., always entered), we incorporate DMM heuristics to help fully identify the coordination structure residing within the database.

## 6.1. Exploratory data reduction using principal components analysis

Applying Principal Components Analysis (PCA) allows us to investigate the strength of in<sup>fl</sup>uence (dimensionality) of various components upon variable groups. For this study, we use it as a data reduction technique and to provide empirical support to the appropriate level of abstraction underlying the descriptive nature of the constructs motivating the data instantiation. This approach is compatible with that advocated by [37].

With a sample size of 12,062 incidents, after removing the elements where the coded variance is zero<sup>5</sup>, of the original 39 variables, dimensionality of the remaining 23 is examined. Criteria used to determine the primary number of components include a priori hypothesis, the scree plot (see Fig. 3), and the interpretability of the solution.

The scree plot and component matrix (see Table 3) con<sup>fi</sup>rm the a priori hypothesis which postulates <sup>fi</sup>ve components. All suggest one major component and four other components may best describe these elements. Substantively, in examining this subset of the data model elements, we note the absence of detail on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c anomaly and that the components cluster with respect to different process orientations. The clustering of elements suggests that the component predominantly address speci<sup>fi</sup>c phases of the support process, including those management-level elements that observe process evaluation.

## 6.3. Model finalization steps

Recall that there exists a set of variables exhibiting zero invariance that are always instantiated (i.e., invariant-positive) indicating they are important elements for process execution. In examining the empirical nature of each of these elements, they all result from actual IT Services Help Desk process usage. Acknowledging that the PCA analysis on grouped data was applied to a larger set of variables, we identify four factors. Given that the residual error analysis in CFA suggested the potential for more factors, we specify the fourth, <sup>fi</sup>rstorder factor as Context with two data elements (the two invariantpositive data elements Description and Item) that relate directly to the incident (its manifestation).

For the remaining invariant-positive elements, because they are always instantiated by all analysts in a contextual database populated by analyst and organizational discretion, we can conclude that they have an important role to play in the process. Similar to model merging [4,5,42,43], we consider the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions applied to the elements and their instantiated data with only moderate attention to element labels and align them with the most similar data elements we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed within the CFA analysis step. We treat the factor structure similar to a supertype in applying data model merging (DMM) for complex matching [4] using instance-based, linguistics, and auxiliary user input as the criteria and de<sup>fi</sup>ne expressions relating to the relationship between the factors and their associated data elements. We omit direct paths to indicate that these relationships are inferred rather than quantitatively identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, which conforms at a generalized level to the requirements of complex matching [4] differentiating between equal and similar relationships. ## 7. Discussion

In this paper we analyzed an outsourced, help desk database used actively by an IT services business process. We identify the underlying process coordination to reveal four primary constructs (Observe, Discover, Produce, and Context) motivating the process. The resulting model is not limited to merely vocabulary or taxonomy, but one that provides revelations of how the organization is supporting its business process. The identi<sup>fi</sup>ed structure can aid process coordination improvement.

![](/api/attachments/MX8YNGW2/fulltext/images/193e0a92948c65ae137cc1864a876aeb82c8123155977c1a883f31ff5effc41a.jpg)  
Fig. 5. IT services coordination model.

Referring back to Fig. 5, note that each of the constructs has data elements associated with it. The constructs reveal organization-level motivation from the IT services help desk provider. Notice that more data elements are associated with the Observe and Discover constructs than are associated with the Produce and Context constructs. This suggests organizational priorities favoring the <sup>fi</sup>rst two constructs. This seems to indicate an organization for which ef<sup>fi</sup>cient customer response is the most important objective. In an outsourcing relationship, this may be a typical orientation as cost is a frequently cited reason for outsourcing [20,26,29]. Service-level agreements generally target ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency as a primary metric, so the many elements for Observe help provide the IT services organization a rich orientation on how to address ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. The attention apparently given to Discover regards who the customer is, where they are located, and their contact information. Within this model and client feedback, it would appear that governance over process ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient and the SLA attention is adequate.

With regard to the Produce construct we conjecture that the speci<sup>fi</sup>c actions of the analyst(s) are not captured precisely. Audit Trail and Work Log are text-based <sup>fi</sup>elds, which require an incidentby-incident search for governance support. Assignee, which is motivated by both Discover and Produce constructs, allows for one name only, so multiple analyst identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is not captured. Created By remembers who took the original request, but should the incident be transferred from one analyst to another to another, one would need to search text-based <sup>fi</sup>elds, such as Audit Trail or Work Log, to see if others were involved. Assignee apparently overwrites an analyst's name.

Finally for Context, very little is captured with precise detail. This is surprising as improvements in this support process are likely to be tied to those incidents where the anomaly exhibits common characteristics. For instance, if a virus were introduced, it is quite possible that it could hit every computer at the client site. Paradoxically, if support analysts were to resolve each incident quickly, within this organization they apparently have criteria to demonstrate how responsive they are. But, is this truly what the client wants? Would not the ability to understand the characteristics of the virus in order to anticipate and prevent further manifestations be an important goal of the client? The discovered model suggests that the current database may make prevention dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and forward-thinking anticipation very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. The point here is that the database suggests that incident response is valued much more than problem identi<sup>fi</sup>- cation. The actual resolution cannot be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed with any reliability within this database. Ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency may be the intent of the database from the provider's perspective, but the SLAs relating to this database (refer to Table 1) do have multiple references to problem resolution that extends beyond responsiveness. Our results suggest that SLA conformance for effectiveness criteria is lacking.

With regard to technical management, consider the granularity of the data elements. In a database, information when captured can be precise and granular (terms or phrases) or coarse (free-form text). An advantage of term precision is that monitoring and analysis can be assisted fairly easily by a variety of data retrieval tools. Coarse data storage refers to text-based <sup>fi</sup>elds. The advantage is that the analyst is not constrained and can enter what they <sup>fi</sup>nd interesting; one disadvantage is that analysis and monitoring is complicated. When text-<sup>fi</sup>elds use non-standard terms and phrasing, the value of the information is constrained largely to the incident level only, complicating effective governance. Data retrieval tools also are limited and require more user intervention in order to be valuable. Consider the Context elements. The Item element is granular, but categorized at a very abstract level, such as User Issue, Technical Question, or Request. The Description element is coarse and appears to ignore the opportunity to document how the request or problem is actually resolved. One must search incident by incident or by using trial and error browsing attempts. A manager does not have such time. SLA objectives that note a preemptive requirement in problem identi<sup>fi</sup>- cation should require more precise characterization of anomalies and require resolution detail.

At another level of analysis, refer back to the Observe set and the candidate-level data elements. There are two elements here of interest: First Call Resolved and User Acknowledged. These are yes/no responses, but their presence here indicates not that some responses are ‘no’ but that no information is provided. In this case, review Table 2 to see that both of these elements are only instantiated about one-third of the time. Is that an acceptable level with respect to the client's expectations of supplier responsiveness? These types of questions and interactions may help facilitate changes to the process.

Beyond conformance to SLAs, the model identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this research is useful for other activities. In dynamic organizations, reorganizations and mergers often require groups deemed as similar to come together. The model can be a basis for facilitating collaboration or process improvement in cognitive-intensive (i.e., analysis) processes. Coordination processes involving requests and problems span disparate service, design, and manufacturing domains. The ability to identify a common coordination model can in<sup>fl</sup>uence collaboration, process improvement, and application development that incorporate the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed model.

## 7.1. Stakeholder feedback

Stakeholder involvement was minimal at the beginning of this study to minimize external in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the interpretation of the data. The database was made available with no access to the stakeholders by the primary researcher until the conclusion of the data analysis. Subsequently, a semi-structured feedback session was conducted where stakeholders reviewed the results and concurred with the conclusions and the model.

Initially, the stakeholders indicated a general satisfaction with the partnership with their outsourcing provider, but also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed some overarching problems. The provider was emphasizing that the client's personnel needed better training on the overall usage of their Help Desk. In fact, the client had noted issues relating to problem resolution, but was having dif<sup>fi</sup>culty invoking change from the provider. The client's quality assurance of service-level conformance was largely a manual process, thus, making it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to provide consistent and on-going evidence of the appropriate service-level quality intended by the client.

A bene<sup>fi</sup>t from this research was the ability to provide statistical evidence that the provider was focused on ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency over quality of service. The lack of anomalies and problems and their speci<sup>fi</sup>c resolutions (Context construct data) was a major cause of this dissatisfaction. The stakeholders indicated an intention to use results of this study to aid in further re<sup>fi</sup>nement of SLA metrics to improve service quality. In follow up interactions, the researchers in this study were able to ascertain that customer satisfaction surveys from the client's participants on the provider's service increase from approximately 50% to 80% with a substantial portion of that improvement attributed toward quality of service improvements.

Our research extends the concepts of process and organizational mining into process coordination. It adheres to the tenet in these disciplines to acknowledge that empirical behavior frequently differs from intentional design. It contributes by allowing the organization to identify a process coordination model that drives a cognitiveintensive or knowledge-intensive process. The incident database record acts as an ‘event log’ of relevant data seen necessary to document to achieve the organizational process' objectives.

Interestingly, the candidate elements are those more remarkable to research. While the invariant-positive data elements re<sup>fl</sup>ect consensus and conformance, the discretionary quality of the candidate elements allows us to identify the underlying motivation for their inclusion and the intensity in which they are populated. This is a bit paradoxical, but potentially valuable for research and organizations who seek to improve upon service processes. Instead of seeking conformance toward process execution, organizations may bene<sup>fi</sup>t by encouraging more speculative declaration from their analysts. This would allow for greater documentation of the elements that each analyst <sup>fi</sup>nd interesting or valuable within the business process. Periodic assessment of the organizational corpus (or even the differences between novices and experts), as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by this methodology could assist with organizational learning and change management.

From a more practical perspective, the IT organization (outsourcing provider) and the client organization both bene<sup>fi</sup>t from identifying the IT services model. From a technical perspective, the model offers a method of enhancing the storage and retrieval value by de<sup>fi</sup>ning meaningful aggregation. Research is promising on the ability of enriching retrieval based on semantic meaning [13]. This orientation allows for the data model to be seen as the universe of discourse. Aggregate indices can be developed at various levels: on the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed model as the declared organizational cognition and/or on the data elements that are motivated by a construct criteria. Criteria may consider all data elements loading upon a factor or separation of high (or low) factor loadings. Further, model identi<sup>fi</sup>cation can drive grid partitioning of data in <sup>fi</sup>rms where multiple organizations may need access to this data. For example, Observe data elements may be allocated to a grid that emphasizes management analysis; whereas, an anomaly grid may be assigned to analyst investigations. This type of grid orientation is supportive to library systems [54].

This type of model can enhance process monitoring and governance of IT service performance. With regard to IT services, particularly those bound to SLAs, this model identi<sup>fi</sup>cation provides a mechanism to assess gaps between process execution and SLA focus. The client stakeholders indicated that the results of this study highlighted to them a gap in the orientation of the analysis process. The gap was a lack of focus on analyzing speci<sup>fi</sup>c problems and how they should be resolved. They believed that they could close or narrow that gap by requiring more data elements relating to Contextual Purpose to be recorded. It is not uncommon for management to misconstrue how knowledge is applied within its organization. Their belief system may be focused at intentional knowledge (what they believe should exist) rather than objective knowledge (actual organizational practice) [41].

Our perspective on coordination varies from much extant literature. Instead of coordination of tasks, we consider coordination with regard to artifacts. First, we view the process database as a coordination of recorded data elements, whose coordination patterns reveal latent constructs and the extent in which these latent constructs are affecting the actual process execution. Another type of coordination in this setting occurs between client and outsourcing provider which we can observe, at least in part, from the SLA. By relating the analysis database and our resulting model, we can assess the process coordination.

## 8. Conclusion

As many business processes, such as help desks, are outsourced, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of patterns in process execution can work with SLA agreements to improve mutual understanding and conformance to agreed-upon ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness objectives. We contribute to the literature in examining request and problem analysis processes in the context of a help desk as a process of not just organizational coordination, but one of inter-organizational coordination between provider and client. We focus on the process database to provide evidence not of work<sup>fl</sup>ow coordination, but of coordination regarding the type of knowledge addressed within this analysis process. We provide a novel, quantitative approach to identify underlying coordination constructs. Finally, we align the service-level agreement within the context of coordination.

We incorporated two quantitative techniques, PCA and CFA, to assist in model identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. PCA was used in this exploratory study to provide empirical support for data reduction. As data driven analysis, it is very speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the sample within this organization. For greater generalizability particularly with respect to a parsimonious, theory-driven analysis, CFA was utilized. The CFA analysis provided quantitative support for the process model and it allowed for the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of four primary constructs (Observe, Discovery, Produce, and Anomaly). Those data elements with invariant-positive characteristics need alternative assistance, which we supplied with declared, heuristic rules. While knowledge still requires contextual relevance and human interpretation, we strengthened its identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the addition of the quantitative techniques.
