# Personalized task recommendation in crowdsourcing information systems — Current state of the art

David Geiger ⁎, Martin Schader

Business School, University of Mannheim, Germany

a r t i c l e i n f o

Available online 15 May 2014

Keywords: Crowdsourcing Task recommendation Task matching Task routing Recommender system Recommender agent

## a b s t r a c t

Crowdsourcing information systems are socio-technical systems that provide informational products or services by harnessing the diverse potential of large groups of people via the Web. Interested individuals can contribute to such systems by selecting among a wide range of open tasks. Arguing that current approaches are suboptimal in terms of matching tasks and contributors' individual interests and capabilities, this article advocates the introduction of personalized task recommendation mechanisms. We contribute to a conceptual foundation for the design of such mechanisms by conducting a systematic review of the corresponding academic literature. Based on the insights derived from this analysis, we identify a number of issues for future research. In particular, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight the need for more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant empirical results through large-scale online experiments, an improved dialog with mainstream recommender systems research, and the integration of various sources of knowledge that exceed the boundaries of individual systems.

## 1. Introduction

Crowdsourcing is an umbrella term for approaches that harness the diverse potential of large groups of people via an open call for contribution over the Web. Using information technology as a facilitator, crowdsourcing organizations implement socio-technical systems to channel the contribution of human workforce, knowledge, skills, or perspectives into the generation of digital information products and services. Such crowdsourcing information systems have recently gained in popularity for a variety of organizational functions such as problem solving, knowledge aggregation, content generation, and large-scale data processing.

All crowdsourcing systems are built on the same basic principle of contributor self-selection: Organizations publish their tasks to an often unrestricted public on the Web and interested individuals decide freely whether they are willing and able to contribute. As opposed to traditional employee models, this principle enables crowdsourcing organizations to access the vast potential of contributors with various backgrounds. Many organizations have discovered the value of this scalability and diversity provided by crowdsourcing approaches for a plethora of applications.

While the self-selection principle allows potential contributors to select tasks according to their individual preferences, the heavily increasing amount of opportunities to engage in crowdsourcing efforts across a wide range of platforms easily leads to an information overload situation. Indeed, several studies [1–3] have shown that contributors struggle to <sup>fi</sup>nd those tasks that closely match their personal interests and capabilities. High search costs threaten to reduce the motivation to participate and cause contributors to settle for less suitable tasks, effectively decreasing the quality of the results. As crowdsourcing organizations, in turn, miss out on many good contributions and need to deal with the problem of sub-optimal contributions instead, the overall ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of crowdsourcing approaches decreases considerably.

In this article, we suggest approaching the issue by designing mechanisms for personalized task recommendation. Recommender systems are a category of tools and techniques that provide suggestions of potentially useful items based on individual preferences [4]. They have achieved enormous success in many areas with similar problems such as product recommendation in e-commerce or content personalization in information and entertainment portals. As our analysis will show, little research has addressed the use of recommender systems in a crowdsourcing context. Moreover, existing studies on task recommendation focus on the development of approaches for speci<sup>fi</sup>c use cases and thus remain isolated from each other.

We believe that by channeling the separate research efforts into a comprehensive perspective we can develop a better understanding of the existing practices and derive insights that exceed the boundaries of individual systems. Consequently, the aim of this article is to lay a conceptual foundation for future research on the design of personalized task recommendation mechanisms that assist individuals in <sup>fi</sup>nding suitable tasks and thus create bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for both contributors and requesters. To this intent, we conduct a systematic review of the academic literature, focusing on (i) the context, (ii) the techniques, and (iii) the evaluation of current research.

The remainder of this article is structured as follows: We <sup>fi</sup>rst lay out the research context for this article including the basic mechanics of crowdsourcing approaches, their relevance for different system types, and a detailed argument for the opportunities of personalized task recommendation (Section 2). We proceed by describing the methodological approach taken to conduct a systematic literature review (Section 3), followed by a presentation of the current state of the art (Section 4). Based on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings and well-established recommender systems literature, we discuss a number of key issues for future research on the design of task recommendation approaches (Section 5). We conclude by brie<sup>fl</sup>y summarizing the contributions and limitations of this article (Section 6).

## 2. Research context

## 2.1. Crowdsourcing information systems

Although isolated examples for crowdsourcing approaches can be found throughout the centuries [5], the development of the Internet and ‘Web 2.0’ technologies has drastically leveraged the reach and the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of connecting with large groups of people. Ever since, researchers and practitioners from various <sup>fi</sup>elds have begun to explore how to tap into the potential of crowds in a multitude of contexts.

It is only recently that such approaches have been considered related phenomena. The term “crowdsourcing” [6] has since attracted a lot of attention, even if its exact understanding still varies [7]. Relevant research is rooted in <sup>fi</sup>elds as diverse as computer science [8] and management [9] and is applied in many areas that have discovered crowdsourcing as a potentially useful approach. Consequently, research questions mostly center on speci<sup>fi</sup>c use cases and individual aspects of crowdsourcing. This fragmentation of the research landscape makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to establish a comprehensive knowledge base and to provide organizations with structured guidance for the design of their crowdsourcing initiatives.

In earlier work [10], we therefore suggested a more integrated perspective to study the socio-technical design of the information systems that realize Web-based crowdsourcing efforts. Well-founded sociotechnical approaches study information systems within their organizational context, ensuring that the design of their individual components is aligned with this context and with each other [11–13]. The work system approach, for instance, de<sup>fi</sup>nes an information system as “a system in which human participants and/or machines perform work (processes and activities) using information, technology, and other resources to produce informational products and/or services for internal or external customers” [11].

Building on this de<sup>fi</sup>nition, we introduced crowdsourcing information systems as socio-technical systems that provide informational products and services by harnessing the potential of large groups of people via the Web. All of these systems employ variations of a generic crowdsourcing process, which is facilitated by information technology: Crowdsourcing organizations, often called ‘requesters,’ publish their tasks via an open call to a public, which in most cases is not restricted to particular groups. An ex ante unknown group of individuals in this ‘crowd’ decide freely if they are willing and capable of contributing to a task. This essential part of the process is sometimes called “self-identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of contributors” [14]. Individuals that have chosen to participate submit their contributions, which are then aggregated and selected [9] in different ways to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the system function, depending on the particular type of crowdsourcing system.

## 2.2. Organizational functions of crowdsourcing information systems

According to general systems theory, a system cannot be understood by analyzing its constituent parts alone, but only by determining its function in the supersystem, i.e., the surrounding system of which it is a part [15], cited in [16]. Like any information system, a crowdsourcing system thus needs to be <sup>fi</sup>rst considered in terms of its function within the organizational environment before one can derive details on its “features and component parts” and how they enable this function [16]. Consequently, in order to gain a deeper and comparative understanding of the respective socio-technical designs, we developed a distinction of fundamental system archetypes along their organizational function.

The development of typologies provides structures to organize the body of knowledge and to study relationships among otherwise disorderly concepts [17,18]. By enabling a more differentiated perspective, a typology of systems therefore also allows us to channel existing research results and attribute divergences, for example, with respect to the design of task recommendation approaches, to systematic differences [19]. We will brie<sup>fl</sup>y summarize the development and the outcome of this typology as it is used to guide the analysis in this article. For further details we refer to the original article [10].

Following the de<sup>fi</sup>nition in Section 2.1, the generic function of any information system can be perceived as the processing of information in order to provide informational products and services [11]. As opposed to ‘traditional’ information systems, the product or service that is provided by a crowdsourcing information system – and thus its speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational function – is essentially determined by contributions from the crowd. The most comprehensive characteristic to differentiate between crowdsourcing information systems along their organizational function is therefore how they make use of contribu tions from the crowd to deliver the aspired result.

Drawing on concepts used in systems theory and following a structured method for IS taxonomy development, we derived two dimensions that effectively determine this “meta-characteristic” [18]: We distinguished systems according to (i) whether they seek homogeneous vs. heterogeneous contributions and (ii) whether they seek a nonemergent vs. an emergent value from these contributions.

A system that seeks homogeneous contributions values all (valid) contributions equally. Homogeneous contributions that comply with the prede<sup>fi</sup>ned speci<sup>fi</sup>cations are seen as qualitatively identical; the system is geared to mere quantitative processing. In contrast, a system that seeks heterogeneous contributions values these contributions differently according to their individual qualities. Relevant qualities are determined by the speci<sup>fi</sup>c task and thus vary from objective measures, such as test results, to subjective perceptions, such as clarity or esthetics. Heterogeneous contributions are seen as alternatives or complements and are processed accordingly.

A system that seeks a non-emergent value from its contributions derives this value directly from all or some of the individual contributions in isolation. In such systems, an individual contribution delivers a <sup>fi</sup>xed value, which is independent of other contributions. A system that seeks an emergent value from its contributions, however, can only derive this value from the collective of contributions and the relationships between them. An individual contribution therefore only delivers value as part of the collection of contributions as a whole.

Combining these two dimensions resulted in the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of four archetypes of crowdsourcing information systems with distinct organizational functions, as depicted in Fig. 1.

Crowd processing systems seek non-emergent value that derives directly from large quantities of homogeneous contributions. Valid contributions represent qualitatively identical chunks of work. These systems utilize the additional bandwidth and the scalability provided by a crowdsourcing solution for quick and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient batch processing to minimize the use of traditional organizational resources [8]. Prominent examples include Mechanical Turk, Galaxy Zoo, or Recaptcha.

Crowd rating systems also rely on large amounts of homogeneous contributions but seek a collective value that emerges only from the quantitative properties of the collection of contributions. Contributions thus represent ‘votes’ on a given topic. By aggregating a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient number of these votes, these systems accurately deduce a collective response that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the “wisdom of crowds” [5], such as a collective assessment on TripAdvisor or a collective prediction on the Hollywood Stock Exchange.

Fig. 1. The four archetypes of crowdsourcing information systems [10].

Crowd solving systems seek non-emergent value that derives directly from the isolated values of their heterogeneous contributions. Contributions are qualitatively different and thus represent alternative or complementary solutions to a given problem. These systems are built to approach ‘hard’ problems with well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned evaluation criteria (e.g., in the Net<sup>fl</sup>ix Prize or on FoldIt) or ‘soft’ problems that do not have an optimal solution, such as ideation contests (e.g., on InnoCentive) or ‘make-to-order’ digital product contests (e.g., on 99designs).

Crowd creation systems seek a collective value that emerges only from the accumulation of a variety of heterogeneous contributions and their relationships. Contributions have a complementary share in the collective outcome depending on their individual qualities and their relationship with others. These systems aggregate contributions into a comprehensive artifact such as user-generated content platforms (e.g., app stores), ‘make-to-stock’ production of digital content (e.g., stock photography), or knowledge aggregation (e.g., Wikipedia).

As stated above, the described systems are archetypes with distinct organizational functions. In practice, many crowdsourcing efforts are built on hybrid systems that combine some of these functions. Moreover, we note that our understanding of a crowdsourcing system is based on the fundamental mechanics of its processes as described above, regardless of any particular <sup>fi</sup>eld of application. In some instances, this approach might be considered rather inclusive as compared to some other de<sup>fi</sup>nitions in the literature, which attempt to delineate crowdsourcing from related concepts [20,21].

## 2.3. The value of crowds

Crowdsourcing approaches have had large success in all of the aforementioned contexts due to two inherent properties: scalability and diversity. The open nature of contributions enables crowdsourcing systems to reach a very large number of potential contributors and thus to scale very well. Moreover, these potential contributors, unlike traditional employees, come from a wide variety of different backgrounds, which make for a high level of diversity in the respective crowd. Past research on Amazon Mechanical Turk, for instance, indicates a considerable variance in demographics [22]. Crowdsourcing systems draw on these properties, scalability and diversity, in different ways according to their organizational function [10]

Crowd processing systems utilize the scalable bandwidth of a crowdsourcing approach for time-ef<sup>fi</sup>cient batch processing. The diversity of interests within the crowd enables these systems to recruit suf<sup>fi</sup>cient contributors for a variety of tasks, most of which harness basic abilities of the human brain and do not require special skills.

Crowd solving systems seek to increase the chance of receiving highquality contributions. These systems aim to gather insights on problems that often bene<sup>fi</sup>t from diversity in form of alternative perspectives or speci<sup>fi</sup>c skills. One obvious way is a “broadcasting” approach [23], which tries to reach as many and as diverse potential contributors as possible. Related studies have shown that many solutions are delivered or improved by non-obvious contributors that come from domains lateral to a problem [24], a phenomenon also called “serendipity” [25].

Crowd rating systems rely on large numbers of diverse contributors to generate a representative result, which either re<sup>fl</sup>ects a surveyed spectrum of opinions or collective assessments and predictions. Typically, the larger and more diverse a crowd that rating systems can assemble, the more accurate the results become. In fact, Surowiecki [5] states diversity as one of the prerequisites for the “wisdom of crowds” to emerge.

Crowd creation systems, similarly, rely on large numbers of diverse contributors to harness multiple perspectives, distributed knowledge, and different skills. A crowdsourcing approach enables these systems to aggregate truly comprehensive artifacts that feature a variety of aspects re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the individual diversity of their contributors

## 2.4. The difficulty of finding the right tasks

In order to achieve the necessary scalability and diversity, crowdsourcing systems are faced with the challenge of reaching and enlisting a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient number of suitable individuals. In line with the fundamental self-identi<sup>fi</sup>cation principle, it is essentially up to the individual contributors to identify those tasks that best match their personal preferences.

Research into contributor motivation across all types of crowdsourcing systems shows that contributors choose tasks according to a complex multidimensional construct of motivational factors, which are weighted differently among individuals [26–34]. While the <sup>fi</sup>ndings con<sup>fi</sup>rm the role of extrinsic factors such as payment, they highlight in particular the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of intrinsic motivation, i.e., “doing something because it is inherently interesting or enjoyable […] rather than for some separable consequence” [35]. This means, perhaps not surprisingly, that an individual's task preferences are not merely based on explicit and often task-agnostic ‘separable’ incentives, which can be set by the task requester. Instead, they depend to a considerable extent on the match of intrinsic personal interests with the very nature or ‘content’ of a task. As these inherent properties of a task cannot be changed without altering the expected outcome, an organization's means to attract more intrinsically motivated contributors are often limited, for example, to optimizing task designs [27,36] or using intricate gami<sup>fi</sup>cation approaches [37].

In addition to the match with extrinsic and intrinsic interests, another important factor that determines task choice is the compliance of an individual's capabilities with the respective task requirements. These capabilities often take the form of speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge or skillsets, which are necessary to make a potentially valuable contribution. Required capabilities may be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed explicitly by the task requester or result implicitly from the nature of the task. Some capabilities can be formalized as “quali<sup>fi</sup>cations” [38,39] that potential contributors need to obtain, e.g., by passing a corresponding test, before they are allowed to contribute to certain tasks. In many cases, however, the requesters need to rely on contributors' perceived compliance with the explicit or implicit requirements. Obviously, the more speci<sup>fi</sup>c a task and its requirements are, the more restricted is the group of potential contributors.

Summarizing these observations, task choice preferences in crowdsourcing are to a large extent determined by a combination personal interests and capabilities. Both of these factors are heavily dependent on the individual contributor and neither can be easily in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by task requesters. Furthermore, they may shift over time and may also vary depending on contextual factors such as location and time of day, among others.

Several factors, however, cause interested individuals to struggle with the almost in<sup>fi</sup>nite opportunities of engaging in crowdsourcing activities to <sup>fi</sup>nd those tasks that they are strongly willing and capable of participating in. Firstly, the crowdsourcing landscape is largely scattered and most of the sites and their tasks remain hidden to the vast majority of the crowd. The rising popularity of crowdsourcing approaches in recent years has led to a heavily increased number of initiatives, which require contributors to perform diverse tasks and roles [40]. As of September 2013, crowdsourcing.org, one of the leading crowdsourcing industry websites, maintains a directory of almost 2500 crowdsourcing examples in a plethora of application domains. Secondly, the more prominent projects, such as Wikipedia, and marketplaces, such as Mechanical Turk or InnoCentive, which act as an intermediary for multiple crowdsourcing organizations, accumulate a large number and variety of tasks in the same system. Without adequate assistance, though, individuals cannot navigate the body of tasks within these systems and may easily suffer from information overload. Thirdly, individuals are faced with the challenge of accurately assessing the match of their personal capabilities with the (perceived) requirements of a task. A failure to do so will result in either overcon<sup>fi</sup>dent or under-con<sup>fi</sup>dent decisions, which both effectively prevent potential contributors from working on the right tasks.

A well-suited example to observe the issue is the Mechanical Turk platform. Mechanical Turk offers a variety of tasks, which draw on typical human abilities, such as natural language processing or providing personal opinions [41], and increasingly require speci<sup>fi</sup>c cognitive skills [42]. As of September 2013, mturk.com lists over 1500 currently active “HIT groups” bundling a total number of almost 300,000 individual tasks (“HITs”, Human Intelligence Tasks) as calculated by the MTurk-

Tracker [43]. The list of HIT groups extends over 150 pages and can be sorted by generic criteria such as “HIT Creation Date” or “HITs available.” Interested individuals need to maintain an account with Mechanical Turk, browse and select from the list of HIT groups, and eventually submit their results [44].

Research into contributor or ‘worker’ behavior on Mechanical Turk con<sup>fi</sup>rms the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of <sup>fi</sup>nding suitable tasks. In a survey on the impact of task properties, workers reported “HIT sounds interesting/ enjoyable” among the highest-rated criteria for choosing a task [36]. Through an analysis of task disappearance rates for different sorting criteria, however, Chilton et al. [1] <sup>fi</sup>nd that workers mostly choose from “the <sup>fi</sup>rst page of the most recently posted tasks and the <sup>fi</sup>rst two pages of the tasks with the most available instances.” As these sorting criteria do not relate to the actual content of the tasks, this situation is suboptimal in terms of a match with personal interests and capabilities, and workers seem to make a trade-off with respect to search costs. Yet, insights gained from a corresponding contributor survey reveal that about 25% of the workers seem to be willing to go to great lengths and browse dozens of pages in order to <sup>fi</sup>nd suitable tasks. Several workers speci<sup>fi</sup>cally complain about the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of <sup>fi</sup>nding “good” tasks, which clearly shows the need for better mechanisms [1].

Similar results are presented by studies of other crowdsourcing systems. Dror et al. [2] state that “users of Yahoo! Answers <sup>fi</sup>nd it hard to search for questions they would like to answer” and “mainly skim through long and dynamic lists of open questions.” Stankovic et al. [3] conclude from their practice at Hypios, a crowd solving intermediary, that “simply posting a problem online is not enough to attract diverse and innovative solutions,” but platforms need to proactively identify potential solvers. While Jeppesen and Lakhani [24] show that many successful contributions are made by solvers from lateral domains, contributing different perspectives, many individuals will only look at tasks in their primary <sup>fi</sup>eld of expertise, not realizing on their own that they possess the right capabilities to solve tasks from other <sup>fi</sup>elds [23].

## 2.5. Opportunities of Personalized Task Recommendation

In order to assist potential contributors with the discovery of suitable tasks according to their personal preferences, we believe that new approaches to support the self-identi<sup>fi</sup>cation process are required. Many information systems, for example in the context of e-commerce, include such mechanisms in the form of personalized recommender systems. Although a few authors have mentioned the challenge of improving the alignment of contributors and tasks in speci<sup>fi</sup>c crowdsourcing contexts [45,46], current crowdsourcing information systems do not adequately address this need. In most cases, the corresponding design features are limited to standard search functionality, task sorting, and <sup>fi</sup>lter mechanisms that are based on generic criteria such as thematic category, payment, or date. Unlike personalized recommendation approaches, such criteria cannot appropriately cover the inherent characteristics of particular tasks; nor can they take into account the complex combination of individual interests and capabilities. While a few recommender systems also recommend items based on general characteristics, e.g., popularity, the concept usually refers to techniques that make recommendations based on a model of individual user preferences [4]. In line with the literature, we will therefore use the terms recommender system and personalized recommender system interchangeably.

A personalized task recommendation approach in crowdsourcing will aim to facilitate the match of individual interests and capabilities with the right tasks and thus create potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for both contrib utors and requesters. Contributors that do not need to invest high search costs or settle for suboptimal tasks are likely to maintain a higher motivation. Yuen et al. [47] argue that more contributors could be attracted in the long run if they found suitable tasks more easily. Cosley et al. [48] show that theory in economics and social psychology suggests that “reducing the cost and increasing the personal value of contribution would motivate members to participate more.” Their argument is partly based on the collective effort model, which states that personal value depends, among others, on the individual “meaningfulness and intrinsic value of the task” [49]. Furthermore, the model predicts that an individual's motivation to contribute to collective tasks is higher if the contributions to the collective product are perceived as unique. Intuitively, this applies to those crowdsourcing systems that emphasize collaboration among contributors (as opposed to competition) and the effects have been con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in such contexts [50,51]. By highlighting the value of individual capabilities, a matching of contributors and tasks might induce such a perception of uniqueness, see [52].

From a requester perspective, task recommendation will increase the probability of attracting contributions from individuals that are highly motivated and possess the right capabilities. For tasks with very speci<sup>fi</sup>c requirements, such as in crowd solving systems, this can even be critical for success. Currently, however, requesters miss out on many good contributions as their tasks are often not discovered by the right individuals. Moreover, less-skilled contributors, potentially motivated by monetary incentives, may submit contributions that result in sub-standard or noisy output instead [42]. This effectively decreases the overall ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the crowdsourcing approach and the quality of the result [47,53]. In systems with <sup>fi</sup>xed, unique task assignments, these contributors will take tasks away from genuinely skilled individuals who have not been able to <sup>fi</sup>nd these tasks in time [42]. Some systems seek to counter the adverse effects of less-skilled contributors by employing quali<sup>fi</sup>cation-based restrictions of potential contributors [54] or crowd-based mechanisms to assure the validity of homogeneous contributions, such as majority votes or peer reviews [55,56]. All of these measures, however, further reduce the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency by increasing the cost or the duration of a task and are thus no alternative to personalized task recommendations.

## 3. Methodology

In order to analyze the current state of personalized task recommendation in crowdsourcing information systems, we conducted a systematic literature review. We followed the methodological guidelines as described by Webster and Watson [57] and Levy and Ellis [58]. This section will brie<sup>fl</sup>y summarize our implementation of these guidelines to accumulate a body of literature and motivate the used search parameters.

We chose not to restrict the literature search to speci<sup>fi</sup>c outlets or disciplines for several reasons. Firstly, crowdsourcing has only recently been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as an overarching phenomenon [14] and has as such aroused the interest of academic researchers. As a consequence, the vast majority of crowdsourcing articles at this stage has not yet found their way into high quality journals and, instead, is published at (peerreviewed) conferences. Secondly, crowdsourcing is a socio-technical approach with many potential applications. Corresponding research therefore comes from an increasing variety of <sup>fi</sup>elds studying how to tap into the potential of crowds. Some of these <sup>fi</sup>elds, e.g., computer science, also attribute a higher value to conferences as primary publication outlets [59]. Finally, our experience and initial scans indicated that the body of knowledge with respect to the emerging topic of personalized task recommendation is very limited. As our work aims to identify and derive insights from the few existing studies and thus to provide a foundation for future research, we decided to pursue an inclusive approach.

Consequently, we conducted an extensive keyword search on Google Scholar. The search engine was chosen due to its comprehensive coverage across the boundaries of individual databases. On the downside, this approach tends to generate a large number of irrelevant results, particularly when conducting isolated searches for terms that are used in a variety of contexts, such as ‘recommender system.’ In line with our research questions, we thus employed a combination of keywords from two groups: (i) terms that refer to crowdsourcing approaches, for example, ‘crowdsourcing,’ ‘crowd,’ ‘crowdsourced,’ or ‘community,’ and (ii) terms that cover the recommendation aspect such as ‘task recommendation,’ ‘task routing,’ ‘task matching,’ ‘recommender system,’ or ‘collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering,’ among others. The keyword selection was re<sup>fi</sup>ned iteratively based on the results of our search queries.

Search results on Google Scholar were sorted by relevance, which is a combination of textual match and academic impact as measured, e.g., by citations. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed potentially relevant articles by scanning the titles and abstracts of the search results for indicators of task recommendation in crowdsourcing. Several hundred results were scanned for the various keyword combinations until no further relevant articles could be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. We then proceeded to read articles in detail to determine whether they actually described, developed, or evaluated an approach for matching tasks with contributors in crowdsourcing information systems as de<sup>fi</sup>ned in Section 2.1.

Subsequently, we conducted backward and forward searches [57] on the accumulated body of literature to identify further articles and ensure we did not miss any major contributions. As Levy and Ellis [58] note, keywords of IS literature tend to change regularly, especially such fairly recently introduced ‘buzzwords’ as crowdsourcing. Therefore, this step also enabled us to identify other, similar research streams that are not termed crowdsourcing but are based on the same mechanics. We note that the results may not be exhaustive since crowdsourcing approaches for speci<sup>fi</sup>c applications have been studied using various terminologies. Nevertheless, we believe that our approach results in an accurate depiction of the current state of the art.

The search process ended when our attempts to identify additional articles by extending the search with further keywords or by following cross references did not result in any relevant results. This is in line with the ending conditions described in Webster and Watson [57] and Levy and Ellis [58].

The accumulated body of literature comprises a total of 27 articles. As predicted, few of the articles have been published in journals [60–62] while the vast majority has been published in conference proceedings. Non-published articles such as working papers and term papers and duplicate publications were excluded. One paper that could not be accessed was also excluded [63]. Another study did not provide suf<sup>fi</sup>cient details about the recommendation approach [64]. Table 2 gives an overview of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed literature and the respective outlets.

## 4. Findings

This section summarizes our <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding the emerging state of personalized task recommendation in crowdsourcing information systems. Our analysis focuses on the context of current research, the techniques used to generate personalized recommendations, and the evaluation of existing approaches. Note that some of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed articles do not provide insights on all three aspects and thus do not appear in all subsections.

## 4.1. Recommender context

As a <sup>fi</sup>rst level of analysis, we have considered the context of the existing studies. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that research on personalized task recommendation is conducted in various streams that remain largely isolated from each other. As described in Section 2.2, we classi<sup>fi</sup>ed current approaches with respect to the organizational function that the underlying crowdsourcing system addresses. In contrast to a large part of the analyzed studies, which take a rather narrow view on crowdsourcing, this perspective enables us to draw a comprehensive picture of task recommendation across the fragmented research landscape. The classi<sup>fi</sup>cation will also allow us to develop a comparative understanding of systematic differences regarding the design of task recommenders. In some cases, divergences can be attributed to fundamental characteristics of the respective system types, such as their organizational functions, the type of contributions they seek, and the design of their internal processes. Table 2 lists the context of the analyzed studies grouped by system type. So far, we did not identify any task recommendation research in the context of crowd rating systems.

Several studies are concerned with the development of recommendation approaches in the context of crowd processing systems. Ambati et al. [42] and Yuen et al. [47,65] propose recommendation engines for microtask marketplaces such as Amazon Mechanical Turk. Organizations typically use these marketplaces to process large quantities of data, e.g., for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, transcription, or search tasks. Taking a slightly different perspective, Satzger et al. [66] describe an approach to preselect the crowd of potential contributors to such tasks based on their capabilities. To a similar intent, Ho and Vaughan [53] and Difallah et al. [67] develop approaches for the semi-automated assignment of contributors to processing tasks. Although task assignment, as opposed to task recommendation, puts less emphasis on the self-identi<sup>fi</sup>cation principle, it is based on the same idea of matching tasks to suitable contributors.

A relatively large body of research focuses on task recommendation in the context of question answering communities such as Yahoo! Answers. While many questions in these communities represent individual rather than organizational problems, the function and the mechanics of the crowdsourcing systems implemented to solve them are similar. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of tasks approached in such systems, reaching from questions such as “What's a good travel destination in Europe?” to “How do I solve this Poisson distribution problem?” [2], presents an ideal use case for personalized recommenders. The analyzed studies have developed approaches to match these questions with potential answerers. In a similar context, two studies address the problem of matching threads and potential experts in discussion forums [68,69]. Horowitz and Kamvar [70], <sup>fi</sup>nally, describe a social search engine that routes questions to persons that are most likely to answer them.

Four articles study the application of recommendation techniques in the context of crowd creation systems. Cosley et al. [48] and Yuan et al. [60] focus on the challenge of matching Wikipedia articles and editors. As the level of coverage and quality on Wikipedia grows, it gets increasingly dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for potential editors to <sup>fi</sup>nd suitable articles to contribute to. Jamjoom et al. [62] present Cyano, a social networking enterprise application that uses a crowdsourcing approach to capture and maintain information on problem and change management processes for outsourced IT infrastructures. Cyano includes mechanisms to “recommend subject information that users may <sup>fi</sup>nd interesting.” Cosley et al. [52] analyze different recommendation approaches to identify entries of MovieLens, an online movie database, that contributors might be willing to work on.

## 4.2. Recommender techniques

Recommender systems research is an evolving <sup>fi</sup>eld that explores an ever increasing variety of techniques to match users with items. While the exact algorithms used by these techniques change frequently, they all require certain kinds of knowledge in order to generate good recommendations. Based on a general distinction of the knowledge sources that are available to recommender systems [71], we classify the analyzed approaches according to their use of individual, collaborative, and content knowledge sources. Table 3 gives an overview of the results of our analysis.

## 4.2.1. Individual contributor knowledge

Knowledge about the individual users and their preferences plays a central role in any personalized recommender system [4]. In addition to knowledge about a contributor's interests, as collected in most other recommenders, task recommenders in crowdsourcing also model implicit or explicit knowledge about a contributor's capabilities (see Section 2.4). The analyzed approaches make use of various kinds of knowledge sources to build pro<sup>fi</sup>les of their contributors' interests and capabilities.

Burke and Ramezani [71] distinguish between knowledge that is derived implicitly from a contributor's behavior and knowledge that is derived explicitly from their expressed opinions. In most cases, such knowledge is obtained by drawing on transactional information that re<sup>fl</sup>ects a contributor's historical interactions with a system [4], in particular with its tasks. As Horowitz and Kamvar [70] put it, the rationale for this is that “if you want to be able to predict what kind of content a user will generate […], <sup>fi</sup>rst examine the content they have generated in the past.”

Almost all approaches obtain knowledge from an individual's past contribution behavior, i.e., from the tasks that they contributed to. Yuen et al. [65] also consider viewed and selected tasks. Some of the recommenders in question answering communities also consider the questions that an individual asked; however, this has no relevance for the context of organizational crowd solving systems with a clear separation of requesters and solvers. While most recommenders follow a passive approach in obtaining behavioral knowledge, two crowd processing systems include proactive mechanisms that probe a variety of contributor capabilities: Ho and Vaughan [53] describe an up-front exploration phase during which tasks are assigned randomly to determine individual worker skill levels. Similarly, Satzger et al. [66] propose to automatically assign assessment tasks with known results to improve the con<sup>fi</sup>dence levels for speci<sup>fi</sup>c individual skills.

A small number of approaches derives additional knowledge from a contributor's opinions by considering <sup>fl</sup>agged questions [2,72], rated movies [52], or refused recommendations [70,73]. In addition to opinions about speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks, a few recommender systems also rely on more generic background information. In three cases, contributors can specify lists of self-assessed skills or interests [2,66,70]. Furthermore, two approaches extract information from structured and unstructured external sources, such as social network pro<sup>fi</sup>les [67,70].

As opposed to ‘classic’ recommender systems, some task recommendation systems in crowdsourcing make use of an additional source of knowledge: the contributions provided by each individual. A large part of the analyzed approaches in crowd processing and crowd solving systems derives knowledge about a contributor's capabilities by assessing the individual contribution quality. In crowd processing systems, this assessment refers to the validity of a contribution, i.e., whether it was accepted by the requester or by an automated quality control mechanism. In crowd solving systems, contributions are assessed using a rating provided by the requester or other contributors. A number of crowd solving recommenders also incorporates the content of a contribution, i.e., the text of an answer, as an additional source of individual knowledge. One approach [74] even builds the contributor model entirely from an individual's answers without considering the answered questions.

## 4.2.2. Content knowledge

Content-based recommendation techniques aim to recommend items whose features match the information stored in a user's pro<sup>fi</sup>le [75,76]. Consequently, given the availability of knowledge about a contributor's interests and capabilities, the quality of content-based task recommendations depends on a recommender's ability to analyze and model the essential content of tasks.

The vast majority of the analyzed recommendation approaches make use of content knowledge. This knowledge is derived from unstructured textual descriptions of a task, such as its title or the instructions, and from structured classi<sup>fi</sup>cations in form of keywords, tags, categories, or links. Further relevant task features include meta-data about the task, such as requester, reward, or allotted time.

As with individual knowledge, some task recommendation approaches in crowdsourcing derive additional content knowledge from the collective contributions to a task. Three recommenders in the context of crowd solving systems complement the given task description with the information contained in all [2,69] or the best answers [77] to a question.

## 4.2.3. Collaborative knowledge

Collaborative or social knowledge is knowledge about the larger community of users in a recommender system [71]. Unlike content based recommendation approaches, pure collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering techniques do not require knowledge about the characteristics of items. Instead, they generate recommendations based on the interaction patterns of groups of users with the system [78]. Collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering re<sup>fl</sup>ects the idea that a user is likely to have a similar preference on an item as other users with similar interaction patterns did. Moreover, a user will have similar preferences on two items if other users have expressed similar preferences on them [79]. We note again that, in a crowdsourcing context, preferences can refer to both implicit and explicit measures of a contributor's interests and capabilities with respect to a particular task.

All of the analyzed task recommendation approaches in crowd creation systems and few of the recommenders in other systems make use of collaborative knowledge sources. These approaches generate recommendations by considering patterns of collaborative behavior [48,62,65, 68], opinions [52], or both [2]. Four of the analyzed recommenders actually implement hybrid approaches that combine content and collaborative knowledge sources [2,48,65,68].

## 4.3. Recommender evaluation

In order to <sup>fi</sup>nd some indications as to the general success and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of personalized task recommendation approaches, we analyzed the evaluations conducted by the existing studies. A few studies had to be excluded as they either did not conduct an evaluation (e.g., [62]) or the effect of task recommendation was not discernible (e.g., [66,70]). We examined the remaining studies according to the experimental settings, the respective samples, the evaluated properties and corresponding metrics, and any relevant results. Table 4 gives an overview of the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation results.

Shani and Gunawardana [80] describe three types of experimental settings to evaluate recommender systems in general: Offline experiments are simulated without user interaction on a pre-collected data set. The vast majority of the analyzed task recommendation studies are evaluated in this manner. These evaluations are either based on publicly available datasets, such as from various question answering sites, forums, and Wikipedia, or on self-collected data, e.g., from dedicated tasks [47,53]. In contrast, user studies assess the system through the interaction with a small group of test subjects, who receive actual recommendations in a controlled environment. Only one of the existing approaches [67] is evaluated in a user study. Online experiments, <sup>fi</sup>nally, are performed by actually deploying the system in a real environment. Three studies in the context of crowd creation systems conduct such online experiments on Wikipedia [48,60] and the MovieLens platform [52].

Various properties of a recommender system can be evaluated in these experimental settings [80]. Most of the task recommendation studies, however, focus on assessing the prediction accuracy of their approaches. This is generally done in an of<sup>fl</sup>ine experiment by calculating metrics that describe how well an algorithm can reproduce the historic contributor–task relationships in a sample. The existing studies use a great variety of accuracy metrics, most of which are widely used in information retrieval: precision, recall, mean absolute error (MAE), mean average precision (MAP), mean reciprocal rank (MRR), area under ROC curve (AUC), etc.

As these metrics are highly dependent on the respective (sub-)samples and are mostly used by the analyzed studies to compare different techniques, algorithms, and parameters, we refrain from discussing the isolated results. Instead, we exemplarily select two studies that include a comparison with a random baseline: Yuen et al. [47] show that their approach can predict contributor ratings with a mean average error of 0.5 compared to a value of 1.08 at random selection. Similarly, the approach presented by Castro-Herrera [68] signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly outperforms a random recommender at predicting if an individual has contributed to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c thread. For the analyzed seven samples, the described content-based technique achieves a precision of 9.53% to 39.11% and a recall of 14.29% to 49.04%, while the random approach only achieves up to 2% in both cases.

In addition to prediction accuracy, Hu et al. [73] also assess the coverage, i.e., the fraction of all tasks that is recommended, and Zhou et al. [69] the scalability of the respective approaches. As to the general bene<sup>fi</sup>t of task recommendation, however, maybe the most interesting property of a recommender is its utility, i.e., “the value that either the system or the user gains from a recommendation” [80]. All three online experiments and the user study calculate metrics that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the utility of the task recommender to the respective system by measuring the impact on contributions. Cosley et al. [48] determine the fraction of recommendations that led to an actual contribution to Wikipedia as 4.3% as opposed to a mere 1.0% when giving random recommendations. In a longer-term study of the same recommender, Yuan et al. [60] compare the mean levels of contributions between adopters and non-adopters. While adopters on average contributed less before adoption $( \mu _ { a } =$ $9 2 7 < \mu _ { n a } = 1 0 5 0 )$ , the contribution pattern was reversed after adopting task recommendations $( \mu _ { a } = 7 8 9 > \mu _ { n a } = 5 6 3 )$ ). Difallah et al. [67] measure utility as the fraction of correct contributions and <sup>fi</sup>nd that their approach on average results in a 29% improvement compared to traditional task selection on Mechanical Turk.

Cosley et al. [52], <sup>fi</sup>nally, evaluate the impact of their task recommender on the contributions to MovieLens, an online movie database. They measure the number of unique editors, movies edited, and <sup>fi</sup>elds edited, which are all dominated by a random baseline approach. This may, however, be the result of a mismatch between the recommendation approach and the nature of the task. The applied collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering technique recommended relatively popular movies that had received high ratings by many people. Consequently, the movie information had probably been reviewed many times before with little room for improvement.

## 5. Discussion

Our survey of the current state of the art shows that research on personalized task recommendation is still in an early state and fragmented across various types of crowdsourcing information systems. Building on the existing studies, we will discuss a number of issues that we believe future research should address in order to build a solid foundation for the systematic design of task recommenders. In line with Webster and Watson [57], who state that an emerging topic will “bene<sup>fi</sup>t from exposure to potential theoretical foundations”, we draw on insights from mainstream recommender systems research where appropriate. Table 1 gives a brief summary of the discussed issues.

## 5.1. Applications and limitations

Based on the general argument presented in Section 2.5 and the early but promising results of existing studies, personalized task recommendation appears to have the potential to enhance a variety of crowdsourcing approaches. Further research is required to determine the most promising applications of task recommenders within each of the system types and their limitations: Do the results from question answering communities hold for other instances of crowd solving systems? Is task recommendation in crowd creation systems useful for domains other than knowledge aggregation, such as journalism or software development?

While our search did not turn up any studies on task recommendation in crowd rating systems, we believe that this type of system also holds signi<sup>fi</sup>cant potential for task recommenders. Many systems that seek to conduct collaborative evaluations, surveys, or predictions should be able to improve the overall quality of their results by recommending the respective rating tasks to contributors that are familiar with the particular items. Several indicators can be considered to determine suitable contributors, such as purchase or rental transactions for the rating of movies and travel bookings for the rating of hotels. Useful information could also be extracted from sources external to the particular crowd rating system. If a contributor, for instance, uses location-based services such as FourSquare to regularly ‘check in’ at a restaurant, they probably possess the necessary knowledge to rate that particular place, see also [81].

## 5.2. Sources of individual knowledge

Our analysis of the literature has shown that personalized task recommendation systems can select from a variety of individual knowledge sources. Although in theory these sources are available to most crowdsourcing systems, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that many existing approaches do not tap their full potential, which may partly be due to the lack of corresponding data in of<sup>fl</sup>ine experiments. We highlight a few issues toward creating more comprehensive contributor models.

Following [4], we suggest to perceive the relationship between a contributor and a task as an interaction that comprises a broad set of recorded lifecycle events, potentially including the context in which the events occurred. Events can be derived implicitly from a contributor's behavior, e.g., when a task is viewed, started, or completed. If possible, however, a system should also record events that re<sup>fl</sup>ect a contributor's explicit opinions about a task, such as personal assessments and comments. Depending on the type of an event, it can include a rating within scalar, binary, or unary categories [79] and thus re<sup>fl</sup>ect varying degrees of individual interests or capabilities.

An interaction also includes any available assessment of an individual's capabilities regarding a contribution to the respective task. Depending on the organizational function of a crowdsourcing system and thus the type of its contributions, different assessments can be conducted. Systems with heterogeneous contributions, i.e., crowd solving and crowd creation systems, can assess the (comparative) quality of individual contributions and, indirectly, the corresponding capabilities of their contributors. Indicators for such an assessment are highly dependent on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c task and reach from automatically calculated objective measures to a subjective rating by the requester or other contributors. Systems with qualitatively identical homogeneous contributions, i.e., crowd processing and crowd rating systems, can instead assess the validity of a contribution and some contextual factors (e.g., submission time). Again, different approaches are available, such as automated checks, quality control mechanisms, or a manual inspection by the requester. We note that it is often dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to determine the truthfulness of contributions to crowd rating systems as they essentially represent subjective views, see [10].

Sometimes, however, a system cannot derive suf<sup>fi</sup>cient knowledge from a contributor's interactions with tasks. This may happen if a contributor only recently joined (the ‘new user’ problem) or if a system generally has limited interaction, e.g., when the available tasks are complex and time-consuming. In some cases, a recommender may be able to obtain more interaction knowledge by proactively asking contributors to rate or contribute to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c selection of tasks. Recommender systems research provides further input on the application of various “active learning” techniques [82].

Another option to “bootstrap” [66] an individual contributor's pro<sup>fi</sup>le may be to ask contributors to provide some background information on their general interests and capabilities. The entry of such information can be facilitated in various ways using structured forms, keyword or item lists, interview techniques, or gami<sup>fi</sup>cation approaches [83]. We note, however, that asking contributors to maintain explicit pro<sup>fi</sup>les imposes additional costs and will, in some cases, lead to an inaccurate depiction of their true interests and capabilities [48,76,84]. Finally, if a recommender maintains such task-agnostic pro<sup>fi</sup>les, it may also attempt to derive some knowledge from the collective content of a contributor's contributions, e.g., from the text of the provided answers or articles.

## 5.3. Connecting external knowledge sources

Almost all of the contributor pro<sup>fi</sup>les that are built in existing task recommendation approaches are limited to information that is collected internally. Literature suggests that the quality of these pro<sup>fi</sup>les could be considerably enhanced by harnessing information that is external to the respective system. By perceiving a contributor not only as an isolated component of one particular system but also as a connected individual that is part of many systems, we can draw on a rich variety of digital information already present on the Web and thus mitigate sparse data and create more exact pro<sup>fi</sup>les. This information may come, for example, from other crowdsourcing systems [85], knowledge communities [86], social networks [67,70], social tagging systems [87–89], blogs [90,91], expert directories [92,93], and research publications [3,94]. Some of these sources provide semantically linked data [95] that enables the analysis of “user traces in structured form” [3]. Others are rather unstructured and require the application of information retrieval and text mining techniques to extract relevant information [96–98]. While researchers have studied the use of these sources to <sup>fi</sup>nd experts on speci<sup>fi</sup>c topics, little seems to be known about integrating the information into uni<sup>fi</sup>ed, overarching user pro<sup>fi</sup>les, particularly in a crowdsourcing context.

Such integrated pro<sup>fi</sup>les also present a promising opportunity to create new task recommenders that exceed the boundaries of individual crowdsourcing systems and thus account for the scattered nature of the crowdsourcing landscape. Most of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed recommenders have been developed in the context of crowdsourcing systems with a large amount of tasks, such as “third party” [9] marketplaces for crowd processing and solving or extensive crowd creation platforms. Naturally, these systems have access to a large user base and can collect a rich amount of information. Smaller or less known systems, however, often struggle to attract a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient number of suitable contributors. Although some of the tasks in these systems might be a perfect match to the interests and capabilities of a particular individual engaged in crowdsourcing activities, chances are high that this person will never discover the respective system. The development of ‘metarecommenders,’ which maintain integrated contributor pro<sup>fi</sup>les and recommend tasks from multiple systems, could mitigate this problem and increase the visibility of suitable tasks.

Note that an aggregation of personal data and organizational tasks from multiple sources will raise a number of privacy and legal concerns. Nevertheless, we believe that such integrated approaches present a promising avenue to increase the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of crowdsourcing by bringing about a shift from the current platform-centric to a more contentoriented perception of crowdsourcing tasks.

## 5.4. The right recommendation technique

Mainstream recommender systems research provides an extensive body of knowledge on all stages of recommender development. Based on our analysis of the literature, however, it appears to us that the design of personalized task recommendation systems in crowdsourcing could bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a more substantial application of such “foundational knowledge” [99]. By adapting this knowledge to a crowdsourcing context, researchers can draw on insights regarding, for instance, the selection and implementation of suitable techniques.

All of the surveyed task recommendation approaches are based on variations of content-based or collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering techniques. Few approaches make use of hybrid recommendation techniques, although recommender systems literature explicitly highlights the increase in ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency when combining the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of different techniques and thus mitigating their respective weaknesses [100]. Furthermore, few approaches consider additional information such as demographics, context, or social relations, which have been used in other types of recommender systems to improve the quality of recommendations. Community-based or social techniques [4], for instance, use measures of social relatedness to generate recommendations from the ratings of related users. Seen by some as a subtype of collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering, these techniques also do not require knowledge about the items themselves.

As with any recommender system, the choice of the employed approach should be made with the characteristics of the application domain in mind. Burke and Ramezani [71] describe six factors in particular: heterogeneity, risk, churn, interaction style, preference stability, and scrutability. The authors discuss how these factors impact the availability and quality of different knowledge sources and, consequently, how they can be used to determine suitable techniques or combinations thereof.

To give an example, many crowd processing systems, especially intermediary marketplaces, operate on high churn domains, that is, ones in which the available tasks change constantly and rapidly. This leads to increasingly sparse transactional knowledge and diminishes the chance of overlaps in interaction patterns between users. Consequently, such systems pose a challenge for pure collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering techniques. An ef<sup>fi</sup>cient personalized task recommendation in this case should therefore make (additional) use of content-based techniques, which generalize from individual tasks by deriving characteristic features.

In addition to guiding the fundamental choice of the recommendation approach, recommender systems research also provides insights on common design issues, such as dealing with sparse data [79,101], various biases [102], or malicious users [103,104]. Furthermore, when it comes to actually implementing a recommendation technique, existing libraries already provide a wide variety of algorithms. Among some of the most popular open source toolkits are, for instance, Apache Mahout, MyMediaLite, LensKit, GraphLab, and Easyrec.

## 5.5. The role of capabilities and contributions

Our analysis shows that personalized task recommendation differs from traditional recommendation scenarios in several ways. For one, the item preferences predicted by traditional recommender systems mainly re<sup>fl</sup>ect the intrinsic and extrinsic interests of an individual. As described in Section 2.4, however, an individual's preferences in a crowdsourcing context may to a considerable extent also depend on a match of personal capabilities with those required for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c task.

Furthermore, the quality of a recommender system can be judged not only from the perspective of its users but also from that of the organization that runs it. In many traditional scenarios, the satisfaction of the latter party follows directly from the satisfaction of the users' needs and wants. One of the most important functions of commercial recommender systems, for instance, increasing the number of sales, is achieved by pointing users to the items they are interested in and thus likely to buy [4]. In crowdsourcing systems, however, there are limits to this relationship. As opposed to a product recommender, the mere fact of accepting a task recommendation is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient. A requester's goal is not to simply ‘sell’ their tasks to anybody, but to obtain highvalue contributions from individuals with the right capabilities. The crowd, after all, is not a crowd of passive consumers but rather one of service providers with diverse potential. Similar ideas have been brought forward in the context of the collaborative creation of value between producers and consumers [20,105]. It is up to future research to determine to what extent task recommendation systems should consider the requester perspective and, for instance, balance diverging personal interests and capabilities when generating recommendations.

In any case, a personalized task recommendation approach will, implicitly or explicitly, model knowledge about a contributor's capabilities, such as expertise, skills, or domain knowledge. To some extent, this knowledge can be derived from traditional knowledge sources such as contributor behavior and opinions. If possible, however, it appears more informative to obtain an external assessment of capabilities as demonstrated by contributions on speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks. As laid out in 5.2, task recommender systems in crowdsourcing can make use of contributions as an additional source of knowledge on individual capabilities as well as interests.

Contributions may also serve as a source of content knowledge to further carve out the features of tasks in form of, e.g., responses to crowd solving tasks or changes to an existing Wikipedia article. Therefore, in contrast to traditional recommendation scenarios, an item is determined not only by inherent or given features, but also through the users' input on these items. Some insight on this topic might be gained from recent recommender research on the value of user-generated content in social tagging systems [89,106,107] and product reviews [108].

In summary, personalized task recommendation approaches in crowdsourcing possess at least two unique characteristics that set them apart from most traditional recommendation scenarios: the need to consider capabilities in addition to interests in order to make good recommendations and the ability to consider contributions as an additional source of knowledge. We believe the further study of these characteristic differences presents a valuable topic for joint research efforts that may eventually also add to the body of knowledge in mainstream recommender systems research.

## 5.6. The utility of personalized task recommendation

It appears to us that one of the most fundamental issues for future research on personalized task recommendation is the empirical evaluation of its general utility to the crowdsourcing process. Existing research has presented promising results in the context of crowd creation systems; the majority of studies, however, focus on comparing the accuracy of various recommendation techniques in of<sup>fl</sup>ine experiments. While these experiments demonstrate that, in theory, predicting contributor preferences works well, they have a number of shortcomings that limit their expressiveness.

Firstly, the available data sets usually lack knowledge about a contributor's preferences for most of the tasks. An of<sup>fl</sup>ine evaluation therefore cannot reliably assess the accuracy of a prediction for a contributor–task pair that is not contained in the sample. Moreover, the recommender's ability to build a comprehensive pro<sup>fi</sup>le of individual contributors, and thus its potential to make good recommendations, is limited by the sources of knowledge present in the available samples.

Secondly, in many cases, a large part of the data that underlies an of<sup>fl</sup>ine experiment re<sup>fl</sup>ects observed contributor behavior. As discussed in Section 2.4, such implicit behavior may already be suboptimal due to a minimization of search costs, the inadequacy of sorting criteria (if any), or manipulation by some requesters, e.g., through frequent reposting of tasks [1]. Consequently, assessing a recommender's accuracy in terms of how well it reproduces such data sets cannot convey any knowledge about its ability to improve the match between contributors and tasks.

Thirdly, as Shani and Gunawardana [80] put it, “accurate predictions are crucial but insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient to deploy a good recommendation engine.” Even if a task recommender succeeds at making ‘good’ predictions, we do not know if contributors will accept them, nor if they actually lead to higher-quality results: Do recommendations, on an overall scale, improve the outcome of a crowdsourcing system?

In order to address these issues, future research needs to conduct large-scale online experiments in the context of real crowdsourcing systems. We will have to develop adequate metrics that determine the utility of task recommendation approaches for both requesters and contributors and, ideally, can be contrasted with meaningful baselines, such as a random or no-recommendation approach. In addition, recent research [80,109] highlights the importance of further recommender system properties, such as novelty, serendipity, or diversity, which might be of particular interest in a crowdsourcing context.

## 6. Conclusion

The aim of this research is to contribute to a conceptual foundation for the systematic design of personalized task recommendation approaches in crowdsourcing information systems. To this intent, we <sup>fi</sup>rst laid out an argument as to the opportunities of such approaches by drawing upon research on the motivation and behavior

While the relatively small number of existing studies might be considered a limitation of our work, this is to a large extent an inherent characteristic of every literature review into an emerging topic [57]. Due to the variety of crowdsourcing systems and the fragmented nature of corresponding research, however, we cannot rule out the possibility that further relevant research is conducted in communities that remain disconnected from those analyzed, using different keywords. To the best of our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst systematic analysis of personalized task recommendation in crowdsourcing. Based on the results of this state-of-the-art analysis and well-known recommender systems literature, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several issues for a future research agenda.
