# Self-regulated learning strategies and software training

Jane Gravill \*, Deborah Compeau <sup>1</sup>

The University of Western Ontario, London, ON, Canada

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 11 February 2007 Received in revised form 9 November 2007 Accepted 14 March 2008 Available online 16 May 2008

Keywords: Training Self-assessment Self-regulated learning IT domains

## A B S T R A C T

We investigated one aspect of the growing trend towards self-management within organizations by examining the learning processes that individuals use when learning computer software alone in a webbased training environment, and found that individuals learn in different ways. Software training in any context requires individuals to apply self-regulated learning strategies to achieve their learning objectives. Self-regulated learning strategies are the processes that they use to manage their own learning. As self-guided training gains popularity, self-regulated learning strategies become increasingly important, because the learner typically has more control of their training. We found that self-regulated learning strategies led to better outcomes in our study of managers and professionals learning to use an ERP system. We also gained insights into the most popular learning strategies. This work enhanced our understanding of user processes when learning computer software. It should assist organizations in maximizing the return from their investment in training employees and managing knowledge.

## 1. Introduction

The use of technology is embedded in most business activities today. Thus, it is important for employees to learn new skills as technology changes or new technology is introduced. Employees are increasingly expected to assess and manage their own training needs [4,14–16]. Self-management is a trend that offers benefits and needs to be better understood [21,25]. We focused on the challenges faced by employees when learning new IT. Research has provided a general perspective on the nature of self-regulated learning in educational contexts [10,13,19,23].

Web-based training is self-managed when an organization provides the software training programs to learn new skills, but the individual actively controls the process to achieve his or her objective to acquire new skills. Web-based software training is easy and inexpensive compared to traditional training methods [20]. Employees choose the best approach for learning the material and thus gain the skills they need. To manage these self-regulated learning experiences effectively, individuals make self-directed choices of the actions they will undertake or the strategies they will invoke to meet their goals. These processes are self-regulated learning strategies.

Given the trend toward self-management of web-based training, we need to understand the learning mechanisms that employees adopt in order to improve the effectiveness of such training programs [1,9,18,22,24].

## 2. Research model and hypotheses

We used the Resource Allocation Model (RAM) in our investigation. It is primarily founded on Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory of Self-Regulation [3], which expands on Social Cognitive Theory by assessing self-monitoring, self-judgment, and selfreaction. Self-monitoring is the mechanism by which individuals gain information on their own performance by setting realistic goals and evaluate their progress toward them. Self-judgment involves the processes by which individuals compare their performance or actions to the personal standards they developed in a particular domain. These standards play a key role in their activities and are formed in a variety of ways; e.g., by reactions of others to their behavior, the social sanctions of others; or direct evaluation. Self-reaction represents the activities undertaken to regulate actions, such as re-reading instructions to ensure fully understood material and by practicing a new skill.

The model focuses on self-regulation in the context of learning and shows how devoting attention toward self-management activities, or regulating a learning experience, is needed to improve learning. Thus, individuals evaluate their own effort and performance during a given task or learning goal to determine the level of resources they direct toward activities to achieve the learning objective.

Kanfer and her colleagues divided attention into three components:

1. On-task attention: the degree to which one focuses on the task at hand during training. Learners may not focus their attention on the process.

2. Off-task attention: how much individuals participate in distracting activities during training [e.g., checking email, daydreaming].

3. Attention to self-regulated learning: completing the task at hand without reflecting on how one is learning or what challenges are being encountered.

Each will independently influence the learning process. The Resource Allocation Model assumes that knowledge is directly influenced by self-regulation because it is the effectiveness of selfregulated learning strategies that determines the outcome of an individual’s learning process.

From our perspective, the most demanding stage of learning is the early phase [11]. Later in the process attentional resource demands are reduced and the speed of task accomplishment substantially increases. Understanding the successful learning strategies of individuals during the early learning phase helps organizations gain the most from their training and software investment. Most of the learning strategies involve reflective thinking during the learning process, are related to goal setting habits, or involve analytical thinking and selfmonitoring.

The RAM has been tested in the U.S. Air Force context where new recruits were responsible for learning the skills necessary to perform their duties. The nature of the model, based on individuals use of self-regulating activities, is applicable to learning in this highly self-managed environment, such as the WWW.

Fig. 1 presents our research model. It included three learning outcomes: declarative knowledge (‘‘know what’’ type), procedural knowledge (‘‘know how’’ type), and self-efficacy (confidence in ability to perform a task). These three are generally considered to be the major learning outcomes [12]. Each is expected to be influenced by one or more of the attention types. Self-regulated learning strategies are expected to influence all three outcomes. On- and off-task attention are expected to influence self-efficacy and indirectly influence the other outcomes.

Motivation-to-learn was included as a control variable because training outcomes might be influenced by an individual’s desire to learn the material in the training program and, hence, the total effort they apply to various learning activities. As a control variable, the motivation-to-learn construct was placed as an antecedent to every construct. This effectively removed its effects from other paths.

## 2.1. Self-regulated learning strategies

The RAM tells us that self-regulation requires individuals to monitor, evaluate, and control their learning activities. Individuals benefit from increased self-knowledge and feedback about their own learning process. This increased self-knowledge and feedback can improve learning outcomes. Those receiving more knowledge or feedback about their ability or learning process increase their opportunity to re-evaluate their progress and improve their skills. Self-regulation improves performance, and self-regulatory learning strategies have been found to be related to higher levels of declarative knowledge [7].

Thus, we proposed:

H1. Self-regulated learning strategies will be positively related to declarative knowledge.

H2. Self-regulated learning strategies will be positively related to procedural knowledge.

Participating in the self-regulatory processes allows individuals to increase their confidence in their abilities as they become more familiar with their performance in a domain [28]. The increase in feedback gained through self-regulatory activities increases confidence in the tasks the user is able to complete. This effect has been shown in previous studies [29]. Thus:

H3. Use of self-regulated learning strategies will be positively related to self-efficacy.

## 2.2. On- and off-task attention

We expected that greater on-task attention would be positively related to self-efficacy. As individuals allocate effort to the on-task activities involved in learning the material, their confidence about their skills increases.

H4. On-task attention will be positively related to self-efficacy.

Participating in off-task activities during learning distracts individuals and reduces the time available to practice new skills. Therefore, we expected that individuals who directed effort toward off-task attention and distracting activities would demonstrate lower levels of self-efficacy about their new skills.

H5. Off-task attention will be negatively related to self-efficacy.

## 2.3. Self-efficacy

Confidence in one’s ability has been shown to positively influence learning outcomes and performance in the computer software domain and individual’s confidence results in improvement. Thus, our final two hypotheses were:

H6. Self-efficacy will be positively related to declarative knowledge.

H7. Self-efficacy will be positively related to procedural knowledge.

2.3.1. Study 1

We conducted semi-structured interviews with 27 knowledge workers from a variety of organizations who had utilized software training in the prior year. Interviewees were asked to describe their experiences in acquiring new computer software-related skills at work; the difficulties they encountered during these experiences; how they overcame them; and how they managed their computer software learning at work. We also asked interviewees for examples of particularly successful learning experiences; what they thought contributed to this success; and for any techniques they used during their computer software-related learning. Each interview lasted 35 to 45 min, was tape-recorded, and transcribed.

## 3. Results

Interviewees were categorized into three broad roles: (i) management; (ii) professional; and (iii) technical. The majority (56%) of the interviewees were professionals. This category included participants with occupations such as Account Analyst, Learning Division Coordinator, Cost Accountant, and Marketing Representative. Managers were 33% of the interviewees; they included participants with titles such as Manager, Vice-President, or General Manager in Marketing, Human Resources, and Finance. The technical group (11%) consisted of participants with occupations such as System Administrator, Help Desk Analyst, and Senior Programmer. All interviewees were full-time employees.

Analysis of responses indicated that self-managed learning remained by far the most common way of learning to use computer software. Most interviewees indicated that 80% or more of their learning was conducted in a self-directed manner, as opposed to classroom or formal training. This phase of the study confirmed that, despite changes in the way they use computer software in their work, employees still learned primarily in a self-directed manner.

In describing their computer software learning experiences, most interviewees referred to their early activities as ‘starting up tutorials or training programs to figure out how to get the software going’, or ‘reading some of the manual to get an idea of what to do’. Interviewees commonly felt they had merely been ‘thrown software’ that they needed to learn to complete a task, and explained how they would use it to learn what was needed to finish their job. When asked how they learned, many talked about ‘learning bits or portions of the software’ to accomplish required tasks. If difficulties were encountered, people commonly asked coworkers, found online or local tutorials or training programs, used software help features, etc. Over 75% of participants said they used online training or tutorials to learn computer software at work. All participants commented that they were frequently distracted from their self-directed computer software learning experiences by email and voicemail messages or by colleagues or supervisors.

A key purpose of the interviews was to tailor the constructs in the research model to the domain of computer software training. We obtained a 15-item measure, which had been used to study self-regulated learning. We identified five additional strategies that were being used.

## 3.1. Establishing specific work task goals

Twenty-two of the 27 interviewees said that they establish specific work goals or learn to complete specific work tasks during learning. Within the transcripts, there were 55 passages where learning for specific work goals, etc. was mentioned. The following were typical comments.

I usually have something I am learning for completing a specific work task, so I am motivated to get the job done and to do so I need to learn the new skill. Like learning Excel for my additional responsibilities. I stay on track by focusing on what I need to learn, and not starting to explore too much.

When I’m learning new software first of all I have to set a work goal with a specific software, because software packages these days have so many different applications.

## 3.2. Focus on time spent learning

Twenty of the 27 interviewees mentioned time when describ ing their computer software-related learning experiences at work. Within these interviews, participants mentioned time in 58 passages. Time is clearly important, as shown in the following examples:

I always assess whether my expenditure of time is worth it.

It’s just time-management. . . especially when you’re trying to do it for work and you need to know it.

## 3.3. Note-taking

Eighteen of the 27 interviewees said that they took notes at some point during their learning experiences. There were 30 passages where participants mentioned this. They would discard the notes once they were comfortable with the new skills. An interviewee said:

I’ll constantly write something down. . . Once I internalize it, I throw it away.

We added note-taking to the existing self-regulated learning strategy measure. Interviewee comments suggested that software complexity plays a role in frequent note-taking.

## 3.4. Self-reward

Six of the 27 interviewees said that they rewarded themselves for completing tasks. Self-reward was mentioned in eight passages in these six interviews. This strategy is consistent with the Social Cognitive Theory view of self-management [8]. One interviewee described their reward strategy as:

If I need to learn something to get something done, I tell myself I can have a reward if I get it done. I’ll leave work early or buy myself that pair of pants I wanted. It may not happen, as I rarely leave work early, but it helps.

We added self-rewards for achieving learning goals as a self regulated learning measure.

## 3.5. Frustration monitoring

Ten of the 27 interviewees commented on the frustration they feel during computer software-related learning experiences. They mentioned frustration in 14 passages. Some consciously monitored their frustration level; one commented that he used frustration as a measure:

I use a frustration barometer to tell how I’m doing when I’m trying to learn new stuff. If I get too frustrated, . . .maybe I need to start that part over again.

The five additional self-regulated learning strategies are consistent with our underlying theoretical approach. The RAM explains that individuals who engage in self-regulated learning consciously allocate effort toward activities they believe will help them effectively manage their learning experience. Additional and adapted items are identified in Appendix A by an asterisk.

## 3.5.2. Measures

The self-regulated learning strategies construct was measured using 20 items. Declarative knowledge was measured using an 11item multiple-choice test. Procedural knowledge was measured using a hands-on test, where participants were asked to complete 12 exercises resulting in a single score which was used in our analyses. Both knowledge tests were included in the Oracle 11i Navigator online training program package and were based upon the range of functionality covered in the training program. The questions addressing the application interface, software features and functionality, submission of requests, printing, and user profile information. Self-efficacy was measured using 10 items [6]. The adapted on-task and off-task attention constructs were measured using six and four items, respectively. Motivation-to-learn was measured using four items [17]. Measurement items are shown in Appendix A, while motivation-to-learn items are in Appendix B. All measures other than the knowledge tests were measured using a Likert scale that ranged from 1-Strongly Disagree to 7-Strongly Agree.

## 3.5.3. Pilot study results

Two items were reworded to improve reliability. One item measuring self-awareness (#9) and one item measuring selfregulated learning strategies (#8) were slightly reworded to improve clarity.

## 3.5.4. Main study results

One hundred and forty study packages were delivered to knowledge workers in a large pharmaceutical organization; 85 were returned completed for a response rate of 60%. Most participants stated that they were professionals (65%) or management (17%). Five percent were technical, and 13% administrative, clerical, or operational. Most (56%) were male. Their average age was 35 (ranging from 21 to 60 years) and they had an average of 10 years work experience (ranging from 1 to 33 years). Most had undergraduate degrees (27%) or had some undergraduate education (60%). Ten percent had completed graduate education or had some graduate education (3%). Almost all (95%) were full-time employees.

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics of the participants. The mean declarative knowledge test score was 63% and the mean procedural knowledge score was 65%. These scores were broadly distributed, suggesting that the knowledge testing experience was sufficiently challenging to reasonably test our study model.

Table 2 shows the rank order of specific self-regulated learning strategies used by participants during the computer software training program. The prime learning strategy was ‘‘I considered whether the material would allow me to accomplish specific work tasks.’’ This suggests that learning was primarily conducted to achieve specific work tasks or objectives, and that aligning learning to these tasks was a key focus of self-regulated learners. The second strategy was note-taking. Employees took notes to help them learn and remember new skills. The third strategy was in deciding what skills needed the most practice during training. Participants were less likely to use frustration monitoring, self-reward, or selfadministered questions.

3.5.4.1. Assessment of the research model. We tested our model using the Partial Least Squares (Version 3.00 Build 1017) analytical method, estimating the measurement and structural models simultaneously.

Four construct measures (self-regulated learning strategies, offtask attention, self-efficacy, and motivation-to-learn) met reliability and discriminant validity criteria in the initial model with internal consistency reliabilities greater than 0.7. All constructs were more strongly correlated with their own measures than with any of other construct, demonstrating discriminant validity. No changes were made to these four measures, because all satisfied reliability and discriminant criteria, with the exception of one offtask attention item that had a loading of 0.69. We did not assess the validity of the procedural knowledge measure because it consisted of a single percentage score for each participant. However, there were measurement problems with declarative knowledge and ontask attention. We reviewed the measurement items and dropped several from the final test of the research model; four questions were removed from the declarative knowledge test, leaving 11 items. One item was removed from the on-task attention measure leaving a five-item measure.

Two additional items in the on-task attention measure had low loadings. Research applying the RAM was based on U.S. Air Cadets performing flight maneuvering tactics and it was largely performance-oriented [to correctly perform the flight maneuver]. In contrast, our work was learning-oriented with a performance element (to learn to use the software package rather than to perform a specific task). We conducted a factor analysis to better understand the underlying problem of the on-task attention measure. Two factors were extracted, explaining 81% of the variance in the items. Table 3 shows the rotated factor solution.

We reviewed the on-task attention factor analysis and split the on-task attention measure into two sub-dimensions: learn and ontask. The on-task attention measure was originally intended to capture individual attention to both learning and performing new skills. As both were of interest, both sub-dimensions were included for the remainder of the study. Both dimensions were expected to demonstrate a positive relationship with self-efficacy. The factor analysis indicated that on-task attention item #5 demonstrated a cross-loading. However, since the loading on one factor (perform) was significantly higher than on the other (learn), the item was retained to test on-task attention in the context of the nomological net.

The changes were incorporated into the revised research model, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Hypothesis 4 was split into two parts to accommodate the two on-task attention dimensions of learn and perform. The revised Hypothesis 4 was:

H4a. On-task attention: Learn will be positively related to selfefficacy.

H4b. On-task attention: Perform will be positively related to self efficacy.

The internal consistency reliabilities in the revised model was greater than 0.7 and all constructs were more strongly correlated with their own measures than with any of the other constructs. Appendix A provides the loadings of each of the items on each of the constructs. Individual item loadings for all constructs in the revised model were above 0.70, excluding two on-task attention:- learn items. While these two items were below 0.70, the internal consistency reliability for on-task attention:learn was above 0.70 so the items were not dropped. Revising the measures for declarative knowledge and on-task attention improved their loading. Having made these changes to the measurement model, we examined the structural model.

## 4. Discussion

Overall, our research supported the argument that individuals use a variety of self-regulated learning strategies when learning computer software at work. The use of self-regulated learning strategies enhanced all three learning outcomes in the online computer software training context of our study.

## 4.1. Study 1: interviews

Analyzing the interview data indicated that interviewees primarily conducted their computer software learning in a selfregulated manner with thought toward specific work tasks or timelines and clear objectives that needed to be met. Individuals tended to approach their self-directed experiences differently. Many turned to online tutorials or software training, co-workers, or their organization’s technical support desk to meet their software training needs. This involved learning by usage [5]. Another learned via usage in combination with training, while still another preferred to read related instructional material before using the software. This suggested that individuals should adopt the learning process that works best. Firms should also realize that individuals learn in different ways, and that providing a variety of methods is important. It is therefore important to design and organize software training programs so that employees have clear roadmaps identifying specific deliverables for each module of a training program.

## 4.2. Study 2: field test

## 4.2.1. Self-regulated learning strategie

We found a strong positive relationship between the effective use of on-line learning strategies and the three outcomes (declarative and procedural knowledge, and self-efficacy). Moreover, individuals use different strategies to manage their learning experience.

Self-regulated strategies had a stronger relationship with declarative than procedural knowledge. Early in the learning process, where declarative knowledge develops, cognitive resources are in high demand because individuals are faced with a new domain where they have to learn initial knowledge. Later, procedural knowledge develops, and individuals may have fewer resource constraints.

We recommend that organizations encourage employees to set specific work task goals prior to engaging in software learning. Online training programs should be designed to promote this specific goal setting strategy. Conducting this exercise prior to training may help employees ensure they are embarking on the appropriate training module for their specific objective.

## 4.2.2. On-task attention

Splitting on-task attention into two dimensions provided useful insight into the relationship between individuals’ on-task attention and other constructs in the study as the two dimensions related differently. On-task attention: perform was significantly related to self-efficacy while on-task attention: learn was not. Findings indicated that individuals who focused their on-task attention on performing their new skills and correctly performing steps in the training program increased their self-efficacy. This finding is consistent with the theoretical premise that practice in a particular domain increases confidence in that domain [3]. Item descriptors for the on-task:perform construct such as ‘performing steps in the program correctly’ are essentially the same as practicing the new skill. The item descriptors for the on-task:learn dimension are much less related to practicing a new skill and are more associated with learning tasks such as ‘reading the training material’ or ‘learning specific tasks’. This finding suggests that the significant positive relationship between on-task:perform and self-efficacy is a result of the close association between ontask:perform and practice activities.

## 4.2.3. Off-task attention

Off-task attention demonstrated a significant relationship with self-efficacy. This supported the notion that those who were distracted during learning did not have the opportunity to gain confidence in their abilities.

## 4.2.4. Self-efficacy

This demonstrated a positive relationship with both declarative knowledge and procedural knowledge. The strong relationship between self-efficacy and other learning outcomes indicated that confidence in ability played a key role in the learning process, and was an important outcome. The important role of self-efficacy was also evident in our interview analysis. Interviewees commented on their confidence in their computer software ability, and its influence on their learning.

Confidence clearly played an important role in the computer software learning process. Individuals reported that it influenced aspects of their learning, such as persistence, and desire to stay focused on the task. The interview data also supported the idea that individuals’ confidence was influenced by their computer learning experience. If participants were not confident, they had difficulty focusing on the learning task or they avoided learning altogether.

## 4.3. Implications for management

We recommend that organizations promote self-regulated learning strategies by drawing employees’ attention to the benefits of monitoring, evaluating, and managing their own learning experience. Facilitating and promoting an adaptable learning environment is key. We found that employees learn on their own in different ways. We also recommend that training software programs incorporate tools that support various self-regulated learning strategies. For example, including skills assessment tools at various stages will assist individuals to determine which skills require more practice, or where to focus learning efforts.

## 4.4. Implications for research

We identified the self-regulated learning strategies used in the online computer software training context and showed that they positively influenced learning outcomes. This study demonstrated that the RAM was applicable to the computer software training context by using an expanded self-regulated learning measure to examine the particular learning mechanisms involved. Based upon the qualitative interviews, all study measures were adapted to the organizational context. Increasingly, research is conducted within the organizational environment as research continues to uncover the important differences in results based on student and employee samples.

## 4.5. Limitations

Although the field study approach implemented here provided external validity, it also reduced the level of control we had over the study. We believed that the disadvantages of a field study were outweighed by the benefits of having employees participate in training in a real work situation. To maintain as much control as possible, the study packages included clear instructions about study procedures and contact information for any questions or concerns about the study.

Another limitation was in its volunteer basis: subjects voluntarily participated in the study but the training was mandatory. Finally, while the sample size of 85 respondents was adequate for PLS analysis, it was relatively small.

## 5. Conclusion

Our study was designed to gain a better understanding of employee learning computer software at work. Employees are increasingly responsible for evaluating their own learning needs. Yet, research indicates that individuals have difficulty in managing or regulating their own activities [2]. Our study showed that selfregulated learning strategies influenced learning outcomes and that individuals use different learning strategies to meet their own needs. The findings helped us better understand one aspect of selfmanagement within organizations and should benefit organizations that want to improve their return on software and training investments by suggesting appropriate working conditions conducive to effective self-regulated learning, and by suggesting design alternatives for online software training programs.

## Appendix BMotivation-to-learn control variable

This measure [17] captures individuals’ motivation to learn the material covered in the training program. It is measured using a 7- point scale that ranges from 1-Strongly Disagree, to 7-Strongly

Agree. This measure has been validated and shown to be reliable in several studies [26,27]:

(1) I am very much interested in taking this training class.

(2) I am excited about learning the skills that will be covered in this training class.

(3) I will try to learn as much as I can from this training class.

(4) I am motivated to learn the training material in this class.
