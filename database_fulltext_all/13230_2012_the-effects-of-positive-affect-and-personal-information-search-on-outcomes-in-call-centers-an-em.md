# The effects of positive affect and personal information search on outcomes in call centers: An empirical study

Lorna Doucet <sup>a,1</sup>, Sherry M.B. Thatcher <sup>b,2</sup>, Matt E. Thatcher <sup>c,</sup>⁎

<sup>a</sup> Management, China Europe International Business School, 699 Hongfeng Rd., Pudong, Shanghai 201206, PR China

<sup>b</sup> Management and Entrepreneurship, University of Louisville, 2301 S. 3rd St., Louisville, KY 40292, United States

<sup>c</sup> Computer Information Systems, University of Louisville, 2301 S. 3rd St., Louisville, KY 40292, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Keywords: Information search Personal information source Positive affect Call center Productivity Job satisfaction

## a b s t r a c t

Despite the increasing availability of computer-based information sources within organizations, employees continue to rely on other people as important sources of information. However, relatively little is known about the relations among positive affect, personal information search, and related outcomes — especially in organizations. Using data from 276 service representatives in one organization we <sup>fi</sup>nd that positive affect is positively related to the likelihood of searching for information through personal sources. Moreover, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the relations between the likelihood of searching for information via personal sources and work out comes, such as productivity and job satisfaction, are moderated by positive affect. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that positive affect negatively moderates the relations between personal information search and productivity, but positively moderates the relations between personal information search and job satisfaction. As we will discuss, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings not only contribute to the IT and organizational behavior literatures but also have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications for practitioners.

## 1. Introduction

In today's increasingly information-based economy managers must identify the factors that contribute to the effective transfer and use of information within organizations [2,69]. In many organizations employees have access to a diverse set of information sources when completing tasks and solving problems. For example, employees may seek and acquire information from a variety of personal information sources (i.e., sources that require direct contact with people) such as co-workers, supervisors, job coaches, and people outside of the organization. Alternatively, employees may seek and acquire information from a variety of impersonal information sources (i.e., sources that do not require contact with people) such as corporate manuals, print media (newspapers, trade publications), the Internet, and knowledge management systems. Recent research has focused on the development, management, and use of computer-based, impersonal information sources within organizations [4]. However, despite the growing availability of such computer-based sources, employees sometimes resist their use and often continue to rely on other people as important sources of information [19,48,69]. In other words, employees have different means of, and preferences for, searching for information [58]. In order to improve organizational effectiveness and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, managers must understand how individual differences among employees affect their information search behaviors and, hence, related work outcomes.

Positive affect has been found to be important in the workplace. For example, positive affect has been found to have a bene<sup>fi</sup>cial effect on performance evaluations, pay, and social support received from supervisors and coworkers [60]. Positive affect has also been shown to have an important effect on micro-processes associated with important work outcomes [39,47]. For example, in laboratory studies, researchers have found that positive affect facilitates ef<sup>fi</sup>cient decision processes [42], creative problem solving [41], and extensive information search [60]. Finally, in another set of laboratory studies in the IT literature, researchers have found that positive mood affects the user acceptance of [21,23], user intention to use [22], and effective usage of [20] various decision support systems. However, the role that positive affect plays in affecting information search behaviors and related work outcomes in the context of information-intensive organizations is not well-documented. An important contribution of our work is the examination of the relations among positive affect, information search behaviors, and employee performance in a <sup>fi</sup>eld setting.

Previous work in the IT literature has particularly focused on factors that drive the adoption and use of computer-based information sources, such as databases, knowledge management systems, executive information systems, and the Internet. These factors include task characteristics such as task complexity and task uncertainty [4,9], information source characteristics such as quality, accessibility, usefulness, and ease of use [4,11,28,37,69], and information-seeker characteristics such as age, profession, knowledge of the search domain [11,37], and level of positive affect [20–23].

Although organizations have become increasingly reliant on impersonal information sources through the use of technology, employees do not always use the technology or search aids provided to them by their organization [49]. In fact, Zimmer et al. [69] found that an individual's choice of information source (personal versus impersonal) depends critically on the accessibility and quality of each information source. They further noted that, “While the focus [in the IT literature] has been squarely on technology-mediated information sources, other research shows that, in some contexts, information seekers prefer to consult other individuals…” [p. 299]. Thus, it is important to understand the factors that drive the use of personal information sources and the implications of using such sources even when employers heavily promote the use of impersonal information sources.

In this paper we examine how positive affect impacts an information seeker's search behaviors in organizational contexts. By exploring the relations among positive affect, personal information search, and important work outcomes, our paper begins to address the gap in the literature on information search behavior. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our work examines how an information seeker's level of positive affect drives the intensity with which that individual uses personal information sources and how positive affect moderates the impact of personal information search intensity on work outcomes such as productivity and job satisfaction.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses

Fig. 1 presents a graphical illustration of the model guiding our research. Below, we discuss the theoretical development of this model.

## 2.1. Positive affect

Affect has been de<sup>fi</sup>ned in a number of ways [8,46]. We consider affect to be a broad term that encompasses moods, emotions, and feelings [23,29]. Positive affect refers to a related set of positively valenced and highly energized affective experiences, which include joviality, self-assurance and attentiveness [65]. In this paper, we refer to low positive affect as negatively valenced, low energy affective experiences, including sadness and lethargy [65]. Other researchers contrast positive affect (joviality) with neutral states [39,46].<sup>3</sup> Moods are less intense, more pervasive and enduring than emotions and feelings — and do not necessarily have a speci<sup>fi</sup>c cause [23,29]. In this paper, we focus on mood because it is more relevant to our research setting. Hence, although we use the term “affect” in this paper — we are referring to enduring mood as opposed to <sup>fl</sup>eeting emotion.

Affect can be conceptualized as a temporary state or as a stable individual difference — usually referred to as trait positive affect. Trait positive affect is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an enduring disposition to experience positive, energized emotions across various situations [63]. In this paper, we use the term positive affect to include both state positive affect and trait positive affect as trait positive affect is conceptually and empirically related to state positive affect [52].

Positive affect has been shown to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on work behaviors and attitudes [39,47]. For example, people who are experimentally induced to experience high positive affect are likely to rate their job satisfaction higher than people who are not so induced [6]. Research has also demonstrated that positive affect has an effect on decision making [38]. For example, Elsbach and Barr [26] found that people experimentally induced to experience a positive mood were much less likely to use a structured decision protocol than people experimentally induced to experience a negative mood. Staw and Barsade [59] found that positive affect enhances decision quality. Positive affect has also been shown to enhance creativity [27,48] and helping behaviors [29]. Finally, recent work in the IT literature has used laboratory experiments to examine the impact of positive mood on the acceptance and use of decision support systems (DSS) [20–23]. This work found that decision makers with a positive mood are more likely to accept and use a DSS that supports uncertain tasks, are more likely to use a greater number of informational cues to solve problems, and are more likely to make accurate judgments.

## 2.2. Information search behaviors

In our study, we focus on the relations between positive affect and information search behaviors. Studies of information search have examined both intensity of search behaviors [25,30] and information source utilization [51]. Information sources have been characterized by a variety of dimensions: format (e.g., oral vs. documentary; textual vs. audio-visual/multimedia; paper-based vs. electronic), status (e.g., personal vs. impersonal, formal vs. informal, published vs. unpublished), and location (e.g., internal vs. external) [43]. One of the most useful dimensions for characterizing information sources is the personal–impersonal dimension [8,43,69]. As mentioned earlier, information derived from personal sources is obtained from a person (e.g., organizational colleagues, family members, or external professional contacts) while information available from impersonal sources is stored in forms (e.g., formal reports, corporate manuals, print media, and the output of databases and knowledge management systems) that can be disseminated to many individuals [43,51,69].

Organizations have become increasingly reliant on impersonal information sources through the use of technology [4,9,11,28,37,69]. However, despite the growing availability of such computer-based sources, employees still continue to rely on other people as important sources of information [19,49,69]. Several studies have documented reasons why employees do not restrict themselves to impersonal information sources. For example, Glover et al. [31] found that individuals bypass impersonal information search tools (and use alternatives search methods such as personal sources) because these tools inhibit their learning experience. Whitecotton et al. [66] found that total reliance on an impersonal information search tool is inadequate when it is not possible to capture all relevant information with that tool. Boatsman et al. [5] found that individuals who compete with others who use a similar information tool may feel the need to use an alternative search method to obtain a competitive advantage. Finally, Zimmer et al. [69] note that while the IT literature has focused attention on the development, implementation, and use of impersonal information sources, individuals within organizations continue to make extensive use of personal sources. Thus, it is important to understand the implications of using personal information sources even when employers heavily promote the use of impersonal information sources. Therefore, we limit this study to understanding the relationship of positive affect on personal information search and the resulting outcomes. For the purposes of our paper, we use the term personal information search to describe an individual's behavior whereby she seeks answers or information from a person, such as a superior, a peer, or an organizational colleague (who may or may not be housed in the same department).

Some research has found that the use of personal information sources is associated with positive outcomes such as: the receipt of useful information [44], receipt of actionable knowledge [16], individual performance ratings [15], and a decrease in project completion time [35]. However, other research shows that use of personal information sources is perceived to yield positive outcomes in particular situations — such as later in a decision process for industrial buyers [50]; foreign investment decisions [7]; and younger manufacturing <sup>fi</sup>rms faced with strategic uncertainty [51].

In summary, although organizations increasingly rely on computerbased impersonal information sources, employees continue to rely on other people as sources of information. Given the dearth of research on the drivers and impacts of personal information search in the IT literature, we focus our work on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of an individual's level of positive affect on her use of personal information sources and the impact of such use on work outcomes.

## 2.3. Positive affect and information search behaviors

There are multiple mechanisms that are likely to link positive affect to personal information search. In the following section, we offer three key mechanisms: cognitive processing style, personal values related to stimulation, and preference for socializing.

First, previous research has shown that positive affect is positively related to a cognitive processing style characterized by use of a wide variety of information sources [38]. For example, in a managerial simulation [59] groups of subjects high in positive affect processed the information provided in the scenario, and subsequently requested additional information. Groups that were low in positive affect were less likely to request additional information, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the relative narrowness of their information search. Similarly, in a laboratory experiment Djamasbi [20] found that people high in positive affect were more likely than those low in positive affect to request informational cues from a decision support system when solving a problem. Other research has shown that subjects high in positive affect use a wider variety of categories for sorting information [40]. Hence, we expect people high in positive affect to be more likely to engage in personal information search than people low in positive affect.

Second, positive affect has been positively associated with valuing stimulation or “an exciting life” [24,56]. Hackman and Oldham [33] found that employees may <sup>fi</sup>nd stimulation in work that requires them to use a variety of skills. Since personal information search requires a wide variety of skills – including skills in persuasion [12], negotiation [45], and emotional intelligence [10] – we expect that employees high in positive affect will <sup>fi</sup>nd work involving extensive personal information search to be more stimulating than work that does not. Furthermore, Wrzesniewski and Dutton [67] found that employees actively mold their job characteristics to suit their individual needs. As such, we expect that, when given the opportunity, employees high in positive affect will choose to engage in more personal information search activities than employees low in positive affect.

Third, positive affect has been found to be associated with increased socializing [63]. This may occur for a variety of reasons. First, positive affect is positively associated with interpersonal attractiveness [32,60]. The attractiveness of a high positive affect person serves as an inducement to others to engage in frequent and prolonged social interactions with that person [60]. Second, people high in positive affect have more positive evaluations than people low in positive affect; such positive evaluations lead high positive affect people to be more attracted to others [32]. This increased attraction to others provides an inducement for high positive affect people to engage in frequent and prolonged social interactions with others [3]. Given that personal information search provides an opportunity for socializing, we argue that people high in positive affect should be more likely to use personal information search than people low in positive affect.

Based on this discussion of the impact of positive affect on an individual's cognitive processing style, value for stimulation, and value for socializing, we present the following hypothesis:

H1. Positive affect will be positively related to personal information search intensity.

## 2.4. Personal information search and work outcomes

## 2.4.1. Productivity

The performance of individual workers can be characterized in terms of the quantity and the quality of the work produced. The <sup>fi</sup>rst outcome we consider is the quantity of the output produced by an employee. We use the term productivity to refer to the quantity of work produced by an employee in a given time period. An employee's productivity is a function of the speed with which she gathers information necessary to complete her work. This includes the speed with which the employee communicates with others who have relevant information. Personal information sources may affect productivity in two distinct ways: (i) they can act as a very fast/ef<sup>fi</sup>cient source of information [43], and (ii) they can provide an opportunity for unproductive socializing [17].

Individuals who use personal information sources may be more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient because they can engage in two-way communication with the information source. Two-way communication, or increased interactivity, should facilitate the searcher's ability to explain what type of information she requires, which should then allow the information source to provide a response in a timely fashion. This is particularly important when the nature of the request is ambiguous or equivocal [17,18]. In general, clari<sup>fi</sup>cation of ambiguous issues is best achieved via rich communications. Rich communications are most likely to succeed via rich media, such as face-to-face or telephone contact [18] (e.g., personal information sources). In addition, in contrast to lean, one-way communication channels (e.g., databases), personal sources do not require the searcher to construct and execute an elaborate search routine to <sup>fi</sup>nd information. That is, personal information search does not require the information seeker to spend the time and resources to acquire any specialized search capabilities.

Personal sources of information also provide an opportunity for socializing [17]. Roy [57] describes how employees regularly engage in impromptu social interactions regarding both serious personal issues, such as advice on apartment hunting, and less serious issues re<sup>fl</sup>ected in horseplay and comedic exchanges. Roy argues that these interactions increase job satisfaction by compensating for dull, routine work. These interactions tend to occur in face-to-face interactions, with colleagues who are physically nearby, or with colleagues who interact with each other on work-related matters [62].

To the extent that personal information sources are used for their ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency properties, use of these sources should improve productivity as they are likely to reduce information search time. To the extent that personal sources are used for socializing, use of such sources should decrease productivity as time is spent on non-task related activities, thereby slowing the collection of information.

Whether or not the potential for high productivity is realized with the use of personal sources is likely to depend on the positive affectivity of the person engaged in information search. As argued earlier, personality should in<sup>fl</sup>uence the extent to which a given individual is likely to use personal sources for socializing. People high in positive affect are more likely to enjoy, and engage in, socializing behavior [63]. Thus, the use of personal information sources for socializing is associated with a decrease in productivity. Based on this discussion we present the following hypothesis:

H2. The relation between personal information search and productivity is moderated by positive affect, such that at high levels of positive affect, personal information search intensity is negatively related to productivity and at low levels of positive affect personal information search intensity is positively related to productivity.

## 2.4.2. Quality of work

For our purposes, quality of work is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as conformance to speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. For example, if service interactions are designed to provide correct answers to customers, then service interactions that provide customers accurate information are of higher quality than service interactions that provide customers inaccurate information. The designs or speci<sup>fi</sup>cations for a given product or service are typically established by management [55], yet ideally re<sup>fl</sup>ect both customer preferences and organizational interests. In our context we consider quality work to be work that adheres to standards set by management.

The use of personal information sources allows for rich communications in terms of framing a question/request and receiving a customized response. That is, by using the rich communication media associated with personal information search, an employee searching for information can communicate the subtleties of the question(s) being asked, and hence, is more likely to receive responses that conform to predetermined speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. Therefore, the employee is more likely to produce work of high quality. O'Reilly [53] provides some empirical support for this prediction. In a study of workers in a county welfare agency, he <sup>fi</sup>nds that a worker's reliance on a supervisor as an information source is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and negatively associated with errors — where errors may be interpreted as outcomes that do not conform to speci<sup>fi</sup>cations.

However, reliance on personal information sources for knowledge poses some inherent risks. For example, personal sources may misunderstand the question and therefore provide inappropriate information (that does not conform to speci<sup>fi</sup>cations). Also, given that supervisors, coworkers, and others in a workplace typically have their own task demands – separate from those of the employee requesting information – they may have limited cognitive resources to devote to helping the employee [68]. Due to such limitations, individuals from whom information is being requested may neither pay adequate attention to the question being asked nor devote adequate effort to thinking about and/or communicating an appropriate response. Finally, the use of personal information sources provides an opportunity for socializing [17,57,62] — which may also draw attention away from the work at hand. This may decrease the probability that the employee gathers appropriate information from the information source. Taken together, we expect that the potential for misunderstanding, cognitive limitations due to task demands, and opportunities for socializing may decrease the usefulness of personal information sources in obtaining information that is consistent with speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. For these reasons, we expect that the quality of work may suffer when using personal information sources.

Whether the potential for high quality responses is realized or whether the limitations of using personal information sources are exposed is likely to depend on the positive affectivity of the person engaged in information search. For people high in positive affect, we have reason to expect that increases in use of personal information sources may be negatively associated with quality of work. This expectation is based on the predication that, as argued earlier, the use of personal sources will be positively associated with time spent socializing, which will detract from an employee's ability to gather high quality information.

However, for high positive affect people, an increase in personal information search intensity may also be positively associated with quality of work. Given the positive relations between positive affect and achievement values [56] high positive affect people may be particularly motivated to derive the potential quality bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from this information source [18]. They may spend time communicating the subtleties of the problem to the information source, and pay special attention to the high quality information provided by the information source. In addition, employees high in positive affect may be particularly well suited to overcome certain disadvantages typically associated with the use of personal information sources. That is, given that people high in positive affect are more socially oriented, and hence, may be more socially skilled, they may be better able to avoid miscommunications and non-cooperation from information sources [3]. Hence, a valuable personal information source, such as a supervisor, is more likely to enjoy interacting with a high positive affect employee. The information source's desire to interact with the high positive affect employees may motivate the source to assign more cognitive resources to the employee's information request. This leads to the prediction that the relations between personal information search and quality of work may be more strongly positive for people high in positive affect than for people low in positive affect.

Since we predict that positive affect is positively associated with both the costs of socializing associated with personal information sources and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of rich communication media, we offer the following competing hypotheses.

H3a. The relation between personal information search and quality of work is moderated by positive affect, such that at high levels of positive affect, personal information search intensity is negatively related to quality of work and at low levels of positive affect, personal information search is positively related to quality of work.

H3b. The relation between personal information search and quality of work is moderated by positive affect, such that at high levels of positive affect, personal information search intensity is positively related to quality of work and at low levels of positive affect, personal information search is negatively related to quality of work.

## 2.4.3. Job satisfaction

We predict that the relations between personal information search intensity and job satisfaction will be moderated by positive affect. As we argued earlier, people high in positive affect are likely to be affected by a desire for stimulation [24] and have a propensity to enjoy and engage in socializing [63]. Hence, for high positive affect people, increases in personal information search should be positively related to job satisfaction, to the extent that utilization of personal information sources provides the expected stimulation and enjoyment. As argued earlier, the richness of personal communication may help employees obtain information that is in conformance with predetermined standards. Since high positive affect people tend to value achievement more than others [56], they will respond more favorably to the use of personal information sources than others. Also, the stimulation associated with the use of interpersonal skills [34] in gathering information from personal sources should appeal to high positive affect people. Finally, the opportunity for socializing provided by the use of personal information sources should be particularly appealing to high affect employees [63].

These relations should not hold for low positive affect people, as they do not tend to place as much value on the opportunities for achievement, stimulation, and socializing associated with personal information search. Hence, we expect positive affect to moderate the relations between personal information search and job satisfaction in the following manner.

H4. The relation between personal information search and job satisfaction is moderated by positive affect, such that at high levels of positive affect, personal information search intensity is positively related to job satisfaction and at low levels of positive affect personal information search intensity is negatively related to job satisfaction.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Sample, setting, and procedures

The site for this research was the telephone service center (i.e., call center) for a large retail bank. The telephone center was located in a major metropolis in the southern United States. There were two separate data sources used for the study: (i) surveys of individual differences, information search behavior, and job perceptions and (ii) archival data on individual employee productivity and service quality. Employees who worked as service representatives (dealing directly with customers) were invited to participate in the study during regular working hours. The information search behaviors of these employees were of particular interest to the organization.

The typical service interactions in this setting consist of bank customers contacting the call center for a variety of services. The most common types of service requests include account balance inquiries, account transaction inquiries, and change of address requests. Service interactions typically last approximately two minutes, and range from under one minute in duration to over 20 min, depending on the complexity of the service request. This environment is particularly well-suited to the study of information seeking behaviors because the nature of the job is to provide a wide variety of information to a wide range of customers.

Initial invitations to participate in the study were administered by the bank's scheduling department. The scheduling department randomly selected employees for participation in the study and informed their supervisors of the employees' scheduled participation time and location. This type of scheduling is very common in this organization. The scheduling department schedules meals, breaks, vacations, and training sessions for the entire call center. As such, supervisors are accustomed to releasing employees upon demand by the scheduling department. Once employees arrived at the survey administration site, they were informed that participation was completely voluntary and that they had the choice to return to their regular work. No employees withdrew from the study. Surveys were completed in a meeting room with one of the authors present to answer questions and collect the completed surveys. 286 employees completed the survey. However, due to missing data in either the survey or the archival data, the sample size for analysis ranges from 129 to 206.

The organization compiled a dataset including the performance statistics of the employees involved in the study. These data were compiled for the two-month period following the survey administration. The organization provided this dataset to the authors.

## 3.2. Measures

## 3.2.1. Productivity

This measures the average time (in seconds) it took the employee to complete a service interaction. This average covers all service interactions by the employee over a two-month period, immediately following the administration of the survey. These data are automatically compiled by the organization's computer monitoring system and are typically referred to as “average call times.” Since high call times are associated with lower productivity, we multiplied each employee's average call time by −1, in order to transform lower call times into higher productivity scores and higher call times into lower productivity scores.

## 3.2.2. Quality of work

This measures the average service quality achieved by the employee. This average covers ten randomly selected service interactions over a two-month period following the administration of the survey. Each of the ten randomly selected service interactions was tape recorded and rated by a service quality expert within the <sup>fi</sup>rm. The ratings evaluate whether the service provided by the employee generally conformed to the speci<sup>fi</sup>cations set by the organization. Each interaction was rated as one for did conform and zero for did not conform. The relevant speci<sup>fi</sup>cations included elements of interpersonal style and technical accuracy. These data are collected and compiled every month by the quality assurance department of this organization. These data were used for evaluating the average quality of work produced by the employees.

## 3.2.3. Job satisfaction

This measures the degree to which the service representative is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with aspects of her job. This was measured using the 11-item job satisfaction scale created by Hackman and Oldham [33]. The scale consists of four items measuring growth satisfaction, two items measuring satisfaction with job security, two items measuring satisfaction with compensation, three items measuring satisfaction with coworkers, and three items measuring satisfaction with supervisors. The response scale was a seven-point scale with 1 = extremely dissatisfied and 7 = extremely satisfied. The mean of the 11 items was used for this measure and the Cronbach's alpha for the job satisfaction scale was .90.

## 3.2.4. Positive affect

This measures the general positive affect of the service representative using three items from the well-being scale of the Multidimensional Personality Questionnaire (MPQ) [61]. The three items that were used are: Most days I have moments of real fun or joy; Everyday I do some things that are fun; and I usually find ways to liven up my day. The response scale used was a seven-point scale, where 1 = strongly disagree and 7 = strongly agree. The mean of the three items was used for the measure and the Cronbach alpha for the positive affect scale was .75.

## 3.2.5. Personal information search intensity

This measures the likelihood that a service representative will use an information source that requires social contact when serving a customer. First, a list of potential information sources that require social contact was compiled in cooperation with key informants from the organizations. The resulting list included the following personal information sources: job coach; help desk; other representatives in the same work group; other departments; branches; special processing unit; and supervisor. For each of these information sources, respondents were asked the following question: How likely is it that you would use [insert specific information source here] when serving a customer? The response scale consisted of seven responses, ranging from 1 = very unlikely to 7 = very likely. The mean of these responses was used for this measure. Although the means for each individual information source were not used in our analyses, they are presented here to provide insight into the general work context. The means and standard deviations for the individual information source intensities, presented in descending order, are: other representatives in same workgroup (mean=6.62, sd=1.46); help desk (mean=4.99, sd=1.84); other departments (mean=4.55, sd=1.84); supervisor (mean=4.19, sd=1.86), job coach (mean=3.73; sd=2.02); branches (or other representatives outside the workgroup) (mean=3.20, sd=1.94); and special processing unit (mean=3.09, sd=2.02).

## 3.2.6. Control variables

In this study we controlled for gender, age, organizational tenure, education, and work unit. Previous research has shown that gender may be related to positive affect – at least in the case of clinical depression – with women reporting more clinical depression than men [63]. Age, organizational tenure, and education are expected to be related to information search behavior as previous research has found that experts (those with more knowledge) are less likely to engage in personal information search [36]. Finally we control for job category (online banking, service line, or specialty cues) to ensure that potentially unobserved aspects of the job categories did not in<sup>fl</sup>uence our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Gender was measured via survey responses. Male respondents are coded as one, female respondents are coded as two. Organizational tenure was measured via survey responses. Respondents were asked to report the month, day, and year when they started working for the organization. These responses were converted into years by calculating the years elapsed between the date when they started working for the organization and the date they completed the survey. Age was measured via survey responses where respondents were asked to indicate their date of birth. This information was transformed into age (in years) at the time of the survey administration. Education was measured on the survey using a seven-item format. Education was measured as follows: 1 = some high school; 2 = high school graduate; 3 = some college; 4 = college graduate; 5 = some post-graduate education; 6 = master's degree; 7 = PhD. Online banking indicates whether the employee worked in the online banking unit. Representatives in the online banking unit serve banking customers who use online banking products, such as online checking and savings accounts. These products allow customers to review account information and process transactions online. This was coded as 1 = online banking, 0 = other units (service line or specialty cues). Service line indicates whether the service representative worked in the service line unit. Representatives in the service line unit serve banking customers who use branch-based bank ing products, such as branch-based checking and savings accounts. These products allow customers to review account information and process transactions in person or over the telephone. This was measured as 1 = service line. 0 = other units (online banking or specialty cues). Specialty cues was treated as the omitted variable, and hence, not measured directly. Representatives in the specialty cues unit serve banking customers with special needs. For example, one specialty would consist of foreign language services. ## 5. Discussion

In this study we examined the relations between positive affect and the likelihood of using personal information sources when searching for information. We also examined the role of positive affect in moderating the relations between personal information search intensity and various work outcomes. Based on data from service representatives in one <sup>fi</sup>nancial services call center, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that positive affect is positively associated with the likelihood of using personal sources to <sup>fi</sup>nd information. In addition, the relations between personal information search intensity and two work outcomes – productivity and job satisfaction – were found to be moderated by positive affect. For individuals high in positive affect, personal information search intensity has a negative impact on productivity but a positive impact on job satisfaction. For individuals low in positive affect, personal information search intensity has a positive effect on productivity but a negative effect on job satisfaction. These results show that positive affect is an important factor that drives individual information search behavior and that affects important work outcomes. In addition, the results suggest that the relationships among positive affect, information search behavior, and work outcomes are more complex than originally thought.

Although our work shows that positive affect is positively associ ated with the likelihood of using personal information sources, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the utilization of such sources by high positive affect people brings a mixed blessing. First, people who are high in positive affect and who are more likely to use personal information search, report higher job satisfaction. However, these same people are also less ef<sup>fi</sup>cient when serving customers. In this sense, when the typical high positive affect person follows her natural preferences regarding information search behavior, she will be more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with her job, but less productive. This creates a dilemma whereby job satisfaction and productivity cannot be simultaneously facilitated by choices of personal information search behavior. This <sup>fi</sup>nding may indicate that, according to person–job <sup>fi</sup>t framework, that high positive affect people are not well suited for being productive in such an environment. However, according to our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, low positive affect people face a similar dilemma. Low positive affect people are less likely to use personal information sources, but when they do so they perform better to the detriment of their job satisfaction.

This leads to troublesome managerial implications. It appears that managers may need to choose between maximizing productivity and maximizing job satisfaction. However, it is also possible that managers may look for other ways to compensate employees for searching for information in ways that are not natural or preferred. One limitation with this implication is that it requires managing high and low positive affect service representatives differently. In order to improve productivity, managers would need to encourage low positive affect employees to use personal information sources, yet discourage high positive affect employees from using such sources. This differential treatment may cause feelings of injustice, which might cause a variety of problems [14].

Alternatively, managers could focus on the underlying mechanisms responsible for our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. For example, to the extent that high positive affect employees who use personal information sources are less productive because they are socializing while searching, managers could look for ways to decrease such behavior directly, as opposed to urging high positive affect employees to refrain from using personal information sources. In this case, all employees would be treated the same – that is – all employees would be discouraged from socializing while searching for information. However, the extent to which this is practical is an open question. Furthermore, this would not help the low positive affect service representatives (who prefer to avoid personal information sources) to improve their productivity. This could only be accomplished by encouraging the low positive affect employees to use personal information sources more often. An alternative is to try to de<sup>fi</sup>ne a desired intensity of personal information search and motivate that behavior by encouraging employees to adhere to the desired intensity. This will encourage employees to engage in the appropriate level of personal information search.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may indicate that one possible reason that individuals use personal information searches is to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll social interaction needs. Thus, as Cook [13] states, there may be inherent con<sup>fl</sup>icts between an organization's mandate requiring individuals to use a decision support system (especially where it is believed that search strategies maximize decision quality or accuracy) and the employee's desire to engage in search strategies using these systems. An important implication of this <sup>fi</sup>nding is that managers may want to provide scheduled times when employees can either interact with others or have time alone.

Importantly, our analysis illustrates that personal information search intensity is negatively, signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly correlated with quality of work (r = −.20, p b .01) regardless of affect. This result suggests an important cost of the use of personal information sources in this call center. Although the use of personal information sources in this call center may improve productivity for low positive affect employees and improve job satisfaction for high positive affect employees it decreases quality for both low and high affect employees.

We propose the following explanations for the negative relation between use of personal information sources and quality in this call center. First, it may be the case that the employees in the call center are not able to effectively communicate their questions to the personal sources. Second, it may be the case that employees tend to rely on personal sources for questions and tasks that are more complex and, therefore, more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to convey effectively to the personal source. Third, the cognitive limitations of the personal sources, due to other work demands on their time, may inhibit these sources from understanding the question asked by the employee or from devoting the time necessary to develop and communicate an effective, high quality response. Finally, the use of personal information sources provides an opportunity for socializing which may distract the employee and personal source from the work at hand and reduce the quality of the interaction.

Despite the negative correlation between personal information source intensity and quality, it is important to discuss the lack of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relations (as shown in the regression results) between personal information search, positive affect, and the quality of work. Closer examination of the quality scores indicates that the average quality score is 0.92, on a scale from 0.00 to 1.00. The standard deviation for the quality scores is .06. Based on discussions with managers at this organization, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that these scores are not completely accurate. Internal evaluators avoid giving low quality scores – even when they are deserved. Hence our measure of quality does not re<sup>fl</sup>ect the true underlying quality at this organization – which restricts our ability to adequately test our quality hypothesis.

## 6. Conclusions and limitations

As in all research, this study has some limitations. These data were collected from one <sup>fi</sup>nancial services call center in the southern part of the United States. We do not know if these results can be generalized beyond this setting. For example, the degree of friendliness observed in an organization is likely to vary by organization [54]. Therefore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings – which indicate that socializing may reduce the productivity of high positive affect employees engaged in personal information search – may not generalize to areas that are not as friendly and where there may be strong norms against socializing. For example, one of our interviewees said, “…everyone here is so helpful. They are very, very helpful. In New York it was different. People are not as helpful… they [my colleagues] would give you a piggy back ride over to the bathroom. They are just so nice it almost throws you for a loop.” Future research should examine the effect of regional norms regarding socializing on the relations between affectivity, information search behavior, and work outcomes.

Furthermore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may not be relevant in organizational settings with differential access to information sources. For example, if an employee has little access to personal information sources (e.g. telecommuting or isolated location), then understanding the relations we found between positive affect, personal information search, and work outcomes is of little value. For example, one of our informants explained that her personal information sources consisted of “generally those people that are close around.” So, if an employee is removed (physically or psychologically) from people these <sup>fi</sup>ndings are not likely to be helpful in explaining or improving their work outcomes. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are only relevant in settings in which personal information sources are reasonably accessible.

Another limitation of our study is our inability to establish causal relations between our variables, due to the design of the study as a naturalistic <sup>fi</sup>eld study. Although there is a time lag between the collection of our independent variables and our performance variables, we cannot de<sup>fi</sup>nitively rule out reverse causation. Experimental studies would be required to address this limitation.

Limitations aside, this study is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst to examine the complex relations between positive affect, information search behaviors, and important work outcomes in a real-world setting (as opposed to a laboratory setting). Although a single study does not provide conclusive evidence, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that previous frameworks outlining the factors that drive information search behavior and related work outcomes may be incomplete. Future frameworks and theoretical models should not overlook the importance of individual personality traits (such as positive affect) on these relationships. We hope that future research will continue to expand our understanding of the way affectivity in<sup>fl</sup>uences behaviors and outcomes, especially in the workplace.
