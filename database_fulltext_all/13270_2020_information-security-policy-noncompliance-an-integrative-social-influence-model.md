## Abstract

Despite the significant advancements made in understanding the factors that drive employees' compliance and noncompliance behaviours with information security policy (ISP), less is known about how different factors interact to impact such behaviours. Having been drawn on the social information processing theory, this research develops an integrative model that investigates how ethical work climate, beliefs, and neutralization interact to jointly explain ISP noncompliance. The model is tested via a survey of a broad cross section of employees. Neutralization, perceived cost of compliance, and perceived cost of noncompliance are found to significantly impact ISP noncompliance. Egoistic, benevolent, and principled climates are found to differentially influence neutralization and individuals' cognitive beliefs about the cost and benefit of ISP compliance versus noncompliance. Neutralization appears to be a more important moderator of the belief-noncompliance relationship than the principled climate.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

With the upsurge in data security breaches and the resultant substantial and dire losses, safeguarding information assets has become a strategic priority for many organizations (Lowry, Posey, Bennett, & Roberts, 2015). Evidenc suggests that a large proportion of data security breaches stem from end user noncompliance with information secu rity policies (ISPs) (Li, Zhang, & Sarathy, 2010; Lowry & Moody, 2015). Consequently, a rich stream of research ha been developed to investigate factors that contribute to employees' compliance and noncompliance—referred to hereinafter as (non)compliance—with ISPs. Many studies in this research stream have focused on identifying differ ent beliefs to explain ISP (non)compliance. A review of the literature indicates that the beliefs frequently examined are largely grounded in individuals' calculation of benefits and costs associated with ISP (non)compliance. Another line of this research stream draws on the neutralization theory and suggests that individuals can apply various neutralization techniques to redefine and characterize their ISP noncompliance as situationally appropriate. Neutraliza tion has been documented as an important contributor to ISP noncompliance and computer misuse because it allow individuals to rationalize their deviant action and frees them from the guilt associated with the action (Siponen & Vance, 2010; Siponen, Vance, & Willison, 2012; Willison, Warkentin, & Johnston, 2018). Other facilitators/inhibitor of ISP (non)compliance include, but are not limited to, ISP mandatoriness (Lowry & Moody, 2015), perceived information security climate (Chan, Woon, & Kankanhalli, 2005), organizational or personal norms regarding ISP (Guo, Yuan Archer, & Connelly, 2011; Herath & Rao, 2009), personal moral beliefs (Hovav & D'Arcy, 2012), and individual dispo sitions (ohnston. Warkentin. McBride. & Carter. 2016)

At the centre of the belief-focused studies is the assumption that desirable beliefs (eg, ISP compliance is benefi cial, and noncompliance is costly) motivate/prohibit ISP compliance/noncompliance, whereas undesirable beliefs (eg ISP noncompliance is beneficial, and compliance is costly) encourage/discourage noncompliance/compliance. Empiri cal research seeking to confirm this assumption has yielded inconsistent results, suggesting that important contin gencies that affect the belief-(non)compliance relationship may be missing from the literature (Cram, Proudfoot, & D'Arcy, 2017; D'Arcy & Herath, 2011). Potential contingencies that may intervene to complicate the belief-(non) compliance relationship include both the external work environment and internal cognitive dynamics. In this research, we focus on the ethical work climate and neutralization

Defined as the prevailing perceptions of institutionalized values, policies, and reward systems that delineate what is considered right (Victor & Cullen, 1988), ethical work climate captures an organization's overall goals and prioritie along with the ethical criteria for the appropriate ways to weigh competing goals, choose priorities, and resolve trade-offs. An ethical climate that prioritizes rule and law compliance would overlap with the organization's ISPs to provide congruent norms and expectations for ISP compliance. But when the goals and priorities endorsed by an eth ical climate contradict the requirements of ISPs, even employees with pro-ISP beliefs may feel pressured to violat the ISPs in an effort to ensure that organizational goals and priorities are achieved. In this sense, the possible pres sure stemming from the ethical work climate may emerge as a moderating factor, rendering the salient beliefs imma terial to employees' (non)compliance decision making.

Nonetheless, conceptualizing ethical work climate as a moderator tells only part of the story. The ISP literature has also acknowledged the potential conflicts between ISPs and other organizational priorities and values (Herath 8 Rao, 2009; Post & Kagan, 2007). For example, an employee may face the choice between taking a company laptop home without authorization to complete work on time and complying with the ISPs but missing the designated dead line. Or an employee may need to choose between giving unauthorized data/computer access to a close coworke to help that person meet performance goals and declining to give such access while knowing that the coworker may not meet the goals. Such predicaments inherently dictate that employees seek clarity on appropriate behaviour from relevant social references indicative of the organization's goals and priorities. By providing guidance on which interests are held in higher esteem and are rewarded when dealing with trade-offs and conflicts, ethical work climate may influence ISP (non)compliance in ways that extend beyond the moderating role stipulated above.

Further, the increasing prevalence of conflicting work demands in today's organizations presents employees with both convenient excuses and a strong need for the use of neutralization to justify noncompliance behaviours. Violat ing organizational ISPs by taking a company laptop home to use, for instance, may feel easily justifiable if the actio results from the pressure to complete work before a designated deadline. Nevertheless, we contend here that th pressure to prioritize other work demands over ISPs does not take away emplovees' sense of responsibility and guil: when they choose to violate ISPs. The existence of conflicts and trade-offs may, on the contrary, make employee aware of alternative courses of action and require them to exercise volitional and purposeful choices about whic demand to prioritize, satisfy, or neglect. The volitional and purposeful nature of this choice increases the sense of responsibility and subsequently the sense of psychological distress, confronting employees with a strong need to neutralize such uncomfortable feelings by explaining away their wrongdoings (Tsang, 2002). Neutralization allows employees, even those with the prerequisite pro-ISP beliefs, to violate the ISPs but still preserve the positive sens of self that comes with having done the right thing. Therefore, in addition to its widely identified role of being a direct driver of ISP noncompliance, neutralization may serve, together with the ethical work climate. as a moderato of the belief-noncompliance relationship

Currently, most ISP (non)compliance studies focus on personal ethics rather than on ethical work climate, and the interaction between neutralization and salient beliefs remains largely unexplored. Extant studies that examin the impacts of beliefs, neutralization, and work climate on ISP (non)compliance have drawn on distinct conceptua underpinnings and hitherto remained mostly independent of each other. Further, the antecedents of neutralizatio and beliefs and the contingencies in the beliefs-(non)compliance relationship remain poorly understood, limiting organizations' abilities to devise effective interventions to nurture and leverage favourable beliefs and to inhibit neutralization and undesirable beliefs

To address these important voids in the literature, this research draws on the social information processing the ory (SIPT) and proposes an integrative model to examine how ethical work climate, beliefs, and neutralization interact and jointly impact ISP noncompliance in contexts where ISP requirements are at odds with other interests at work The SIPT posits that individuals look to their social environments for cues to construct and interpret work reality an subsequently to develop their beliefs and determine appropriate ways to behave (Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978). In lin with the SIPT, the proposed model conjectures that ethical work climate serves as a vital social information cue cen tral to individuals' efforts to make sense of the priorities between ISP compliance and other conflicting interests at work. In this sense-making process, the ethical work climate is expected to act as an antecedent of beliefs and neu tralization reasoning. Through an alternative path, ethical work climate may moderate the belief-noncompliance rela tionship because the pressure imposed by the climate to prioritize the competing interests over ISP compliance may strengthen the efficacy of the undesirable beliefs to motivate noncompliance but nullify the effects of pro-ISP belief in preventing noncompliance

The SIPT recognizes the role of social environment without minimizing the importance of the cognitive dynamics in human behaviour. The SIPT having been followed, the proposed model conceptualizes cognition (beliefs and neutralization) as the process intervening between the ethical work climate and ISP noncompliance, suggesting that by and large, the ethical work climate needs to be transformed and internalized with individuals' cognitive system to exert its influence on ISP noncompliance. Neutralization, although not a traditional variable in much of the SIPT literature, is conceptualized as an important cognitive factor in the proposed model not only because it has been documented as an important predictor of ISP noncompliance but also because it is intimately linked to the ethical wor climate.

## 2 | ISP (NON)COMPLIANCE LITERATURE

Given that any information security solution loses its efficacy in the presence of end-user negligence of organiza tional ISPs (Puhakainen & Siponen, 2010), researchers have recently begun focusing on identifying factors that con tribute to ISP (non)compliance. Appendix A1 summarizes the major survey-based studies in this area. Twenty-four of the 31 studies focus on identifying salient beliefs that significantly contribute to (non)compliance intentions and behaviours. To classify these diverse beliefs into a sensible set, we carefully compared the definitions and measurement items used for the belief constructs to distinguish concepts that initially appear interchangeable and reconcil those that initially appear different. This review suggests that the majority of the belief variables identified in Appen dix A1 are largely grounded in the rational choice framework (RCF), which posits that individuals determine how the will act by calculating the benefits and costs associated with ISP (non)compliance (Bulgurcu, Cavusoglu, & Benbasat

2010; Li et al., 2010). Four broad groupings of beliefs (benefits and costs of compliance and benefits and costs of noncompliance) result from the RCF. The beliefs can be further categorized to reflect different referents, for example, benefits/costs for the individual versus for the organization. Appendix B shows the majority of the belief variables identified in Appendix A, their measurement items, and how they fall into the four meaningfu RCF categories.

More recently, researchers have also applied neutralization theory (Sykes & Matza, 1957) to examine ISP noncompliance. The theory posits that in order to break a norm/rule, people must apply neutralization to rationalize the action and neutralize the guilt associated with the deviant behaviour. Neutralization allows an individual to justif the deviant behaviour as situationally acceptable and thus frees the individual to engage in the deviant behaviour without feeling guilt (Sykes & Matza, 1957). Neutralization theory is robust for explaining a wide range of deviant behaviours, including youth violence (Agnew, 1994); digital piracy (Morris & Higgins, 2008; Siponen et al., 2012); consumer-initiated fraud (Strutton, Vitell, & Pelton, 1994); and computer abuse, information security (IS) misuse, and ISP noncompliance (Siponen & Vance, 2010; Willison et al., 2018).

Despite the expansion of this important body of knowledge, important gaps remain in the ISP (non)complianc literature. First, the deeply entrenched assumption that desirable beliefs motivate/inhibit ISP compliance noncompliance, whereas undesirable beliefs encourage/discourage ISP noncompliance/compliance can be problem atic because there are undoubtedly many instances where individuals do not act in accordance with their beliefs Empirical research seeking to confirm this assumption has yielded mixed results. Although desirable beliefs such as response efficacy, perceived sanction, and perceived threat are found to positively impact ISP (non)compliance (Boss Galletta, Benjamin Lowry, Moody, & Polak, 2015; D'Arcy, Hovav, & Galletta, 2009; Herath & Rao, 2009; Ifinedo, 2012; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Pahnila, Siponen, & Mahmood, 2007; Siponen, Pahnila, & Mahmood, 2010), no significant relationships are detected between these beliefs and ISP (non)compliance in other studies (Boss et al. 2015; Guo et al., 2011; Hovav & D'Arcy, 2012; Li et al., 2010; Pahnila et al., 2007; Siponen et al., 2010; Siponen & Vance, 2010; Son, 2011).

While the inconsistent findings suggest that important contingencies may exist, the moderators of the belief-(non)compliance relationships have not been critically assessed in the literature. Appendix A shows that only six out of the 31 studies examined the interactions between beliefs and other factors such as persona norms and fear appeal. Other possible moderating conditions include neutralization and the ethical climate within which ISP (non)compliance decisions are being made. Currently, most ethics-related ISP (non)compliance studies focus on personal ethics (Hovav & D'Arcy, 2012; Li, Sarathy, Zhang, & Luo, 2014; Myyry, Siponen, Pahnila, Vartiainen, & Vance, 2009). To the best of our knowledge, only one study has examined the anteced ent role of the principled climate in ISP noncompliance (Yazdanmehr & Wang, 2016). Similarly, with the excep tion of the work by Willison et al. (2018), studies have focused primarily on the main effects of neutralizatio on ISP noncompliance and computer misuse. The use of neutralization motivates ISP noncompliance even fo people who retain pro-ISP beliefs, suggesting that neutralization may act as another important moderator of the belief-noncompliance relationship.

Finally, the antecedents of neutralization and beliefs remain poorly understood. Only four out of the 31 stud ies examined the antecedents of the four categories of the rational choice beliefs, and none focused on th antecedents of neutralization (see Appendix A). This is an important omission because the lack of knowledge i this area would limit organizations' abilities to devise effective interventions to nurture and leverage favourable beliefs and to inhibit neutralization and undesirable beliefs. Further, the four studies implicitly presumed that individuals develop beliefs in responses to the objective elements of organizational ISPs, such as the existence and awareness of ISPs (Bulgurcu et al., 2010; D'Arcy et al., 2009). Nevertheless, prior research has shown that individuals perceive and respond as much to social cues as to the objective elements of their world (Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978).

## 3 | ETHICAL WORK CLIMATE

Ethical work climates are the prevailing perceptions of institutionalized values, policies, and reward systems that delineate what is considered right (Victor & Cullen, 1988). Extant research suggests that most organizations appear to have a distinct and dominant ethical climate type (Victor & Cullen, 1987; Victor & Cullen, 1988). To capture th different types of ethical work climate, Victor and Cullen (1988) developed a two-dimensional typology. The ethica criterion dimension refers to three major bases of moral judgment: egoism (maximizing self-interest), benevolenc (maximizing joint interests), and principled (adherence to laws and standards). And the locus of analysis dimension encompasses three referent groups that are the sources of moral reasoning: individual, local (organization), and cos mopolitan (larger economic or social system outside of the immediate organization)

The intersection of the two dimensions results in nine theoretical ethical climate types. The egoistic-individual the egoistic-local, and the egoistic-cosmopolitan climates respectively endorse the pursuit of self-interest (eg, mone tary rewards and power), the organization's interest (eg, strategic advantage, profit, and production goals), and th society's interest (eg, efficiency). The key characteristic of an egoistic climate is self-regarding and “acting exclusivel in one's own self-interest” (Jones, Felps, & Bigley, 2007, p. 138). Egoists are concerned with the welfare of others only if it affects their self-interest (Jones et al., 2007). By contrast, the benevolent-individual, the benevolent-local and the benevolent-cosmopolitan climates respectively promote maximizing the joint interests of other individuals regardless of organizational membership (eg, friendship and reciprocity), the joint interests of people within a group or organization (eg, team interest and team play), and the joint interests of other individuals outside of the organization (eg, social responsibility). A benevolent climate is “other-regarding” and is characterized primarily by concerns for all who are affected by the decision (Jones et al., 2007). And in the principled climate, organizational norms support decision making based on self-chosen principles and moral standards (individual), organizational procedures and poli cies (local), and laws and professional codes external to the organization (cosmopolitan) (Victor & Cullen, 1988), Fac tor analyses from previous research using the Ethical Climate Ouestionnaire developed by Victor and Cullen (1988 did not tap all nine types of ethical climate but suggested that ethical work climate types were primarily defined b the ethical criteria dimension rather than by the locus of analyses (Cullen, Victor, & Bronson, 1993; Vaicys, Barnett, & Brown, 1996; Victor & Cullen, 1988; Wimbush, Shepard, & Markham, 1997). Thus, the current study focuses on the criteria dimension (egoistic, benevolence, and principled) for the ethical climate types.

## 4 | THE SIPT AND THE PROPOSED MODEL

The SIPT posits that meaning can be socially constructed and that people's perceptions and justification reasoning reside in subjectivity and socially constructed reality (Fulk, Steinfield, Schmitz, & Power, 1987; Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Zalesny & Ford, 1990). As such, individuals look to their social environments for cues to construct and interpret work reality and subseguently to develop and adapt perceptions, reasoning, and behaviours to their social con text (Fulk et al., 1987; Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Zalesny & Ford, 1990). In the work environment, social cues include behaviour and interaction cues exhibited by supervisors, peers, and the general culture or climate of the organization The more ambiguous a situation, the stronger the influence of social cues on individuals (Rice & Aydin, 1991 Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978). Empirical work attests to the consistent impacts of social information on employees (Chan et al., 2005; Chen, Takeuchi, & Shum, 2013; Fulk et al., 1987; Rice & Aydin, 1991; Thomas & Griffin, 1983; Umphress, Labianca, Brass, Kass, & Scholten, 2003). As conflicting interests and demands have become a common workplace phenomenon, employees making ISP compliance decisions experience ambiguities concerning the priori ties between ISPs and the competing demands and concerning the norms of handling the dilemma. Because ambigu ous situations motivate individuals to interpret and perceive things in ways that are dictated by social context (Rice & Aydin, 1991; Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978), the ambiguity involved in ISP (non)compliance decisions motivates the employees to seek clarity from relevant social cues to appraise and construe the priorities between competing work demands. Those who contemplate violating the ISPs may be confronted with a strong need to neutralize the guilt and psychological distress associated with their anticipated deviant act. As will be discussed in the following section, the same social cues, in this case, the ethical work climate, are expected to be closely linked to the neutrali zation need of the employees. Thus, the SIPT provides a promising framework for integrating ethical work climate beliefs, neutralization, and noncompliance behaviours under one common umbrella.

In what follows, we draw on the SIPT to propose an integrative model of ISP noncompliance (Figure 1) and to contextualize both the main constructs and the construct relationships from the SIPT for the proposed model.

## 4.1 | Variables in the model

## 4.1.1 | Ethical work climate

Research has long recognized that social norms such as organizational climate are critical sources of social cues in employees' work environment (Zalesny & Ford, 1990). Perceived ethical work climate is used in the model because individuals do not respond to the work climate directly “but must first perceive and interpret their environment” (Carr, Schmidt, Ford, & DeShon, 2003, p. 605).

When feeling unable to simultaneously fulfil ISP requirements and other competing workplace demands, employees will respond based on organizational norms indicative of the organization's goals and priorities. With domain-specific foci, information security factors do not reveal priorities of competing facets and thus fall short of providing guidance on what response is considered appropriate when ISP compliance contravenes another major workplace interest. Thus, employees need to appraise and construe the priorities through other referent frames. The three ethical climate types identified in the literature reflect an encompassing and pervasive aspect of an organiza tion's environment concerning the overall organizational goals and priorities between self-serving interests, otherserving interests, and adherence to laws and principles (Victor & Cullen, 1988). By providing guidance on which interests are held in higher esteem and are rewarded when dealing with trade-offs and conflicts, ethical work climate has the appropriate foci and breadth to impact ISP noncompliance. Supporting this argument, researchers have increasingly emphasized the potentially important role of the more encompassing climate on domain specific issues Studies have found important cross-domain implications from ethical work climate to service outcome (Myer, Thoroughgood. & Mohammed. 2016). from climate regarding emplovee relations, organizational support, and leader member exchange to workplace safety (Hofmann & Morgeson, 1999; Wallace, Popp, & Mondore, 2006) and to customer service outcome (Schneider, White, & Paul, 1998). Researchers have also highlighted the relevance of ethics in the security, privacy, and IS domain (Lowry, Dinev, & Willison, 2017; Mingers & Walsham, 2010). Personal ethic and ethical work climate have both been found to directly or indirectly impact ISP (non)compliance (Hovav & D'Arcy 2012; Li et al., 2014; Myyry et al., 2009; Yazdanmehr & Wang, 2016).

## 4.1.2 | Information security antecedents

The primary focus on ethical climates in our theorizing is not intended to discount the importance of other well established and potentially confounding information security-related factors such as sanctions, facilitating conditions and subjective norm. Because the role of information security factors in ISP (non)compliance is relatively bette understood (Bulgurcu et al., 2010; D'Arcy et al., 2009; Li et al., 2014; Lowry & Moody, 2015), these factors ar included only as controls in the model

## 4.1.3 | Ethical work climate and beliefs

Attitude is not included in the model because both the ISP (non)compliance literature (see Appendix A) and the general IS literature (Venkatesh, 1999; Venkatesh & Davis, 1996) suggest that attitude is not a consistent predictor of intention/behaviour. The converging themes observed from the literature review (see Appendix B) provide a ratio nale for the choice of three belief variables. Benefits of noncompliance is not included in the model because it is con ceptualized and empirically measured in the literature as the logical converse of perceived cost of compliance—that i to say, perceiving compliance as costly and inconvenient is essentially the same as perceiving noncompliance as ben eficial and as saving time and effort. Perceived benefit of compliance and cost of noncompliance are both included because they are conceptualized and empirically measured as two distinct constructs, with the former emphasizing the rewards for ISP compliance and the latter focusing on the penalty for ISP noncompliance. High rewards for com pliance do not equal low penalty for noncompliance and vice versa.

The SIPT literature has explicitly documented social cues as the key force in the development of various beliefs at work, including employees' perceptions of organizational justice (Umphress et al., 2003), work-family conflict (Bhave, Kramer, & Glomb, 2010), and task characteristics (Thomas & Griffin, 1983). In the ISP (non)compliance litera ture, researchers have found that social cues such as top management practice, coworker socialization, and supervi sory practice impact compliance behaviour through employees' perceptions of the security climate (Chan et al., 2005). In line with these findings, the model proposes that ethical work climate complements domain-specific information security factors to serve as an important antecedent of beliefs.

Nonetheless, conceptualizing ethical work climate as an antecedent of beliefs tells only part of the story. The SIPT studies identify normative expectation as an important mechanism for social information to exert its influence on behaviour (Chen et al., 2013; Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Zalesny & Ford, 1990). By setting the normative expecta tion that only behaviours congruent with the priorities of the organization are acceptable, the same ethical work climate that provides a basis for the development of beliefs may also impose conformity pressure on employees. Depending on the degree of emphasis an ethical work climate places on rules and policies as opposed to other com: peting interests at work, employees are expected to experience varying degree of pressure to prioritize the competing interests over ISP compliance. High pressure, in turn, strengthens the effectiveness of the undesirable beliefs to motivate noncompliance but nullifies the effects of pro-ISP beliefs in preventing noncompliance. The anticipated moderating role of the ethical climate is consistent with the conjecture that social information may exert normative influence on people's judgement and behaviour such that people may conform to the norms behaviourally regardless of their beliefs (Deutsch & Gerard. 1955). This argument having been supported, the moderating effects of confor mity pressure from normative expectations on belief-behaviour relationships have been observed in various settings (Bock, Kankanhalli & Sharma 2006: Göckeritz et al 2010: Wenzel 2004) Compared with the egoistic and the benevolent climate, the principled climate measure captures, with less ambiguity, the relative priority placed on rules and policies as opposed to other workplace interests. This is so because a high score on the principled climate measure indicates higher priority for rules and policies, and a low score indicates higher priority for workplace interest other than rules and policies. In contrast, although a high score in the egoism climate measure indicates higher prior ity for self-serving interests, a low score could indicate higher priority for other-serving interests instead of for rule and policies. The same ambiguity exists for the benevolent climate measure. Thus, the proposed model focuses on the principled climate as the moderator of the belief-noncompliance relationship

## 4.1.4 | Ethical work climate and neutralization

In their seminal work, Salancik and Pfeffer (1978) explicitly expatiated the influence of social environment on justifi cation/neutralization. Nevertheless, neutralization has not been a focus in much of the SIPT literature. The propose model includes neutralization as an important cognitive factor for several reasons. First, neutralization has been iden: tified as a salient driver of ISP noncompliance (Siponen et al., 2012; Siponen & Vance, 2010; Willison et al., 2018), perhaps because of its ability to allow employees to engage in deviant acts while remaining convinced that they ar doing the right thing. After all, the desire to maintain a positive sense of self is one of the most persistent human motivations (Hales, 1985). By erroneously characterizing their noncompliance acts as justifiable and situationally appropriate, neutralization allows employees to convince themselves that they are making the right choice and thus avoid feeling any negative emotions that are normally evoked by the deviant acts.

Second, the possible conflicts between ISP requirements and other organizational priorities endorsed by the ego istic or benevolent climate heighten the need for the use of neutralization. It is conceivable that employees may fee that they are not responsible for their noncompliance behaviours given the conformity pressure imposed by the ethi cal work climate. But here we argue that citing pressure does not take away employees' sense of responsibility and guilt when they choose to violate ISPs. On the contrary, the conflicts and trade-offs between ISP requirements and ethical work climate may raise the awareness of the alternative courses of action, and employees must exercise voli tional and purposeful choices about which interest to prioritize, satisfy, or neglect. Thus, employees are expected to feel a sense of responsibility and a sense of guilt and distress for their volitional and purposeful noncompliance acts which in turn heightens their need to explain away their wrongdoing through neutralization and to relieve them from feeling guilty or distressed.

Finally, the same ethical work climate that heightens the need for neutralization may also facilitate or constrain the use of neutralization. Salancik and Pfeffer (1978) suggest that social cues influence neutralization reasoning because people refer to the features of their environment to develop socially acceptable and legitimate reasons to justify their behaviours. In the same vein, here, we argue that employees refer to the features of their ethical work climate for reasons and excuses that are considered legitimate in that particular ethical climate. Employees working in the egoistic or benevolent climate may use the pressure to achieve the conflicting priorities and goals endorsed b the climate to justify their ISP noncompliance behaviour. In contrast, the priorities and values endorsed by the princi pled climate overlap with the organization's ISPs to provide congruent norms and expectations regarding ISP compli ance, leaving employees with no room to use the ethical climate as a justification for their noncompliance behaviour Explicitly linking neutralization with conflicts in norms and interests, Sykes and Matza (1957) contend that people deviate from certain norms not always because they reject those norms, but because they accord other competing norms and interests with higher precedence. As will be expatiated in the hypothesis development section, the four mechanisms-attentional, normative, social learning, and, justification, mechanism-that, underlie the influence of social cues on beliefs also apply to explain the influence of the ethical context on neutralization. Although developed independently, the neutralization literature has recognized the social nature of neutralization and has suggested that the use of neutralization does not emerge out of thin air (Hamlin. 1988). All neutralizations are learned patterns of thought prevalent in society (Sykes & Matza, 1957), and individuals primarily draw from a repertoire of culturally approved excuses to justify behaviours (Murphy, 1999). Considering that neutralization is a salient driver of ISP noncompliance and is intimately linked to the ethical work climate, the proposed model extends the traditional SIPT framework to encompass the neutralization construct given the context of this research.

## 4.2 | The SIPT mechanisms that underlie the social influence of ethical work climate

The SIPT studies outlined four different mechanisms that underlie the influence of social cues on beliefs and behaviours: attentional, normative, social learning, and justification mechanism (Chen et al., 2013; Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978 Zalesny & Ford, 1990). Following these studies, we argue that an ethical climate will guide employees' ISP compli ance decisions by (a) directing employees' attention to the information that is aligned with the goals and priorities of the organization (attentional mechanism); (b) setting the normative expectation that only beliefs, neutralization, and behaviours that endorse the priorities of the organization are acceptable (normative mechanism); (c) compelling employees to adapt their beliefs, neutralization, and behaviours to fit the ethical climate (social learning mechanism) and (d) enabling or constraining the use of the priorities that are incongruent with ISPs to justify one's noncompliance behaviours (justification mechanism). In the following section, we develop the hypotheses in the con text of (a) how the three types of ethical work climate differ in terms of their priorities, goals, and the resultant pressure that motivates employees to defy their organizational ISP in order to achieve the priorities and goals endorsed by the climate and (b) how such differences and the four mechanisms explain the interrelationship among ethical cli mate types, beliefs, neutralization, and ISP noncompliance.

## 4.3 | Hypothesis development

## 4.3.1 | Ethical work climate types as antecedents of beliefs and neutralization

The egoistic climate promotes self-interest and organizational gains such as personal rewards, profitability, and pro ductivity at the expense of other values that include rule compliance (Victor & Cullen, 1988). Some may argue that in situations where conflicting workplace interests are absent, individuals working in an egoistic climate have the incen tive to comply with organizational ISPs in expectation of gaining rewards or other benefits for complying. But such situations are expected to be few and far between in such organizations, given that theoretical and empirical work finds that work climates that downplay laws, rules, and code of conduct are associated with increased conflicts in work expectations and interests (Jaramillo, Mulki, & Solomon, 2006; Mulki, Jaramillo, & Locander, 2008; Schwepker, Ferrell, & Ingram, 1997; Valentine, Godkin, & Varca, 2010). Adherence to ISP can require substantial amounts of time and effort, cause substantial inconvenience at work, and result in unintended outcomes (eg, reduced productivity missed deadlines, and jeopardized performance) that run counter to the values and priorities promoted in an egoistic organization. This may explain why employees often perceive ISP compliance as inconvenient and counterproductive and as obstacles to personal and organizational gains (Herath & Rao, 2009; Post & Kagan, 2007; Wall, Lowry, & Bar low, 2015).

In providing guidance for the handling of the trade-offs and conflicts between the priority of the organization and ISP compliance, the egoistic climate operates to shift employees' attention away from the desirable aspects of ISP compliance to prevent employees from choosing compliance at the expense of the organization's priorities (eg, profitability and productivity). It directs people's focus to the undesirable aspects instead so that the priorities and core values of the organization will not be sacrificed for the sake of ISP compliance. This attentional mechanism thus hinders the development of pro-ISP beliefs and facilitates the formation of undesirable beliefs. A dominant atten tional focus on the unfavourable aspects of compliance may further trigger the development of neutralization rea soning given the ease of using the negative aspects of compliance to justify one's noncompliance behaviours (justification mechanism). Choosing between ISP compliance and organizational goals such as profitability or produc tivity requires volition and personal discretion on the part of the employees. The volitional and discretionary natur of such decisions intensifies the feeling of responsibility and shame when one chooses to scarify ISP compliance confronting one with the need to neutralize any uncomfortable feelings associated with the noncompliance behav iours (Tsang, 2002). Thus, an egoistic climate presents employees with both convenient excuses and a strong need for neutralization.

As previously noted, the unintended outcomes stemming from ISP compliance run counter to the values and priorities promoted in an egoistic organization, suggesting that ISP compliance will not be held in high esteem in the organization. Research on safety compliance suggests that in an egoistic climate, behaviours contributing to short-run profitability, production goals, and efficiency are more likely to receive immediate extrinsic reward tha safety-compliance behaviours (Parboteeah & Kapp, 2008; Wright, 1986). The phenomenon of sacrificing securit law compliance to achieve conflicting economic and noneconomic goals has also been documented at the organiza tional level (Wall et al., 2015). Given the anticipated low priority allotted to rules and policies, an egoistic organiza tion is likely to disapprove pro-ISP beliefs at the expense of profitability and productivity and to accept neutralization reasoning and the undesirable belief that compliance is costly for the purpose of productivity an profitability improvement (normative mechanism). When the approved undesirable belief and neutralization becom entrenched in the organization (normative mechanism), employees will learn to adapt their neutralization- and ISP related beliefs to fit the egoistic ethical climate (social-learning mechanism). Those who adapt well are more likel to remain with the organization, whereas those who do not are likely to leave (Victor & Cullen, 1988). Thus, an ego istic climate is expected to be negatively associated with pro-ISP beliefs but positively associated with undesirabl beliefs and neutralization.

H1: Perceptions of egoistic climates are negatively related to perceived benefit of compliance (H1a) and perceived cost of noncompliance (H1b) but positively related to perceived cost of compliance (H1c) and neutralization (H1d).

Research suggests that people who are benevolent tend to be less cognizant of self-interest, laws, or rules (Kohlberg, 1981). Similarly, people working in a benevolent climate are likely to be more concerned with the maxi mum collective gains rather than with self-interest, rules, and laws. Although the positive effects of a benevolent cli mate have been documented in different contexts such as work satisfaction (Elçi & Alpkan, 2009), organizationa commitment (Cullen. Parboteeah. & Victor. 2003). and various in-role and extra-role citizenship behaviours (Leung 2008), the impacts of a benevolent climate in this context may not be as straightforward. With the well-being of the greatest number of stakeholders as the primary source of reasoning, a largely benevolent climate directs employees attention to the interests of diverse groups of people to balance and maximize the collective gains. This attentiona mechanism renders employees working in such a climate to be sensitive to the particular effects, both positive and negative, of organizational ISPs on different groups of people. Consequently, employees working in a benevolent climate are likely to understand, accept, and develop the pro-ISP beliefs (benefits of compliance and cost of noncompliance), because ISP compliance indeed promotes the interests of those whose data are at risk and those who choose to comply. They are also equally likely to understand, accept, and develop unfavourable beliefs (cost of compliance) because the inherent costs associated with ISP compliance may pose a hindrance to the well-being (such as productivity and profitability) of fellow colleagues and the organization. Through the normative mechanism, a benevolent climate will probably accept and legitimize diverse and sometimes opposing views, such as the pro-ISP beliefs and the unreceptive beliefs and neutralization reasoning toward ISP. Through the social-learning mechanism, individuals in turn learn and assimilate these socially acceptable views into their belief scheme and neutralization reasoning. Concerns for the interests of diverse groups of people also facilitate the development of neutralization reasoning because the interests of certain stakeholders may well contravene the requirements of ISP, and such conflicting interests can easily be used to justify noncompliance behaviours (justification mechanism). Employees working in a benevolent climate inevitably experience cognitive dissonance and distress resulting from the opposing beliefs they simultaneously maintain about ISPs. Neutralization is consequently brought to bear for the relief of such dissonance and distress (Tsang, 2002).

Supporting this argument that a benevolent climate may encourage employees to intake and accept different sometimes conflicting perspectives, researchers have found a positive relationship between benevolence, empath concerns, perspective taking (Silfver, Helkama, Lönnqvist, & Verkasalo, 2008), and prosocial rule breaking in th workplace (Vardaman, Gondo, & Allen, 2014). Thus, we hypothesize:

H2: Perceptions of benevolent climates are positively related to perceived benefit of compliance (H2a), perceived cost of noncompliance (H2b), perceived cost of compliance (H2c), and neutralization (H2d)

Principled climates endorse the application and interpretation of rules in the normative expectations, and adher ence to laws, professional codes, and organizational policies are valued and rewarded in practice (Victor & Cullen 1988). The values and priorities endorsed by the principled climate reinforce what is promoted by the ISPs. Thus, i providing guidance for the handling of the trade-offs and conflicts between ISP compliance and other competing work demands, a principled climate will emphasize the benefits of compliance, the cost of noncompliance, and the inappropriateness of neutralization and will downplay the cost of compliance (attentional mechanism). Through the normative mechanism, organizations with a principled normative structure are also likely to set pro-ISP beliefs as normative expectations and disapprove of beliefs about the costs of compliance and justifications of ISP noncompliance. To fit in with the ethical climate, individuals are likely to internalize the socially acceptable pro-ISP beliefs and refrain from perceiving ISP compliance as costly and from using neutralization to justify ISP noncompliance (social-learning mechanism). A principled ethical climate characterized by ethical value, the code of conduct, and ethical practice and policy enforcement at an organization has been found to reduce conflicting demands and expectations at work (Jaramillo et al., 2006; Mulki et al., 2008; Schwepker et al., 1997; Valentine et al., 2010). Thus, such a climate is less conducive to the development of neutralization reasoning because fewer conflicting demands can be used to justify noncompliance behaviours (justification mechanism). Thus, we hypothesize:

H3: Perceptions of principled climates are positively related to perceived benefit of compliance (H3a) and perceived cost of noncompliance (H3b) and are negatively related to perceived cost of compliance (H3c) and neutralization (H3d).

## 4.4 | Beliefs, neutralization, and noncompliance behavior

In line with the belief-intention/behaviour framework from the theory of reasoned action and with prior studies that have investigated the relationships between beliefs and behaviours in the context of ISP compliance (Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Herath & Rao, 2009), we propose the following hypotheses:

H4: Perceived benefit of compliance (H4a) and perceived cost of noncompliance (H4b) negatively, and perceived cost of compliance (H4c) positively impact ISP noncompliance behaviour.

The idea at the heart of neutralization theory is that people use justifications to rationalize deviant behaviours and to neutralize the guilt and cognitive dissonance associated with such behaviours (Sykes & Matza, 1957). The us of neutralization renders rational beliefs inoperative, whereupon individuals are able to convince themselves that th deviant behaviours are situationally acceptable (Sykes & Matza, 1957). Research suggests that neutralization is prevalent in a wide range of contexts such as digital piracy (Morris & Higgins, 2008; Siponen et al., 2012), ISP noncompliance (Siponen & Vance, 2010; Willison et al., 2018), and consumer-initiated fraud (Strutton et al., 1994). In line with the neutralization literature, we hypothesize

H5: Neutralization positively impacts ISP noncompliance behaviour.

## 4.4.1 | The principled ethical climate and neutralization as moderators

In the context of this research, the values and priorities endorsed by the principled climate suggest that ISP compli ance will receive higher priority in a principled climate than competing interests at work. Thus, employees working in such a climate will be less likely to feel the pressure to evade ISP in order to satisfy the competing interests. In fact through the attentional mechanism, the principled climate sets a high standard for ISP compliance by shiftin employees' attention to rules and policies, limiting the impact of the undesirable beliefs because even those wit undesirable beliefs may be motivated to meet that standard. Through the normative mechanism, organizations wit a highly principled climate are expected to set the expectations that all types of ISP noncompliance are unacceptable, including those that are motivated by competing workplace interests. Employees working in such organizations therefore learn to refrain from engaging in noncompliance behaviours even when they maintain negative beliefs towards ISPs (social-learning mechanism)

Conversely, a lack of a principled climate shifts employees' attention to competing interests because of the low priority placed on rule compliance (attentional mechanism). The dominant focus on competing interests confronts employees with a strong need to engage in noncompliance behaviours, nullifying the effectiveness of desirable beliefs in preventing noncompliance and strengthening the effects of undesirable beliefs in encouraging ISP noncompliance. At the organizational level, researchers have found that competing goals and prioritie moderate the belief-noncompliance relationship by motivating organizations to violate external security rules and laws (Wall et al., 2015). Through the normative mechanism, organizations with a lack of a principled climate are more likely to approve ISP noncompliance, particularly when such violation is motivated by the need to sat isfy major competing work demands. Thus, employees may learn to engage in ISP noncompliance regardless of their beliefs toward ISPs (social-learning mechanism). Empirical work provides support for the moderating effects of conformity pressure that stem from various normative expectations. For instance, injunctive norm has bee found to moderate the relationship between descriptive norm beliefs and conservation behaviours (Göckerit et al., 2010), social norms to moderate the relationship between deterrence variables and tax evasion behaviou (Wenzel, 2004), and collaborative norms to moderate the relationship between perceived usefulness and knowl edge-seeking behaviour (Bock et al., 2006).

In a somewhat similar vein, neutralization reasoning directs employees to focus on the cost of compliance as an excuse for their noncompliance behaviour, resulting in a stronger influence of cost of compliance perceptions on noncompliance. The same attentional mechanism also shifts employees' focus away from the positive aspects of compliance, rendering the pro-ISP beliefs inoperative under the influence of neutralization. Because neutralizatior may allow employees to feel committed to the organizational ISP requirements and maintain pro-ISP beliefs but, at the same time, defy the ISP requirements, neutralization may overpower the impact of beliefs on ISP noncomplianc behaviour. Thus, neutralization will interact with beliefs to impact ISP noncompliance because those who are able to neutralize their guilt will be more likely engage in noncompliance behaviours regardless of their beliefs, wherea those who are not able to neutralize are likely to act in accordance with their beliefs. Supporting this argument, researchers have long acknowledged the possibility that neutralization would lessen the effectiveness of one's inter: nalized values and beliefs (Chatzidakis, Hibbert, & Smith, 2007; Sykes & Matza, 1957). Justification has been found to reduce the effectiveness of moral goal setting on unethical behaviours (Niven & Healy, 2016). Taken together, th effects of beliefs on noncompliance may be contingent on the level of the principled ethical climate and neutraliza tion (see Figure 1), leading to Hypotheses 6a and 6b.

The moderating effect of the principled climate discussed above points towards the constraining effect of work environment as employees are pressured to conform to what is dictated by the ethical work climate rather than to act in alignment with their ISP-related beliefs. Thus, some may argue that employees may be able to diffuse their responsibility and depersonalize themselves from their noncompliance decisions by citing conformity pressur imposed by the ethical work climate. Absent from a sense of responsibility or guilt, individuals would not have th need to apply neutralization (Tsang, 2002). Nevertheless, we contend that the existence of conflicts and trade-offs between ISP requirements and what is dictated by the ethical work climate may, on the contrary, make employee aware of alternative courses of action and require that they exercise volitional and purposeful choices about whic demand to prioritize, satisfy, or neglect. The volitional and purposeful nature of this choice evokes the sense of responsibility and subsequently the sense of guilt, confronting employees with a strong need to explain away their wrongdoing and relieve themselves from the uncomfortable feelings through neutralization (Tsang, 2002). By simul taneously investigating the moderating effects of the principled climate and of neutralization, we will be able to gai interesting insights about whether one moderating effect may overshadow the other, perhaps because of the differ ence in their effectiveness in assuaging the guilt and distress associated with the noncompliance behaviour.

H6a: Perceptions of the principled climate moderates the belief-noncompliance relationship such that a highly prin cipled climate strengthens the positive effects of desirable beliefs (benefits of compliance and cost o noncompliance) and weakens the positive effect of cost of compliance perception on noncompliance behaviour.

H6b: Neutralization moderates the belief-noncompliance relationship such that neutralization weakens the negative effects of desirable beliefs (benefits of compliance and cost of noncompliance) and strengthens the positive effect of costs of compliance perception on noncompliance

## 5 | RESEARCH METHOD

The hypotheses were tested using survey data collected through a professional market research firm. ## 5.2 | Social desirability bias, common method bias, and nonresponse bias

Research shows that people exhibit lower social desirability when responding to online rather than paper-based surveys (Joinson, 1999). We took additional steps to further minimize the possibility of social desirability bias in the responses. First, prior to respondents taking the survey, we communicated to them that their responses were anony mous and that we did not retain personal identifiable information such as names, email addresses, or IP addresses Second, to protect data transmitted across the Internet, we had all responses sent over a secure connection. Third we endeavoured to be as unobtrusive as possible with data collection and refrained from asking any questions tha could compromise respondents' anonymity. Fourth, we told respondents that participating in the survey was completely voluntary and that they were free to discontinue taking the survey at any time. Finally, we assure respondents that we were conducting the study for academic purposes and that publications using the data collected would report them in an aggregate format.

The potential for the common method bias (CMB) was evaluated using the interconstruct correlations approach (Pavlou, Liang, & Xue, 2006). The latent variable correlation matrix showed that the highest inter construct correlation was 0.70, providing sufficient evidence that CMB was not a major issue. Nonresponse bias was assessed using wave analysis (Roberts, Campbell, & Vijayasarathy, 2016) in which the sample was par titioned into quartiles based on the time at which the survey was completed (Boyer & Hult, 2005). The first and last quartiles (proxy for nonrespondents) were then compared across the respondent and firm characteristic variables. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two quartiles, indicating that nonre sponse bias is unlikely to be a major issue.

## 6.1.2 | Formative constructs<sup>2</sup>

The three basic ethical work climate types were operationalized as second-order formative constructs comprised of first-order reflective subconstructs that represent the locus of analysis dimension of the ethical climate. Martin and Cullen (2006) conducted a meta-analytic review and suggested that the cosmopolitan loci of analysis for the egoism and benevolence dimensions have not typically been observed in empirical research. Thus, the egoism and benevolence constructs in this research consisted of the individual and local locus of analysis, whereas the principled climate construct consisted of individual, local, and cosmopolitan locus of analysis. The validity of the reflective subconstructs was evaluated following the same procedures described in the previous section. All the subconstructs demonstrated sufficient convergent and discriminant validity. To assess the validity of the formative constructs, we followed the steps outlined in extant literature (Peng & Lai, 2012; Vance, Elie-Dit-Cosaque, & Straub, 2008). First, we examined the weight and statistical significance of each subconstruct to assess its contribution to its respective formative construct. The individual locus of analysis subconstruct for the principled dimension's weight fell well below the recommended level of 0.1 (Andreev, Heart, Maoz, & Pliskin, 2009) and was not statistically significant at the 0.05 level, whereupon it was dropped from further analyses. Second, we tested for multicollinearity of the formative indicators by computing variance inflation factors (VIF) to ensure a well-fitting formative model without any redundant items. The VIE values ranged from 1 47 to 1.97 for the inner model and from 1.15 to 3 28 for the outer model lower than the recommended upper-bound cut-off level of 3.3 (Diamantopoulos & Siguaw, 2006), thereby providing sufficient evi dence for a well-fitting formative model.

Third, we conducted the modified multitrait multimethod (MTMM) analysis (Loch, Straub, & Kamel, 2003; Vanc et al., 2008) to assess the convergent and discriminant validity of the formative constructs. The interitem correlation within each climate construct correlate significantly with each other, and the item-to-construct correlation within a given climate construct are significant, providing support for convergent validity. The MTMM analysis results (Table A3) also suggest that the formative constructs demonstrate adequate discriminant validity since the interitem and item-to-construct correlations tended to correlate more highly with each other than with the item used in other constructs (Loch et al., 2003). Collectively, the above analyses provide persuasive evidence of adequat reliability and validity for the measurement model.

## 6.3 | Post hoc analyses: Effect size and mediation analysis<sup>12</sup>

Prior SIPT studies have conceptualized two different effects from social cues to behaviour. With one effect, employees internalize the mental schema imposed by their social environment and adapt their beliefs and neutraliza tion to align with what is considered socially legitimate and acceptable (Chen et al., 2013; Fulk, 1993; Fulk et al., 1987; Gallivan, Spitler, & Koufaris, 2005). With the other effect, employees simply comply with the behavioura expectations without internalizing the mental schema imposed by the environment or adapting their beliefs and neu tralization (Chen et al., 2013; Fulk, 1993; Fulk et al., 1987; Gallivan et al., 2005). Since both effects can independentl account for the influence of a social cue on behaviour, we expect the impacts of ethical work climate on ISP noncompliance to be partially mediated by beliefs and neutralization. We tested for mediation using a bootstrapped confidence interval test approach (Vance, Benjamin Lowry, & Eggett, 2015). ## 7 | DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

The findings of this study lend considerable support to the important role played by ethical work climate and neutral ization in explaining ISP noncompliance. As predicted, neutralization not only exerts a strong main effect on ISP noncompliance but it also strengthens the efficacy of perceived cost of compliance in motivating noncompliance and weakens the efficacy of perceived cost of noncompliance in inhibiting noncompliance. Consistent with our theorizing the three ethical climate types are found to differentially influence why employees come to perceive their organiza tional ISPs in certain ways and why they use neutralization. An egoistic climate is positively related to cost of compli ance and neutralization and is negatively related to cost of noncompliance. In contrast, a principled climate is positively related to benefit of compliance and cost of noncompliance and is negatively related to cost of compliance and neutralization. These results are consistent with findings that have documented the negative impacts of an ego istic climate and the positive effects of a principled climate on various outcome measures (Barnett & Schubert, 2002; Cullen et al., 2003; Elçi & Alpkan, 2009; Leung, 2008). Although previous studies have generally documented th positive effects of a benevolent climate on various outcome measures (Barnett & Schubert, 2002; Cullen et al. 2003; Elçi & Alpkan, 2009; Leung, 2008), findings of this research suggest that a benevolent climate is positively related to benefit of compliance (favourable effects) and to cost of compliance and neutralization (undesirable effects).

The results also suggest that ethical work climate types explain an additional 5.9%, 1.8%, 20.1%, and 16% of the variance in perceived benefits of compliance, cost of noncompliance, cost of compliance, and neutralization, respec tively. When ethical work climate types are considered, no residual impacts of sanction and facilitating condition are observed on multiple beliefs and neutralization, suggesting that the significant impacts from ethical work climat on beliefs and neutralization are more consistent than the impacts from the three information security factors. Post hoc analysis results reveal that cost of compliance and neutralization fully mediate the climate-noncompliance paths, suggesting that ethical work climate is able to impact ISP noncompliance only when individuals internalize the climate and adapt their beliefs and neutralization to match the climate. Given these results and the fact that climate is a rela tively stable and enduring factor that may lead to long-term behavioural outcomes, the advantage of incorporating ethical work climate into ISP (non)compliance research becomes clear.

The inclusion of the two moderators had a significant added effect on ISP noncompliance. The principled climate did not moderate the relationship between cost of compliance and noncompliance once neutralization was also included in the model as the moderator. This suggests that neutralization is a more important moderator of the belief-noncompliance relationship. The lack of a moderating effect from the principled climate and the finding that cost of compliance and neutralization fully mediate the paths from principled, benevolence, and egoism to noncompliance behaviour are consistent with the parallel emphasis the SIPT places on the social environment and the intervening cognitive processes. The social environment provides the basis for individuals to construct their beliefs and neutralization reasoning. Meanwhile, the social environment needs to be transformed and internalized with individuals' cognitive system before it can exert influence on behaviour. In this sense, the ethical work climate fosters or impedes the conditions necessary for neutralization. In turn, neutralization acts as both a predictor and a moderator in explaining ISP noncompliance. The finding that neutralization exerts a stronger moderating effect than the principled climate may confirm our argument, developed earlier, suggesting that neutralization may be more effective than the ethical work climate in freeing individuals from any uncomfortable feelings so that they can engage in ISP noncompliance regardless of their beliefs. Given that the multiplicity of potentially conflicting interests at work is particularly conducive to the use of neutralization, much could be gained from examining how neutraliza tion interacts with other factors to impact ISP (non)compliance

## 7.1 | Contributions and implications

## 7.1.1 | Theoretical contributions

This study contributes to the burgeoning ISP compliance literature on several fronts. First, ISP (non)compliance stud ies focusing on beliefs, neutralization, and ethical climate have drawn on distinct theoretical perspectives and have hitherto remained mostly independent of each other. The integrative model brings the three lines of ISP (non)compli ance studies under a common umbrella, thereby providing a richer and more realistic conceptualization of how beliefs, neutralization, and ethical work climate may interact and intervene to complicate the belief-noncomplianc relationship.

Second, the effects of competing goals and priorities on security rules or law compliance have been examined a the organizational level but not at the individual level (Wall et al., 2015). Thus, a unigue aspect of this research is tha it takes an important step forward in explicitly linking conflicting workplace interests with ethical work climate and neutralization in the theorizing of ISP noncompliance and has yielded several important insights. The results suggest that any ethical climate that gives high primacy to work interests and demands other than laws, rules and policies, be it an egoistic climate or benevolent climate, could be problematic for an organization's ISP compliance initiatives. Fur ther, the study of Wall et al. (2015) suggests that conflicting interests moderate the perceptions-noncompliance relationship at the organizational level (Wall et al., 2015). In contrast, this study suggests that the principled climate (which captures the magnitude of pressure from conflicting workplace interests) is not sufficient to overpower th effects of the beliefs on noncompliance at the individual level. Rather, neutralization seems to be a more important moderator for the belief-noncompliance relationship.

Third, previous research focuses on identifying various beliefs or neutralization techniques to diagnose the ISP compliance problem but assumes much of what ought to be explained regarding how people develop such belief and what facilitates the use of neutralization. Further, despite the inconsistent findings on the belief-(non)compliance relationship in the literature, extant studies have not sufficiently or critically assessed the important contingencies of this relationship. Our research addresses these important omissions in the literature by drawing on the SIPT to develop an integrated model that allows for the modelling and empirical evaluation of possible antecedent and mod: eration effects. Additionally, the dominant ISP compliance literature assumes that beliefs and neutralization ar objectively and rationally developed as a function of the objective elements of ISPs and has missed the effect of social influence on ISP compliance. By demonstrating that social forces such as ethical work climate and subjectiv norm play a critical role in ISP compliance, the present study provides support to the limited ISP literature whic highlights that social forces must be incorporated when theorizing about ISP (non)compliance (Chan et al., 2005; Warkentin, Johnston, & Shropshire, 2011). The finding that neutralization is an important moderator is consistent with the call in the literature to identify the contingency variables for the effects of deterrence beliefs on ISP compli ance (D'Arcy & Herath, 2011).

Finally, this research also contributes to the SIPT literature and the literature on ethical work climate. It expand the boundaries of the SIPT from its original focus on job- and organization-related perceptions. attitudes. and behay iours (Meyer, 1994) to a broader social influence effect on neutralization. It provides further evidence for the cross domain implications from a more encompassing climate on domain-specific issues. Further, in response to the calls to provide theoretical explanation for the linkages between ethical climate and outcome measures (Mayer, Kuenzi, & Greenbaum, 2010; Mayer, Kuenzi, Greenbaum, Bardes, & Salvador, 2009), this paper uncovers two processes (beliefs and neutralization) through which an ethical work climate influences ISP noncompliance behaviour

## 7.1.2 | Practical implications

Consistent with findings from the ISP (non)compliance literature (Bulgurcu et al., 2010: D'Arcy et al., 2009: Herath 8 Rao, 2009; Siponen & Vance, 2010), results from this research suggest that to keep ISP noncompliance to a minimum level, organizations need to take steps to raise employees' awareness of the cost of noncompliance, thwart the per ception that ISP implementation is costly, and prevent the use of neutralization. Second, this research suggests that a shift in organizations' information security strategies and practices may be necessary. Typically, security manage ment strategies and practices place dominant emphases on sanctioning and facilitating conditions such as training and providing assistance. Our results suggest that neutralization plays an important role, as is demonstrated by its significant main and moderating effects in ISP noncompliance. Thus, traditional interventions such as awareness and support programs must be complemented with interventions that address the issue of neutralization. The results also suggest that the narrow focus on “security” may be limited and that without a carefully managed ethical climate, ISP noncompliance is likely to occur. Organizations need to have an effective intangible infrastructure—the ethical work climate—to enhance the desirable beliefs, inhibit the undesirable beliefs, and discourage the use of neutralization in individuals' ISP non (compliance) decision making. The findings from this research suggest that organizations should strive to foster a principled climate to prevent ISP noncompliance. In contrast, an egoistic climate can be a breeding ground for ISP noncompliance. In the ethical work climate literature, researchers have outlined multipronged inter ventions that hold particular promise for cultivating and sustaining an ethical work climate. In a nutshell, strategy for: mulation and the wording of mission statements should address ethical and law-abiding conduct with the sam emphasis as financial performance goals (Cohen, 1993). Doing so prevents the development of norms and system structures that hold employees accountable for performance goals but not for the manner in which those goals were achieved (Cohen, 1993). The ethical value and practices exhibited by those in power are critical in setting the ethica tone and providing employees with a model for acceptable behaviour in the organization (Cohen, 1993). Rules, poli cies, and professional codes and conduct must be thoroughly understood, effectively communicated, and enforce with the same rigor as the financial priorities of the organization (Cohen. 1993: Trevino. 1990), In the context of this research, eliminating an exclusive focus on financial performance goals while placing equal emphasis on rules and laws helps make employees feel less compelled to violate organizational ISPs or to use the pressure to attain financia performance goals as neutralization excuses for the violation.

One intriguing finding concerns the impacts of the benevolent climate on beliefs and neutralization. The result suggest that along with its positive impacts on perceived benefit of compliance, a benevolent climate could have unintended negative consequences on perceived cost of compliance and neutralization. With this finding, we are not suggesting that organizations should refrain from nurturing a benevolent climate given that its positive impacts wil likely far outweigh any potential negative consequences. Instead, we wish to raise managers' awareness on the potential unintended negative effects of a benevolent climate in ISP noncompliance. This way, measures can b taken to avoid such unintended effects. Further, the lack of relationship between the benevolent climate and per ceived cost of noncompliance indicates that strict enforcement of penalties for ISP noncompliance might run counte to the benevolent organizational climate, and alternative measures may be needed in organizations with a predomi nately benevolent climate to prevent ISP noncompliance.

## 7.2 | Limitations and directions for future research

Despite its important contributions, certain limitations of this study must be noted. First, as with other survey-based cross-sectional studies, we are unable to make causal conclusions. Future work would benefit from longitudinal or experimental designs to provide conclusive evidence on the causal relationship among the key constructs of the pro posed model. Also, we used the self-report approach to collect noncompliance behaviour data. Future researchers may benefit from using other approaches such as the hypothetical scenario approach to validate the findings from the current study. Second, although great care was taken to survey a representative cross section of working professionals, the majority of respondents in this study had earned a college degree. Although recent research (Ram pell, 2013) suggests that the number of college-educated Americans is increasing, individuals without college degrees were underrepresented in our sample. Future research designs may attempt to replicate the current study using a larger portion of noncollege respondents. Third, drawing upon insights from the SIPT literature, we argue that perceived ethical work climate impacts beliefs and neutralization through the attentional, normative, social learning, and justification mechanisms. Although these arguments are based on solid theorizing, we did not measure or test thes mechanisms. Future researchers may wish to explicitly measure and test whether these and other unexplored mech anisms indeed underlie the impacts of ethical work climate on beliefs and neutralization.

Finally, this research opens many avenues for future research. For instance, this research examined only the rol of ethical climate types in ISP noncompliance. Future research may investigate how the congruence between individ ual ethical values and organizational ethical climate plays a role in an employee's engagement in ISP noncompliance Further, the coexisting positive and negative impacts of benevolent climates on beliefs and neutralization merit fur ther conceptualization and investigation. Also, the lack of a relationship between benevolent climates and perceived cost of noncompliance suggests that fine-grained theorizing is necessary to find additional measures that go beyon penalty.
