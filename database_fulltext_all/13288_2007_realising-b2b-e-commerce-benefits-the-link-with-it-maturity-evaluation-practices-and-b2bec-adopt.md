# Realising B2B e-commerce benefits: the link with IT maturity, evaluation practices, and B2BEC adoption readiness

Chad Lin<sup>1</sup>, Yu-An Huang<sup>2</sup> and Janice Burn<sup>3</sup>

## Abstract

Actual realisation of business-to-business electronic commerce (B2B e-commerce) benefits from IT investments has been a critical issue for large organisations. However, relatively little research has been undertaken to determine the drivers for realising B2B e-commerce benefits within these organisations. A survey research was conducted to examine the relationships between B2B e-commerce benefits, IT investment evaluation methodologies (IEM), IT benefit realisation processes (BRP), B2B e-commerce adoption readiness, and IT maturity in large Australian organisations. An IT investment management model was developed to test these relationships. The results had empirically validated the model and indicate that a higher level of BRP adoption and increased level of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness had a significant direct relationship with B2B e-commerce benefits. In addition, the level of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness, and the level of IEM and BRP adoption were significantly influenced by the level of IT maturity. However, the use of IEM alone had only an indirect positive influence on B2B e-commerce benefits through the higher level of BRP adoption and increased level of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness.

Keywords: IT maturity; IT investment evaluation methodologies; IT benefit realisation processes; B2B e-commerce adoption readiness; B2B e-commerce benefits

## Introduction

According to International Data Corp., global information technology (IT) spending is likely to increase by 6.3% annually in 2006 (Minton et al., 2006). Global IT spending, as forecast by Global Insight, will increase to over U.S.\$3.2 trillion in 2007 (WITSA, 2004). However, despite the increased spending on IT, senior executives are still uncertain about the contribution of IT investments to the organisational bottom line (Love et al., 2005). Similarly, research studies have continued to report contradictory findings of the effect of IT expenditures (e.g. on business-tobusiness electronic commerce (B2B e-commerce)) on organisational productivity (Osei-Bryson & Ko, 2004; Thatcher & Pingry, 2004). It is not surprising, therefore, to see the term ‘productivity paradox’ gaining increasing notoriety as researchers fail to demonstrate a direct and sustainable connection between IT investments and business performance despite large investments in IT over many years (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 2003; Hu & Quan, 2005).

Senior executives in these organisations are often under immense pressure to find a way to measure and relate the benefits of their IT investment to business performance (Al-Yaseen et al., 2006; Pan et al., 2006; Standing et al., 2006), but with singular lack of success. While there are several reasons why IT investments in B2B e-commerce do not deliver expected B2B e-commerce benefits, one major reason is the lack of proper use of IT investment evaluation methodologies (IEM) and IT benefits realisation processes (BRP) (Hitt & Brynjolfsson, 1996; Willcocks & Lester, 1997; Lin & Pervan, 2003; Ward & Daniel, 2006). For example, investigation by Sohal & Ng (1998) found that the potential of IT had not been utilised to meet competitive challenges in large Australian organisations due to the lack of use of proper IEM. Similarly, Ezingeard et al. (1998) reported that half of the U.K. organisations in their study did not adopt proper BRP to identify benefits as well as to ensure these expected benefits were eventually realised.

Recent research indicates that organisations that adopt proper IT evaluation practices (i.e. IEM and BRP) tend to achieve better business performance (e.g. Tallon et al., 2000; Melville et al., 2004). It has been also suggested that there might be a connection between IT evaluation practices and IT maturity (e.g. Hackbarth & Kettinger, 2004). However, other studies have also found that IT evaluation research often ignores the contextual determinants of IT evaluation and relationships with other organisational factors (e.g. Serafeimidis & Smithson, 2003; Byrd et al., 2006). In fact, several studies call for consideration of other organisational drivers such as the level of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness (e.g. Slade & Van Akkeren, 2002; Soliman & Janz, 2004) and its relationship with the business performance. The lack of studies in this area has prompted the authors to look closely at the realisation of B2B e-commerce benefits (as one major determinant of business performance), as well as the underlying determinants and antecedents of IEM and BRP adoption.

Given the financial stakes involved, better understanding of the financial implication of B2B e-commerce investments and their relationships with IT evaluation practices and other organisational drivers in large organisations is warranted. Hence, the main objective of this paper is to examine the relationships between organisational IT maturity, IEM, BRP, B2B e-commerce adoption readiness, and B2B e-commerce benefits in large Australian organisations.

## Theoretical background

Management of B2B e-commerce is becoming an increasingly important but problematic issue in IT investment management. It involves the most disruptive form of change and challenges existing business models, leaving senior executives with little to draw on from their past experience to manage and evaluate benefits arising from B2B e-commerce (Burn & Ash, 2005). For example, the use of enterprise wide systems and interorganisational networks to implement B2B e-commerce affect a diverse range of interest groups where the benefits are typically realised through extensive changes to business practices and decision making (Markus et al., 2000; Ward et al., 2005). Moving to a B2B environment involves a major organisational change. E-commerce forces change to occur in three corporate domains – technology, process, and people – at both a strategic and operational level and to focus on relationships (Norris et al., 2000; Kandampully, 2003). Ash & Burn (2006) found that interactions in e-business environments reflected the level of IT maturity and e-business preparedness in the participant organisations where each component must be aligned, along with the enabling technology, to the strategic B2B initiatives. Managing for such B2B e-commerce investments, therefore, has to encompass organisational capabilities to measure and create benefits and hence requires different approaches or perspectives that can be tailored to organisational needs at different stages of B2B e-commerce growth (Ash & Burn, 2003).

## Research model

The main research objective is to examine the relationships between organisational IT maturity, IEM, BRP, B2B e-commerce adoption readiness, and B2B e-commerce benefits in large Australian organisations. Literature stretching back to Kriebel & Raviv (1980) has emphasised the role of economic and organisational perspectives in the field of information systems (Banker et al., 1998). If either or both of these perspectives influence the realisation of B2B e-commerce benefits then there will be implications for how large Australian organisations manage and evaluate their IT investments. In particular, this will provide a theoretical foundation to understand these organisational drivers (i.e. organisational IT maturity, IT evaluation practices, and B2B e-commerce adoption readiness) and their linkage with the process of B2B e-commerce benefits realisation. Hence, the research model for this study is derived from these two different perspectives. The production economics perspective establishes a direct connection between IT evaluation practices and B2B e-commerce benefits. The organisational perspective forms the basis for conceptualising the linkage between strategic alignment and realisation of B2B e-commerce benefits.

Economic view of IT investment management process The production economics perspective views organisational activities (e.g. the adoption of IEM and BRP) as economic production processes (Banker et al., 1998). In these processes, input factors such as knowledge and practices assist in transforming B2B e-commerce investments into outputs such as B2B e-commerce benefits (see Figure 1(a)).

This perspective reveals two important issues relating to these processes (Figure 1(a)). The first issue relates to one input factor (i.e. BRP) having a mediating role between another input factor (i.e. IEM) and the output factor (i.e. B2B e-commerce benefits). Although there is some evidence that the adoption of IT evaluation practices might be associated with better business performance in organisations (Chan et al., 1997; Tallon et al., 2000; Melville et al., 2004), it remains unclear as to whether or not the use of IEM alone can directly lead to increased B2B e-commerce benefits. Several studies have indicated that the use of both IEM and BRP can assist organisations in ensuring that the desired business changes and benefits are clearly defined, identified, evaluated, and realised (Shang & Seddon, 2002; Lin et al., 2005a; Ward & Daniel, 2006; Lin et al., 2007a). The second issue highlighted by this perspective relates the capability of the organisations to utilise these input factors (i.e. IEM and BRP). Despite the fact that many studies have recognised the need for taking organisational drivers into account when managing IT investments and evaluating their benefits, few have ever considered them (Mahmood & Mann, 1997; Grover et al., 1998).

Organisational view of IT investment management process While the production economics perspective is adapted to examine the effect of IEM and BRP on B2B e-commerce benefits, the organisational perspective is used to view the contribution of the existing organisational drivers (e.g. IT infrastructures and supporting business architecture), which are complementary in supporting these input factors (e.g. IEM and BRP) towards the generation of B2B e-commerce benefits. According to this perspective, organisations wishing to increase their outputs need to have a close linkage between their IT infrastructures and supporting business architecture (Parker, 1996; Byrd et al., 2006). This recognises that both the IT and business domains (i.e. IT infrastructures and supporting business architecture) should be managed and positioned in terms of their respective IT and business focuses (Venkatraman et al., 1993). Organisations wishing to increase their outputs need to have a closer strategic alignment between their exiting organisational drivers (Parker, 1996). Not surprisingly, organisations with a closer strategic alignment between organisational drivers have been found to exhibit better performance (Chan et al., 1997). In other words, in order for organisations to derive significant benefits from their B2B e-commerce investments, senior executives have to ensure that a clear linkage exists between the level of organisational IT maturity and B2B e-commerce adoption readiness (Parker, 1996; Chan et al., 1997; Ash & Burn, 2006) (Figure 1(b)).

A research model of IT investment management Although many studies have acknowledged the need for examining the IT investment management process from different perspectives (Ein-Dor & Segev, 1978), few have actually done so. For example, very few studies have examined the strategic alignment between IT investment management process and organisational drivers from both the economic and organisational perspectives (Tallon et al., 2000; Byrd et $a l . _ { \cdot }$ , 2006). According to Byrd et al. (2006) and Fonstad & Robertson (2006), strategic alignment involves the coordination and integration of linkages between various organisational drivers and IT investment and has a direct impact on organisational performance. Therefore, research in this area is warranted since the lack of strategic alignment between IT investment and organisational drivers can have a significant impact on organisations’ B2B e-commerce benefits (Willcocks & Lester, 1997; Melville et al., 2004). That is, organisational drivers such as organisational IT maturity and B2B e-commerce adoption readiness are complementary in supporting the use of IEM and BRP to generate B2B e-commerce benefits.

It is also arguable that the adoption of IT evaluation practices assists in applying the existing IT infrastructure in the process of generating B2B e-commerce benefits. The adoption of IEM and BRP enables organisations to undertake planning and evaluation activities (Tallon et al., 2000), which will then force them to make some serious efforts to formulate strategies to utilise existing capabilities in terms of organisational IT maturity and B2B e-commerce adoption readiness in order to maximise B2B e-commerce benefits.

Therefore, we extend our argument to posit that closer strategic alignment between the levels of organisational IT maturity, B2B e-commerce adoption readiness, and the adoption of proper IEM and BRP can lead to higher level of benefits from B2B e-commerce investments (Figure 1(c)).

## Research hypotheses

Organisational IT maturity refers to an organisation’s capability to utilise its existing IT infrastructure to obtain business value (Galliers & Sutherland, 1991). It is about the organisation’s ability to effectively deploy IT infrastructure (e.g. organisational strategy and skills) towards the achievement of benefits such as competitive advantage in line with business goals. B2B e-commerce adoption readiness, on the other hand, relates to the degree of fit between the adoption of B2B e-commerce and the values, beliefs, and business needs of organisations as well as goals, interests, and expectations of stakeholders (Bui et al., 2002; Soliman & Janz, 2004). In other words, it is the aptitude of an organisation to adopt B2B e-commerce in accordance with its organisational values and in the interests of its stakeholders. There is some evidence that the level of organisational maturity is correlated with e-commerce readiness (Grant, 1999; Misra & Dhingra, 2002; Rodriguez-Abitia et $a l . ,$ 2005). A higher level of organisational IT maturity is more likely to result in a higher level of organisational readiness to adopt B2B e-commerce and vice versa (Byrd et $a l . ,$ , 2006). For example, an organisation that has reached a more advanced stage in the development of overall goals and strategies is more likely to gain acceptance from its stakeholders towards its organisational values, beliefs, and business needs (i.e. B2B e-commerce adoption readiness). Thus, the first hypothesis is derived:

## H1 Organisational IT maturity is positively related to B2B e-commerce adoption readiness.

Recent research findings also suggest that the level of organisational IT maturity is often associated with the adoption of IT evaluation practices (Lin et al., 2005a). Organisational IT maturity refers to an organisation’s capability to utilise IT to assist organisations in undertaking IT evaluation practices. For example, it has been found that organisations at different stages of B2B maturity adopt different levels of IEM and BRP, reflecting the different value propositions and strategic focus of incremental B2B e-commerce systems (Hackbarth & Kettinger, 2004; Burn & Ash, 2005). The adoption of IEM and BRP is crucial for organisations to measure the contribution of their investments in IT/B2B e-commerce to business performance as well as to find reliable ways to ensure that the business benefits from these investments are actually realised and delivered (Sohal & $\mathrm { N g } ,$ 1998; Thatcher & Pingry, 2004; Al-Yaseen et $a l . ,$ 2006). Therefore, organisations need to ensure that their level of organisational IT maturity is favourable to the adoption of IEM and BRP (Lin et al., 2005b) in order to maximise the likelihood of successful adoption of these practices.

This is not surprising given that the nature of IT/B2B e-commerce investments and the benefits they can deliver have been subject to continuous and dramatic change, hence the evaluation of these investments requires multidimensional measures (i.e. a complex tangle of financial, organisational, social, procedural, and technical threads) (Serafeimidis & Smithson, 2003). The complexities and difficulties in IT evaluation processes often result in frustration and failure. According to Standing & Lin (2007), an organisation’s ability in utilising its existing IT infrastructure to overcome, for example, organisational constraints has a significant impact on the level and type of IT evaluation methods or processes adopted by the organisation. In other words, a relatively high level of organisational IT maturity is required to lower the level of difficulty in adopting IEM and BRP. As a result, organisations with a higher level of IT maturity are more capable of successfully adopting more sophisticated IEM and BRP than those with lower levels of organisational IT maturity (Tsao et $a l . ,$ , 2004). Furthermore, as noted earlier, establishing an explicit and clear strategic alignment between the level of organisational IT maturity and the proper appropriation of IEM and BRP can assist organisations in the process of generating B2B e-commerce benefits. Therefore, we argue that the level of organisational maturity can influence the ability of organisations to successfully adopt both IEM and BRP. Thus, the following two hypotheses are derived:

H2 Organisational IT maturity is positively related to IEM adoption.

H3 Organisational IT maturity is positively related to BRP adoption.

The adoption of IEM and BRP becomes increasingly important for organisations wishing to measure their IT/ B2B e-commerce investments and to ensure that their expected benefits are eventually realised and delivered (Love et al., 2005; Lin et al., 2007a). According to the 2003 SIM survey, measuring the value of IT investment remains one of the top five management concerns (Luftman & McLean, 2004). A review of the literature also reveals that organisations that make extensive use of IEM and BRP have higher perceived benefits from IT investments (Tallon et $a l . ,$ , 2000; Melville et $a l . ,$ 2004; Standing & Lin, 2007). IEM is concerned with making decisions about IT/B2B e-commerce investments. It focuses on the selection and evaluation of projects that offer the greatest benefits against the required investment (Willcocks & Lester, 1997). For example, the use of IEM can help to underscore best practices that organisations can apply to future B2B e-commerce investment evaluation processes and this will have further impact on the organisations’ B2B e-commerce benefits (Lin & Pervan, 2003; Lin et al., 2007a). On the other hand, BRP ensures that expected benefits are identified and realised after a decision to invest has been taken (Ward & Daniel, 2006). It helps organisations to gain a clear understanding of what business benefits are to be obtained and the amount of resources required to achieve these results (Ward et $a l . ,$ 1996; Shang & Seddon, 2002). It can further enhance organisations’ abilities to gain higher B2B e-commerce benefits by ensuring that B2B e-commerce investments remain aligned with desired business outcomes in a changing environment (Ward & Daniel, 2006). Thus, the following two hypotheses are derived:

H4 IEM adoption is positively related to B2B e-commerce benefits.

H5 BRP adoption is positively related to B2B e-commerce benefits.

While the establishment of IEM has been shown to play an important role in the successful adoption and operation of B2B e-commerce, IEM has also been found to have a complementary relationship with BRP in terms of managing, monitoring, and realising B2B e-commerce benefits (Ward et al., 1996; Tsao et al., 2004; Lin et al., 2005a). The adoption of these evaluation practices can assist organisations in obtaining successful outcomes for B2B initiatives. As mentioned earlier, this is because IEM is concerned with making investment decisions about IT projects. It is about assessing and choosing the project or projects that at the outset seem to offer the greatest returns or benefits for the outlay. IEM is also implemented to enable active evaluation over the whole investment life cycle (Lin et al., 2007b). BRP extends investment evaluation further by ensuring expected benefits are monitored, tracked, and realised after a decision to invest has been taken. It also involves planning how and when benefits will be realised and deciding who will be responsible for achieving benefits as well as actually overseeing the realisation of benefits (Ward et al., 1996; Shang & Seddon, 2002). Its application can assist the organisation as a whole in understanding how it can continuously contribute to the results and how it can proactively identify, manage, track, and deliver these benefits (Remenyi, 2000; Ward & Daniel, 2006). BRP is used to assess how well organisations are employing their IT/B2B e-commerce investments to increase the benefits they can leverage from them, and to formulate and implement necessary action plans (Ward et al., 1996).

In other words, both IEM and BRP provide organisations with critical but different processes and tools in the evaluation and leverage of benefits from IT/B2B e-commerce investments throughout the project life cycle. IEM needs to be adopted from the very beginning in terms of making important IT/B2B e-commerce investment decisions and then in active evaluation over the entire project life cycle. BRP is adopted to manage and realise B2B e-commerce benefits. Together, IEM and BRP provide a coherent and comprehensive approach to evaluate, identify, and realise the benefits from IT/B2B investments. Thus, the following hypothesis is derived:

## H6 IEM adoption is positively related to BRP adoption.

IEM can assist in evaluating and making strategic decisions with respect to strengthening the degree of fit between the adoption of B2B e-commerce and the values, beliefs, and business needs of organisations (Bui et al., 2003; Soliman & Janz, 2004). In addition, the adoption of IEM provides a means for organisations to undergo routine, recurring and systematic evaluation of their B2B e-commerce investments from the stakeholders’ perspective. That is, the use of IEM allows organisations to manage stakeholders, and initiate corrective action if needed, at regular intervals in order to ensure that there is always a high degree of fit between the introduction of B2B e-commerce and the viewpoints and/or acceptance of the stakeholders. It can assist in undertaking planning activities that focus on the technical, organizational, and financial aspects of an IT/B2B e-commerce investment as well as in conducting appropriate and timely reviews in which organisations can assess whether the organisational values, management goals, and stakeholder’s expectations are still aligned with its B2B e-commerce strategies (Barua et al., 2000; Tallon et al., 2000). As a consequence, IEM can serve as a learning tool that can help senior management within the organisations to undertake ‘best practice’ (Earl, 1989; Love et al., 2005) and that will ultimately significantly strengthen the organisation’s B2B e-commerce adoption readiness. Thus, the following hypothesis is derived:

## H7 IEM adoption is positively related to B2B e-commerce adoption readiness.

Finally, B2B e-commerce benefits also arise from an explicit and clear strategic alignment with B2B e-commerce adoption readiness. Actual realisation of B2B e-commerce benefits depends not only on organisations’ own efforts to carry out necessary evaluation processes (Bui et al., 2002) but also on their readiness to adopt e-commerce initiatives (Barua et al., 2000; Huang et al., 2004). Senior management and employees within the organisation need to ensure that their goals, expectations, and interests are aligned with the adoption of B2B e-commerce. To do so, organisations must invest in reinforcing their own values, beliefs, and business needs in the process of adopting e-commerce initiatives (Barua et al., 2000). Organisations that cultivate buy-in of B2B e-commerce adoption and communicate their values, beliefs, and business needs while fulfilling the expectations and interest of stakeholders tend to perform better in terms of competitive advantages and e-commerce benefits (April & Cradock, 2000; UNCTAD, 2001; Molla & Licker, 2005). Thus, a higher level of organisational B2B e-commerce adoption readiness will, therefore, result in a higher level of realised B2B e-commerce benefits:

H8 B2B e-commerce adoption readiness is positively related to B2B e-commerce benefits.

## Research methodology

## Measurement

A survey approach was selected for this research as it is an effective means for the collection of a large quantity of data in a time-efficient manner (Sekaran, 1984; Galliers, 1991) and, further, can provide ‘snapshots’ of practices or views at a particular point in time from which inferences may be made. In this way, it can be used to describe realworld situations and make appropriate generalisations (Galliers, 1991). It also has the advantage of being able to focus on problem solving and pursue a rigorous method to identify problems, gather data, analyse the data, and draw valid conclusions (Sekaran, 1984). A survey is particularly useful when a research (such as this one) has clearly defined independent and dependent constructs and a plausible model of the expected relationships that are examined against observations of the phenomenon (Pinsonneault & Kraemer, 1993). Therefore, a survey was adopted in this research to investigate and examine several aspects of IT evaluation practices in large Australian organisations investing in B2B e-commerce.

Respondents were asked to indicate their agreement on a 6-point scale (1 for strongly disagree and 6 for strongly agree) with statements concerning five main constructs (see Appendix A): (1) organisational IT maturity; (2) B2B e-commerce adoption readiness; (3) IEM; (4) BRP; and (5) B2B e-commerce benefits. A 6-point Likert scale was used for all five constructs because the scale for organisational IT maturity was based on Galliers and Sutherland’s Revised Stages of Growth Model (Galliers & Sutherland, 1991) which was represented as six stages, each with its particular set of conditions. Although there has been some debate regarding the number of classes in the ordinal scale, it is generally accepted that no systematic and significant differences exist in 6-point scales over other point scales (Matell & Jacoby, 1971; Wyatt & Meyers, 1987; Chang, 1994).

Higher ratings for these questions indicated: (1) higher level of organisational IT maturity; (2) higher level of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness; (3) higher level of IEM adoption; (4) higher level of BRP adoption; and (5) higher level of B2B e-commerce benefits obtained by the organisation. Reliability analysis (alpha) was conducted on these five main constructs (see Table 1). Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics and Pearson’s correlation for the constructs used in this study. There was no evidence of multicollinearity in the data as the coefficients were all under 0.50.

The organisational IT maturity scale was based on Galliers and Sutherland’s Revised Stages of Growth Model (Galliers & Sutherland, 1991) which can be represented as six stages, each with its particular set of conditions. This scale assesses an organisation’s capability to utilise its existing IT infrastructure to obtain business value. The scale was revised into a four-item measurement. The alpha value for this scale was 0.84, indicating acceptable values of internal consistency (Nunnally, 1978). This scale measured the organisational IT maturity of an organisation in terms of its stage of growth for each of the four elements – strategy, style, skills, and overall goals.

The B2B e-commerce adoption readiness scale was derived from Bui et al. (2002), Slade & Van Akkeren (2002), and Soliman & Janz (2004). The scale measured the degree of fit between the adoption of B2B e-commerce and the values, beliefs, and business needs of organisations as well as goals, interests, and expectations of stakeholders (Bui et al., 2002; Soliman & Janz, 2004). The scale has three items and the alpha value for this scale is 0.73

The IEM and BRP scales were derived from Ward et al. (1996) and Ward & Daniel (2006). The scale measured the use of IEM and BRP of large organisations investing in B2B e-commerce investments. Each scale has three items and alpha values for these two scales are 0.87 and 0.83, respectively. The IEM scale measured the usage, wide use, and effective use of the methodology for the B2B e-commerce investments whereas the BRP scale measured the steps and processes taken to identify, review, evaluate, and realise the benefits during and after the implementation of B2B e-commerce projects.

The B2B e-commerce benefits scale was derived from scales used by Teo & Ranganathan (2004) and Tsao et al. (2004). The scale has three items and the alpha value for this scale is 0.80. The scale measured the benefits (i.e. corporate images enhancement, increased sales, and cost reductions) obtained through the adoption of B2B e-commerce. In the absence of objective data on B2B e-commerce benefits, the IT executives’ perceptions were used. Although there has been some debate regarding the legitimacy of perceptual measures as a proxy for objective measures of B2B e-commerce benefits, research has succeeded in alleviating some of the concerns by showing that perceptual measures of organisational performance has a strong positive relationship with more traditional objective measures (Tallon et al., 2000). For example, a study by Venkatraman & Ramanujam (1987) showed that there was a high degree of correlation between perceptual and objective performance measures in the process of measuring performance of several competing organisations. This reflects the degree of experience of IT executives and their constant involvement in the IT investment process (Watson, 1990). Indeed, several studies have shown that IT executives’ perceptions are critical to understanding how IT affects the performance of organisations (Boynton et al., 1994; Grover et al., 1998).

All measures were analysed for reliability and validity in accordance with the guidelines set out by Jo¨reskog & So¨rbom (1993). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to construct a measurement model composed of four antecedent constructs of B2B e-commerce benefits (IT maturity, B2B e-commerce adoption readiness, IEM, and BRP) and B2B e-commerce benefits using maximum likelihood in LISREL 8.30. All constructs within the model were regarded as separate reflective measures. Overall, the resulting fit indexes indicated that the measurement model fitted the data well: $\chi ^ { 2 } = 1 0 5 . 2 8$ (94 degrees of freedom), $P { = } 0 . 2 0 $ , comparative fit index (CFI) ¼ 0.987, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) ¼ 0.026, goodness-of-fit index (GFI) ¼ 0.932, and adjusted goodness-of-fit index $( \mathrm { A G F I } ) = 0 . 9 0 1$

Moreover, the ranges of all factor loadings and the measurement errors were acceptable and significant at $\begin{array} { r } { \alpha = 0 . 0 5 } \end{array}$ , which provided evidence of convergent validity.

Furthermore, three types of validity were assessed to validate our measurement model: content validity, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. Content validity was established by ensuring consistency between the measurement items and the extant literature. This was done by interviewing IT executives and pilot-testing the instrument. Churchill (1979) has suggested that convergent and discriminant validities should be examined for construct validity. Therefore, we assessed convergent validity by examining composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) from the measures (Hair et al., 1998). Our CR values of the five constructs were between 0.73 and 0.88 and all were above the suggested minimum of 0.70 (Hair et al., 1998). Their AVE values were between 0.49 and 0.66 and these values provided further evidence of convergent validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981) (see Table 1). These AVE values could also be used to assess discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981), which was evident in the results of this study as AVE values for all constructs were higher than the largest squared pairwise correlation between each construct (0.24) (Espinoza, 1999).

## Data collection

This survey was conducted in 2005. Prior to mailing out the main survey, a pilot survey of IT executives of 10 organisations (in addition to interviews with five IT executives) was conducted. The comments about the questionnaire were quite positive and therefore, the questionnaire was not significantly altered for the main survey. For the main survey, questionnaires were sent to IT executives of 900 Australian organisations randomly selected from the top 2000 Australian organisations (Dun and Bradstreet mailing list). Two follow-up mailings were then carried out to increase the response rate. In total, 181 responses were received, representing a response rate of 20.1%. Chi-squared goodness-of-fit tests, on total revenue, number of employees, and industry type, showed that the sample respondents were not significantly different to the target population $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 6 . 7 2 _ { \mathrm { r e v e n u e } }$ and $5 . 9 6 _ { \mathrm { e m p l o y e e } } , \mathrm { d f } = 4 ; \chi ^ { 2 } { = } 1 6 . 4 6 _ { \mathrm { i n d u s t r y } } , \mathrm { d f } { = } 1 0 , P { > } 0 . 0 5 )$ Thus, the sample is representative of the target population. ## Discussion

This paper has developed an IT investment management model that examines the effects of the level of organisational IT maturity, the level of IEM and BRP adoption, and the level of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness on the realisation of B2B e-commerce benefits from both the production economics and organisational perspectives. This model was empirically tested using data collected from large Australian organisations. The results support almost all the hypotheses proposed (except for H4). We argue here that it is not simply the adoption of IT but that it is the way IT is implemented, used, and managed within an organisation that drives B2B e-commerce benefits.

Moreover, the results also provided a number of interesting insights. First, both B2B e-commerce adoption readiness and the use of BRP have mediating effects on the relationship between IEM and B2B e-commerce benefits. While IEM has a positive impact on BRP and B2B ecommerce adoption readiness it has no direct influence on the realisation of B2B e-commerce benefits. It has to be used in conjunction with BRP and/or with an appropriate level of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness to have any significant impact on B2B e-commerce benefits.

Second, the early adoption of IEM can assist an organisation in increasing its level of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness and/or in preparing the subsequent adoption of BRP in the process of realising benefits in B2B e-commerce. The adoption of IEM alone is insufficient in terms of ensuring that the B2B e-commerce benefits expected by organisations are actually realised.

Third, the effects of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness and organisational IT maturity differ in terms of the generation of B2B e-commerce benefits. B2B e-commerce adoption readiness has a strong positive impact on B2B e-commerce benefits whereas organisational IT maturity only affects B2B e-commerce benefits indirectly through B2B e-commerce adoption readiness or the adoption of BRP. This indicates that the level of organisational IT maturity is an antecedent driver of IEM, BRP, and B2B e-commerce adoption readiness in terms of realising B2B e-commerce benefits.

## Conclusions and implications

The results demonstrate that B2B e-commerce benefits can be obtained from a more proactive approach to strategic alignment between organisational IT maturity, B2B e-commerce adoption readiness, IEM, and BRP. The results also indicate that organisational IT maturity has a significant positive impact on the organisation’s level of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness and the level of IEM and BRP adoption. However, the adoption of IEM has only an indirect impact on the organisation’s B2B e-commerce benefits, depending on its level of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness and/or the adoption of BRP.

In summary, this study examines the effects of several organisational drivers on the realisation of B2B ecommerce benefits and, in the process, empirically validates an IT investment management model that will be of use to both researchers and practitioners. The findings provide a number of implications for theory and practice.

## Theoretical implications

A key finding of our study is the positive relationship between IT evaluation practices (i.e. IEM and BRP) and B2B e-commerce benefits (Figure 2). The survey results highlight that evaluation practices assist in the identification, measurement, and realisation of B2B e-commerce benefits. In addition, another organisational driver, organisation’s level of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness also has a direct positive impact on the realisation of B2B e-commerce benefits. The findings demonstrate that a higher degree of fit between the adoption of B2B e-commerce and the values, beliefs, and business needs of the organisation as well as the goals, interests, and expectations of stakeholders can lead to a higher level of B2B e-commerce benefits.

Another key contribution is associated with the development of an integrated IT Investment Management Model. As noted earlier, both the economic and organisational perspectives have played an important role in the field of information systems (Banker et al., 1998). Although many studies have acknowledged the need for examining the IT investment management process from different perspectives (Ein-Dor & Segev, 1978), few have actually done so. This study helps to fill this gap and provides some new findings on the IT investment management process. First, the results show that an economic perspective alone cannot explain the linkage between IT/B2B e-commerce investments and B2B ecommerce benefits. Second, both of these two perspectives have to be taken into account in an integrated manner in order to provide a more complete picture of the IT investment management process. In other words, the integrated model provides organisations with a useful tool to examine the influence of various organisational drivers on the realisation of B2B e-commerce benefits.

This research study also fills a gap in the literature as it for the first time defines the significant role played by organisational IT maturity as an antecedent driver of IEM, BRP, and B2B e-commerce adoption readiness and their roles in realising B2B e-commerce benefits. These relationships have been examined in an integrated model. This study provides an empirical evidence for the importance of an organisation’s capability to utilise its existing IT infrastructure and processes to obtain business value in the context of B2B e-commerce.

Finally, previous research has argued that IT/B2B e-commerce investments do not always lead to benefits (Osei-Bryson & Ko, 2004; Thatcher & Pingry, 2004). This study provides a theoretical explanation as to why. Owing to the complementarity nature of these organisational drivers, they need to be managed together, not in isolation. Focusing on one driver of a strategic alignment and neglecting the other drivers will not lead to desirable performance effects (Grant, 2003). For example, IEM has been found to have no direct influence on the realisation of B2B e-commerce benefits. It has to be used in conjunction with BRP and/or with appropriate level of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness to have any significant impact on B2B e-commerce benefits.

## Managerial implications

Determining whether or not IT/B2B e-commerce investments contribute to business value is still a critical issue for IT executives. This research provides some insights for understanding why some large organisations fail to realise benefits from their B2B e-commerce investments. As revealed by our model, relying on individual drivers of alignment in isolation is not likely to achieve the desired outcomes. This is despite the fact that there is a strategic alignment relationship between IT investment management process and organisational drivers, and the organisational drivers are complementary in terms of realising the expected B2B e-commerce benefits. Moreover, in light of the recent interest in B2B e-commerce investments, it is critical for senior IT executives to carefully assess their B2B e-commerce investments and ensure that the required level of organisational IT maturity, the level of B2B e-commerce adoption readiness, and the level of IEM and BRP adoption are appropriate in the process of realising B2B e-commerce benefits.

Based on our findings we also recommend that senior IT executives in large organisations should focus on making IT an integral part of their business strategy. They should conduct an assessment of their IT and B2B e-commerce investments so that features and benefits can be readily identified and matched with the strategic vision of the company. For example, one such approach is to develop an IT Strategic Impact Grid (Nolan & McFarlan, 2005). This grid can be used to identify firstly, how much the organisation relies on B2B e-commerce for operations and secondly, how much it relies on IT for competitive edge through B2B e-commerce providing new value-added services and products. This analysis will locate the organisation as defensive or offensive IT users and hence determine appropriate strategic modes for both B2B investments and IT investment evaluation processes. Using this, organisations can develop an expected IT benefits evaluation and management plan; determine if sufficient B2B e-commerce benefits exist; and engender the appropriate organisational culture supportive of adopting B2B e-commerce infrastructures and other evaluation processes.

## Limitations and future research

Finally, the authors acknowledge that this study has certain limitations. First, while there has been a lot of research undertaken on IT evaluation as a whole, there has been limited research undertaken in the area of IT benefits realisation and organisational IT maturity in the process of measuring IT investments, and as a result comparisons are difficult to undertake. A second limitation may be seen in the choice of constructs that may not fully capture the complex nature of the BRP of the business environment in which large organisations operate. Equally, benefits and organisational IT maturity do not remain static, particularly during the different stages of an IT project life cycle. ## Dependent constructs

B2B e-commerce adoption readiness (6-point Likert scale, Totally Disagree–Totally Agree; Source: Adapted from Bui et al. (2002), Slade & Van Akkeren (2002), and Soliman & Janz (2004))

 The adoption of B2B e-commerce is consistent with the values, beliefs, and business needs of your organisation

 The adoption of B2B e-commerce is consistent with your top management’s goals.

 The adoption of B2B e-commerce is consistent with your employees’ interests and expectations.

IT investment evaluation methodologies (IEM) (6-point Likert scale, Totally Disagree–Totally Agree; Source: Adapted from Ward et al. (1996) and Ward & Daniel (2006))

 Your organisation has a formal IT investment evaluation methodology

 The IT investment evaluation methodology is widely used in your organization

 The use of IT investment evaluation methodology is effective in ensuring successful information systems

IT benefits realisation processes (BRP) (6-point Likert scale, Totally Disagree–Totally Agree; Source: Adapted from Ward et al. (1996) and Ward & Daniel (2006))

 Your organisation has a formal process to identify IT benefits

 Your organisation takes steps to review IT benefits after implementation

 Your organisation conducts some form of IT benefits evaluation after implementation and the results are fed back to whoever approved the project

 B2B e-commerce has enhanced the corporate image of your organisation

 B2B e-commerce has helped increase sales in your company

 B2B e-commerce has helped to reduce costs of your organisation
