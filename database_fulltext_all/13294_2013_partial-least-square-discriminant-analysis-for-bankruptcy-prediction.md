# Partial Least Square Discriminant Analysis for bankruptcy prediction

Carlos Serrano-Cinca ⁎, Begoña Gutiérrez-Nieto

Department of Accounting and Finance, University of Zaragoza, Spain

a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 29 June 2011 Received in revised form 13 September 2012 Accepted 25 November 2012 Available online 3 December 2012

Keywords: Bankruptcy Financial ratios Banking crisis Solvency Data mining PLS-DA

## a b s t r a c t

This paper uses Partial Least Square Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) for the prediction of the 2008 USA banking crisis. PLS regression transforms a set of correlated explanatory variables into a new set of uncorrelated variables, which is appropriate in the presence of multicollinearity. PLS-DA performs a PLS regression with a dichotomous dependent variable. The performance of this technique is compared to the performance of 8 algorithms widely used in bankruptcy prediction. In terms of accuracy, precision, F-score, Type I error and Type II error, results are similar; no algorithm outperforms the others. Behind performance, each algorithm assigns a score to each bank and classi<sup>fi</sup>es it as solvent or failed. These results have been analyzed by means of contingency tables, correlations, cluster analysis and reduction dimensionality techniques. PLS-DA results are very close to those obtained by Linear Discriminant Analysis and Support Vector Machine.

## 1. Introduction

Bankruptcy prediction from <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios using mathematical models is a classical approach in data mining research. Since Beaver's [7] pioneer work, based on univariate ratio analysis, many different techniques have been employed in this context. Altman [3] used Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA); Ohlson [42] used Logistic Regression (LR); Marais et al. [35] used Decision Trees such as Id3, C4.5 and Random Trees; Tam and Kiang [57] used Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), a neural network model and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN); Serrano-Cinca [54] and du Jardin and Séverin [18] applied Self Organizing Feature Maps; Fan and Palaniswami [21] used Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Sarkar and Sriram [52] applied Naive Bayes (NB). Techniques of ensembles, such as Boosting or Bagging, have been applied by Foster and Stine [26], who combined C4.5 and Boosting; while Mukkamala et al. [39] combined Bagging and Random Tree (BRT). See Olson et al. [44] for a recent comparative analysis on data mining methods for bankruptcy prediction. This paper applies Partial Least Square Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) to the 2008 banking crisis in the USA. To the best of our knowledge, this technique has not previously been applied to bankruptcy prediction.

Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression combines features from Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Multiple Linear Regression [60]. PLS is a mathematical estimation approach that builds a model by sequentially adding data points so that model parameters are continuously updated. PLS models are popular in structural model building and in regression analysis. PLS-DA is based on the PLS regression model, being the dependent variable a categorical one. This approach is useful for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation tasks [6]. For example, PLS-DA is a standard tool in Chemometrics, the science that analyzes chemical data [62]. Its attraction resides in its ability to successfully address the problem of multicollinearity [6,59].

Multicollinearity is a major problem when building models based on <sup>fi</sup>nancial data. Financial analysts request tools able to accurately predict distress from <sup>fi</sup>nancial data. But they also want to model bankruptcy symptoms, by identifying the relevant variables. However, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to select an appropriate model when using collinear data, as there is no unique data reduction method, and different orderings of the hypothesis testing procedure result in different models, something that affects interpretability. Multicollinearity and model selection procedures in regression have long been debated in Econometrics; see for example Hendry and Mizon [28]. Only when regressors are orthogonal, any model selection procedure ends up by identifying the same model. This is certainly not the case with <sup>fi</sup>nancial data. A possible solution is to do regression analysis on principal components, which are orthogonal by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, but this always results in a loss of information in the data set since only a small number of components are employed in the distress prediction model. In this paper we consider if the PLS-DA methodology offers a possible way forward in this context. The paper poses two research questions: RQ1: How does PLS-DA perform in terms of model interpretability, when facing correlated data, compared to other techniques? The case of multicollinearity in <sup>fi</sup>nancial information will be specially studied. RQ2: How does PLS-DA perform in terms of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation accuracy, compared to other techniques?

The paper compares PLS-DA with 8 algorithms previously used in distress prediction (LDA, LR, MLP, KNN, NB, SVM, C4.5, and BRT). The data used relates to the 2008–2011 USA banking crisis. The paper joins a long literature on bankruptcy prediction. Many studies analyze bankruptcy, and compare the results obtained by the different techniques, see Ravi Kumar and Ravi [50] and Demyanyk and Hasan [16] for a revision. It can be concluded that no technique is clearly better than others, because it depends on the problem analyzed and the performance measure chosen, Caruana and Niculescu-Mizil [9]. LDA is optimum if data satis<sup>fi</sup>ed some statistical properties. Unfortunately, <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios are not normally distributed, as shown by Ezzamel and Mar-Molinero [20], and this calls for the use of alternative techniques. LR needs fewer requisites, and being regression based, results can be interpreted in a standard way. When relationships are not linear, MLP performs well, because neural networks are universal approximators, as Hornik et al. [29] have shown. But results by MLP are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to interpret. By contrast, decision tree algorithms, such as C4.5, have the advantage of resulting in general rules that can be incorporated in the design of expert systems. Boosting and Bagging techniques improve the performance of decision trees. The strength of PLS-DA stems from its capability to deal with multicollinearity, because it transforms original variables into orthogonal components. It is also robust to missing data and skew distributions, see Cassel et al. [10]. It can also deal with the ‘too few cases/too many variables’ problem. These problems are common in <sup>fi</sup>nancial information; a priori, PLS-DA seems to be a promising technique.

In order to compare PLS-DA with the rest of the techniques, a benchmark on which to analyze performance has to be established. But performance can be measured in many ways. Ferri et al. [24] analyze the behavior of 18 different performance metrics; being accuracy, precision and F-score the most popular. One technique can obtain better results than other by using a given performance measure; but it can obtain poorer results by using another performance measure, Caruana and Niculescu-Mizil [9]. But it can also be the case that two techniques achieve the same performance, but one of them correctly classi<sup>fi</sup>es some cases and the other one correctly classi<sup>fi</sup>es different cases. In this paper we analyze — by means of contingency tables and Phi correlations — coincidences and divergences in classi<sup>fi</sup>cations produced by the 9 techniques. Each technique generates a score for each bank, which can be interpreted as a solvency measure. This resulted in a two-way table with banks in rows and the 9 scores in the columns. This table can be analyzed with multivariate analysis, by using Cluster Analysis (CA) and Categorical Principal Components Analysis (CATPCA). This approach results in a taxonomy of techniques, it being specially interesting to know the techniques closest to PLS-DA.

The paper analyzes the 2008 banking crisis. Financial data are taken from the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), an independent agency created by the US Congress to maintain stability and public con-<sup>fi</sup>dence in the banking system. The crisis has caused the failure of an important number of USA banks. 140 banks failed in 2009, 157 in 2010, and there are still banks in dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in 2011, since the crisis is still not over. The FDIC [22] af<sup>fi</sup>rms that there are a good number of banks, 884, on their “problem list.” The procedure followed by this paper, based on pattern recognition, will allow the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of these banks.

The following section presents the conceptual background, with an emphasis on presenting the PLS-DA technique. The third section presents the empirical study, focusing on performance comparison. Finally, conclusions and bibliography are presented.

## 2. Conceptual background

Failure prediction from the analysis of <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios has been a fruitful research line, as shown by the state of the art revisions [16,50,64]. The use of <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios as a tool to analyze <sup>fi</sup>nancial distress has a long pedigree. The work by Beaver [7], who used univariate analysis, marks a starting point. But companies' health has a multivariate nature, which is not captured by Beaver's approach. Altman [3] introduced the use of multivariate techniques, particularly Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA). LDA derives a linear combination of ratios which best discriminate between failed and non-failed <sup>fi</sup>rms. An overall score, known as Altman's Z-score, can be calculated from LDA, and used to estimate the <sup>fi</sup>nancial health of a company. LDA depends on several restrictive assumptions, such as linearity, normality, or independence among predictors. But these assumptions are seldom satis<sup>fi</sup>ed [19]. Ohlson [42] took a step forward by applying Logistic Regression (LR). In common with LDA, LR weights <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios and obtains a score. LR and LDA share the same underlying linear model, but LR does not require multivariate normality, it only requires that variables are distributed in the exponential family [34]. This implies that it is not necessary for some of the restrictive assumptions of LDA to hold. Despite these differences, both LDA and LR obtain very similar results in practical applications with <sup>fi</sup>nancial information. According to the empirical study by Lo [34], the null hypothesis that LDA and LR are equivalent may not be rejected.

A different approach is followed by Curram and Minguers [14] who use decision trees. Decision trees employ a recursive partitioning algorithm to induce rules on a given data set. Widespread algorithms in failure prediction are Id3 and C4.5 by Quinlan [47]. Decision trees have been successful in obtaining useful bankruptcy prediction rules. However, decision tree algorithms may suffer from over<sup>fi</sup>tting: the algorithm reduces training set error at the cost of an increased test set error.

Tam and Kiang [57] employed Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), a neural network model to predict bankruptcy. Hornik et al. [29] proved that under certain weak conditions, multilayer feedforward networks perform as a class of universal approximators. This explains their powerful capability for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. A meta-analysis performed by Adya and Collopy [1] reveals that neural networks outperformed alternative approaches in 19 out of the 22 analyzed studies; but they warn that the bias against publication of negative results may mean that successful applications are over-represented in the published literature. It is dif<sup>fi</sup>- cult to obtain an optimal combination of MLP parameters that produces the best prediction performance: there is a large number of controlling parameters, such as the number of hidden layers, the number of hidden nodes, the learning rate, the momentum term, epochs, and transfer functions [55].

k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) is one of the most straightforward machine learning algorithms [25], which is very useful when there is no prior knowledge about the distribution of the data. Employed by Tam and Kiang [57] and Park and Han [45] in bankruptcy prediction, KNN is basis fundamental for many Case Base Reasoning (CBR) developments, widely used in bankruptcy prediction.

The aim of Support Vector Machine (SVM) is to <sup>fi</sup>nd the best hyperplane that produces the largest separation between failed and non-failed <sup>fi</sup>rms. It has been applied to bankruptcy prediction by Fan and Palaniswami [21]. SVM are based on few restrictive assumptions, and they are obtaining very promising results in failure prediction [50].

The Naive Bayes (NB) algorithm is based on conditional probabilities. NB looks at the historical data and uses Bayes' Theorem to calculate the probability of an event occurring given the probability of another event that has already occurred. Sarkar and Sriram [52] used NB for predicting failure. Among the advantages of NB, Sun and Shenoy [56] highlight that it does not have any requirements on the underlying distributions of the variables; it does not require complete information for observations, and is easy to understand because the relationships among variables are explicitly represented. They empirically compare LR and NB, <sup>fi</sup>nding that there is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference (at the 5% level) between both models' performance.

In recent years, techniques of ensembles combining multiple classi-<sup>fi</sup>ers, like Boosting or Bagging (or bootstrap aggregating) have been developed. They have been applied to bankruptcy prediction by Foster and

Stine [26]. Alfaro et al. [2] blend C4.5 and Boosting. Mukkamala et al. [39] blend Bagging with Random Tree (BRT). Boosting and Bagging algorithms are appropriate to improve classi<sup>fi</sup>cation ability. The main difference between them is that Bagging trains each individual classi<sup>fi</sup>er independently while Boosting trains each individual classi<sup>fi</sup>er dependently [30].

PLS regression (PLS) combines features from Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and multiple regression. PLS tries to provide a dimension reduction strategy in a situation where we want to relate a set of independent variables to a set of dependent variables. But PLS is a supervised method because it uses the independent as well as the dependent variables, whereas PCA is a non-supervised method that considers only independent variables. PLS was developed by Wold [60], trying to solve the multicollinearity problem. This is its main advantage with respect to Ordinary Least Square (OLS) regression, because the PLS components are orthogonal by de<sup>fi</sup>nition. PLS is a useful method when there is a large number of explanatory variables [27]. PLS is also robust to missing data and skew distributions [10]. These are frequent problems when dealing with <sup>fi</sup>nancial information [15]. Yang et al. [63] use feature selection based on PLS and later apply SVM to predict bankruptcy.

Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) is a PLS regression of a set of binary variables on a set of predictor variables [46]. PLS and PLS-DA are standard tools in Chemometrics, and widely used in Genetics and other knowledge areas, see Wold et al. [61] and Kjeldahl and Bro [31]. However, to the best of our knowledge, it has not been used before in failure prediction, credit scoring or similar task that use <sup>fi</sup>nancial information.

## 3. Empirical study

## 3.1. Sample and data

The USA banking crisis, which can be traced to 2008, and is still not over in 2012, sets the framework for this paper. The database comes from the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), and is publicly available on the Internet. 2008 accounting statements from 8293 banks are taken. For each bank 17 <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios were extracted, trying to capture the main issues in banking <sup>fi</sup>nancial health. Ratios and their definition can be found in Table 1.

The banking business consists of buying and selling money. Because money is a standardized product, it is said that banking is a business of intermediation, where margins are crucial. The <sup>fi</sup>rst six ratios relate incomes and expenses to assets. They are: yield on earning assets (INTINCY), cost of funding earning assets (INTEXPY), net interest margin (NIMY), noninterest income to earning assets (NONIIY), noninterest expense to earning assets (NONIXY), and net operating income to assets (NOIJY). The next set of ratios concern pro<sup>fi</sup>tability; they relate pro<sup>fi</sup>ts to equity or assets: return on assets (ROA), pretax return on assets (ROAPTX), return on equity (ROE), and retained earnings to average equity (ROEINJR). The next ratio measures ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, EEFFR, a key aspect in banking. Berger and Humphrey [8] review 130 studies on ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions. EEFFR has been de<sup>fi</sup>ned in such a way that the higher the value of the ratio, the lower the bank's ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Delinquency is measured with the ratio NPERFV, which relates assets that are past due 90 days or more to assets. The last set of ratios measures solvency. They are relevant, because they measure the capital strength of the bank: net loans and leases to deposits (LNLSDEPR), equity capital to assets (EQV), and three ratios that include risk-weighted assets (RBC1AAJ, RBC1RWAJ and RBCRWAJ), whose de<sup>fi</sup>nitions come from Basel Bank for International Settlements [5] and Federal Register [23].

An exploratory data analysis was performed, with results presented in Table 2. Mean, median and standard deviation of the <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios are displayed into two groups: 320 failed and 7973 non-failed banks. Several univariate mean and median tests were performed to assess the univariate discriminatory power of each ratio. Most <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios show statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between solvent and failed banks. Outlier analysis was also performed, but no entity was dropped from the data set because it was felt that atypical <sup>fi</sup>rms are of interest; they usually are close to failure or, contrarily, they are the best <sup>fi</sup>rms. As a general rule, they should not be simply removed from the sample. However, outliers can affect the parameters' estimation in most of the models. Outliers which are in<sup>fl</sup>uential observations should be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed [11]. The only ratio that presented extremely high values was LNLSDEPR, because there were 6 non-failed banks that hardly collect deposits. These 6 banks are in the test sample, so they did not affect the parameter estimation performed by the algorithms.

In bankruptcy studies, it is common to take a sample of failed and solvent <sup>fi</sup>rms, to apply cross validation, jackknife or bootstrap. Lau [33] criticized some of the early studies because holdout samples were drawn from the same time period as the original samples, and this is not intertemporal validation. This is not our case. Another strength is that the entire population of the USA banking sector is analyzed. Algorithms were trained from the point of view of a <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysts situated at the end of 2009. At that time, 2008 annual statements were available, and the 140 failed banks in 2009 were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Failed banks were matched with non-failed banks of the same size. The paired matched sample technique is commonly used in bankruptcy studies [7,65]. To sum up, the trained sample had 140 failed banks and 140 non-failed banks.

The test sample consisted of 180 banks that failed after 2009 (157 failed in 2010 and 23 failed until February 2011, when data were collected), and 7833 non-failed banks. The crisis is still not over, which differs from other studies. FDIC [22] claims that there are 884 banks in their “problem list”, but they do not make the details of this list publicly available, because that could provoke a run bank. Pattern recognition algorithms will obtain a list of banks with similar <sup>fi</sup>nancial features to those already failed. The Type I error denotes to the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of failed <sup>fi</sup>rms as solvent ones, while the Type II error refers to the classi<sup>fi</sup>- cation of solvent <sup>fi</sup>rms as failed ones. When analyzing the test results, Type I error rate is relevant, because the model identi<sup>fi</sup>es as solvent banks that are already failed. But Type II error rate is not relevant because non-failed banks according to the algorithm could still go bankrupt.

## 3.2. Multicollinearity

Multicollinearity arises when there is a strong linear correlation among two or more explanatory variables. High correlations arise naturally in this type of data since ratios share a common numerator or denominator. This can imply that the signs of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for each variable are not as expected in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysis knowledge. Financial information suffers from multicollinearity and the 17 ratios in the study can also suffer from it. Table 3 displays in column a) the expected sign, according to the domain knowledge on <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysis. These signs are coherent with the univariate analysis in Table 2, which shows the relationship between each <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratio and the solvent/ failed status of the banks.

The Variance In<sup>fl</sup>ation Factor (VIF) is commonly used to detect multicollinearity. The VIF is based on the proportion of variance the ith independent variable shares with the other independent variables in the model. If all the variables are uncorrelated with each other, the VIF is 1. As a rule of thumb, a VIF of 10 or greater reveals multicollinearity, considering the cautions stated by O'Brien [41]. Column b) in Table 3 displays VIF values. It can be appreciated that multicollinearity is a severe problem. This is because ROA, ROE, NOIJY, ROAPTX, and ROEINJR are all pro<sup>fi</sup>tability ratios, and the correlation among them is high. The same happens with Basel solvency ratios: RBC1AAJ, RBC1RWAJ, and RBCRWAJ.

Multicollinearity is a major problem in most statistical techniques. Table 3 shows this effect in the Logistic Regression (LR), the most widely used technique in bankruptcy studies. A LR model with the 17 <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios has been calculated. Column c) in Table 3 displays the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient values. The sign of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient is inconsistent with the domain knowledge in 6 out of 17 variables. For instance, it seems that the higher the ratio of income to assets (NOIJY) is, the higher the probability of the bank going bankrupt. This cannot be true. Column c) also shows the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients' signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. Some <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios, relevant in <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysis knowledge, are not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant within the logistic regression. For example, according to this model, none of the three solvency ratios (RBC1AAJ, RBC1RWAJ and RBCRWAJ) are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant.

The most common way of dealing with the multicollinearity prob lem is to use a stepwise procedure. The results of this procedure are displayed in column d). This procedure reduces the number of variables, by obtaining a parsimonious model. But the stepwise procedure has not been speci<sup>fi</sup>cally designed to solve the multicollinearity problem, as some authors have warned. There are various stepwise methods and it is common for them to produce different results. Austin and Tu [4] claim that automated variable selection procedures for logistic regression generate unstable models for predicting. It can be noted that 4 variables enter the parsimonious model, but one of them, NOIJY, has a negative sign, when the theory and the univariate study af-<sup>fi</sup>rm that there is a positive relationship between the NOIJY ratio and bank survival. This is again caused by multicollinearity. In fact, the correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient between NOIJY and ROE (return on equity) is 0.811, which is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and high. To sum up, the researcher has to interpret the logistic regression coef<sup>fi</sup>cients carefully (wrong signs as well as magnitudes of the regression coef<sup>fi</sup>cients), although applying stepwise procedures.

PLS-DA is a technique specially designed by Wold [60] to deal with the multicollinearity problem. This is done by using components that are orthogonal to each other. Columns e) and f) of Table 3 show PLS-DA results. Column e) displays the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation function and column f) the Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) statistic of Wold et al. [62]. The VIP score of a predictor summarizes the contribution a variable makes to the model. The higher the VIP value is, the more important the <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratio is. Those variables with a VIP score higher than 1 are considered as relevant.

In the face of multicollinearity, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) suffers from the same problems as logistic regression. The full LDA model (column g) presents misleading results, making a careful analysis necessary. A way of minimizing the impact of multicollinearity is to apply a stepwise procedure and to calculate the structure matrix, which computes the correlations between variables and standardized canonical discriminant functions. Using this procedure, the results obtained are correct (column h).

Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) are not severely affected by multicollinearity, but model interpretability is very low. One can try to identify which variables are relevant, but multicollinearity makes things hard. ## 3.3. Performance comparison

There are many performance measures for supervised algorithms. Caruana and Niculescu-Mizil [9], Ferri et al. [24] and Martens et al. [36] provide a description of the most frequently used. This study has calculated 6 of them: accuracy, Type I and Type II error rates, precision of failed and precision of non-failed, and F-score. Most bankruptcy studies emphasize accuracy. Type I error rate is also important, because the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation of a failed <sup>fi</sup>rm is considered more costly than the misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation of a healthy <sup>fi</sup>rm. 1 minus Type II error rate is named recall. Often there is an inverse relationship between precision and recall, where it is possible to increase one at the cost of reducing the other [12]. For this reason, it is important to calculate the F-score [58], de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the weighted harmonic mean of precision and recall.

## 3.4. Comparison of coincidences, divergences and scores

It is common to compare pairs of techniques on the basis of correctly classi<sup>fi</sup>ed cases. This can be done by using one or several measures, such as F-score, accuracy or precision. This approach should only be used to compare techniques' performance, but does not result in obtaining robust conclusions on the similarities or differences among techniques. If two techniques result in the same measure, it means that they have correctly classi<sup>fi</sup>ed the same number of banks, but this does not imply that both techniques classify a given bank in the same group. The same happens with two teachers that pass the same percentage of students, but they pass different students.

Table 4 permits comparisons; for example, between K-nearestneighbors (KNN) and Naive Bayes (NB). KNN presents an F-score of 96.87, while NB presents an F-score almost identical, 96.88. If we only take into account the F-score, we will conclude that the technique most alike to KNN is NB. But it is more revealing to know how each bank is classi<sup>fi</sup>ed by each technique. For this reason, we think that two techniques can be described as being similar if they classify the banks in the same group, not if they result in similar percentages of correctly classi<sup>fi</sup>ed cases. In fact, the algorithms of KNN and NB are totally different. For this reason, we propose that coincidences and divergences obtained for every pair of algorithms should be analyzed.

A Cluster Analysis has been obtained from these 0–1 data. The analysis was performed using Ward's method. Cluster Analysis was also performed using other clustering rules, but the results were similar. Fig. 1 shows the dendrogram. Different clusters are displayed. One of them contains the two decision tree techniques (C4.5, BRT) with NB. LR and MLP are very similar, which is not surprising, because LR can be considered a particular case of MLP without hidden layer [53]. LDA is similar to SVM, and this cluster also contains PLS-DA. KNN joins them to form one of the large clusters. The use of Cluster Analysis for algorithm classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is not new. Michie et al. [37] use it as a part of their Statlog study, one of the most comprehensive studies that compare techniques. They use standardized error rates as variables and recognize that “there are some surprising errors in the clusterings”. In our opinion, the method used here for clustering, based on coincidences rather than on error rates, is more revealing.

A more precise procedure is proposed. Beyond the failed/non failed classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, each technique provides a score. Two banks can be classi-<sup>fi</sup>ed as solvent, but a 0.9 score is better than a 0.6 score; similarly, two students may have passed an exam but a high mark is better than a bare pass. This score can be interpreted as a bank's health indicator, and after transformation, as a default probability. A given bank can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as solvent by all the analyzed techniques. This supposes that all the techniques assign a 1 to the bank. However, the techniques really provide a score for every bank, which is a continuous variable transformed later into a dummy variable. For example, the <sup>fi</sup>rst bank in the sample has been classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as solvent by all the 8 techniques. PLS-DA has assigned a 0.97 score, SVM a 0.96 score and Boosting C4.5 a 0.74 score; we can conclude that PLS-DA is closer to SVM than to Boosting C4.5. Scores have been calculated for each bank under each one of the techniques, and a table of correlations between techniques, using scores as observations, has been calculated. Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana [40] examine the relationship between the predictions made by different algorithms and true posterior probabilities. They conclude that some techniques, like MLP or bagged trees predict well-calibrated probabilities, while others such as NB or SVM have biases. To avoid this problem, the Spearman's rank correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient among scores was calculated, which is non-parametric. Table 6 shows Spearman's correlations in brackets. Results of the score analysis are coherent with those obtained from the 0–1 table: the correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient between PLS-DA and LDA is 0.93, which is the highest one.

The scores can also be analyzed with multivariate techniques. To avoid bias problems, the scores have been treated as ordinal variables, by using Categorical Principal Components Analysis (CATPCA). Fig. 2 displays the two <sup>fi</sup>rst principal components. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one explains most of the variance (86.4%) and can be interpreted as bank's solvency. The more to the right a bank is located in Fig. 2, the higher its solvency. The <sup>fi</sup>gure also summarizes the results of the 9 classi<sup>fi</sup>cation techniques. Every technique is represented as a vector in the <sup>fi</sup>gure. The second principal component makes it possible to differentiate between groups of techniques. C4.5, BRT and NB point upwards to the second principal component. PLS-DA, LDA and SVM point downwards. In the middle MLP, LR and KNN are placed. These results are coherent with Cluster Analysis' results.

## 4. Conclusions

This paper compares Partial Least Square Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) with other 8 techniques widely used for classi<sup>fi</sup>cation tasks.

It has performed an empirical study with data of the 2008–2011 USA banking crisis.

The paper has shown that the technique is useful for dimensionality reduction and data selection. The sign of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients were as expected by the theory and the exploratory analysis. PLS-DA performs very well in the presence of multicollinearity because it has been designed to deal with this issue. So, the PLS-DA model interpretability is satisfactory.

In performance terms, the techniques obtain different results depending on the performance measures chosen. Some techniques have more accuracy but less recall. This justi<sup>fi</sup>es the use of performance measures like the F-score, the weighted harmonic mean of precision and recall. The sole analysis of the performance measures obtained by each technique is not enough; in fact, the results could be misleading, especially when obtaining similar performance measures. The study examines what is behind performance, by analyzing how each bank is classi<sup>fi</sup>ed according to the 9 techniques. With this aim, a contingency table has been calculated to compare, in a paired way, the classi<sup>fi</sup>cations of each technique. The paper has also analyzed the scores assigned to each bank by all the different techniques. Results have been analyzed by means of correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients and Cluster Analysis. Results allow obtaining a taxonomy of techniques which classify banks in a similar way. Logistic Regression and Multilayer Perceptron are found to be close. The same happens to Boosting C4.5 and Bagging Random Tree. This approach is another contribution of the paper. To the best of our knowledge, none of the analyzed studies follow a similar approach; they just compare performance measures. It was found that PLS-DA results resemble Linear Discriminant Analysis and Support Vector Machine results. PLS-DA has the advantage that is not affected by multicollinearity, because its components are orthogonal.

The USA banking crisis is still not over. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation does not comment on open <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions, but it recognizes that there are many banks in risk of failure. The score analysis of each of the 9 techniques, interpreted by means of Categorical Principal Components Analysis, allows identifying those <sup>fi</sup>rms with dif<sup>fi</sup>culties.
