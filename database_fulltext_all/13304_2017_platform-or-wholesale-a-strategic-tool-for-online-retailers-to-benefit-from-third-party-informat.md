# PLATFORM OR WHOLESALE? A STRATEGIC TOOL FOR ONLINE RETAILERS TO BENEFIT FROM THIRD-PARTY INFORMATION<sup>1</sup>

Young Kwark Warrington College of Business, University of Florida, 1454 Union Road, Gainesville, FL 32611 U.S.A. {young.kwark@warrington.ufl.edu}

Jianqing Chen and Srinivasan Raghunathan

Jindal School of Management, The University of Texas at Dallas, 800 West Campbell Road, Richardson, TX 75080 U.S.A. {chenjq@utdallas.edu} {sraghu@utdallas.edu}

Online retailing is dominated by a channel structure in which a retailer either buys products from competing manufacturers and resells to consumers (wholesale scheme) or lets manufacturers sell directly to consumers on its platform for a commission (platform scheme). Easy access to publicly available third-party information such as product reviews that facilitate consumers’ purchase decisions is another distinctive and ubiquitous characteristic of online retailing. We show that retailers can use the upstream pricing scheme, wholesale or platform, as a strategic tool to benefit from third-party information. Information on the quality dimension homogenizes consumers’ perceived utility differences between competing products and increases the upstream competition, which benefits the wholesale-based retailer but hurts the platform-based retailer. Information on the fit dimension, in constrast, heterogenizes consumers’ estimated fits to the products and softens the upstream competition, which hurts the wholesale-based retailer but benefits the platform-based retailer. Consequently, when the precision of the third-party information is high (low), a retailer can benefit from third-party information by adopting the wholesale (platform) scheme if the quality dimension plays a dominant role and by adopting the platform (wholesale) scheme if the fit dimension is dominant. Furthermore, the effect of precision improvement on the retailer’s profit depends on the pricing-scheme choice and the relative importance of quality and fit attributes in consumers’ evaluations of products. For instance, when the fit dimension is dominant, increasing the precision can hurt the wholesale-based retailer but benefit the platform-based retailer.

Keywords: Third-party information, pricing scheme, online reviews, competition, game theory

## Introduction

Online retailing has been continuously growing for the past few years. Mulpuru (2012) reported that online retail sales reached \$200 billion in 2011 and accounted for 7% of overall U.S. retail sales. Two distinctive features of online retailing have been well documented (Abhishek et al. 2016; Chen and Xie 2008). One, in addition to selling products using a wholesale scheme in which the retailer purchases products from manufacturers and then resells to consumers, an online retailer (e.g., Amazon) often lets others sell their products on its platform for a commission fee for each sale which we refer to as platform scheme. Two, regardless of the pricing scheme, online retail platforms and third-party websites (e.g., CNET.com and Consumersearch.com) routinely provide features that enable consumers and experts to post product reviews and read others’ reviews. Such publicly available and easily accessible third-party information influences consumer decisions (Cone 2010; MarketingCharts 2007). In particular, third-party information has become an important information source for consumers to mitigate uncertainty about product quality and fit to their needs (Chen and Xie 2008). This paper aims to understand how third-party information affects retailers’ pricing-scheme choice.

In the presence of third-party information, an online retailer needs to rethink not only how the information affects consumers, but also its impact on upstream firms, especially because a retailer generally cannot control the availability or content of third-party information and its influence on consumers, but a dominant retailer is often able to control its relationships with upstream manufacturers. Consequently, from a retailer’s perspective, the impact of third-party information on manufacturers and its upstream strategies is significant.

One important decision related to a retailer’s upstream relationship with manufacturers is the pricing scheme adopted by the retailer. It appears that the pricing scheme—wholesale or platform—is a key strategic variable for online retailers. For instance, Amazon uses the wholesale scheme for only 7% of the more than two million products in the “Electronics” category; the remaining 93% are sold under the platform scheme (Jiang et al. 2011). The relative fraction of items sold using these schemes also depends on product category. For instance, while Amazon directly sells 16.7% of shoes, only 3.1% of products in the “Sports & Outdoors” category and 3.2% of products in the “Jewelry” category are sold directly.

The increasing role of product reviews and other third-party information in consumers’ purchase decisions has attracted researchers’ attention and this has led to a research stream that has examined the effect of various aspects of online product reviews on market outcomes, primarily focusing on the demand side (e.g., Archak et al. 2011; Clemons et al. 2006; Forman et al. 2008; Goes et al. 2014). Furthermore, the ubiquity of platforms in online retailing has given rise to research examining how a retailer’s pricing-scheme choice (wholesale versus platform) is affected by factors such as product popularity (e.g., Jiang et al. 2011), marketing effort (e.g., Hagiu and Wright 2014), and platform technologies and user search skill (e.g., Chen et al. 2016). Our work complements these two streams of research by examining the effect of third-party information on the supply-side competition between the manufacturers and, consequently, on retailers pricing-scheme choice.

We show in this paper that a dominant retailer can indeed use the upstream pricing scheme to benefit from third-party information. Specifically, we show this result by answering the following key questions: How does third-party information affect competition between upstream sellers in the online retailing industry? How does the pricing scheme affect the impact of the third-party information on the retailer and upstream sellers? How different is this impact of the third-party information under different relative importance levels of quality and fit attributes in consumers’ evaluation of products? Under what conditions should the retailer choose one pricing scheme rather than the other (i.e., wholesale versus platform)?

To address the above questions, we develop a game-theoretic model in which a retailer either directly sells two substitutable products produced by different manufacturers or provides a platform for the manufacturers to sell their products. The products differ in both quality and fit to consumers’ needs. Quality of a product refers to the degree of excellence of the product and consumers agree on the preference order of quality in that they all prefer high quality to low quality. In constrat, fit is consumer-specific: different consumers have different needs, with some consumers perceiving one product more suitable than the other product and others perceiving the opposite. Each consumer has her own assessment of the quality of each product and its fit to her need. One or more third parties provide additional information in both the quality and fit dimensions. We distinguish the case in which the quality dimension plays a dominant role in determining consumers’ perceived utility differences between the two products (the quality-dominates-fit case) and the case in which the fit dimension plays an important role such that the fit is critical for some consumers (the fit-dominates-quality case). In general, products evaluated based on objective indices such as product performance, reliability, and durability (e.g., digital cameras) are likely to be qualitydominant, whereas products evaluated based on subjective consumer-specific indices such as experience attributes, features, and aesthetics (e.g., jewelry) are more fit-dominant.

Our main result reveals that quality and fit information play very different roles in changing the upstream competition, and whether the retailer can benefit from the third-party information depends on its pricing-scheme choice, the precision of the third-party information, and the relative importance of quality and fit attributes in consumers’ evaluation of products. When the third-party information mitigates consumers’ uncertainty about quality and fit of competing products, the retailer’s pricing schemes are critical in understanding the effect of third-party information on retailers because it changes the upstream competition. Information on the quality dimension homogenizes consumers’ perceived utility differences between competing products and intensifies the upstream competition, which benefits wholesale-based retailers but hurts platform-based retailers. Information on the fit dimension heterogenizes consumers’ estimated product fit and softens the upstream competition, which hurts wholesalebased retailers but benefits platform-based retailers. Consequently, retailers can use the pricing scheme effectively to their advantage in the presence of third-party information: when the precision of the third-party information is high (low), a retailer can benefit from third-party information by adopting the wholesale (platform) scheme if the quality dimension plays a dominant role and by adopting the platform (wholesale) scheme if the fit dimension is dominant.

Additionally, we find that consumer surplus is higher under the platform scheme than under the wholesale scheme in both the quality-dominates-fit case and the fit-dominates-quality case. While improved precision of the third-party information increases the consumer surplus and social welfare when the quality difference between the products suggested by the information is mild in the quality-dominates-fit case, it does not necessarily increase the social welfare or consumer surplus in the fit-dominates-quality case. These findings together suggest that in the quality-dominates-fit case, when the retailer chooses the wholesale scheme over the platform scheme, the retailer benefits from the third-party information at the expense of consumers and the society but an improvement in precision can benefit all parties. On the other hand, in the fit-dominates-quality case, when the retailer chooses the platform scheme over the wholesale scheme, all parties can benefit from the third-party information but an improvement in precision may only benefit the retailer.

The key drivers for our results are the variance-altering and mean-shifting effects of the third-party information on the consumer’s valuation distribution. Third-party information reduces the variance of consumers’ perceived quality difference between the products, but increases the variance of consumers’ perceived product fit. While the variance-reducing effect intensifies the competition between manufacturers, the variance-increasing effect softens the competition. The meanshifting effect shifts the mean of the consumer’s perceived quality difference between the products in favor of the product with favorable information, which provides a competitive advantage to its manufacturer. These effects of third-party information play a critical role in determining retailers preference between different pricing schemes.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. We review the related literature in the next section. We then lay out the model. Subsequently, we derive the results of the effect of the third-party information on the upstream competition, the retailer, consumer surplus, and social welfare. Next, we analyze the retailer’s preference between the two pricing schemes and how third-party information might shift the retailer’s preference. We also present which pricing scheme is beneficial to consumers and social welfare. Finally, we present extensions of our main model and show our main results are robust before presenting our conclusions.

## Literature Review

Two streams of research are closely related to our study. The first stream is the work on pricing schemes. With the recent emergence of the agency model (equivalent to the platform scheme in our paper), researchers have studied firms’ strategic or tactical decisions under this pricing scheme. For example, Jiang et al. (2011) study a retailer’s strategic choice of products to be sold under the platform scheme when product demand is uncertain to the retailer. Hao and Fan (2014) examine e-book pricing under the agency model and find a complementary relationship between e-books and e-reader devices (e.g., Kindle) in driving price changes. Hagiu and Wright (2014) show that an intermediary’s platform-scheme choice can be affected by the level of marketing effort made by the retailer (upstream sellers) under the wholesale (platform) scheme. Johnson (2012) shows that the market outcome for a retailer under different pricing schemes depends on the consumer lock-in effect of competing retailers and the differentiation between them. Abhishek et al. (2016) study retailers’ pricing strategies in a context where online retailers compete with a traditional brick-and-mortar channel. They find online retailers would prefer the platform scheme when the online channel cannibalizes the traditional channel, whereas they prefer the wholesale scheme when the online channel stimulates demand in the traditional channel. The focus of these papers is on the selection of a pricing scheme or a contract term in a channel when retailers compete with other retailers or with other channels. We extend this stream of research by modeling the influence of third-party information on consumers’ decisions and demonstrating how third-party information affects a retailer’s choice between the wholesale and platform pricing schemes.

The second stream of research related to our study is the work that examines the effect of third-party information. Thirdparty information affects consumer decision making and thus firm profits because consumers face uncertainty in the online market (e.g., Dimoka et al. 2012; Ghose 2009; Hong and Pavlou 2014). Several recent studies have analyzed the effect of one type of third-party information, product reviews, on firms. Researchers find that the variance of product ratings (Clemons et al. 2006), rating volume (Duan et al. 2008; Liu 2006), reviewer and product characteristics (Forman et al. 2008; Shen 2008; Zhang and Zhu 2010), and text reviews (Archak et al. 2011) all impact sales. Recent studies have also started to examine different aspects of product reviews: the effect of review characteristics and product type on the review helpfulness (Mudambi and Schuff 2010), the effect of negative emotions embedded in reviews on review helpfulness (Yin et al. 2014), the role of product reviews as a new measure of product types (Hong et al. 2012) and as a tool to reduce the uncertainty of product attributes (Hong and Pavlou 2014; Niculescu and Wu 2014), the effect of reviews on pricing strategy in the presence of strategic consumers (Yu et al. 2016), interactions between promotional marketing and product reviews (Lu et al. 2013) and between recommendations and product reviews (Jabr and Zheng 2014), herding behavior in product reviews’ effects (Duan et al. 2009), and factors that affect review-posting behavior and review generation (Dellarocas et al. 2010; Gao et al. 2015; Goes et al. 2014; Lee et al. 2015; Rice 2012; Zhang and Zhu 2010). Our study also examines how third-party information such as reviews affects consumers and online retailers. However, we abstract away any specific type or aspect of third-party information and investigate how such information affects online retailers via its impact on upstream players in a channel structure, considering different upstream pricing schemes.

Our study also relates to the existing analytical work that models third-party information which enables consumers to identify products matching their needs (Chen and Xie 2008) or estimate their true utilities more accurately (Li et al. 2011; Sun 2012). These studies typically consider the effect of product reviews in a context of sellers selling directly to consumers. For example, Chen and Xie (2008) study how a seller should adjust the amount of information it provides to consumers in response to consumer reviews. We differ from this stream of literature in that we consider a channel structure with a retailer either providing a platform for competing manufacturers to sell their products or selling directly to consumers; furthermore, consumers face two dimensional uncertainty about a product in our model—both the product quality and the fit to their needs. Kwark et al. (2014) focus on how online product reviews affect upstream competition when manufacturers respond strategically to the reviews. In contrast, we study how different pricing schemes affect the impact of the third-party information on the channel members and consequently on online retailers’ pricing-scheme choice. Shaffer and Zettelmeyer (2002) analyze the effects of thirdparty information on different channel members’ profits when they bargain over the division of profits. In their model, all consumers have the same product information, additional information has the same qualitative impact (positive or negative) on every consumer, and sellers have perfect knowledge of all product information. In our setting, however, consumers are uncertain about both product quality and fit to their needs, and we study how third-party information affects the product competition by changing consumers’ perceived utilities. More importantly, we focus on the effect of thirdparty information on retailers and consumers under wholesale and platform schemes.

Stated more generally, different from the above streams of research, the focus of our study is on the effect of publicly available third-party information on an online retailer’s pricing-scheme preference and shows the effect varies depending on the retailer’s pricing-scheme choice (wholesale versus platform) and the type of information conveyed by thirdparty information (in the quality versus the fit dimension).

## Model

We consider a retailer, R, that carries two products, A and B produced by different manufacturers, and uses one of two pricing schemes (wholesale or platform). Under the wholesale scheme, the manufacturers sell their products to the retailer and the retailer resells them to consumers. Under the platform scheme, the manufacturers sell their products directly to consumers on the retailer’s platform, and the retailer charges a commission fee for each sale. Products A and B are imperfect substitutes. We call the manufacturer that produces Product A (B) Manufacturer A (B). The marginal production cost for each product is assumed to be zero. Each consumer has a unit demand.

Consumer Utility: Following Kwark et al. (2014), we consider that each product is characterized by a quality attribute and a fit attribute. The quality attribute represents the vertical dimension in the sense that every consumer prefers high quality to low quality. The fit attribute represents the horizontal dimension in the sense that preferences vary across consumers. These concepts and the dichotomy between the two are well established in economics literature (e.g., Sutton 1986; Tirole 1988) and have been widely borrowed in the field of information systems (e.g., Hong and Pavlou 2014; Wattal et al. 2009). The quality of a product determines the maximum value that a consumer derives from the product, which is denoted as $x _ { i } , i \in \{ A , B \}$ The products may not perfectly fit any particular consumer, and thus consumers incur misfit costs. As in typical location models of product differentiation, the misfit cost is modeled as the degree of misfit $\lambda , \lambda \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ , times a unit misfit cost, t. A consumer’s degree of misfit to Product A is negatively correlated to the misfit to Product B. In particular, when the degree of misfit between a consumer and Product A is λ, the degree of misfit between the consumer and Product B is (1 – λ). A consumer’s utility from product $i , U _ { i } ,$ is the maximum value that the product offers net of the misfit cost. A consumer’s net utility from a product is the utility less the retail price. Denoting the retail price as $p _ { i } , i \in \{ A , B \}$ , we can formulate the net utilities derived from Products A and B for the consumer with a degree of misfit λ to Product A as

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} V _ {A} = U _ {A} - p _ {A} = x _ {A} - \lambda t - p _ {A} \\ V _ {B} = U _ {B} - p _ {B} = x _ {B} - (1 - \lambda) t - p _ {B} \end{array} \right.
$$

Therefore, the net utility difference between Product A and $B ,$ $V _ { A } \mathrm { ~ - ~ } V _ { B } ,$ for the consumer with the degree of misfit $\lambda$ to Product A is

$$
\begin{array}{c} V _ {A} - V _ {B} = (U _ {A} - U _ {B}) - (p _ {A} - p _ {B}) \\ = (x _ {A} - x _ {B}) + (1 - 2 \lambda) t - (p _ {A} - p _ {B}) \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

We call $( x _ { A } - x _ { B } )$ the quality difference of the two products. Unless otherwise indicated, we call λ the degree of misfit. We assume that the (true) quality difference between the products is zero. A continuum of consumers of measure 1 has different (true) degrees of misfit λ with a uniform distribution.

Product Uncertainty and Third-Party Information: Different from the standard vertical and horizontal differentiation models, consumers do not know the true quality difference of the two products or their true degrees of misfit. Consumers have two information sources signaling these unknowns: third-party information and their own information sources other than the third-party information. We denote as $x _ { R }$ the perceived quality difference revealed by the third-party information, which is common to all consumers. We denote as $x _ { C }$ a consumer’s own assessment of the quality difference based on her own information sources other than the thirdparty information; this difference assessment varies across consumers. We model $x _ { C }$ as a draw from a uniform distribution over $[ - \varepsilon , \varepsilon ] .$ Consumers combine the common public assessment, $x _ { R } ,$ and their own assessments, $x _ { C } ,$ , to form their judgment of the quality difference. As shown by Bates and Granger (1969) using the minimum variance estimation, the consumer’s posterior expected quality difference conditional on the two sources of information becomes

$$
\gamma x _ {C} + (1 - \gamma) x _ {R}\tag{2}
$$

where $\gamma , \gamma \in ( 0 , 1 )$ , depends on the relative precision of the two information sources. The weight on the third-party information, $( 1 - \gamma )$ , is high when the precision of the thirdparty information is high.<sup>2</sup> Intuitively, consumers adjust their quality assessments because of the additional information from the third parties, and the extent to which the information affects consumers’ assessments depends on the relative precision and confidence between their own assessments and the comments in the third-party information.

Similar to the approach often used in the literature $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ Johnson and Myatt 2006; Ruckes 2004), the uncertainty in the misfit is modeled such that a consumer observes a signal $s ,$ which is equal to the consumer’s true degree of misfit with probability $\beta ,$ and with probability $( 1 - \beta )$ is uninformative and follows the true distribution; that is, $\mathrm { P r } ( s = y | \lambda = y ) = \beta$ and $\mathrm { P r } ( s \neq y | \lambda = y ) = 1 - \beta ,$ , where $y \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ With more accurate information provided by the third-party information, consumers know better about their idiosyncratic fit, which is modeled as a greater $\beta .$ Based on Bayesian updating, we can derive $\operatorname { E } ( \lambda | s = y ) = [ \beta y + ( 1 - \beta ) / 2 ]$ (see the proof in the appendix).<sup>3</sup> Substituting $[ \gamma x _ { C } + ( 1 - \gamma ) x _ { R } ]$ and the expected degree of misfit into Equation (1), the expected utility difference between Products A and B for the consumer with perceived quality difference $x _ { C }$ and misfit signal $s = y$ is then

$$
\mathrm{E} \left(U _ {A} - U _ {B} \mid x _ {C}, y\right) = \left[ \gamma x _ {C} + (1 - \gamma) x _ {R} \right] + (1 - 2 y) \beta t\tag{3}
$$

Different consumers receive different misfit signals, y. The uncertainty model for the misfit signal implies that the signals satisfy a uniform distribution over $[ 0 , 1 { \bar { ] } } . ^ { 4 }$ The retailer and manufacturers do not know an individual consumer’s perceived quality difference or misfit signal, but they do know their distributions.

Timing of the Game: The sequence of events under each pricing scheme is as follows. Under the platform scheme, in Stage 1 of the game, the retailer announces the commission rate k and a fixed fee m. In Stage 2, manufacturers set retail prices $p _ { i }$ simultaneously knowing the commission and the fixed fee retailer charges. In Stage 3, consumers evaluate the product-utility difference and make their purchase decisions, and the manufacturers pay commission fees to the retailer. Under the wholesale scheme, manufacturers set wholesale prices $w _ { i }$ simultaneously in Stage 1. In Stage 2, the retailer sets retail prices $p _ { i } .$ In Stage 3, consumers make their purchase decisions by evaluating the product-utility difference. Under both pricing schemes, the third-party information is observed by consumers, the manufacturers, and the retailer before they make their decisions.

Finally, we denote the reservation profit of manufacturer i as $\mu _ { i } .$ The reservation profit represents the profit a manufacturer can obtain if it sells its product through an alternative channel. We assume that the two manufacturers’ reservation profits are equal when the manufacturers are ex ante symmetric; however, a manufacturer that has more favorable third-party information about its product will have a higher reservation profit than the one with less favorable third-party information. Consumers’ own estimates about product quality differences and their misfits are their private information. All other model parameters are common knowledge. All players are risk neutral.

Demand Functions: In the last stage of the game, consumers learn the expected utility differences between two products. Based on Equation (3), we can formulate a consume ${ \bf \ddot { s } }$ expected net utility difference as

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{E} (V _ {A} - V _ {B} | x _ {C}, y) & = \mathrm{E} (U _ {A} - U _ {B} | x _ {C}, y) - (p _ {A} - p _ {B}) \\ & = [ \gamma x _ {C} + (1 - \gamma) x _ {R} ] + (1 - 2 y) \beta t - (p _ {A} - p _ {B}) \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

in which $\operatorname { E } ( U _ { A } - U _ { B } | x _ { C } , y )$ is the consumer’s expected utility difference, as defined in Equation (3). Clearly, besides consumers’ own assessments, third-party information affects consumers’ perceived net utility differences between the two products by changing γ and $\beta .$ We focus our analysis on the cases in which third-party information plays a mild or moderate role in changing the competition between the two manufacturers such that, in equilibrium, the two manufacturers are competitive. The extreme case in which the additional difference revealed by third-party information is so dramatic such that one manufacturer has a dominant advantage in the market is not considered in this study.

Following Kwark et al. (2014), we next distinguish two cases:

Quality-dominates-fit case: Consumers’ own assessment of the quality dimension dominates the fit dimension such that there exist consumers who have the lowest fit with Product A but derive higher net utility from it than from Product B because their own assessment of the quality dimension is favorable for Product $A ,$ and there also exist consumers who have the lowest fit with

Product B but derive higher net utility from it. Figure 1a illustrates the quality-dominates-fit case.

Fit-dominates-quality case: The fit dimension dominates consumers’ own assessment of quality dimension such that consumers who have perfect fit with a product always derive a higher net utility from that product, regardless of their own assessment of the quality dimension. Figure 1b illustrates the fit-dominates-quality case.

The essential features that distinguish the above two cases are similar to those that distinguish search goods and experience goods, two important concepts of product types discussed widely in the economics literature (e.g., Garvin 1984; Nelson $1 9 7 0 ;$ Sutton 1986). Search goods are likely to come under the quality-dominates-fit case, and experience goods often come under the fit-dominates-quality case. In general, products evaluated based on the objective indices such as product performance, reliability, and durability are likely to be quality-dominant (Garvin 1984). Examples include digital cameras, GPS dervices, and hardware. Products evaluated based on subjective consumer-specific indices such as experience attributes, features, and aesthetics are more fitdominant (Sutton 1986). Examples include jewelry and video games.

Notice that the above demand functions (5) and (7) are functions of $( p _ { A } - p _ { B } )$ Therefore, if consumers have no outside option, when the retail prices of both products are increased by the same margin, the demand for each product stays the same. We assume that some consumers may find an outside option (e.g., buying from a different retailer) more attractive than buying their preferred product from this focal channel, and that the number of such consumers is increasing in the product prices. This assumption ensures that the retailer or manufacturers suffer a penalty in the form of reduced demand for increasing product prices. We model this outside option using parameter α (α 0 R<sup>+</sup>) which denotes the marginal decrease in the demand for a product from a marginal increase in its price. Essentially, when the price of a product is increased, the marginal decrease in demand for that product comes from two sources: (1) some consumers switch from that product to the other product but still buy from the focal channel (the marginal decrease caused by switching between products within the focal retailer is 1/(2γε) in the quality-dominates-fit case and 1/(2βt) in the fitdominates-quality case), and (2) some consumers switch from the focal channel to an outside option (the marginal decrease caused by switching to the outside option is α). For example, some consumers who only consider purchasing a manufacturer’s product to which they are loyal, may or may not purchase the product depending on its price. We model this outside option at an aggregate level in order to focus our attention on the effect of third-party information and to ensure tractability, but we can show our results continue to hold by modeling the consumer group explicitly.

From Equations (5) and (7), we notice that each manufacturer’s base demands in both cases take the same structure except the coefficients of the terms in the brackets (i.e., 1/(2γε) in quality-dominates-fit versus 1/(2βt) in fitdominates-quality).

In this section, we compare the retailer’s equilibrium profits under the two different pricing schemes. We show that the third-party information may reverse the retailer’s preference between the two schemes in terms of its profit. For ease of exposition, we next use the symmetric case with $x _ { R } = 0$ to illustrate the profit comparison. We use superscript of p and w to indicate the platform scheme and the wholesale scheme, respectively, for the retailer’s profit.

Proposition 5 essentially states that the retailer prefers the platform scheme to wholesale scheme if and only if the upstream competition is mild (a small 1/(2τ)). As explained in Section 3 for Equations (17) and (20), the coefficient of the price difference term in demand functions, 1/(2τ), measures the substitutability between the two products: the larger the coefficient is, the more substitutable the two products are and the more intense the competition between the two manufacturers is. When the upstream competition is low, the retailer prefers the platform scheme to the wholesale scheme, because under the platform scheme manufacturers’ profits are high and so is the retailer’s. In contrast, under the wholesale scheme low upstream competition leads to high wholesale prices which leaves the retailer little room to exploit the market. As explained previously, an increase in the upstream competition benefits the retailer under the wholesale scheme, but hurts the retailer under the platform scheme. When the upstream competition is high enough, the retailer prefers the wholesale scheme over the platform scheme.

Moreover, Proposition 5 indicates that a retailer’s preference between the two pricing schemes depends on the precision of third-party information and the relative importance of the quality and fit attributes in consumers’ product evaluation. In the quality-dominates-fit case, the threshold 1/(2τ) becomes 1/(2γε). When the precision (1 – γ) is high, the threshold is high and thus the retailer is more likely to prefer the wholesale scheme. In the fit-dominates-quality case, the conclusion is the opposite: when the precision (β) is high, the threshold is low and thus the retailer is more likely to prefer the platform scheme. We can show the retailer’s preference between the two pricing schemes may switch from one to the other as the third-party information becomes more precise. In addition, such a switching occurs in only one direction, and the direction is opposite in the quality-dominates-fit case and the fit-dominates-quality case.

Corollary 9 In the presence of symmetric third-party information in terms of quality, (a) in the quality-dominatesfit case, when the precision of the third-party information in quality, (1 – γ), increases beyond the point $( 1 - \gamma ^ { * } )$ , where $\gamma ^ { * }$ is the one such that $1 / ( 2 \gamma ^ { \ast } \varepsilon ) = \xi$ (ξ defined in Equation (35)), the retailer switches preference from the platform scheme to the wholesale scheme; (b) in the fit-dominates-quality case, when the precision of the third-party information in fit increases beyond the point $\beta ^ { * } ,$ , where $\beta ^ { * }$ is such that $1 / ( 2 \beta ^ { * } t )$ $= \xi ,$ the retailer switches preference from the wholesale scheme to the platform scheme. Moreover, the other direction of preference switching cannot occur as the precision of the third-party information improves.

The intuition is as follows. In the quality-dominates-fit case, an increase in the precision of the third-party information increases the upstream competition, which benefits the retailer under the wholesale scheme but hurts the retailer under the platform scheme. When the upstream competition is driven high enough by the increased precision, the retailer’s preference switches from the platform scheme to the wholesale scheme. A switch in preference from the wholesale scheme to the platform scheme cannot occur because, if the retailer is better off with the low-precision third-party information under the wholesale scheme, then the increased upstream competition engendered by the improved precision can only improve the retailer’s position under the wholesale scheme. In the fitdominates-quality case, in contrast, more precise third-party information reduces the upstream competition. Therefore, the possible switching is in the opposite direction to that in the quality-dominates-fit case.

Next, we examine the effect on a retailer’s profit of the change in the perceived quality difference revealed by the third-party information $( x _ { R } )$

Proposition 6 When the perceived quality difference revealed by the third-party information increases, (a) the retailer’s profit increases in both the quality-dominates-fit case and the fit-dominates-quality case, regardless of the pricing scheme; that is, $\partial \pi _ { R } / \partial x _ { R } > 0 ; ( b )$ ) the marginal profit increase under the platform scheme is greater than that under the wholesale scheme; that is, $\partial \pi _ { R } ^ { p } / \partial x _ { R } > \partial \pi _ { R } ^ { w } / \partial x _ { R }$

Notice that the perceived quality difference revealed by the third-party information (i.e., a positive $x _ { R } )$ leads to the meanshifting effect, making the demands asymmetric in terms of the two manufacturers’ potential market sizes. This asymmetry softens the competition between the two manufacturers and benefits the retailer. When $x _ { R }$ increases, the asymmetry increases and the retailer benefits more. The marginal benefit under the platform scheme is greater than that under the wholesale scheme because of the double-marginalization issue embedded with the wholesale scheme. These results suggest that retailers have an incentive to highlight the perceived quality difference, and the incentive is greater under the platform scheme.

We next compare consumer surplus and social welfare under the two pricing schemes.

Proposition 7 In both the quality-dominates-fit case and the fit-dominates-fit case with symmetric third-party information, consumer surplus and social welfare are higher under the platform scheme than under the wholesale scheme.

Consumer surplus is always higher under platform scheme in both the quality-dominates-fit case and the fit-dominatesquality case because the retail prices are lower under the platform scheme than under the wholesale scheme. The prices under the platform scheme are lower than those under the wholesale scheme because the wholesale scheme suffers from double marginalization not present in the platform scheme. The lower prices in turn attract more consumers to the platform scheme compared to the wholesale scheme. Similarly, social welfare is higher under the platform scheme than under the wholesale scheme because more consumers purchase under the platform scheme.

Proposition 7 provides an important insight regarding the recent debate about the agency-pricing practice for e-books. At issue is who–authors, publishers or retailers–benefit from the agency model. We find that the agency model (i.e., the platform scheme) can lead to lower equilibrium prices than the traditional wholesale model. This lower price in the platform scheme has also been found in recent literature (e.g., Abhishek et al. 2016; Tan and Carrillo 2016), which suggests the positive effect of an agency-pricing model on both sellers and consumers. Our results also support the argument that the agency model not only can increase the consumer surplus, but might also increase the retailer’s profit.

To summarize the sections “Effect of Third-Party Information” and “Comparison of Retailers’ Profits Under Two Pricing Schemes,” the main finding is that online retailers can use the upstream pricing scheme effectively to their advantage in the presence of third-party information. The appropriate pricing scheme depends critically on whether quality or fit plays a dominant role in consumers’ assessment of the product and on the precision of the information. Under high precision, while the retailer will prefer the wholesale scheme if quality dominates fit, he will prefer the platform scheme if fit dominates quality. However, consumers and society are always better off under the platform scheme than under the wholesale scheme. A key driver for the above results is the impact of third-party information on the upstream price competition.

Although various factors, such as the popularity of a product (Jiang et al. 2011), may affect retailers’ pricing-scheme choices, our results underscore the importance of the precision of third-party information and the relative importance of the quality and fit dimensions in consumers’ evaluation of a product’s utility. Our findings are also largely consistent with practice. For example, with accumulated online product reviews and increased information precision, Amazon sells only 5.9% of toys and games using the wholesale scheme, whereas it sells 21.9%, a relatively high proportion, of “GPS & Navigation” products using the wholesale scheme (Jiang et al. 2011). One plausible reason for this differentiation, based on our results, is that the products in the toys and games category are evaluated mainly based on personal subjective taste (Gu and Xie 2013; Hong et al. 2012), corresponding to the fit-dominates-quality case, and the products in the “GPS & Navigation” category are evaluated mainly based on objective indices (Archak et al. 2011, Hong et al. 2012), corresponding to the quality-dominates-fit case.

## Different Precisions Among Consumers

In the baseline model, we assume a uniform precision of consumers’ own assessment and that consumers incorporate the third-party information in the same fashion (by assigning weight $( 1 - \gamma )$ on the third-party information). In this section, we show our results generalize to the case when the consumers have different precisions or assign different weights to the third-party information. For illustration, we consider the case with symmetric third-party information in terms of quality and two groups of consumers with different precisions. We assume the proportions of high-precision and lowprecision consumers to be $a _ { H }$ and ${ { a } _ { L } } ,$ respectively, and $a _ { H } + a _ { L }$ $= 1$ The weight assigned by each group to the third-party information is assumed to be $( 1 - \gamma _ { H } )$ and $( 1 - \gamma _ { L } ) _ { : }$ , respectively, for high-precision and low-precision consumers in the qualitydominates-fit case (with $( 1 - \gamma _ { H } ) > ( 1 - \gamma _ { L } ) ~ ,$ ) and $\beta _ { H }$ and $\beta _ { L }$ in the fit-dominates-quality case (with $\beta _ { H } > \beta _ { L } )$ , respectively. All other model parameters remain the same as in our baseline model.

Similar to the demand functions in Equation (8), the demand function for the quality-dominates-fit case can be derived when $x _ { R } = 0$ as

$$
\begin{array}{l} D _ {A} = a _ {H} \left[ \frac {1}{2} - \frac {p _ {A} - p _ {B}}{2 \gamma_ {H} \varepsilon} \right] + a _ {L} \left[ \frac {1}{2} - \frac {p _ {A} - p _ {B}}{2 \gamma_ {L} \varepsilon} \right] - o p _ {A} \\ D _ {B} = a _ {H} \left[ \frac {1}{2} + \frac {p _ {A} - p _ {B}}{2 \gamma_ {H} \varepsilon} \right] + a _ {L} \left[ \frac {1}{2} + \frac {p _ {A} - p _ {B}}{2 \gamma_ {L} \varepsilon} \right] - o p _ {B} \end{array}

## Conclusion

We examine the effect of third-party information (e.g., online product reviews) in a channel structure with a retailer carrying two substitutable products. The retailer may use a wholesale scheme and sell products by itself or use a platform scheme and let manufacturers sell directly to consumers. Consumers face uncertainty in both the product quality and the fit to their needs, and third-party information provides additional information that reduces their uncertainty. Consumers agree on the preference order of the attributes in the quality dimension and have idiosyncratic preferences for the attributes in the fit dimension. We identify the quality-dominates-fit case in which the quality dimension plays a dominant role in determining consumers’ perceived utility differences of the competing products and the fit-dominates-quality case in which the fit dimension plays a more important role. We show that retailers can strategically use the upstream pricing scheme to their advantage in the presence of third-party information. The appropriate pricing scheme depends critically both on whether quality or fit plays the dominant role in consumers’ assessment of the product and on the precision of the information. We identify the impact of the third-party information on the upstream price competition as the primary driving force behind our results.

Our findings have significant implications for online retailers. Online retailers have been deploying a variety of technologies to mitigate consumer uncertainty and match consumers with their preferred products. Online review platforms and recommendation systems are a few examples. Our results suggest that the effect of the same third-party information on a retailer can be quite opposite depending on the pricing scheme used. For example, some third-party information may be related to the quality dimension. The retailer should always welcome more precise information of this kind if the retailer uses the wholesale scheme, but might want to avoid such information under the platform scheme. Information that provides fit information about products can benefit the retailer more under the platform scheme but may decrease the retailer’s profit under the wholesale scheme. The rule of thumb for fostering third-party information is that the information to be revealed should be tailored according to the retailer’s pricing scheme. Under such circumstances where retailer can benefit from this information, retailers should encourage and even induce consumers or third parties to generate such information. For instance, retailers can make the review platform easy to use to facilitate the review-generating process, and, in particular, they may provide some review templates to encourage users to generate information about product quality or fit.

While controlling the third-party information generation and access process, manipulating the third-party information displayed, and adjusting marketing-mix elements including strategic pricing are options that a retailer may have at its disposal to increase benefit from third-party information, these options may also suffer from issues such as a potential decrease in consumers’ trust of the retailer. Alternatively, the contract that the retailer enters into with upstream sellers can be a strategic tool that a retailer can use to its advantage. The upstream contract is less likely to suffer from issues related to consumer mistrust. Finally, the product category, in the sense of whether fit or quality plays a dominant role in consumer decision making, significantly affects a retailer’s optimal choice. As we noted in the introduction section, anecdotal observations suggest that the relative fraction of products for which Amazon uses the wholesale scheme or platform scheme varies across product categories; however, whether the relative importance of quality and fit varies across these categories and whether this relative importance influences Amazon’s choice of pricing scheme are open empirical questions.

Another important implication for online retailers relates to the assessment and measurement of third-party-information precision because the precision affects the choice of the retailer’s pricing scheme. One possibility is to collect and use data from consumers about the usefulness of this information. Several online review systems and forums that provide answers to consumers’ questions have started to implement a feature that allows consumers to rate the usefulness of reviews and answers. Online retailers can examine this rating data to estimate the precision of third-party information. More importantly, online retailers will find it beneficial to design an appropriate rating form that will enable them to estimate precision more accurately.

Related to the recent debate on the agency model in the ebook industry, we show that consumer surplus and social welfare are higher under the platform scheme than under the wholesale scheme. For the product category in which fit dominates quality (e-books is likely to fall under such a category) as the precision of third-party information increases, the retailer is more likely to prefer the platform scheme. Therefore, a win-win outcome that benefits all parties may arise under the platform scheme for such product categories.

This paper suggests a few directions for future research. First, in this paper we consider products can only be sold via one of the two pricing schemes. It will be interesting to see how the results differ if a product is being sold via both the wholesale and the platform schemes. Second, this paper does not consider the effect of pricing scheme on information generation. This would be an interesting future research direction (e.g., how the different pricing schemes affect information generation and how the number of reviews affects the retailer’s preference between pricing schemes). Third, our paper provides several testable hypotheses. Rigorous empirical testing will complement this work.
