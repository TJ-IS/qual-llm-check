# Optimal Policy for Software Patents: Model and Comparative Implications

Matt E. Thatche r and David E. Pin gry

Matt E. Thatche r is an Associate Professor of Computer Information Systems at the College of Business, University of Louisville. He holds a B.S. in economics and an M.A. and Ph.D. in information systems from the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania. His research examines the strategic and economic effects of IT with a focus on four themes: IT value, software patent policy design, IT outsourcing, and the social costs of information privacy. His work has appeared in Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Communica tions of the ACM, Decision Support Systems, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Journal of Financial Services Research, and Journal of Information Technology Theory and Application.

David E. Pingry is McClelland Professor of Management Information Systems and Economics at the Eller College of Management, University of Arizona, where he has been since 1974. He holds a Ph.D. in economics from Purdue University and an A.B. in Economics from the University of Illinois. Professor Pingry was also on the faculty at Virginia Tech, held visiting faculty positions at Purdue University and Texas A&M, and held the Treibick Visiting Endowed Chair in the Connecticut Information Technology Institute at the University of Connecticut. His current interests are decision support systems, patent policy, and IT productivity.

Abstract: We use a duopoly model of quality–price competition between a software innovator and an imitator to determine the socially optimal software patent policy and to assess the social welfare implications of alternative patent policies. We find that the optimal patent policy is to grant patent protection for the entire life of the innovative software product (i.e., set infinite patent length) and to set the novelty and nonobviousness requirement for attaining a patent (i.e., patent height) and the scope of patent protection (i.e., patent width) such that both the innovator and imitator produce higher-quality products than they would in the free market. This policy not only maximizes social welfare but also makes the innovator, imitator, and consumers better off than in the free market. However, counter to intuition, we find that firms exhibit lower research and development intensity under the socially optimal patent policy than they do under free market competition. While highly stylized, the model offers a useful framework within which researchers and regulators can think about the economic trade-offs among three patent policy parameters (length, height, and width) simultaneously.

To gain compe titive ad vantage in an incre asingly d igital e conom y, many firms engage in costly research and development (R&D) activities to develop innovative software applications and software-based business methods. At the same time recent advances in software reverse-engineering techniques have reduced the cost and time to imitate these innovations [4, 36], making it more difficult for innovators to recoup their costs and, therefore, reducing their incentive to innovate. Increasingly, software innovators have turned to the patent system to attain protection from cheap, quick imitation.<sup>1</sup>

Figures 1 and 2 (which are based on data from [5]) illustrate the rapid growth in the number and percentage of software patents awarded since 1982.<sup>2</sup> Over 38,000 software patents were granted in 2007, accounting for almost 21 percent of all patents. A recent empirical analysis of software patents [5] identified three reasons for this increase in software patent propensity over the past decades: (1) growth in the R&D spending on software activities, (2) improvements in the productivity of software developers, and (3) improvements in the cost-effectiveness of software patents due to changes in patent law that have made software patents easier to attain. We discuss the changes in patent law below.

Patent law requires that an innovation under review must be new, useful, and nonobvious to a person of ordinary skill in the relevant field. Once a patent is awarded, it provides the patent holder with a scope of protection from imitation for a maximum of 20 years. In this paper, the novelty and nonobvious requirement is referred to as the patent height. Once a patent has been awarded, the scope of protection is referred to as the patent width, with wider patents providing more protection from imitation. Finally, the duration of patent protection is referred to as the patent length. A patent policy is defined by the combination of patent height, width, and length set by the patent authority.

Over the two decades preceding 2007, a combination of legislation and court decisions strengthened patent protections by lowering the height [17, 18, 19], increasing the width [32], and increasing the length [33] of software patents. In fact,

Critics say the Federal Circuit [a specialized court established by Congress in 1982 to oversee patent laws] has fostered an expansion of patent rights that has made them both more potent and easier to obtain . . . by expanding the types of inventions that can be patented and broadening the scope of monopoly a patent affords its holder. [6, p. A 1].

According to Adam Jaffe, Dean of Arts and Sciences at Brandeis University, the Federal Circuit’s rulings “converted patents from handguns to bazookas, and at the same time stopped requiring permits to get them” [6, p. A1].

However, more recently, the U.S. Supreme Court has weakened some patent protections by reversing several Federal Circuit court rulings. Some of these U.S. Supreme Court decisions have made it more difficult for firms to acquire new patents by tightening the height (i.e., the novelty and nonobviousness) requirement [7, 23]. For example, in KSR v. Teleflex (2007),<sup>3</sup> Teleflex Inc. sued KSR International for violating its patent on connecting an adjustable vehicle control pedal to an electronic throttle control. KSR argued that merely combining these two elements was obvious, and therefore not patentable. The Federal Circuit court, using its teaching-suggestion-motivation (TSM) test for obviousness, rejected KSR’s claim and determined that Teleflex’s innovation was nonobvious.<sup>4</sup> However, the U.S. Supreme Court overturned the Federal Circuit court and rejected the TSM standard for obviousness set by the lower court. This ruling tightened the nonobviousness standard, making it more difficult for firms to acquire new patents.

In addition to tightening the height requirement, recent U.S. Supreme Court decisions have also made it more difficult for firms to defend existing patents by narrowing the patent width [7, 23]. For example, in eBay Inc v. MercExchange, L.L.C. (2006),<sup>5</sup> the court determined that eBay’s buy it now feature infringed a software-based businessmethod patent held by MercExchange LLC. However, the U.S. Supreme Court unanimously ruled that a permanent injunction should not automatically be imposed against a patent infringer. Instead, a federal court must use the four conditions traditionally used to determine if an injunction, when requested by a patent holder, should be imposed.<sup>6</sup> This decision narrows the scope (or width) of patent protections.

As another example, in Microsoft v. AT&T (2007),<sup>7</sup> AT &T claimed that Microsoft had infringed its patent for voice-compression software when it sold its Windows operating system to foreign computer makers [25]. The U.S. Supreme Court denied AT &T’s claim. This ruling limits the enforceability of U.S. patents for software installed overseas (unless the patent holder holds a valid patent there) and significantly reduces Microsoft’s (and other firms’) liability exposure in patent-infringement lawsuits. This ruling also narrows the scope (or width) of patent protections.

Despite the economic importance of this ongoing public policy debate, the information technology (IT) and economics literatures have yet to either model the socially optimal combination of patent height, patent width, and patent length or formally examine how these policy instruments interact to affect firm strategies, consumer choices, and social welfare. Therefore, two major policy reports recently called for more economic research on patent policy design [27, 40]. In fact,

Over the past twenty-five years, the incorporation of economic thinking into antitrust has provided significant insights that have substantially improved the development of antitrust law and competition policy. The Federal Circuit and the PTO [Patent and Trademark Office] may also benefit from much greater consideration and incorporation of economic insights in their decision-making. [40, p. 21]

It is in this spirit that Thatcher et al. [39] developed a duopoly model of quality–price competition in which software firms compete in idea generation, product development, and product pricing. In this model, they identified the socially optimal mix of patent height and width, assuming infinite patent length. Here we extend this model to incorporate patent length as an endogenous policy instrument. First, we analytically solve the model for the base case, in which firms compete without a patent in the free market. Second, we identify the set of patent policies that, if set by the patent authority, will provide the innovator profit incentive to seek a patent. Finally, we use nonlinear optimization techniques to solve numerically for the socially optimal policy within this set of incentive-compatible policies.

We find that the optimal patent policy is to grant patent protection for the entire life of the innovative product and to set patent height and width such that both the innovator and imitator produce higher-quality products than they would in the free market. This policy not only maximizes social welfare but also makes all stakeholders (the innovator, imitator, and consumers) better off than in the free market. However, counter to intuition, we find that firms exhibit lower R&D intensity under the socially optimal patent policy than they do under free market competition. While highly stylized, the model presented in this paper offers a useful framework within which researchers and regulators can think about the economics issues related to software patent policy design.

## Literature Review of Patent Policy Models

The IT lite rature has e xam ined the ways firm s use IT to gain competitive advantage. For example, firms may develop innovative software applications that help reduce costs, improve product quality, differentiate products, increase consumer switching costs, establish alliances, and raise barriers to market entry. However, at the same time recent advances in software reverse engineering techniques have reduced the cost and time to imitate these software innovations, making it more difficult for innovators to leverage their R&D investments for competitive advantage [4, 36]. In many cases, software innovators have turned to various intellectual property protections—such as copyrights and patents—to protect their software from cheap, quick imitation by individuals and competitors.

Some work in the IT literature has examined the determinants and economic implications of software copyright violations (e.g., software piracy) in different contexts [8, 9, 10, 16, 20, 30, 31, 42]. However, the ability of copyrights to protect software innovators from imitation by competitors is limited since, unlike patents, copyrights do not protect the functionality of software. Given the ability of patents to protect software functionality, the dramatic increase in the use of software patents by firms as a strategic tool [5], the many disagreements over the proper setting of policy for software patents [6, 7, 17, 18, 19, 23, 27, 32, 33, 40], and the dearth of work in the IT literature devoted to these issues, our paper examines the optimal policy for software patents from a theoretical perspective that is grounded in economics.

We leverage a specific set of papers to derive and analyze the socially optimal patent policy design in the software arena. These papers examine the socially optimal choice of a single policy instrument [12, 21, 29, 34], or the socially optimal combination of two policy instruments [11, 13, 15, 22], in the context of a one-shot innovation. In the fourth section, we extend this work by modeling three policy instruments (height, width, and length) simultaneously.

Early patent design literature, initiated by Nordhaus [29], examined the socially optimal patent length [21, 34, 37]. This work commonly assumed that innovators patent all innovations and that a patent provides full protection from improvements (infinite patent height) and full protection from imitation (infinite patent width) while the policy is in force.

Later work examined the socially optimal mix between patent length and patent width [11, 13, 15, 22]. This work commonly assumed that an innovative product has already been developed, a patent has been awarded for the product, and patent height is infinite. Given these assumptions, the goal of the patent authority is to fine-tune the patent length and width (ex ante) to provide the patentee with an exogenously determined prize at minimum social cost.

Dijk [12] examined the socially optimal patent height in a duopoly where two firms compete in improvements to a basic invention. This work assumed that a basic invention has already been developed, a patent has been awarded for the basic invention, and the patent policy provides the patentee with infinite patent length and infinite patent width. In Dijk’s model, a competitor that wants to offer a competing product (or invent around the patented invention) must develop a product that meets or exceeds the patent height, which sets a minimum improvement level the competitor must offer to avoid patent infringement. Patent height is set by the patent authority and is exogenous to the model. A standard vertical differentiation model is used to analyze the competition in improvements between the patentee and its competitor, where product qualities are interpreted as improvements to the basic invention.

Thatcher et al. [39] examined the socially optimal mix between patent height and patent width, assuming infinite patent length, in a duopoly where two firms compete in improvements to a basic software product. While this model built on certain definitions and modeling conventions developed in the patent design literature, in contrast to previous models it placed the patent authority in its proper position as the first mover with the firms responding to policy incentives. This model characterized the set of policies that provide the innovator with profit incentive to seek a patent and examined the impact of these policies on R&D expenditures, product qualities, and product pricing.

In this paper, we extend the model examined in Thatcher et al. [39] by incorporating patent length as an endogenous policy instrument. We examine the impact of patent length on the optimal combination of patent height and width, firm strategies, social welfare, and the distribution of welfare among the innovator, imitator, and consumers. This analysis is then used to determine the socially optimal software patent policy.

## The Free Market Model (Without Patents)

The Fre Marke t Mode l se rve s as the found ation of, and the baseline for, the Patent Model to be presented later. A detailed understanding of the Free Market Model is necessary to properly interpret the Patent Model and its results. Therefore, although the Free Market Model is presented in Thatcher et al. [39], we present the model, some of the derivations, and the results here to ensure continuity for the reader.

We use, as a baseline, a four-stage duopoly model of an R&D race and quality–price competition between two software providers in a free market—that is, in the absence of patents. In the first stage, two firms compete in an R&D race to generate a novel idea (or feature) that, in and of itself, has no commercial value. In the second stage, the winner of the R&D race (or the innovator) leverages the novel idea to design and develop a product, with a quality level of its choosing, at a substantial fixed cost. In the third stage, the loser of the R&D race (or the imitator) observes the innovator’s product quality and decides to what extent it will imitate the innovator’s product at zero fixed costs (via software reverse engineering). In the fourth stage, the firms simultaneously set prices for their software products and offer them to the market. Since the firms compete in software products, marginal costs are assumed to be zero. Figure 3 presents a graphical representation of the four stages of the Free Market Model.

The probability of idea generation time x being within time t is $p r ( x < \tau ) = 1 - e ^ { - \alpha \tau }$ . Therefore, the probability that Firm 1 wins the race (and generates a novel idea before Firm 2) is

## Product Development (Stages 2 and 3)

In Stages 2 and 3, we model the competition in product quality between the innovator and imitator as a sequential game. In the second stage, the innovator leverages the novel idea (generated by winning the R&D race) to design and develop a commercial product of quality $s _ { n } \in [ 0 , \infty ]$ . We assume that the innovator produces quality $s _ { n }$ for the full life of the product. The fixed production cost associated with the product quality is

$$
C _ {n} = k \frac {s _ {n} ^ {2}}{2},\tag{3}
$$

where $k > 0$ is a cost of quality parameter, the size of which affects the marginal cost of improving product quality. The parameter represents the innovator’s production efficiency where a lower k represents higher efficiency. The quadratic form of this cost function has intuitive appeal, is widely used in the industrial organization and IT literatures [1, 2, 14, 28, 35], and has been used to analyze a diverse range of products including software products and information goods.

Since the imitator lost the Stage 1 R&D race, it lacks a novel idea of its own and is left to use software reverse-engineering techniques to imperfectly deduce and imitate the innovator’s novel idea. In the third stage, the imitator observes the innovator’s product quality and develops its own commercial product (or imitation) of quality $s _ { { \scriptscriptstyle m } } \in [ 0 , \infty ]$ . We assume that the imitator produces $s _ { { } _ { m } }$ for the full life of the product. Given that recent advances in software reengineering techniques have dramatically reduced the cost to imitate software products, we further assume that the imitator’s fixed production cost is zero, $C _ { { \scriptscriptstyle m } } = 0$ . This assumption provides the greatest incentive for the imitator to match or even exceed the innovator’s product quality. However, as we will see, the imitator will not find it profitable to do so when facing a vertically differentiated market in the pricing stage. The innovator’s product and imitator’s product are assumed to be perfect substitutes except in quality. The payoffs in the product development stages for the innovator and imitator, respectively, are

$$
\pi_ {n} = R _ {n} - C _ {n}\tag{4}
$$

and

$$
\pi_ {m} = R _ {m},\tag{5}
$$

where ${ \cal R } _ { n } ^ { \mathrm { ~ ~ } } ( R _ { m } )$ is the expected revenue earned by the innovator (imitator) from product sales in the fourth stage.

## Product Pricing (Stage 4)

In Stage 4, both firms observe the product qualities set in the previous stages and then simultaneously set prices for their products. Following the standard models of vertical product differentiation [12], we assume that consumers have heterogeneous tastes regarding product quality and prefer high-quality products to low-quality products. We also assume that each consumer purchases either one product or nothing at all. The indirect utility of an individual consumer is $( \mathsf { { \boldsymbol { \theta } } } \boldsymbol { s } _ { n } - \boldsymbol { p } _ { n } )$ if he or she buys from the innovator, $( \theta s _ { { \scriptscriptstyle m } } - p _ { { \scriptscriptstyle m } } )$ if he or she buys from the imitator, and zero if he or she does not buy a product, where $p _ { i } , i \in \{ n , m \}$ is the price of Firm i’s product and q is a consumer taste parameter that indicates the intensity with which a consumer prefers product quality. The taste parameter q is uniformly distributed with density 1.

Consumers with a taste parameter q, where} $( p _ { _ n } - p _ { _ m } ) / ( s _ { _ n } - s _ { _ m } ) < \tilde { \Theta } \leq 1$ , buy the highquality product from the innovator since $( { \tilde { \Theta } } s _ { \scriptscriptstyle n } - p _ { \scriptscriptstyle n } ) \geq ( { \tilde { \Theta } } s _ { \scriptscriptstyle m } - p _ { \scriptscriptstyle m } ) . ^ { 8 }$ Consumers with a taste parameter ${ \hat { \boldsymbol { \theta } } } ,$ , where{ $p _ { { _ m } } / s _ { { _ m } } < \hat { \Theta } < ( p _ { { _ n } } - p _ { { _ m } } ) / ( s _ { { _ n } } - s _ { { _ m } } )$ , buy the low-quality product from the imitator since $( \hat { \Theta } s _ { _ m } - p _ { _ m } ) > ( \hat { \Theta } s _ { _ n } - p _ { _ n } )$ . The remaining consumers buy nothing. Without loss of generality, we normalize the number of consumers in the market to 1. Therefore, the vertically differentiated demands for the innovator and imitator, respectively, are

$$
Q _ {n} = 1 - \frac {p _ {n} - p _ {m}}{s _ {n} - s _ {m}}\tag{6}
$$

and

$$
Q _ {m} = \frac {p _ {n} - p _ {m}}{s _ {n} - s _ {m}} - \frac {p _ {m}}{s _ {m}}.\tag{7}
$$

Because the purpose of this model is to examine quality–price competition for software products, we assume that marginal production costs are zero. Therefore, the profits earned by the firms in the fourth stage are

$$
R _ {i} = p _ {i} Q _ {i}, \quad \forall i \in \{n, m \}.\tag{8}
$$

In this model, social welfare is defined as the sum of innovator profits, imitator profits, and consumer surplus. Therefore, given the product qualities and prices set by both firms, social welfare is

$$
\omega = \int_ {\frac {p _ {n} - p _ {m}}{s _ {n} - s _ {m}}} ^ {1} \theta s _ {n} d \theta + \int_ {\frac {p _ {m}}{s _ {m}}} ^ {\frac {p _ {n} - p _ {m}}{s _ {n} - s _ {m}}} \theta s _ {m} d \theta - \frac {k s _ {n} ^ {2}}{2} - \frac {h}{2} \Bigl (\alpha_ {1} ^ {2} + \alpha_ {2} ^ {2} \Bigr),\tag{9}
$$

where the first two terms on the right-hand side of Equation (9) are the Stage 4 benefits realized by the innovator, the imitator, and their consumers (i.e., the area under the demand curves); the third term is the Stage 2 development costs incurred by the innovator; and the last term is the total Stage 1 R&D expenditures incurred by both firms. Finally, given the definition of social welfare, consumer surplus is

$$
\Omega = \omega - \pi_ {n} - \pi_ {m}.\tag{10}
$$

In this game, both firms know the payoff functions as common knowledge and know the history of the game thus far. The competition between the two firms is analyzed by solving for the subgame perfect equilibrium of the game through backward induction. ## The Patent Model

In this se ction, we de scribe the d uop oly m ode l when the innovator has the opportunity to attain a patent for the product it develops in Stage 2. In the patent model, the patent authority, with the goal of maximizing social welfare, initially sets a policy defined by three instruments—patent height (m), patent imitation level (l), and patent length (t). The patent height m is the minimum product quality the innovator must develop in the second stage to be awarded a patent. The patent imitation level l is the maximum product quality the imitator may produce in the third stage if the innovator is awarded a patent, where $0 \leq \lambda \leq \mu . ^ { 1 0 }$

In the context of our model, a lower imitation level l represents a wider scope of patent protection. In fact, $\lambda = 0$ provides the innovator with the widest scope of protection while the patent is in force. Consistent with much of the economics literature [12, 13, 15, 22], we assume—to ensure model tractability—that the criterion for judging patentability (patent height) and infringement (patent imitation level) is precise and known to both firms and to the patent authority. Finally, the patent length t is the proportion of the software product life for which the patent holder is protected from imitation, where $0 \% \leq t \leq 1 0 0 \%$ . It is the inclusion of this third policy parameter— patent length—that is the critical contribution of this paper; it allows us derive the results and propositions presented in later sections.

In the second stage of the patent model, the innovator must decide whether to compete in the free market (as described in the previous section) or to seek a patent. If the innovator does not seek a patent and does compete in the free market, then the market will result in the NPE as described in Proposition 1 and Table 1. Alternatively, if the innovator does seek a patent, we assume that the innovator will meet the patent height set by the patent authority $( \mathrm { i . e . , } s _ { _ n } = \mu )$ and patent the technology that will underlie its innovative product. In this case, the imitator’s quality response to the innovator in Stage 3 will be restricted to $s _ { { \scriptscriptstyle m } } \in [ 0 , \lambda ]$ for t proportion of the product life.<sup>11</sup> After the patent expires, the innovator will continue to offer product quality of $s _ { n } = \mu$ to the market. However, the imitator will be allowed to adjust its product quality choice according to its best response function, Equation (19).

The model development that follows provides a new contribution by incorporating a third critical policy parameter—patent length (t)—in the patent model analysis. Based on this discussion, if the innovator decides to avoid the free market and instead decides to attain a patent under patent policy $( \mu , \lambda , \ t )$ , then the innovator profit function in Stage 2 is

$$
\pi_ {n} (\mu , \lambda , t) = t \pi_ {n} (\mu , \lambda) + (1 - t) \pi (\mu , s _ {m} (\mu)),\tag{22}
$$

where the first term on the right-hand side of the profit function represents the profit earned by the innovator during the patent length and the second term represents the profit earned by the innovator after the patent expires. Substituting Equations (15) and (19) into Equation (22) gives

$$
\pi_ {n} (\mu , \lambda , t) = \mu \frac {t (2 0 \mu + \lambda) (4 \mu - 7 \lambda) - (2 4 k \mu - 7) (4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2}}{4 8 (4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2}}.\tag{23}
$$

Similarly, if the innovator attains a patent in Stage 2 under patent policy $( \mu , \lambda , t )$ , we can rewrite the imitator profit function as

$$
\pi_ {m} (\mu , \lambda , t) = \mu \frac {(4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2} - t (4 \mu - 7 \lambda) ^ {2}}{4 8 (4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2}},\tag{24}
$$

the social welfare function as

$$
\omega (\mu , \lambda , t) = \mu \frac {(4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2} (1 1 - 1 2 k \mu) - t (8 \mu - 5 \lambda) (4 \mu - 7 \lambda)}{2 4 (4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2}} - \frac {h}{2} \left(\alpha_ {1} ^ {2} + \alpha_ {2} ^ {2}\right),\tag{25}
$$

the consumer surplus function as

$$
\Omega (\mu , \lambda , t) = \mu \frac {7 (4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2} - t (1 6 \mu - \lambda) (4 \mu - 7 \lambda)}{2 4 (4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2}},\tag{26}
$$

and, finally, the R&D intensity $\mathrm { a s } ^ { 1 2 }$

$$
\alpha_ {1} (\mu , \lambda , t) = \alpha_ {2} (\mu , \lambda , t) = \frac {1}{2} \sqrt {\frac {\pi_ {n} (\mu , \lambda , t) - \pi_ {m} (\mu , \lambda , t)}{h}}.\tag{27}

Equations (23)–(27) present the innovator and imitator profits, consumer surplus, social welfare, and R&D intensity realized when the innovator attains a patent in Stage 2 under a given patent policy $( \mu , \lambda , t )$

## The Patent Equilibrium Region

The patent equilibrium region (PER), the critical analytical artifact generated by this model, is defined as the set of policies that, if set by the patent authority, provide the innovator profit incentive to attain a patent. The innovator has profit incentive to attain a patent if the innovator would earn more profit with a patent under patent policy $( \mu , \lambda , t )$ than it would in the free market,

$$
\pi_ {n} (\mu , \lambda , t) \geq \pi_ {n} ^ {*} (s _ {n} ^ {*}, s _ {m} ^ {*}).\tag{28}
$$

Solving the above inequality for $\lambda$ gives

$$
\lambda \leq \frac {\mu \left(7 6 8 k \mu (2 4 \mu - 1 7 t - 7) + 3 9 2 + 1 9 2 \sqrt {- 6 t k \mu (9 6 k \mu (2 4 k \mu - 9 t - 7) + 4 9)}\right)}{2 (9 6 k \mu (2 4 k \mu - 7 + 7 t) + 4 9)}.\tag{29}
$$

Equation (29) defines a three-dimensional region of policies that, if set by the patent authority, result in a patent equilibrium (PE) instead of the NPE (i.e., free market equilibrium). Figure 4 illustrates the PER for a given patent length, t = 80 percent. The vertical axis maps the innovator’s product quality (and patent height) and the horizontal axis maps the imitator’s product quality (and patent imitation level). The imitator’s best-response curve (i.e., Equation (19)) maps the imitator’s best response to each potential quality choice made by the innovator in the free market. The NPE represents the product quality choices $( s _ { n } ^ { * } , s _ { m } ^ { * } )$ made by both firms in the free market (i.e., in the absence of patents). Finally, the PER defines the combinations of patent height and patent imitation level that, if set by the patent authority, provide the innovator profit incentive to meet the height and patent its product for the given patent length (in this case t = 80%). The boundary of the PER is an iso-profit curve for the innovator. Any policy that lies on the PER boundary generates the same Stage 2 profit for the innovator as in the free market NPE (i.e., Equation (28) becomes an equality).

To clarify the PER , assume that the patent authority sets a patent policy at point $( \mu _ { a } , \lambda _ { a } )$ in Figure 4. In this case (by the definition of the PER in Equation (28)), the innovator maximizes profits by attaining a patent; that is, the innovator will develop a product that meets the height, patent the product, and constrain the imitator to the patent imitation level for the duration of the patent (80 percent of the product life). After the patent expires the innovator would keep its product on the market, but the imitator would be allowed to adjust its product quality according to its best response curve as shown at point $( \mu _ { a } , s _ { _ m } ( \mu _ { a } ) )$ . The PER is the target the patent authority must hit to give the innovator profit incentive to seek a patent (see the definition of the PER in Equations (28) and (29)). If the patent authority sets any policy outside of the PER , then the innovator will not seek a patent and the firms will compete in a free market, resulting in the NPE. That is, if the patent authority sets patent height or patent imitation level too high—for example, point $( \mu _ { b } , \lambda _ { b } )$ in Figure 4—it will miss the target altogether, rendering the policy impotent and resulting in the NPE. In this case, the innovator is deterred from seeking a patent due to the substantial fixed costs that the innovator would have to incur in Stage 2 to develop the large product quality required to attain the patent height. Finally, the policy instrument combinations that lie on the boundary of the PER (which is an innovator iso-profit curve) are policies for which the innovator earns the same Stage 2 profits under the PE and the free market equilibrium.

In summary, the innovator will only seek a patent if the profit $( \pi _ { n } \left( \mu , \lambda , t \right) )$ earned with a patent exceeds the profit $( \pi _ { n } \left( s _ { n } , s _ { m } \right) )$ earned in the free market. If the innovator does attain a patent, then the innovator and imitator set product qualities $( \mu , \lambda )$ 9 respectively, for t proportion of the product life and product qualities $( \mu , s _ { _ m } ( \mu ) )$ after the patent expires (see Figure 4). Alternatively, if the innovator does not seek a patent, then the innovator will compete with the imitator in the free market, resulting in the NPE product qualities $( s _ { n } ^ { * } , s _ { m } ^ { * } )$ for the life of the product (see Table 1).

## Welfare Distribution in the PER

We now use this model to examine the impact of alternative policies within the PER on social welfare. Figure 5 maps the PER for patent length of 80 percent and the iso-welfare curves for society and consumers that intersect the NPE. Note that all patent policies (or combinations of patent height and imitation level) that lie on these iso-welfare curves generate the same level of welfare as generated under free market competition.

By definition, any policy set within the PER improves the innovator’s profit when compared to the NPE (see the definition of the PER in Equation (28)). Bad policies (within the PER) lie below both the consumer and social welfare iso-curves. These policies benefit the innovator at the expense of consumers and will adversely affect social welfare when compared to the NPE. These policies are generally characterized by lower patent heights. In fact, the policy in the PER that generates the lowest level of social welfare has the lowest feasible height combined with a full scope of protection from imitation. This area of the PER is consistent with many critics’ characterization that software innovators misuse the patent system to capture monopoly power without providing consumers the value of substantial innovation. As such, legislation and Federal Circuit court decisions over the past two decades that have lowered the patent height [17, 19] for software products while simultaneously widening the patent width [32] may be socially detrimental as critics claim.

The mixed policies lie above the social welfare iso-curve but below the consumer’s curve. These policies also benefit the innovator at the expense of consumers; however, in this case total social welfare increases. Although these policies may be more palatable because of the net gain in social welfare, the patent authority may still have concerns about setting a policy that simultaneously limits competition and adversely affects consumers.

Finally, the good policies lie above both iso-welfare curves. These policies improve the welfare of both the innovator and consumers, resulting in an improvement in social welfare. These policies are generally characterized by higher patent heights than the bad policies. This suggests that recent U.S. Supreme Court rulings (e.g., KSR v. Teleflex, 2007) that have increased the patent height standard may be socially beneficial. Interestingly, this area also shows that providing the innovator with full monopoly use of its product (i.e., l = 0) during the patent length may benefit consumers and improve social welfare (compared to the NPE), as long as the patent height is set sufficiently high. This suggests that legislation and Federal Circuit court decisions over the last decades that have decreased the patent imitation level for software products [32] may not necessarily be harmful to society unless the existing patent height is set too low. Clearly, the patent authority must be concerned not only with locating and hitting the PER but also with where to aim within the PER in order to avoid harmful outcomes.

## The Effects of Marginal Changes in Policy Instruments

In this se ction, we spe cifically conside r p ate nt p olicie s that lie within the PER and use comparative static analysis to examine the individual effect of a marginal change in each policy instrument, given a PE, on R&D expenditures and social welfare when holding the other two policy instruments constant. This analysis assumes that the legislature (which sets patent length) and the patent examiners and courts (which interpret patent height and patent width) do not or cannot coordinate to maximize social welfare over all three policy instruments simultaneously. This is certainly consistent with the real world.

## R&D Expenditures (Stage 1)

Not surprisingly, the partial derivative of the total Stage 1 R&D expenditure function $[ c _ { _ { R \& D } } = ( 1 / 2 ) h ( \alpha _ { _ 1 } ^ { _ 2 } + \alpha _ { _ 2 } ^ { _ 2 } ) ]$ with respect to patent length is positive:

$$
\frac {\partial \left(c _ {R \& D}\right)}{\partial t} = \frac {\mu (4 \mu - 7 \lambda)}{3 2 (4 \mu - \lambda)} > 0,\tag{30) \( ^{13} \}
$$

and the partial derivative with respect to patent imitation level is negative:

$$
\frac {\partial \left(c _ {R \& D}\right)}{\partial \lambda} = - \frac {3 t \mu^ {2}}{4 (4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2}} <   0.\tag{31}
$$

Proposition 3: Given a patent policy set within the PER, a marginal increase in patent length (holding patent height and imitation level constant) increases total R&D expenditures.

Proposition 4: Given a patent policy set within the PER, a marginal decrease in patent imitation level (holding patent height and length constant) increases total R&D expenditures

To help explain these results, we note that the R&D intensity of both firms is directly linked to the difference in expected innovator and imitator profits $( \pi _ { n } - \pi _ { m } )$ in the product development stages; this difference may be interpreted as the economic benefit achieved by an innovating firm, or the economic incentive for winning the Stage 1 R&D race (see Equation (27)). Of course, the size of the economic incentive $( \pi _ { n } - \pi _ { m } )$ depends on the patent policy set by the patent authority. A marginal increase in patent length allows the innovator to exert its market power for a longer portion of the product life and gives the imitator less time to compete freely with (or respond optimally to) the innovator after the patent expires. Alternatively, a marginal decrease in patent imitation level increases the market power exerted by the innovator during the patent life without affecting the firms’ quality choices after the patent expires. In both cases, the result in product development stages is an increase in expected innovator profits and a decrease in expected imitator profits, resulting in an increase in the economic incentive for winning the Stage 1 R&D race. This leads to an increase in each firm’s R&D intensity and in total Stage 1 R&D expenditures.

On the other hand, the partial derivative of the total R&D expenditure function with respect to patent height cannot be signed:

$$
\frac {\partial \left(c _ {R \& D}\right)}{\partial \mu} = \frac {(4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2} (1 - 8 k \mu) + t (8 \mu (2 \mu - \lambda) + 7 \lambda^ {2})}{3 2 (4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2}}.\tag{32}
$$

The sign of this derivative depends on the innovator’s cost of quality parameter (or production efficiency) such that

$$
\text { if } k <   \frac {(4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2} + t (8 \mu (2 \mu - \lambda) + 7 \lambda^ {2})}{8 \mu (4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2}}, \text { then } \frac {\partial c _ {R \& D}}{\partial \mu} > 0, \text { otherwise } \frac {\partial c _ {R \& D}}{\partial \mu} <   0.
$$

This leads to the following proposition:

Proposition 5: Given a patent policy set within the PER, the impact of a marginal increase in patent height (holding patent length and imitation level constant) on both firms’ investments in R&D activities will depend on the efficiency of the firms’ Stage 2 production capabilities when transforming a novel idea into a commercial product. Specifically,

(a) if the firms’ production capabilities are relatively efficient (i.e., k is sufficiently low), then an increase in patent height will lead to an increase in Stage 1 R&D expenditures;

(b) otherwise, if the firms’ production capabilities are relatively inefficient, then an increase in patent height will lead to a decrease in Stage 1 R&D expenditures.

That is, if the firms’ Stage 2 production capabilities are relatively efficient, then an increase in the patent height (which given a low k will be relatively cheap for the innovator to attain in Stage 2) will increase the difference in second-stage profits and, therefore, increase both firms’ R&D expenditures in Stage 1. Alternatively, if the firms production capabilities are relatively inefficient, then an increase in the patent height (which given a high k will be relatively costly for the innovator to attain in Stage 2) will reduce the difference in second-stage profits and, therefore, decrease both firms R&D expenditures in Stage 1.

These results have significant implications for regulators setting patent policy to stimulate R&D activities and encourage innovation. Not surprisingly, a wider scope of protection from imitation or a longer duration of protection will provide economic incentives for more R&D intensity. Similarly, setting higher patent heights in industries characterized by efficient, modernized infrastructures for product development may increase economic incentives for R&D. However, setting higher patent heights in industries characterized by less-efficient, legacy infrastructures may reduce economic incentives for R&D investments. As a result, regulators must be particularly careful to consider the production capabilities of competing firms before setting patent height since increasing the patent height may actually reduce R&D expenditures in certain contexts.

## Social Welfare

The partial derivative of the social welfare function with respect to patent length is negative:

$$
\frac {\partial \omega (\mu , \lambda , t)}{\partial t} = - \frac {\mu (4 \mu - 7 \lambda) (8 \mu - 5 \lambda)}{2 4 (4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {2}} <   0.\tag{33}
$$

That is, an increase in patent length, holding height and imitation level constant, reduces social welfare since longer patents give the imitator less time to compete freely with (or respond optimally to) the innovator after the patent expires.

Proposition 6: Given a patent policy set within the PER, a marginal increase in patent length (holding patent height and imitation level constant) reduces social welfare.

The partial derivative of the social welfare function with respect to patent imitation level is positive:

$$
\frac {\partial \omega (\mu , \lambda , t)}{\partial \lambda} = \frac {t \mu^ {2} (2 0 \mu - 1 7 \lambda)}{2 (4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {3}} > 0.\tag{34}
$$

That is, a decrease in patent imitation level (or an increase in the width of protection), holding height and length constant, reduces social welfare since it increases the market power exerted by the innovator during the patent without affecting the firms’ quality choices after the patent expires.

Proposition 7: Given a patent policy set within the PER, a marginal decrease in patent imitation level (holding patent height and length constant) reduces social welfare.

However, the partial derivative of the social welfare function with respect to patent height cannot be signed:

$$
\frac {\partial \omega (\mu , \lambda , t)}{\partial \mu} = \frac {(4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {3} (1 1 - 2 4 k \mu) - t (1 2 8 \mu^ {3} - 9 6 \mu^ {2} \lambda + 1 2 \mu \lambda^ {2} - 3 5 \lambda^ {3})}{2 4 (4 \mu - \lambda) ^ {3}}.\tag{35}
$$

While Propositions 6 and 7 may be intuitive, it may be surprising that the directional impact of patent height on social welfare depends on the instantiations of patent height, patent width, and patent length. Of course, the imitator will adjust its product quality to $s _ { m } ( \mu _ { t = 3 4 \% } ^ { * } )$ after the patent expires (i.e., it will respond according to its best response function). Given such a short patent length, the consumer realizes substantial benefits at point $( \mu _ { t = 3 4 \% } ^ { * } , s _ { m } ( \mu _ { t = 3 4 \% } ^ { * } ) )$ after the patent expires. In fact, as illustrated in Figure 10, if patent length is set to 34 percent, consumer surplus is maximized when patent policy is set at $( \boldsymbol { \mu } _ { t = 3 4 \% } ^ { * } , \lambda _ { t = 3 4 \% } ^ { * } )$ . On the other hand, as illustrated by the location and shape of the imitator’s iso-welfare curve, the imitator would prefer to move down from point $( \boldsymbol { \mu } _ { t = 3 4 \% } ^ { * } , \lambda _ { t = 3 4 \% } ^ { * } )$ along the PER to a point where patent height is lower and patent imitation level is higher (or patent width is more narrow). However, such a move along the PER adversely affects consumers more than it benefits the imitator.

Referring back to Figure 8, the policies that lie on line rsv are the optimal combination of height and imitation level for each PER . Each of these policies is a candidate for the socially optimal policy over all three policy instruments. We find that the optimal patent policy is to set patent length to 100 percent and to set patent height and imitation level at point v in Figure 8. That is, it is socially optimal for the patent authority to set patent length to 100 percent as long as the height is set sufficiently high to encourage the production of a high-quality product and the patent imitation level is set sufficiently high to promote some competition from the imitator over the product life.

A key contribution of our model relative to Thatcher et al. [39] is that we relax the assumption of infinite patent length and explore all ranges of t to examine the economic trade-offs among the three patent policy parameters. Interestingly, it turns out that Thatcher et al. [39] made a very good assumption. That is, the socially optimal policy that we find over the range of patent lengths is the same solution found by Thatcher et al. [39] under the assumption of infinite patent length.

Proposition 9: The socially optimal patent policy is to grant the innovator patent protection for the entire life of the innovative product and to set patent height and imitation level such that both the innovator and imitator produce higher-quality products than they would in the free market. This policy not only maximizes social welfare but also makes all stakeholders (the innovator, imitator, and consumers) better off than in the free market.

We note that the innovator is better off under the socially optimal PE than under the NPE because it earns the same Stage 2 profits in either case but spends less on Stage 1 R&D expenditures under the socially optimal PE (see Proposition 10 and discussion below).

Having solved for the socially optimal patent policy, we now compare the Stage 1 R&D intensity exhibited under the socially optimal patent policy to the R&D intensity exhibited in the free market NPE:

Proposition 10: Both firms, the innovator and imitator, invest less in Stage 1 R&D (or exhibit lower R&D intensity) under the socially optimal PE than they do under the NPE (or free market competition).

To understand the intuition behind this result, we consider the equilibrium value of R&D intensity for each firm under a PE. Equation (27) shows that each firm’s choice of R&D intensity in Stage 1 under a PE is

$$
\alpha_ {1} (\mu , \lambda , t) = \alpha_ {2} (\mu , \lambda , t) = \frac {1}{2} \sqrt {\frac {\pi_ {n} (\mu , \lambda , t) - \pi_ {m} (\mu , \lambda , t)}{h}}.
$$

That is, R&D intensity depends directly on the difference in profits expected by the winner (innovator) and loser (imitator) of the R&D race in the remaining stages of the competition. The greater is the difference in expected profits, the higher is the R&D intensity due to competitive threat. As discussed above, if the patent authority sets the socially optimal patent policy (point v in Figure 8), the innovator earns the same Stage 2 profits as it would in the NPE, but the imitator earns more profits under this policy than it would in the NPE. Therefore, the difference in expected profits between the innovator and imitator in the product development stages is less under the patent policy at point v than in the NPE, resulting in lower R&D intensity under the socially optimal patent policy. However, it is important to note that the socially optimal policy does lead to more investment in software development by the innovator in Stage 2, resulting in higher-quality products in the market once the R&D race is won (see Proposition 9).

## Conclusions

Based on the m ode l, the reader may be tempted to believe that the patent authority should be able to clearly see the different PERs (or policy targets) over time and identify a portfolio of patent policies optimized across products and industries. However, there are implementation issues that affect the patent authority’s ability to see the PER and to set effective policy. Specifically, the data requirements for setting good patent policy are enormous. The patent authority must collect accurate information about the characteristics of consumer demand, the characteristics of the innovator’s cost structure, the innovator’s cost of quality parameter, and the imitator’s best response function, as well as forecast the patent life for each innovation. In addition, the patent authority must develop and maintain a complete, up-to-date repository of prior artifacts to assess the novelty of the innovator’s product. Since all of the information may change over time, it must be continuously collected, updated, and maintained to identify the size and location of the PER, and therefore, identify socially good policy options. In some cases, the PER may be impossible to identify since this information may not be available to the patent authority. A great deal of empirical research is needed to determine the perverse effects of these data issues and to determine ways to overcome them.

Although highly stylized, the model presented in this paper offers a useful framework for researchers, regulators, and policymakers to use to think about the economic aspects of software patent policy design and implementation [38]. It also opens the possibility for empirical explorations and model extensions. Many issues arose in the construction, analysis, and review of this model that call for future extensions. For example, future work may extend the model to examine the impact of sequential, cumulative innovation, consider software competition among more than two firms, and consider the impact of blocking patents. In addition, future work may compare and contrast the costs, benefits, and trade-offs among alternative intellectual property protection mechanisms—such as trade secrets, copyrights, and private protection technologies— and examine the socially optimal portfolio of protection mechanisms (both public and private). In addition, future work may also consider alternative product development cost structures and attempt to generalize the results beyond the software-specific assumptions in our model (e.g., zero imitation costs and zero marginal costs).

While the economic model presented in this paper demonstrates the potential benefits and pitfalls of awarding software patents, it also exposes the enormous amount of information required to attain the benefits while avoiding the pitfalls. The balance of the richness of the model (placing the patent authority in the first-mover position and the inclusion of all three critical policy parameters) and the simplicity of the model (using specific functional forms to derive the critical PER artifact) has created a framework that stimulates and enables a disciplined discussion of the critical economic trade-offs in making patent policy.

In this Appendix, we demonstrate that this is indeed the unique equilibrium of the free market competition model by showing that the innovator has no economic incentive to switch roles and enter the market as the low-quality provider. To prove this, we first assume that Equations (1)–(5) still hold.

We now reconsider the pricing competition in Stage 4 by assuming that the innovator has chosen to be the low-quality provider. The indirect utility of an individual consumer is $( \mathsf { { \boldsymbol { \theta } } } \boldsymbol { s } _ { n } - \boldsymbol { p } _ { n } )$ if he or she buys from the innovator, $( \theta s _ { { \scriptscriptstyle m } } - p _ { { \scriptscriptstyle m } } )$ if he or she buys from the imitator, and zero if he or she does not buy a product. $$
Q _ {m} = 1 - \frac {p _ {m} - p _ {n}}{s _ {m} - s _ {n}}\tag{A1}
$$

and

$$
Q _ {n} = \frac {p _ {m} - p _ {n}}{s _ {m} - s _ {n}} - \frac {p _ {n}}{s _ {n}}.\tag{A2}
$$

The profits earned by the firms in the fourth stage are presented in Equation (8).

$$
p _ {m} (s _ {n}, s _ {m}) = \frac {2 s _ {m} (s _ {m} - s _ {n})}{4 s _ {m} - s _ {n}}\tag{A3}
$$

and

p _ {n} (s _ {n}, s _ {m}) = \frac {s _ {n} (s _ {m} - s _ {n})}{4 s _ {m} - s _ {n}}.\tag{A4}

$$
\pi_ {m} \left(s _ {n}, s _ {m}\right) = \frac {4 s _ {m} ^ {2} \left(s _ {m} - s _ {n}\right)}{\left(4 s _ {m} - s _ {n}\right) ^ {2}}\tag{A5}

s _ {m} (s _ {n}) = 0.\tag{A9}

$$

$$

Therefore, given its role as the quality leader, the innovator will choose to produce the high-quality product as presented in the paper, making both firms better off.

The result derived above is consistent with the work of Moorthy [28], which demonstrates that the leader in a sequential quality game will exercise its first-mover advantage by choosing to produce the high-quality product and force the follower to differentiate its product by choosing to produce the low-quality product.
