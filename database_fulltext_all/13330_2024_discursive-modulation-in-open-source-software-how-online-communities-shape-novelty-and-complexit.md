# DISCURSIVE MODULATION IN OPEN SOURCE SOFTWARE: HOW ONLINE COMMUNITIES SHAPE NOVELTY AND COMPLEXITY<sup>1</sup>

Aron Lindberg School of Business, Stevens Institute of Technology, Hoboken, NJ U.S.A. {aron.lindberg@stevens.edu}

Nicholas Berente Mendoza College of Business, University of Notre Dame, Notre Dame, IN U.S.A. {nberente@nd.edu}

James Howison School of Information, The University of Texas at Austin, Austin, TX U.S.A. {jhowison@ischool.utexas.edu}

Kalle Lyytinen Weatherhead School of Management, Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, OH U.S.A. {kalle@case.edu}

We study the development of two open source software (OSS) web frameworks to understand how OSS communities shape software novelty and complexity in the absence of strong organizational hierarchies. We examine how projects engage in distinct “discursive modulation practices” to imprint the community’s shared core doctrines and design principles onto the software thereby shaping its novelty and complexity. We borrow the concept of modulation from audio synthesis to explain how a preexisting signal—in our case, the ongoing community discourse—is modulated to produce varying sounds—in our case, the novelty and complexity of the software. The concept of modulation offers a lens to understand how emergent, community-wide development activities are influenced by filtering discursive positions and mixing those positions, thereby shaping the artifact’s novelty and complexity. Our research shows that the modulation of novelty exhibits a range from “proximal” to “distal” searches for new features, while the modulation of complexity varies between “integration” and “deprecation.” By drawing on these concepts, we formulate a theory that explains how modulation results in alternative OSS community approaches to shaping software novelty and complexity and how this process reflects and is reflected in the resulting software artifact.

Keywords: Open source software, online communities, discourse, discursive modulation, novelty, complexity

## Introduction

Research on open source software (OSS) development explores how distributed communities of autonomous developers coordinate to create novel software artifacts while managing their complexity, without the benefit of strong organizational hierarchies (Winter et al., 2014). This research suggests that distributed OSS development is enabled by modularization (Langlois & Garzarelli, 2008), which allows developers to work independently to solve simple problems while deferring more difficult problems until incremental progress renders them tractable (Howison & Crowston, 2014). Because all interdependencies within software cannot be managed through modularization and superpositioning, developers deal with residual interdependencies via emergent routines through which they discursively integrate software knowledge (Lindberg et al., 2016).

Though prior research provides valuable insight into how OSS communities address coordination problems, it says little about how OSS communities shape the software artifact along dimensions such as novelty and complexity. What we know is that OSS communities share distinct goals and values (Maruping et al., 2019), reflected in their design principles and core doctrines. These principles and doctrines guide how software artifacts are shaped and how development should proceed. Community discourse plays a visible role in creating such normative expectations and standards—developers discuss in order to integrate diverse perspectives and knowledge (Tuertscher et al., 2014). This allows OSS communities to tackle thorny problems at the level of the community and its software for issues such as technology roadmaps, architecture, and so on, each of which requires concentrated community efforts (Shaikh & Vaast, 2016). But how does such discourse shape developer activities, which ultimately produce software with varying characteristics?

Since the early days of software development, software novelty and complexity have been recognized as two important characteristics that software developers need to address (Highsmith & Cockburn, 2001; Zmud, 1980). As projects develop software with increasingly novel features, complexity—the volume and variety of components and their relationships—also tends to grow (Lehman, 1980, 1996). Over time, growing complexity incurs escalating maintenance costs and creates steeper learning curves for developers and users (Benaroch & Lyytinen, 2023). Yet without novelty, the software usefulness will eventually deteriorate due to weak compatibility with evolving standards and external components, which results in limitations to features and functionality (Izurieta & Bieman, 2013). As a result, software can lose its competitive edge (Ghapanchi et al., 2014; Ravi, 2007).

Choices about appropriate levels of novelty and complexity also relate to OSS work (Butler et al., 1999; Parker et al., 2017). By prioritizing varying levels of novelty and complexity, the OSS community signals which types of participants it seeks to attract because OSS projects attract developers whose values align with the dominant community values concerning “good design” (Maruping et al., 2019). Managing complexity and pursuing novelty in OSS communities requires system-level coordination that extends across the entire project lifecycle in terms of how the community attracts participants and how it shapes their activities. Thus, we ask the following research question related to the role of discourse in shaping these two important dimensions: How do OSS communities use discourse to shape the novelty and complexity of the software they develop?

To answer this question, we present a comparative field study of two mid-sized OSS projects, Django and Rails. Both are popular web development frameworks. We develop a process theory of “discursive modulation” that explains how OSS communities enact the design principles and core doctrines that shape their software artifacts through discourse. We draw on the notion of modulation used in audio synthesis to describe how this discourse operates using filtering and mixing that shape emergent development activity in a way that is consistent with the community’s core doctrines and design principles.

By comparing different ways that community members filter and mix, we identify practices that encourage either proximal (near prior versions of the artifact) or distal (far away from prior versions of the artifact) search approaches to novelty, and either integration (disciplined and coherent adding of new features to the artifact) or deprecation (pruning old features while adding new features) approaches to complexity. We theorize that these practices manifest in the properties of code produced by the communities through a process of mutual shaping between artifact properties and principles guiding its construction. As described below, the concepts and theory emerged using abductive reasoning throughout the research process (Lindberg, 2020), but for clarity, we review them first, as a scaffolding for the reader (Kerr, 1998).

## Conceptual Development

Software is malleable and evolves continually (Lehman, 1980; Kallinikos et al., 2013; Yoo et al., 2010). Developers use a variety of approaches to shape novelty (proximal vs. distal) and deal with the complexity (integration vs. deprecation) of evolving software (Table 1).

Because technology, business, and use environments are constantly changing, software developers need to continuously evolve their software to fit such changing circumstances (Jarke et al., 2011; Kitchenham et al., 1999). As new requirements and technologies emerge, developers typically evolve the software by adding new features (David & Rullani, 2008; Noel & Schankerman, 2013; von Krogh et al., 2003). Because this novelty enhances the overall use-value of the software, fostering novelty is central to software development (Huang et al., 2017), accounting for over 40% of all development efforts over the lifetime of the software (Benaroch & Lyytinen, 2023).

The same challenge is faced by OSS developers (Faraj et al., 2016), who often contribute for the purpose of generating novelty; this is also one of the reasons organizations pay employees to participate in OSS development (Bonaccorsi & Rossi, 2006; Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003; Roberts et al., 2006; Shah, 2006).

Novelty emerges by extending existing software functionality to support additional use cases, often combinatorically, given the modular organization of the software (Arthur, 2009; Messerschmitt & Szyperski, 2003). Modularity allows new and existing modules to be combined in often unpredictable ways, enabling new features. Novelty can be defined as the conceptual distance between the features of the current artifact and the new artifact (Katila & Ahuja, 2002; Kyriakou et al., 2017, 2022), and the development of novel software can be framed as a search process (Newell & Simon, 1972; Nickerson & Zenger, 2004). Novelty, when understood in terms of the scope and size of the search space traversed during software development can be conceptualized as a continuum between proximal and distal novelty related to the current software artifact (Knudsen & Srikanth, 2014; Lopez-Vega et al., 2016; Nelson & Winter, 1982).

Proximal novelty manifests in the incremental evolution of software features. During proximal development developers search for solutions close to the existing software artifact, keeping the conceptual distance between the current artifact and the new version under development relatively small (Kyriakou et al., 2017, 2022). Proximal novelty represents incremental, iterative enhancements of current functions and features, which often involves fine-tuning or expanding an existing feature set through evolutionary refinement (Arthur, 2009). Established software generally evolves incrementally through proximal novelty (Jørgensen, 2001; Mandal et al., 2011). In OSS, developers have been shown to defer major changes and expansions until incremental accretions to current software make such larger problems more tractable (Howison & Crowston, 2014).

Occasionally, however, software evolves in a distal manner and makes leaps in novelty. This requires developers to search in a wide-ranging manner (Katila & Ahuja, 2002) to produce a richer variety of original, distinct features that are conceptually distant from existing features. Such features can evolve the artifact in larger “leaps” (Afuah & Tucci, 2012; Piezunka & Dahlander, 2015), which has been observed in OSS development (Conaldi & De Vita, 2011; Medappa & Srivastava, 2019).

Either way, increasing software novelty tends to engender software complexity. Lehman observed this early on: “as an evolving program is continually changed, its complexity, reflecting deteriorating structure, increases unless work is done to maintain or reduce it” (1980, p. 1068). Software complexity can be defined by the number of components of a software and their internal and external interrelationships, typically denoted as “dependencies” (MacCormack et al., 2006), which have been shown to often dramatically increase development effort and cost (Banker et al., 1993; Banker & Slaughter, 2000; MacCormack & Sturtevant, 2016). Complexity has also been shown to be harmful to users and the market performance of software (Chapin et al., 2001). Hence, one of the main tasks of any software development is how to manage complexity during the evolution of the software (Lehman, 1980).

Modularization is the predominant solution for managing complexity in all types of software (Parnas, 1972), including OSS (MacCormack et al., 2006; Parnas, 1972). Software modularization involves the encapsulation of elements of code behind standardized interfaces. This results in chunking the code into callable modules and black-boxing internal dependencies within the modules. Inter-module dependencies are defined by interfaces that reduce and make visible the web of dependencies between modules. Modularization, however, can rarely address all dependencies within the software. Other, often hidden, dependencies lurk in the software and are exacerbated as the software grows, eventually requiring remodularization and refactoring of the codebase (Jaaksi, 2007;

Pantiuchina et al., 2020). OSS software, in particular, is highly sensitive to growing complexity, stemming from the distributed agency of developers (Capiluppi et al., 2004; Kagdi et al., 2007). Since most OSS developers work on a few individual modules by layering code on top of existing code (Howison & Crowston, 2014), related interdependencies inevitably remains, requiring additional coordination (Lindberg et al., 2016).

Given the limits of modularization in tackling complexity, complementary approaches are needed to manage software evolution. We characterize such approaches along a continuum from integration to deprecation. The integration approach involves the systematic, disciplined effort to rewrite code to enable new features and functions in such a way that it maintains consistency with the existing artifact to simplify maintenance (Jaaksi, 2007; Lindberg et al., 2016). This can involve more frequent refactoring to minimize or coordinate interdependencies (Pantiuchina et al., 2020). Such approaches are widely used in OSS communities (Vassallo et al., 2019).

The deprecation approach, in contrast, refers to discontinuing support for software components such as features and functions (Sawant et al., 2018a). Such pruning of components decreases complexity by reducing interdependencies (Jaaksi, 2007; Simon, 1969). Deprecation occurs for several reasons, such as taste, financial concerns, lack of fit with current use cases, aging technologies, or the lack of maintenance support (Sawant et al., 2018b). The downside of deprecation is that it often undermines the usefulness of the software for those who rely on previous versions. Deprecation has also been shown to be a necessary feature of OSS development when new features supersede previous features, or previous features have become redundant, unsupported, or flawed (Sawant et al., 2018b).

## Discursive Modulation Practices

How then, do OSS communities shape software novelty and complexity given the absence of a strong hierarchy? Leaders of OSS communities such as core developers, corporate participants, and members of various steering committees set goals for software projects. At the same time, they also espouse generic principles for project participation and ways of producing code. They also seek to maintain some level of control over the project by setting up technology roadmaps, release cycles, and timetables for new releases (Germonprez et al., 2017; Lindberg et al., 2024; Shaikh & Henfridsson, 2017). Despite these efforts, they remain limited in their ability to control OSS communities and enforce related policies and goals, because most OSS developers are autonomous (O’Mahony & Ferraro, 2007) and work for organizations different from those of project leaders (Agerfalk & Fitzgerald, 2008).

Thus, OSS coordination presents a unique challenge. Developers can solve specific development problems and produce code independently without a traditional decision process (Eseryel et al., 2020). As a result OSS communities are susceptible to collective action dilemmas (Spaeth et al., 2008), making it particularly difficult to manage project-level initiatives and enforce the design principles and core doctrines that are used to shape novelty and manage complexity in traditional software development.

OSS leaders enact governance mostly through communication (Di Tullio & Staples, 2013; Shaikh & Henfridsson, 2017). Other important stakeholders, like corporate participants, also seek to influence OSS communities and do so in part through extensive communication with the community (Germonprez et al., 2017). It is through community discourse that core doctrines and design principles are shared and enforced within OSS communities. Indeed, several studies suggest that discourse serves as the coordination mechanism of last resort for coordination challenges (Lindberg et al., 2016; Sharma et al., 2021). Research, however, has not investigated how the core doctrines and design principles at the community level are sustained, enforced, and enacted through discourse to shape development activities. Discourse has been left as a relatively undifferentiated concept; in this paper, we advance research by differentiating types of discourse and explaining how they relate to project-level design principles and core doctrines.

We identify a process that we describe as “discursive modulation” for how community members shape OSS activity and the resulting software artifact. We accomplish this through what Arthur (2009, p. 122) calls “principle transfer”—a way to apply the principles of one scientific field to another. We draw on signal processing and audio synthesis theory, which uses the concept of modulation to capture how an existing signal can be modified to produce sounds with particular characteristics, when there is no control of the signal at its source (Vail, 2014). In OSS development, where the actions of autonomous developers cannot be controlled at their source, this modulation takes place through discourse in the community.

The concept of discursive modulation offers useful principle transfer from signal processing to OSS because it describes how specific information (i.e., approaches to novelty and complexity) is being imprinted on an already ongoing carrier signal (OSS development) without controlling the carrier signal at its source (i.e., autonomous developers, cf. Pierce, 1980). Modulation offers an explanatory mechanism for how core doctrines and design principles are continuously shared, transmitted, and enforced within the OSS project. This explanation may be especially useful in situations where formal, hierarchical methods of governance are not available.

We are not the first to draw on the idea of modulation. Nickerson & Zenger (2002) applied modulation to describe self-correcting oscillations between competing organizing approaches. Like their analysis, we focus on a process through which oscillations (i.e., alternations between different opinions and viewpoints across community discourse) in self-organized activity can be proactively influenced (i.e., to be consistent with a community’s core doctrines and design principles). However, our application departs from theirs in that we draw directly on principles of audio synthesis to articulate how modulation occurs at the level of discourse.

We argue that the shaping of ongoing discourse in OSS communities is principally accomplished using filtering (i.e., encouraging and discouraging certain discursive positions— different viewpoints on a particular development issue) and mixing (i.e., interrelating different discursive positions; see Table 2). For our analysis, the idea of discursive modulation not only captures how oscillations across discourse unfold but also provides insights into mechanisms that influence discursive oscillations and thereby offers levers to shape such discourse.

Filtering concerns selecting and enforcing varying perspectives on software characteristics. Filtering over time imprints core doctrines and design principles onto the ongoing discourse about how to approach novelty and complexity. Filtering consists of encouragement, discouragement, justification, and the questioning of positions in discussions. The extent to which various positions are encouraged or discouraged reflects the norms and values that steer developers in a shared and desired direction (Ghosh et al., 2004). Norms, which are enforced rules and expectations, are a key coordination mechanism for loosely organized communities such as OSS (Kirsch et al., 2010). OSS developers tend to participate in communities where the shared values and norms are congruent with their own (Krogh et al., 2012; Maruping et al., 2019). Such values include standards for recognizing, sorting, and evaluating software artifacts (Bowker & Star, 1999). Overall, filtering as a mechanism of discursive modulation influences what facets of the artifact software developers attend to, how they deal with related issues, and why they focus on these issues and not others.

Mixing describes how OSS communities integrate diverse perspectives on the artifact and reconcile potential conflicts among them. Mixing can vary between integration and deprecation. In some communities, discourse may unfold in harmonious ways (Bohm, 2004) and involves a search for mutual understanding (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995). Such communities may take an integration approach to mixing perspectives in that they seek to resolve disputes and reconcile, compromise, and integrate diverse perspectives into a consensus approach. In other communities, discourse may iterate between justification and contestation (Tuertscher et al., 2014) without resulting in compromise or consensus. Such communities may take a deprecation approach to mixing perspectives, essentially avoiding the need to form a consensus.

Overall, we posit that discursive modulation and associated mechanisms shape development activities and thereby move the software toward characteristics consistent with the unique nature of the community. The outcome of audio synthesis is called “timbre”—the unique characteristics of the sound produced (Vail, 2014). In our case, the timbre captures a specific approach that the community uses to shape the novelty and complexity of software artifacts. Hence, the way in which core doctrines and design principles are enacted through filtering and mixing constitutes community-level approaches to shaping software novelty and complexity. We also posit that such approaches can shape novelty in either a proximal or a distal way and manage complexity either through integration or deprecation. This creates a specific profile for each OSS community concerning how they attend to software features in the short and long term.

## A Comparative Case Study

To understand how OSS communities shape novelty and complexity in their software, we present a comparative, exploratory case study of two successful full-stack web development frameworks—Django and Rails. The two projects are similar in size and functionality and are hosted on the same development platform (GitHub). Both projects produce popular software and enjoy a reasonable degree of success (English & Schweik, 2007; Wiggins & Crowston,

2010). They are mature projects and have had numerous official releases since their founding (Django in 2003, Rails in 2004.) They are among the largest projects hosted on GitHub, but still significantly smaller than the giants of the OSS world, such as Linux or Apache.<sup>2</sup> Both Django and Rails have built substantial communities of developers and a similar scale of core team members and committers (Django = 68, Rails = 66 at the time of the study). While many developers in both communities are employed by firms, neither is “owned” by a corporation. The projects are large enough to have substantial communities but small enough to be primarily self-organizing. Thus, they are representative cases (Eisenhardt, 1989). Further, as two of the most popular full-stack web development frameworks, the communities are rivals and explicitly distinguish themselves from each other. We sought to characterize that distinction in the community discourses to understand how they shaped novelty and complexity.

## Data Collection

We collected qualitative and quantitative data from both projects. Qualitative data collection focused on the discourse each community relied on to shape novelty and complexity. Quantitative data enabled us to measure the novelty and complexity of the software produced by each project and compare them systematically. The qualitative data corpus consisted of interviews with key developers and archival data for both communities over a six-year period.

We conducted 25 interviews (12 Django developers, 13 Rails developers). Interviewees included founders, core team members, and peripheral contributors. Interviews were semistructured, flexible, and focused on the lived experiences of informants. The duration of interviews ranged from 45 minutes to 3 hours.

The archival data covers a period of six years, from 2011 to 2016, and comprises 15,618 items (an item can refer to a blog post, a magazine interview, or a video clip, etc.) collected from newsletters, as well as 14,280 email threads collected from the main developer email lists from each community. Using a keyword search for items relevant to the development of Rails and Django, we identified 1,231 items across both projects. Further, we randomly sampled 1,428 email threads. The archival data corpus was used for triangulation to expand the set of themes for examination and to observe the discursive modulation of novelty and complexity more directly than what is possible using interviews. Typically, discursive positions were expressed in lectures, talks, or written blogs and commentaries. The newsletter items were selected by examining all 563 issues of two email newsletters, each of which comprised items relevant to the Django and Rails communities, respectively. The data corpus also included audio (podcasts and interviews, ranging from 1 to 2 hours each), video (conference keynotes, panels, talks, and interviews, ranging in duration from 30 minutes to 3 hours each), and text (mailing list conversations, blog posts, interviews, and documentation). Some portions of public audio had already been transcribed by others, and we transcribed segments based on their relevance to our inquiry.

We also collected data from GitHub, which houses the full codebases of Django and Rails (all releases). We used GitHub’s web interface to download the codebases including their directory structures, code files, documentation, and tests for releases between 2011 and 2016.

## Data Analysis

We followed an open, iterative process of data collection and analysis to abductively build theory utilizing both qualitative and quantitative techniques (Berente et al., 2019; Lindberg, 2020; Miranda et al., 2022). The analysis progressed by “zooming in and out” (Gaskin et al., 2014) across interviews, archival data, and code metrics towards analytic generalizations and back to practice. This formed an abductive inferential process (Sami, 2004), identifying practices that constituted each community’s approach to shaping novelty and complexity and relating these findings to the characteristics of the codebases (Goggins et al., 2010).

Overall, the abductive process started broadly and was motivated by our interest in understanding how OSS communities rely on discourse to shape the artifacts they develop in the absence of traditional governance mechanisms. Over time (and in response to the editorial process), the inquiry narrowed to focus on issues of novelty and complexity. The idea of discursive modulation was fleshed out, along with adjustments to the artifact characteristics that we were interested in. What began as an inquiry into generativity and technical debt in OSS communities, ended in explaining how communities discursively shape software novelty and complexity. Such twists and turns are typical of theory development processes. As Charmaz (2014, p. 26) expressed, it is “similar to a camera with many lenses, first you view a broad sweep of the landscape. Subsequently, you change your lens several times and shorten your focal points to bring key scenes closer and closer into view.” This process required that we “be willing to alter [our] research questions when [we] discover that other questions have greater significance in the field” (Charmaz, 2014, p. 26).

We coded the qualitative data using ATLAS.ti. In total, 637 lower-level codes were generated. Through constant comparison between the data and the literature, we identified and refined codes around how discourse was influenced as well as how it influenced the artifact. Initially, the idea to apply the lens of modulation arose from the first author’s interest in electronic synthesizer music, which, in conjunction with reading the literature while making sense of emerging themes identified in the data, offered a useful lens to capture how discourse influenced the shaping of OSS artifacts.

Our coding iterated back and forth across qualitative and quantitative evidence as well as the literature (Lindberg, 2020). Iterations across data and theorizing took place as follows. First, we identified and formed constructs of interest through the open coding of qualitative data (e.g., influencing novelty.) Through this process, key themes related to discursive modulation (i.e., filtering and mixing) as well as salient aspects of novelty and complexity started to emerge. These insights were constantly validated by empirical and theoretical triangulation (Eisenhardt, 1989). As key themes associated with discursive modulation practices emerged, we returned to the dataset to find evidence of episodes of discursive modulation where acts of filtering, mixing, or both could be observed. We identified 192 such episodes.

Qualitative themes led us to search for variables in the quantitative data to measure and compare the characteristics of the software artifacts in both communities. After we had identified both constructs and related quantitative variables with empirical values, we conducted within-case and between-case comparisons, leading to the additional refinement of constructs. We computed several quantitative measures for software, such as the nesting and branching of folder structures (Capiluppi et al., 2004), in light of the qualitative findings, before settling on operationalizing novelty and complexity in a manner that had good fit with the qualitative data. Quantitative measures informed and validated our conceptual development of discursive practices. For example, we detected differences in the proliferation of new features, which helped corroborate our argument for how the projects shaped novelty proximally or distally.

As noted, we conceptualize novelty in self-referential terms, i.e., a software artifact is more novel the more it has been changed in terms of features and functions in relation to prior versions of the same software. To capture this notion of novelty, we analyzed release notes to count new, major features in each major release. In each set of release notes, new major features were identified using their own headings or bullet points, while minor features were indicated as such or listed as sub-headings or sub-bullets under major features. To control for the size of the artifact in relation to new features, we normalized this measure by dividing the new, major features in each release by the number of files in the release. Such an operationalization of novelty comes with limitations. When comparing the two projects, the measure does not necessarily establish a difference in novelty, as the user may perceive it at a single point of use. This measure, however, can be used to show that one project’s software features change faster than another project’s software features over a set of consecutive versions.

In measuring software complexity, we used “cyclomatic complexity” (McCabe, 1976), which is a common measure computed by constructing a control graph of the software function calls and then tracing the number of possible execution paths in the graph (as created by conditionals, loops, loops within loops, etc.; see McCabe, 1976). As such, cyclomatic complexity captures the degree to which different components of the control flow are interdependent. Higher complexity suggests more interdependencies that need to be addressed when software is modified (Capiluppi et al., 2004; Kagdi et al., 2007; Kallinikos et al., 2013). Cyclomatic complexity was computed for each file in each release. This helped us control for the size of the software artifact. We also computed the total amount of complexity in each release by adding up all the complexity metrics for each file. Our code-based operationalization of complexity imposes certain limitations—it does not allow us to measure “perceived complexity,” i.e., how cognitively taxing a software is to work with (Lehman, 1980), though recent research suggests a strong correlation between the two (Benaroch & Lyytinen, 2023).

## Findings

We narrate our findings in the following order: first, we use our modulation framework to report discursive modulation practices within the Django and Rails communities that were found to shape novelty and complexity. For each community, we describe and interpret several discursive modulation episodes. Then, we present the results of the quantitative analyses, which provide evidence that these approaches to modulation indeed produce different amounts of software novelty and complexity.

## Django’s Discursive Modulation Practices

In the Django community, we found that modulation involves the filtering of discursive positions through appeals to explicit and unitary coding conventions—what we describe as “unitary explicitness.” Further, we identified that the community mixes discursive positions through “structured dialogue,” which serves to integrate a variety of discursive positions. The modulation practices generally tend to strive towards a consensus around a singular solution rather than heterogeneity. Valuing consensus encourages the integration of discursive positions and limits the distance a new version of the software could veer from the current version. Therefore, Django tends to search for proximal novelty when developing new features. Further, because of the need to integrate multiple discursive positions, the community subscribes to an approach to manage complexity emphasizing integration. ## Filtering (Unitary Explicitness)

Django developers view software development as a craft founded on rigorous engineering principles. Several participants within the community have doctoral degrees and they generally perceive themselves to be “serious engineers.” Django community members also share a view that there is a “single best” way of solving technical problems. For example, when discussing the role of templates in Django, a core developer remarked that he disagreed with a particular recent proposal because it allowed multiple ways of achieving a single programming goal: “There should be Only One Way To Do It.” In response, another developer stated: “I agree with [that developer]—this overlaps completely with ‘cycle’ or ‘divisibleby,’ and we prefer Only One Way To Do It. Thanks for the idea though.” Hence, Django developers tend to filter discourse to arrive at unitary solutions to problems they face. To see how filtering occurs in context, consider discursive modulation episode D122 in Table 4.

The notion that there should only be one way to do things is coupled with valuing explicitness in code. Explicit code is characterized by providing direct control of all parameters while also displaying as much informative output as possible when functions are executed. Such code eschews automating functionality “behind the scenes” where it is not explicitly visible. From the point of view of Django developers, this way of approaching code gives them better control over what the code does. The community generally subscribes to the “Zen of Python,” a set of aphorisms describing how Python code, and therefore Django code, ideally should be written. All code needs to be in conformance with the aphorism “explicit is better than implicit.” Developers use these aphorisms in the discourse as criteria to filter out certain code contributions. For example, consider discursive modulation episode D83 in Table 5. Hence, calls for unitary explicitness offer a useful means to filter out unwanted contributions and help decrease the heterogeneity of discursive positions.

To maintain a stable, integrated software artifact, the Django community enacts a mixing approach that focuses on a commonly accepted and standardized process. Different groups of developers focus on specific versions, use cases, plug-ins, etc. Over time, they inevitably construct alternative ways of seeing and organizing the code. Despite the expected homogenizing effects of using unitary explicitness as a standard for filtering, there is still significant room for heterogeneity in how developers write code and express ideas. To curb this, the Django community interrelates multiple discursive positions through structured dialogue. This dialogue is respectful, non-combative, and reflects amicable relationships among the developers. The mixing of discursive positions across developers is carefully orchestrated by expressions of willingness to work hard to form a consensus by complying with shared technical standards. An agreedupon process ensuring a structured dialogue on all feature development, therefore, forms a core element of Django’s modulation approach. The process has been formalized and is widely known as the Django enhancement process (DEP), a six-step process to build rough consensus:

Decision making in Django goes by “rough consensus and working code.” The DEP process that [Celebrimbor] started is the right way to work constructively on “rough consensus,” but people who really want this feature need to play their part in coming up with “working code” and the code needs to be well tested to be considered as working. The process specifies that “the Author [of the DEP] ... shepherds the discussions in the appropriate forums, and attempts to build community consensus around the idea” and also asks the proposer to “provide evidence of consensus within the community and discuss important objections or concerns raised during discussion” (DEP Purpose and Guidelines document). ## Novelty and Complexity

The characteristics of the software developed by the Django community are primarily influenced by (1) conservative filtering through unitary explicitness and (2) consensusseeking mixing through structured dialogue. The desire to create singular solutions that have consensus support is associated with producing proximal novelty and an integration approach to managing complexity. Django community members focus on maintaining the stability of the software while refining existing features. Developers aim to act as stewards of a complex yet coherent system. By focusing on stability across a complex system, community discourse seeks to minimize user problems during release transition in relation to the use of older versions or plug-ins of the software. This sentiment is widespread in the data and suggests a conservatism entailing predictable feature development. As expressed by a Django core developer:

We take pride in that we are a boring, slightly predictable framework … If you want something stable and reliable, then Django is a decent choice these days … The community seems to be more focused on stability issues versus banging out brand new things, and all this new stuff. (Core Developer Argon during a podcast interview)

As such, Django tends to produce proximal novelty. Because of inherent conservatism, backwards compatibility is a consistent concern, as evidenced by a discussion on the Django mailing list of how to improve email authentication functionality: “I’m leaning towards an incremental improvement approach, but only if I can see a concrete proposal that articulates what to change, and deals with the backwards-compatibility issues in a not-too-ugly way” (Django BDFL<sup>3</sup> in a mailing list conversation). In response, another developer proposed a particular way of achieving backwards compatibility:

It’s completely backwards compatible … If you’ve got an existing app with normal ForeignKeys to auth.User, the app will continue to work, without any migrations, as long as the rest of your project uses auth.User. It will also co-exist with any configurable app that is configured to use auth.User as the user model. It will only fall down if you have a configurable app that uses a different User model. (Core Developer Bolg in a mailing list conversation)

Other developers noted that this solution was challenging. For instance, developer Isilmo suggested that “there would probably have to be some kind of compatibility layer added to make existing apps work or to provide a simpler migration path.” Concerns with backwards compatibility and coherence across features were commonplace and invited significant attention.

To summarize, the Django community views itself as a steward of a complex technical system that it is incrementally and continuously tuning. Although the community comes up with plenty of new ideas, they are restrained by filtering contributions using the principle of unitary explicitness. Salient contributions are primarily those addressing system inefficiencies or those identifying lacking features from the perspective of the whole community. The mixing of multiple discursive positions is managed by formalized, structured dialogue. Because filtering via unitary explicitness has a homogenizing effect on discussions, structured dialogue then helps to integrate the remaining discursive positions tightly. Consequently, the community approach to novelty focuses on refining features proximal to current functionality. At the same time, maintenance becomes a rather complex endeavor due to Django’s insistence on backwards compatibility and the need to integrate multiple libraries into the framework itself, creating a complex codebase. Integration is thus a crucial practice intended to manage such complexity. We summarize the key dimensions of Django’s approach to novelty and complexity through modulation in Table 8a.

## Rails’s Discursive Modulation Practices

Discursive modulation practices within the Rails community involve filtering focused on facilitating ease of use and expressiveness. This differs from Django’s unitary explicitness in that it tolerates nonexplicit code if it promotes developer happiness and flexibility in how the end user can code their Rails apps. Nonexplicit code is characterized by automation “behind the scenes,” which makes the job of web developers easier, as some things are taken care of without them having to explicitly specify it or know how it works. This allows both users and developers to be more “expressive,” i.e., able to readily develop any features that express their priorities with relatively few lines of code.

Such filtering results in unstructured contestation among multiple points of view of what facilitates ease of use, which may differ significantly across use cases. Unstructured contestation differs from structured dialogue in that it lacks an explicitly defined process and is frequently contentious. Because Rails emphasizes heterogeneity of expression and relatively little integration of divergent discursive positions, the community encourages distal novelty and a deprecative approach to complexity to make room for “cool” new features. In Table 9, we illustrate discursive modulation episode R10, capturing both filtering and mixing in Rails.

When new, major features are developed in Rails, the process unfolds as follows: A developer makes a proposal based on a concern they have discovered in their work. Other developers then add their concerns and alternative proposals for how to solve the problem. The proposals are largely based on each developer’s view of what they deem facilitates ease of use or “programmer happiness.” Their preferences are drawn from the types of use cases they typically engage with. Since the common standard used by Rails is to facilitate ease of use and expressiveness, it is difficult to find singular solutions— people tend to view ease of use differently.

Indeed, the Ruby community is viewed as dynamic and innovative in that it offers multiple ways of expressing the same programming idea. Over time, as the community debates an idea, multiple, often divergent discursive positions, start to crystallize. However, influential developers and core team members tend to gravitate towards a particular proposal based on their view of what constitutes ease of use, which then is implemented. As such, decisions tend to be made in an authoritative manner, rather than democratically through dialogue. The proposals that are eventually implemented tend to focus on adding cutting-edge features, while older features are deprecated to provide room for the new features.

## Novelty and Complexity

Discursive modulation in Rails consists of filtering through norms of ease of use and expressiveness and mixing of discursive positions by unstructured contestation. These practices allow for heterogeneous discursive positions to coexist and rarely lead to consensus. This involves fewer compromises and results in a distal approach to novelty, while complexity is often managed through deprecation.

The Rails community is interested in pursuing cutting edge features and has a strong preference for what they call “sharp tools”—tools used to create “state-of-the-art” web applications, as expressed by DHH:

I try to make simple, sharp tools. I’m OK with the fact that some people will cut themselves because that risk is the flip side of the productivity reward. I encourage people to live up to my high expectations of them. I expect everyone working with Rails to be interested in improving their craft and creating beautiful code. If you don’t give a shit, if you’re not interested in living up to what Rails has to teach you, or you’re happy writing shitty code, then Rails is not going to save you. (DHH in a blog post on his personal website)

This sentiment is prevalent across the discourse of the Rails community. Developers seek out new features and attempt to stay on the “cutting edge.” When such novelty is identified, developers are quick to incorporate it into the Rails codebase. Developers also continuously encourage the community to move quickly and keep current with industry initiatives: “Since this is an industry-wide effort to move towards a common structure for all client-server communication across an endpoint, I think Rails should follow suit and move in this direction” (Developer Durin in a mailing list conversation).

The focus is reflected in the core team’s constant search for best practices and functionalities that can be integrated into Rails. This aligns with the principal attitude that places a high value on “progress”—continuously adopting new technologies for the sake of personal and community growth. The focus on progress helps Rails stay close to the “cutting edge” and maintain its relevance within the larger ecosystem of web development tools. As such, Rails primarily pursues distal novelty, which, in this case, means implementing new features that are quite different from those in the current software artifact (i.e., “leaps” in functionality). Staying on the cutting edge while committing limited resources to advance the codebase requires trade-offs. Largely, this shows up in the Rails community through a heightened concern with regard to “bloat” and “complexity”:

Rails sucks. It’s bloated, slow and doesn’t teach you anything. (Developer Varda comment on a blog post)

Rails is over. It’s a bloated meta framework that requires enormous amounts of peripheral knowledge to understand. (Anonymous developer in a comment on a blog post)

... the Ruby ecosystem has become increasingly sophisticated, making tools like bundler and rvm (or rbenv, if that’s your thing) a part of the standard workflow. And as those tools increasingly became part of the toolchain for experienced developers, we lost track of the complexity of setting up the standard Ruby toolchain. (Developer Kili in a Kickstarter ad for funding Rails development)

Hence, the Rails community is deeply concerned about complexity and its effects and therefore seeks to mitigate it. This is mainly accomplished through consciously deprecating old features and versions. The process, however, also cuts ties with certain technologies and user communities, but this is justified in the name of staying on the cutting edge. Complexity is curtailed while backwards compatibility is reduced. For example, a new feature was criticized by a developer as breaking backwards compatibility: “More generally, changing the semantics in the way that has been described is really confusing two very different things, and makes for a huge amount of search-and-replacing for those of us who read the changelogs and a lot of broken apps for those who don’t” (Developer Elendil in a mailing list conversation). To this concern, DHH responded: “Also, this is why we do major releases, so we can break backwards compatibility in order to improve the API” (DHH in a mailing list conversation).

The overall focus of the Rails community on feature expansion, rather than backwards compatibility, signals that the community values distal novelty and is willing to sever ties to the past through deprecation. As another illustration, a developer noted the following:

The Rails team seems to think that everybody is always running on the cutting edge version and don’t consider people who prefer to stay a few version behind for stability reasons. What’s worse is that plugins/gems might or might not compatible with the version you are updating to, but you will only know that by trying yourself and letting others try and report potential issues. (Developer Meneldil in a blog post)

To summarize, the Rails community focuses on feature expansion to stay on the “cutting edge.” This is associated with a low tolerance for complexity and a tendency to deprecate older features while permitting a faster inclusion of new, exciting technologies and features that stimulate distal novelty. By emphasizing progress and expressiveness, the heterogeneity of the overall codebase is increased, which is further encouraged by Rails community’s use of contestation as a mode of determining preferred solutions. ## Quantitative Measures of Novelty and Complexity in Django and Rails

Based on our qualitative analysis, we expect novelty to be higher in Rails compared to Django, and we expect complexity to be higher in Django. Our findings connect proximal and distal approaches to novelty with the level of new, major features being generated, as well as the integration and deprecation approaches to complexity with different levels of cyclomatic complexity. These measures are described in Figure 1.

Novelty is consistently higher in Rails. In 2016, Rails introduced 143 new major features (0.1 per file), whereas Django introduced 6 new major features (0.002 per file). This suggests that a proximal approach to novelty leads to fewer new major features than a distal approach. Notably, during the study period, Django advanced from Version 1.3 to 1.10, while Rails advanced from Version 3.1 to 5.0, suggesting larger leaps in functionality between the successive versions in Rails compared to Django. Complexity was consistently higher in Django than in Rails. In 2016 Rails had a complexity of 1.5 per file (total complexity = 2,131.5), while Django had a complexity of 2.0 per file (total complexity = 7,274). This suggests that an integration approach to complexity generates higher cyclomatic complexity when compared with a deprecation approach. These differences across the projects held whether we controlled for artifact size or whether total novelty or complexity was considered.

## Theory Development

Prior research has made significant strides in understanding OSS coordination for dealing with specific issues (Howison & Crowston, 2014b; Lindberg et al., 2016; Medappa & Srivastava, 2019) but has remained relatively silent on how communities coordinate to shape the emergent characteristics of OSS artifacts overall, such as the novelty and complexity of software. A potential explanation is that the community’s norms and values are captured in the core doctrines and design principles that shape novelty and complexity. But such norms and values are inert unless they are enacted within a community by some means (Maruping et al., 2019), and it is difficult to enforce doctrines and principles without an established hierarchy. Yet communities can be quite different from each other, and their resulting artifacts can reflect these differences.

We argue that these different community-level norms and values are enacted and enforced through a process of discursive modulation. This process imprints the norms and values of the community and guides developers. Developer discourse “carries” core doctrines and design principles, and modulation is the process through which such elements are imprinted in the community. Discursive modulation influences developer activities that cannot be directly controlled at their source due to the autonomy of the developers (Figure 2).

Two practices make up discursive modulation: filtering and mixing. Filtering involves selecting desirable contributions from a stream of possible contributions. Mixing serves to integrate diverse viewpoints into coherent solutions. Combinations of filtering and mixing modulate streams of discourse and constitute particular approaches to shaping software novelty and complexity. The relationship between filtering and mixing can be traced in both directions; either may precede the other in discourse. Filtering establishes which discursive positions are selected and made available to be mixed to arrive at community-wide positions and decisions. Mixing provides combinations of discursive positions to be evaluated against the core doctrines and design principles of a community through filtering.

The discourse produced by filtering and mixing shapes the development of code, which in turn, influences subsequent development and associated discourse. The discourse imprints core doctrines and design principles onto the software, while the software instantiates those principles and doctrines, thereby reinforcing them as the referent for the community discourse. The mutual shaping between the discourse and the emerging artifact reproduces the dominant approaches for shaping novelty and complexity that characterize the community. Novelty can drive complexity, which must then be managed. The growth of complexity can constrain the amount of new, major features that can reasonably be added to a software artifact.

Each project we studied showed a clear and distinct emphasis on discursive modulation practices. Naturally, the associations between projects and practices that we observed are a function of the cases we sampled. However, based on our intentional sampling and analysis, we can theorize (i.e., make analytical generalizations; see Lee & Baskerville, 2003) about two discursive modulation archetypes (Doty & Glick, 1994) for shaping the novelty and complexity of their OSS software: “tuning” and “expression” archetypes (Table 14).

The first archetype, exemplified by Django’s discursive modulation practices, is what we refer to as the “tuning” approach. This archetype relies on filtering through unitary explicitness This creates an interlocking system of touchstones that any new feature development must adhere to, thereby producing path dependencies (David, 2001). Filtering produces a relatively coherent set of discursive positions and builds a strong common ground for discourse about solutions around which consensus can be achieved. Such common ground is further facilitated by mixing via structured dialogue, where efforts are made to fit any new proposal into an existing framework of shared values, accepted practices, and technical constraints, often necessitating a compromise. Filtering towards unitary explicitness ensures that discursive positions are aligned with clear, largely objective, standards, while structured dialogue establishes coherence and reconciliation across multiple discursive positions.

Hence, the discursive modulation within the tuning approach can be characterized as a constant “structural deepening” of the OSS artifact—the incremental, ever-increasing sophistication of technical arrangements to increase the performance of the artifact across chosen dimensions (Arthur, 2009). Since technical arrangements and discursive positions are conservatively integrated to achieve consensus, the software evolves incrementally. This is reflected in the search for novelty in a proximal manner. Further, as feature development progresses incrementally, since backwards compatibility is highly valued, coordination is needed to address interdependencies across use cases and software functionalities. Therefore, the tuning archetype uses an integration approach to complexity. As such, the tuning archetype seeks to prevent excess proliferation of new features while continuously tending to the integrity of the software. As features are developed incrementally and at a slow rate, the tuning archetype provides strong backwards compatibility and stability. This, over time, leads to accepting the necessary evil of more complex software. Hence, the development approaches enacting the tuning archetype produce quantitatively fewer features and more complex code but also create a more stable and reliable software product, as perceived by the developers.

The second archetype is the “expression” approach, exemplified by the Rails community’s discursive modulation practices. The expression archetype adopts filtering with an eye towards facilitating ease of use and expressiveness. This filtering views software development as creative writing— where the software expresses the person’s coding style and personal knowledge. This form of filtering allows for a great deal of variance of discursive positions, which makes mixing discursive positions difficult and often disharmonic, marked by unstructured contestation. The expression archetype’s emphasis on ease of use and expressiveness can mean different things to different developers, encouraging wideranging searches for cutting-edge solutions to perceived problems and opportunities. Since mixing takes place in an unstructured manner, making it rife with contestation, discursive positions are not always possible to integrate. Rather, some discursive positions ultimately win out over other positions when they are chosen as preferred solutions to problems. The community is constantly in search of “feature expansions,” which can facilitate ease of use and expressiveness. These features are pitted against each other during unstructured contestation, after which a smaller number of features are selected and implemented.

The search for novelty implies that the software can move in multiple directions that are not necessarily consistent with each other and in larger leaps. This is reflected in distal searches for novelty. While the expression archetype provides new features that help the software remain on the “cutting edge,” the approach also makes it more challenging to make room for these new features. Since unstructured contestation makes it difficult to integrate discursive positions, complexity is perceived as a significant threat. Therefore, older features are often deprecated rather than integrated with new features. Generally, the community is prone to sacrifice backwards compatibility while accepting and even welcoming continuous expansion of the software. This comes at the cost of incompatible versions. The upshot of deprecating older features, however, is less complexity. As a result, the expression archetype results in quantitatively more new features and less complex code. ## Discussion

Our theorizing of the relationship between discursive modulation practices and varying approaches to novelty and complexity makes three contributions. First, we extend prevailing accounts of OSS coordination with the process of discursive modulation. Second, we show how this process is mutually constitutive with software artifacts in a way that goes beyond existing explanations. Third, we contribute to research on OSS development by unpacking two archetypical approaches for modulating software novelty and complexity.

## Discursive Modulation and OSS Coordination

In the absence of hierarchy or market mechanisms, OSS communities coordinate development through modularization (Baldwin & Clark, 2000; Sanchez & Mahoney, 1996), open superposition (Howison & Crowston, 2014), and discourse (Lindberg et al., 2016; Sharma et al., 2021). At the same time, research on OSS emphasizes the role of values in explaining contribution behaviors (Bogart et al., 2021; Maruping et al., 2019). The literature, however, does not explain how coordination of development work is infused with values and norms (reflected in core doctrines and design principles) to influence the overall evolution of the software in terms of novelty or complexity without requiring hierarchical forms of governance. Our research shows how design principles and core doctrines of OSS projects become enacted in community discourse and how the discourse then modulates ongoing OSS activity to realize particular approaches to novelty and complexity.

Our theory of discursive modulation provides an example of how higher-level goals and values are translated into and influence activity in self-organizing contexts (Benkler, 2017; Howison & Crowston, 2014b; Levine & Prietula, 2014; Powell, 1990). Dealing with novelty and complexity in selforganizing contexts is a critical area of research in the contemporary digital world. Discursive modulation is one process through which this occurs. This view can be helpful for understanding the ways in which discourse influences the structure and characteristics of peer-produced artifacts (Arazy et al., 2020) and the features of artifacts developed in more structured contexts such as scientific collaborations (Tuertscher et al., 2014) or in-house, corporate software development (Gregory et al., 2018).

At its core, our account of discursive modulation explains how self-organizing processes emerge and shape OSS communities and their products. Research on self-organizing points out that values, norms, and rules need to be internalized by agents such that these elements can be reflected in the technological artifacts (Haki et al., 2020).

These studies, however, do not describe how these values, rules, and norms are reproduced and enforced in a community. The proposed account of discursive modulation and related mixing and filtering practices provides a framework for understanding how core doctrines and design principles are imprinted onto artifacts.

Our view of modulation differs from the classic work of Nickerson and Zenger (2002), which describes modulation in terms of an oscillation across varying governance structures that produces a steady state of performance over time. The intuition in our use of the term is similar in one sense—the process we identify keeps the communities within a steady band of performance that aligns with the principles and doctrines of the community. The overall outcomes of selforganizing, however, are different in that they have their roots in discursive processes rather than governance structures (Nan & Lu, 2014). Consequently, our account of modulation draws on ideas originating from audio synthesis to describe how norms and values are enacted through self-organizing. As OSS communities self-organize, they imprint design principles and core doctrines through discursive practices that filter and mix discursive positions and provide a mechanism to maintain a relatively steady state.

## Discursive Modulation and Technological Artifacts

Our research suggests that discourse and the artifacts it develops relate to each other through a process of mutual shaping. They participate in a mutually constitutive relationship—discourse both shapes and is shaped by technological artifacts. Discourse spans all the modules of OSS projects and helps developers coordinate dependencies not addressed by modular interfaces (Lindberg et al., 2016), thereby shaping the overall artifact. As a result of mutual shaping, the characteristics of the OSS artifacts embody the values of the community. The behaviors of individual developers are influenced, in part because developers whose values are more aligned with community values contribute more (Maruping et al., 2019). Because they align with those values, developers draw on those values in their discourse, and the discourse reproduces values that have already been imprinted on the OSS artifact. As such, discursive modulation forms a sort of module-spanning control hierarchy that monitors and shapes activity across the modular architecture (Henfridsson et al., 2014; Lee & Berente, 2012).

Our findings suggest that there are elements in the structure of software that a modularity, or “mirroring,” perspective (MacCormack et al., 2012) cannot explain. Our research highlights how, through discourse, attitudes toward novelty and complexity indirectly impact how the community develops software and, ultimately, the software itself. Aspects associated with novelty and complexity, such as technical debt and digital options (Rolland et al., 2018), are critical to the technical composition of a codebase. But, as such, they cannot be neatly encapsulated into specific locations in the product structure and architecture. Indeed, our work recasts mirroring theory by pointing out that there is more reflected in technical artifacts than just the structure of the organization. These aspects also reflect the design principles and core doctrines of a community, both of which are constructed and reproduced through discourse. It is not necessarily the structure of discourse that is mirrored in the artifact but rather the values and norms of a community, as expressed within discourse. Hence, modulation is a mutually constitutive process—one of mutual shaping—rather than mirroring per se.

## Discursive Modulation Archetypes

We identify two discursive modulation archetypes: tuning and expression, each with a distinct relationship to novelty and complexity. Archetypes are rich, generative theoretical constructs representing “ideal” types in the Weberian sense and thus cannot be perfectly reflected in any instantiation (Doty & Glick, 1994). Indeed, different OSS communities may employ multiple forms of discursive modulation. For example, OSS projects may approach security issues in an opaque manner (Shaikh & Vaast, 2016), departing substantially from their common open way of doing things. In other words, OSS projects are not monolithic but rather employ multiple ways of working depending on contingencies. As such, we make no claims that the archetypes we identify are universal. Rather, they can serve as useful theoretical constructs that help surface trade-offs among different approaches.

The tuning archetype is consistent with prior software development research: a focus on shared understanding, solid engineering principles, backwards compatibility, and interoperability across the functions of the software. This represents one pathway towards creating high-quality software (Cataldo & Herbsleb, 2008; Paulk, 2015), and is likely to attract developers whose values align with the values of classic software engineering (Maruping et al., 2019).

The expression archetype, however, tells a different story. This archetype emphasizes personal skills, novelty of searches, contestation, and expressive debate, which together form a successful path toward high-quality software. Hence, developers whose values emphasize novelty, creativity, and personal expression may be attracted to the expression archetype, shaping the knowledge, skills, and attitudes available to the community as it moves forward.<sup>4</sup> In either case, it seems likely that the choice of whether to employ a tuning or an expression archetype to discursive modulation impacts the makeup of the evolving OSS community and its product. Hence, discursive modulation may also serve as an important mechanism for work design (Butler et al., 1999; Parker et al., 2017; Tambe et al., 2020) in volunteer-based communities.

Furthermore, our results indicate that in some situations quantitatively larger numbers of features may be associated with quantitatively smaller amounts of complexity and vice versa. This is a counterintuitive finding because current research largely posits that as software evolves, new features are added and need to be integrated, which comes with an attendant increase in complexity (Lehman, 1980, 1996). Hence, our findings suggest that there is not always a clear connection between the number of new features added and the complexity that is engendered in the code. Rather, tradeoffs between encouraging novelty and tempering complexity may need to be understood in terms of qualitatively different kinds of novelty and different approaches to managing complexity. Our findings suggest that a proximal approach to novelty is associated with an integration approach to complexity (i.e., the tuning archetype), while a distal approach to novelty is associated with a deprecation approach to complexity (i.e., the expression archetype), and we propose discursive modulation as the mechanism through which this is achieved.

The relationship between the principles, goals, and values of the community and its developers is important. Maruping et al. (2019) point out the importance of value alignment among developers and their OSS communities and focus primarily on values relating to overall attitudes about learning, knowledge, and helping. Our archetypes extend this perspective by highlighting how important design principles and core doctrines are to the community’s software development. Developers self-select into communities based on their alignment with not only general values but also specific values regarding novelty and complexity. In this sense, the archetypes point to specific stances communities make with respect to how to achieve innovation—what Fayard et al. (2016) refer to as “epistemic stances.” Our work provides a basis for exploring how the kinds of epistemic stances found in traditional innovative organizations may also be reflected in OSS, though these communities are not organized into hierarchies.

## Implications for Practice

Practitioners running OSS projects that seek to establish a strategic approach to novelty and complexity may find archetypes useful both for decision-making and implementation. Our analysis suggests that whichever decisions are made, they are more likely to be effective if efforts are also taken to alter the discourse. Given the literature on leadership in OSS (O’Mahony & Ferraro, 2007), altering community discourse is likely to require project leaders to alter their own discursive practices. Our findings encourage project leaders to consider choices about novelty and complexity, together with approaches to filtering and mixing discourse as a lever for work design, for matching work with participants, and for attracting participants with aligned interests and development approaches (Maruping et al., 2019).

Further, firms involved with OSS communities can draw insights from our analysis, as they build their products in conjunction with those communities (Agerfalk & Fitzgerald, 2008; Sharma, 2023). To understand and influence the OSS projects they rely on, firms will want to assess the strategic choices that projects make about novelty and complexity to ensure they are aligned with the firm’s plans. To accomplish this, our theorization makes it clear that firms should inspect the way that project participants talk to each other. Indeed, rather than, for example, inspecting the speed at which development issues are addressed (Chen & German, 2020; Goggins et al., 2021), firms should pay attention to how discourse unfolds (e.g. Tsay et al., 2014).

Finally, for individuals, experience with OSS is increasingly valuable in the labor market (Germonprez et al., 2017; Marlow & Dabbish, 2013). Individuals seeking projects to which they can contribute should assess the match between their interests, discursive approaches, and a project’s strategic choices regarding novelty and complexity, as this may help them make more valuable contributions (Maruping et al., 2019).

## Boundary Conditions

This study provides an explanation for the linkages between discursive modulation practices and how specific characteristics of software, such as novelty and complexity, are addressed in OSS. Naturally, our observations are a function of the cases we have selected. There are likely other combinations beyond the ones we observed, and an OSS community may evolve over time to utilize different archetypes and combinations of discursive modulation practices. Therefore, we make no claim to exhaustiveness. Note that generating archetypes as a form of theorizing essentially involves creating Weberian ideal types as points of reference for comparison with empirical phenomena (Doty & Glick, 1994). While the modulation practices described occur, to some degree, in both projects, their relative prevalence was sufficiently different to justify treating each as a distinct archetype. Hence, we generalize from our data to theoretical statements and not to a population (Lee & Baskerville, 2003).

Since both of our sampled communities were midsized, successful OSS projects focused on producing web development frameworks, a conservative assessment would consider these characteristics as forming boundary conditions for our theorizing. However, one might speculate that the expression archetype may be more applicable to projects that draw heavily on a “design” logic (i.e., having a higher concentration of community members with backgrounds in design, art, graphics, humanities, etc.), while the tuning archetype may be more applicable to projects that draw more heavily on an “engineering” logic (i.e., having a higher concentration of community members with backgrounds in science or engineering disciplines).

## Conclusion

This study invites us to reconceptualize OSS communities as contested spaces where developers engage with each other discursively, captured by what we refer to as discursive modulation practices. The internal structure of such practices lays the groundwork for revealing alternative ways of identifying, discussing, and negotiating how software novelty and complexity are shaped by self-organizing OSS communities. This provides new insights into the shaping of characteristics of software when traditional, hierarchical mechanisms are unavailable.

Western Reserve University. His research interests include digital innovation, artificial intelligence, and institutional change in organizations. He is senior editor at MIS Quarterly and Information and Organization.

James Howison (ORCID: 0000-0002-5702-149X) is an associate professor in the Information School of the University of Texas at Austin. He received his Ph.D. from the School of Information at Syracuse University. James studies open collaboration, particularly in software development, including open source software development and the development of software in science. His work has been supported by the Sloan Foundation and grants from the NSF, including a 2015 NSF CAREER award and a 2019 PECASE award. James has published in the fields of information systems, computer-supported cooperative work, and information sciences (e.g., MIS Quarterly, ACM CSCW Conference, and Journal of the Association for Information Science and Technology), as well as keynotes for industry and funding agency advisory events. Recently James contributed to the development of CiteAs (http://citeas.org) and Softcite (https://github.com/softcite/), systems to improve incentives for high-quality software work in science.

Kalle Lyytinen (ORCID: 0208-0002-3352-5343) (Ph.D., computer science, University of Jyväskylä; Dr. h.c. mult) is a Distinguished University Professor at Case Western Reserve University and a distinguished visiting professor at Aalto University, Finland. He is among the top five IS scholars in terms of his h-index (100) and has the highest network centrality among the IS scholars. He is an AIS Fellow (2004) and a LEO Award recipient (2013). He has published over 400 refereed articles, edited or written over 30 books or special issues, and won Best Paper awards from AoM, AIS/ICIS, and other societies. He conducts research on digital innovation, complex design work, requirements for large systems, and the emergence and growth of digital infrastructures.
