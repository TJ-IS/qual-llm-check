# Ecological Validity in NeuroIS Research: Theory, Evidence, and a Roadmap for Future Studies

Ali Balapour,<sup>1</sup> René Riedl<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Northern Kentucky University, USA, balapoura1@nku.edu

## Abstract

NeuroIS research involves the application of neuroscience knowledge and tools to investigate information systems (IS) phenomena and research questions. Ecological validity (EV) refers to the ability to generalize results from a laboratory study—usually controlled experiments—to real-world settings. Because NeuroIS research is typically conducted in laboratory settings and relies on relatively intrusive measurement tools (when compared to more traditional data collection instruments), the issue of EV in NeuroIS is critical. Its importance is underscored by the IS discipline’s commitment to both rigor and relevance. In this paper, we discuss the nature and dimensions of EV and why it is important, outline the major threats to EV, and discuss its current status in NeuroIS research. Our status presentation is based on an analysis of all 42 empirical NeuroIS papers published in the Association for Information Systems “basket-of-eight” journals in the period 2007-2023 (i.e., the entire period of existence of the NeuroIS field). To provide guidance for future research, we also outline a roadmap for NeuroIS researchers, showing how to increase EV through research design decisions and study execution. Establishing EV is a task to make NeuroIS studies more relevant to real-world problems. Thus, the present paper is a direct response to recent articles calling for more NeuroIS studies with direct relevance to business and society without sacrificing scientific rigor.

Keywords: Autonomic Nervous System, Brain, Ecological Validity, EEG, External Validity, fMRI, NeuroIS, Veridicality, Verisimilitude

## 1 Introduction

NeuroIS, defined as the scientific investigation of information systems (IS) phenomena based on neuroscience knowledge and tools (Dimoka et al., 2011; Riedl et al., 2010), has opened many doors for new insights in the IS discipline. Neuroscience tools inherently provide unique and unprecedented opportunities to understand how users interact with IT (e.g., Dimoka et al., 2012), even in real-time scenarios, as exemplified by the study of neuroadaptive systems (e.g., Demazure et al., 2021; Riedl & Léger, 2016; vom Brocke et al., 2020).

In the present paper, we address ecological validity (EV) in NeuroIS research. EV is the ability to generalize the results of a laboratory study—typically a controlled experiment—to real-world settings (Parsons, 2016; Schmuckler, 2001). Although EV is a topic that concerns IS research in general (e.g., Benbasat, 1989), such an investigation will be of particular benefit to the field of NeuroIS because the methods (often associated with limitations, such as movement restrictions during human-computer interaction tasks) and research contexts (usually specific laboratory settings) are typically more specific compared to traditional behavioral IS research.

The human nervous system serves as the basis for all perceptions, thoughts, feelings, and behaviors, including IT-related behaviors, and consists of various components (Riedl et al., 2014a; Riedl & Léger, 2016; Weber et al., 2021). Neuroscience methods target and capture responses of the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral nervous system (PNS). The CNS includes the neural tissue of the brain and spinal cord, while the PNS includes all neural tissue outside of the CNS. The PNS can be further divided into the somatic nervous system (SNS) and the autonomic nervous system (ANS). The SNS includes cranial and spinal nerves connected to sensory organs, muscles, joints, and skin, and primarily facilitates movement and sensory transmission (e.g., detection of temperature, touch). In contrast, the ANS includes the sympathetic nervous system (which activates the body), the parasympathetic nervous system (which induces relaxation), and the enteric nervous system (which regulates gastrointestinal function).

Based on the outlined anatomy, there are several neurophysiological measures to capture the activity of the nervous system. NeuroIS researchers have argued that the brain and the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions of the ANS are most relevant for the study of IS phenomena, while the other parts―at least currently―play little or no role (e.g., Riedl & Léger, 2016). Established tools in brain research that have been used in NeuroIS studies are functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), electroencephalography (EEG), functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS); established tools to study the activity of the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions of the ANS are electrocardiography (ECG), galvanometry (electrodermal activity, EDA), eye tracking (pupil dilation), and facial electromyography (fEMG) (Riedl et al., 2020a). In addition, NeuroIS studies based on hormonal measurements (e.g., Riedl et al., 2012; Tams et al., 2014) and molecular genetics (e.g., Browne & Walden, 2020) have been published.<sup>1</sup>

Undoubtedly, it requires excellent professionalism to design and conduct NeuroIS studies that are both rigorous and relevant. Our analyses of the empirical NeuroIS literature (see Section 3 in the present paper and the Appendix) reveal that a notable portion of research could be considered—at least to some extent—to be “artificial” and “nonrepresentative” of the general IT phenomena in business and society.

Already a decade ago, in a NeuroIS research agenda paper, Dimoka et al. (2012, p. 681) outlined the “artificial setting [as one of the] major weaknesses of neurophysiological tools.” In essence, this problem refers to the possible lack of EV. In a seminal paper that would later prove to be one of the milestones in the genesis of the scientific debate on EV, Brunswik (1955, p. 204) wrote:

Under the aspect of representative design, all these systematic experiments must be viewed as ecological single cases or “instances” with artificial elaboration that leaves a large portion of the core elements untouched. Each of these experiments is indisputable in its results, but at the same time is of unscrutinized ecological generalizability.

While the lack of EV is an issue in any scientific field that considers the practical relevance of research results as an important goal, we observe a scientific debate about EV not only in disciplines that rely heavily on traditional behavioral laboratory experiments (as is the case in classical behavioral psychology, for example). Rather, this EV debate also applies to disciplines that seek to explain human thoughts, emotions, behavior, and corresponding deficits on the basis of neuroscientific approaches, such as neuropsychology or clinical neuroscience (Chakrabarti, 2013; Franzen & Wilhelm, 1996; Parsons, 2015; Schilbach et al., 2013). The main reason for this extension of the EV debate to “neuro” disciplines is that experimental settings, stimuli, and participants’ responses may differ significantly from the real-life situations in which the phenomena under study occur (Schmuckler, 2001).

In NeuroIS papers, the threats to EV are evident in multiple dimensions of the studies, and too little attention has been paid to the resulting consequences. The most notable consequence discussed in this paper refers to relevance, defined as the “real-world usefulness of research and its results” (Mohajeri et al., 2020, p. 528). Neglecting EV is associated with conducting studies that ultimately fail to explain or predict a real-world phenomenon or are not useful for designing practicerelevant interventions (Nobel, 2016). Even if the researcher (1) investigates a current organizational or societal problem that practitioners would find “exciting” (as is often the case in NeuroIS research, Riedl et al., 2020a) and (2) applies all methods in a rigorous and highquality manner (e.g., fMRI data collection and analysis techniques), without establishing EV, the findings may ultimately be of little relevance to practitioners.

But why is NeuroIS so susceptible to EV threats? Probably the most important reason that NeuroIS studies are more susceptible to EV threats than non-NeuroIS laboratory studies is the limitations that instrumentation imposes on the experimental setting, making it difficult to replicate the natural environment of task performance. Long ago, Brunswik (1943, p. 262) tellingly argued that laboratory experiments tend toward “narrow-spanning problems of artificially isolated proximal or peripheral technicalities of mediation which are not representative of larger patterns of life.” Field experiments, in contrast, have a significantly higher EV because the stimuli and events surrounding the phenomenon under study occur in its natural habitat (Schmuckler, 2001). Indeed, no scientist could take their MRI machine into the “field” to conduct experiments; instead, the environment is replicated in the laboratory. Moreover, even less stationary neuroscience tools, such as a research-grade EEG, cannot easily be moved into the natural environments where IS phenomena typically occur (e.g., offices or private residences).

In contrast to the behavioral IS laboratory experiments, experimental isolation has been taken to an even greater extreme in many NeuroIS studies. For example, Meservy et al. (2019) used a software development task in an fMRI study, but the task, stimuli, and participant responses were overly simplified to fit the requirements of the fMRI environment (“having participants evaluate forum solutions within an fMRI machine is unavoidably different than a similar task in a more natural setting,” p. 869). This simplification is an inherent property of neuroscience studies in this domain, as demonstrated in a recent review of brain and ANS activity measurement in software development (Weber et al., 2021). The environment, task, stimuli, and participants’ response options often bear little resemblance to the reality of how people develop software. As this software engineering example illustrates, and as our analyses, which include many other IS and related phenomena (e.g., trust in online shopping or IT security—for details, please see Section 3), demonstrate, the limitations associated with EV are too often taken lightly in the existing NeuroIS literature and are often not discussed at all. In short, the challenges associated with EV are often simply ignored in the NeuroIS literature.

We surmise that a main reason for this deficit is the lack of awareness of the exact nature and dimensions of EV among NeuroIS scholars. This assumption is substantiated by the observation that, with the exception of a few notable examples that touched on the topic of the potential artificiality of NeuroIS studies (Dimoka et al., 2012, p. 681; Riedl et al., 2010a, p. 255), the topic of EV has not been addressed at all in the conceptual NeuroIS literature. However, the motivation of the current paper is not to argue against NeuroIS research in general nor is it to argue against specific tools such as fMRI. Rather, our goal is to shed light on EV as an important quality of high-caliber NeuroIS research. More specifically, by drawing attention to EV and discussing potential ways to improve EV in future study design and execution (i.e., research methodology per se) and in related publications (i.e., research communication), we aim to contribute to the advancement of the NeuroIS field.

On the basis of the following Section 2, in which we describe the theoretical background of EV, the present paper addresses two research questions:

RQ1: What is the status of EV in the empirical NeuroIS literature? This analysis, described in Section 3, provides the evidence that determines the EV status of the published NeuroIS research. The database consists of all N = 42 empirical NeuroIS papers published in the Association for Information Systems (AIS) “basket-of-eight” journals<sup>2</sup> in the period 2007-2023. Our analysis covers the entire period of existence of the NeuroIS field, given that the Dimoka et al. (2007) paper coined the term “NeuroIS.”

RQ2: What could be improved in future NeuroIS research to increase EV? In Section 4, we provide an answer to this question, thereby outlining an actionable roadmap for future research efforts.

Finally, we provide concluding remarks in Section 5.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 On the Nature of EV

According to the Merriam-Webster Dictionary, validity is defined as “the quality of being well-grounded, sound, or correct” (Merriam-Webster, 2022). In the scientific literature, validity in its most rudimentary form is concerned with whether the findings from one population apply to another; also, it is often conceptualized as the degree to which an instrument measures what it purports to measure (Carmines & Zeller, 1979; Kelley, 1927). How a measure is judged to be valid is the domain of epistemology. The classic work of Cronbach and Meehl (1955) introduced four types of validity (predictive, concurrent, content, and construct), all of which focus on ensuring robustness in the measurement process and measuring what the researcher claims to measure.

However, around the same time as Cronbach and Meehl, Brunswik (1955) coined the term ecological validity. Specifically, he was concerned with EV of cues. In his conceptualization, Brunswik (1955) referred to the extent to which environmental information provides valid information about a distal stimulus. Later, we observed a transition of this original meaning to EV of experiments, defined as “the extent to which experimental findings can generalize to the ‘real world’ situation that a researcher wishes to understand” (Kihlstrom, 2021, p. 466). This meaning is what scientists typically have in mind today when they talk or write about EV.<sup>3</sup>

EV is a subset of external validity (Kihlstrom, 2021), which is defined as the ability to generalize results to different situations and/or populations (Campbell, 1957). Thus, while external validity asks whether study findings can be generalized to another context (Straub et al., 2004), EV is more specific (and therefore a subset) in that it asks whether results found under controlled laboratory conditions can be generalized to real-world settings (Andrade, 2018). For example, consider the study of user resistance to technology. External validity asks whether the results of a study (e.g., survey) can be generalized to additional populations of users with characteristics that differ from those in the study. While the study participants may have been 20-25-year-old students, one may (incorrectly) generalize the study results to the employed working population in general. In contrast, EV specifically examines whether the results of a laboratory study can be generalized to naturalistic situations, such as the technology resistance that users exhibit in their daily interactions with IT in organizations (e.g., Rivard & Lapointe, 2012).

Importantly, the EV of a study is a judgment and not a calculated statistic (Andrade, 2018; Parsons, 2015), at least for now, because concrete statistics (as we know them for construct validity or predictive validity, among others) do not yet exist. Thus, one and the same study might have a low EV from one person’s perspective, while another might consider it high(er) in EV. Schmuckler (2001) tellingly described the situation when he wrote that what one scholar considers ecologically valid is likely to be “arbitrary, trivial, and ecologically invalid to a different investigator” (p. 424).

Obviously, such a situation is problematic. Therefore, in the current paper, we also present practical suggestions to mitigate the threats to EV in NeuroIS research. Our suggestions relate to procedural standards rather than concrete statistics since it is hardly possible to cast EV in a single metric (Franzen & Wilhelm, 1996, p. 104). Advancing a research field based on procedural standards is associated with increasing academic maturity, a fact that has been emphasized several times in the NeuroIS literature (e.g., Dimoka et al., 2012; Riedl et al., 2014a).

## 2.2 Dimensions of EV

Schmuckler (2001), in a comprehensive review of the EV debate, presented three dimensions of EV: (1) the nature of the research setting or context (concerned with how natural a laboratory study’s environment is), (2) the nature of the stimuli (asking how closely do the laboratory study’s stimuli resemble those encountered in the real world), and (3) the nature of the task and the participant’s required response in relation to real-world behavior.

Related to these three dimensions, but conceptualized at a higher level of abstraction when compared to them, both verisimilitude and veridicality became important aspects in the EV debate (e.g., Franzen & Wilhelm, 1996). Verisimilitude indicates the extent to which the laboratory situation is similar to the situation in everyday life (Franzen & Wilhelm, 1996, p. 93; Parsons, 2016, p. 16). Veridicality indicates the extent to which the behavior of subjects in the lab can predict the phenomenon being studied in everyday life (Franzen & Wilhelm, 1996, p. 93; Parsons, 2016, p. 16).<sup>4</sup>

In Figure 1, we integrate Schmuckler’s (2001) three dimensions into Franzen and Wilhelm’s (1996) and Parsons’ (2016) verisimilitude-veridicality taxonomy. As shown in Figure 1, verisimilitude has three dimensions: the nature of the research setting or context, the nature of the stimuli, and the nature of the task and the required participant response. Veridicality is influenced by verisimilitude and thus by its three dimensions; moreover, the nature of subjects is an important dimension of veridicality (e.g., Franzen & Wilhelm, 1996; Parsons, 2016; Schmuckler, 2001; Zimmerman, 2011).

## 3 What is the Status of EV in the Empirical NeuroIS literature?

## 3.1 Methodology of Literature Search

To determine the status of EV in the empirical NeuroIS literature, we proceeded as described in the following. First, we performed a keyword search in the AIS basket-of-eight journals without a time limitation to identify NeuroIS publications. We replicated the search procedure as reported in an influential NeuroIS review paper (Riedl et al., 2020a). The procedure was essentially as follows. Keywords for the literature search were derived from landmark publications that provide an introduction to the field of NeuroIS (Dimoka et al., 2012, 2011; Riedl et al., 2010a; Riedl & Léger, 2016). For our literature search, we used generic terms that represent the field as a whole, such as “NeuroIS,” “neuroscience,” and “nervous system,” but also terms that are representative of the tools described in these seminal publications, such as “eye” (for eye-tracking techniques) or “heart” (for cardiovascular measures). Because the human nervous system consists of the CNS and PNS (of which the

ANS is a major part with a dominant role in NeuroIS research), and because hormone measurement based on blood, saliva, or urine samples also plays a notable role in NeuroIS research (e.g., Riedl, 2013), we structured the keywords along four dimensions (generic terms, CNS terms, ANS terms, hormone terms; see Table 1). Based on this procedure, we identified 42 empirical NeuroIS papers.

## 3.2 Methodology of Literature Analysis

The 42 papers were electronically searched for the terms “ecological validity” and “validity” to identify direct reference to the topic of the present paper (note that we also added “ecolog\*” to avoid possibly missing papers due to sentence constructions containing word combinations such as “ecologically valid”). We also searched electronically for the following terms related to EV: “real\*,” “natural\*,” and “external.” We added these terms because, during preliminary analyses of the papers, we noticed that authors used EV-related phrases such as “realistic task” (Anderson et al., 2016b, p. 716), “natural setting” (Léger et al., 2014, p. 653), and “external validity” (Meservy et al., 2014, p. 16).

In a further step, we analyzed all 42 papers to identify all text passages containing statements related to verisimilitude and veridicality and their dimensions (see Figure 1). In the Appendix (Table A1) we present the detailed findings of our analyses and code and highlight all text passages that are directly related to both EV (“ecological validity,” “ecologically valid”) and the EVrelated keywords (“real\*,” “natural\*,” “external”) (see bold and underlined keywords in Table A1).

In addition, we coded all text passages that did not contain these keywords but contained references to the four dimensions of EV. For example, regarding the dimension “nature of research setting or context,” we coded the statement “The behavioral study was used to overcome concerns that the setting and artificiality of the fMRI environment might bias the behavioral data” (p. 381) from Dimoka’s (2010) paper. Regarding the “nature of stimuli” dimension, Browne and Walden’s (2020) description “The specific information system we examine here is the product attribute page from BestBuy.com” (p. 752) is an example of a coded statement because this website represents an existing real-life website, not a fictitious one. Regarding the dimension “nature of task and required participant response,” we provide an example from a paper by Nadj et al. (2023), which states that “participants engaged in an invoice matching task … [and] invoice matching represents a typical and widely used office task in companies of all sizes” (p. 1623). For the dimension “nature of subjects,” we documented whether a study used a student sample or other samples (e.g., software developers, employees, supermarket shoppers).<sup>5</sup>

Since a significant part of the identification of text passages relating to EV was carried out via the automated keyword search (“ecological validity,” “validity,” “ecolog\*,” “real\*,” “natural\*,” “external”), the coding process left no room for interpretation in this respect and is therefore completely objective. However, there was room for interpretation when identifying EV-relevant text passages without using the aforementioned keywords. Hence, to secure high objectivity of the coding process, the authors proceeded in such a way that the two authors read through the 42 papers independently of each other and extracted relevant text passages. They then summarized all text passages identified independently by each author that were identical in an Excel file (typically text passages of a few sentences that were identified in the same paragraph of a paper). These text passages were then supplemented by passages that were only identified by one of the two authors in the first run but which were also recognized as worthy of coding by the other author after joint reflection. The result of this straightforward coding process is presented in the Appendix (Table A1) with all identified verbatim quotations.<sup>6</sup>

## 3.3 Results of Literature Analysis

Below we describe the main findings of our analysis, which are summarized in Table 2. In this table, the symbol “■” indicates that a paper reported a study aspect that positively affects the respective EV dimension, and the symbol “□” indicates that a paper did not report such a study aspect (the verbatim statements underlying the classification decision are available in the Appendix, Table A1).<sup>7</sup>

Based on this procedure, the main findings are:

• 15 papers (36% of the 42 papers) reported at least one study aspect that positively affected the “nature of research setting or context” dimension. Coding example: “Experiment 2 contributes by providing an ecologically valid field experiment design that for the first time showed how a realistic repetition of warnings in the field (an average of 4.74 warnings per day over three weeks) results in a decrease of warning adherence” (Vance et al., 2018, p. 375).

• 22 papers (52%) reported at least one study aspect that positively affected the “nature of stimuli” dimension. Coding example: “Off-task messages were formed to distract the individual from the current task, but reflected messages that organizational workers could actually receive in a real work setting” (Galluch et al., 2015, p. 15).

• 17 papers (40%) reported at least one study aspect that positively affected the “nature of task and required participant response” dimension. Coding example: “In contrast to related research, this study includes ecological validity in that applicants deliberately deceived rather than portrayed instructed or sanctioned deception. This action introduces self-selection bias, which in this case more closely reflects real-world deception in interviewing. The experimental evaluation used a realistic job description, online application software, and one-way interviewing— tools and processes with which the target population was familiar” (Twyman et al., 2020, p. 869).

An overall view of our results shows that around a third of the studies examined reported positive EV aspects with respect to “nature of research setting or context” (36%) and “nature of task and required participant response” (40%); for “nature of stimuli” it is more than half of the studies (52%). A probable reason for this difference (i.e., 1/3 vs. 1/2) is that the use of a stimulus with a high EV is easier to realize than a realistic research setting or context as well as a realistic task and response mode for the subjects. For instance, in many NeuroIS studies, it is not too difficult to use screenshots of real user interfaces (e.g., websites) as stimuli rather than fictitious examples. However, it is more difficult to ensure a realistic research setting (e.g., a user in a real-world work environment) as well as a realistic task and required participant response (e.g., a real-world business task performed with an enterprise system).

With regard to the “nature of subjects,” the central finding is that non-student samples were used in half of the studies. To put this into perspective, however, it should be noted that student samples are not negative per se. In one of the analyzed papers, Warkentin et al. (2016) expressed this fact tellingly, writing that “students represent perhaps a more robust sample for testing fear appeals because they have no jobs to lose as would real employees … student subjects are appropriate when real-world experience will not diminish the intensity of their responses to the stimuli” (p. 201). We deal with the use of student samples in more detail in Section 4.4. Next, we discuss the implications of our results for future NeuroIS research. The following discussion is structured along the four dimensions of EV.

## 4 What Could Be Improved in Future NeuroIS Research to Increase EV?

## 4.1 Nature of Research Setting or Context

A critical question in the context of EV is: Is the study’s environment similar to the environment that people encounter in the real world? The environment is essential in that it affects subjects’ perceptions, emotions, thoughts, attitudes, behavioral intentions, and actual behavior in laboratory studies. Ideally, it is the environment in which the phenomenon being studied occurs in practice. On one end of the spectrum, field studies have natural elements and thus represent an ideal task environment, while on the other end of the spectrum, laboratory experiments often represent artificial environments that can differ greatly from the context that people encounter in the real world. Importantly, our analyses of the NeuroIS literature revealed significant variability in the artificiality of the environment. It is understandable that researchers may be discouraged from implementing natural environments for reasons such as the introduction of confounds or the difficulty of creating the natural environment in a laboratory. This fact is expressed, for example, by Warkentin et al. (2016, p. 205), who wrote that they “favored fMRI precision over a study of exact realism in situation.” However, for achieving EV, it is fundamental to bring the laboratory environment as close as possible to the real-world setting.

In this context, the concept of intrusiveness is important, which is defined as “the extent to which a measurement instrument interferes with an ongoing task, which thereby distorts the investigated construct” (Riedl et al., 2014a, p. 26). From a subject’s perspective during data collection, intrusiveness can be broken down into three components: (1) degree of movement freedom, (2) degree of natural position, and (3) degree of invasiveness. We argue that intrusiveness and its three components are critical for assessing the nature of the research setting or context.

Based on Riedl et al. (2014a), we conceptualize (1) the degree of movement freedom as the possibility to move while performing the task; (2) the degree of natural position as the possibility to perform the task in a position similar to the position in everyday life (especially the seated position when using information systems);<sup>9</sup> and (3) the degree of invasiveness based on the whether the recording instruments of a measurement device have to be attached to the body (e.g., electrodes in case of EEG, ECG, and fEMG), inserted into the body (e.g., to draw blood samples to determine levels of specific hormones or to inject radioactive substances necessary for signal detection, as in the case of PET), or whether the measurement environment is generally associated with discomfort (e.g., noise and confinement in MRI scanners).

In Table 3, we classify all applied and proposed NeuroIS tools based on the three intrusiveness components, using the sitting position when using an information system as a benchmark. <sup>10</sup> In Table 3, based on the three components, red cells indicate high intrusiveness, green cells indicate low intrusiveness, and yellow cells indicate moderate intrusiveness.<sup>11</sup>

The analysis documents that brain imaging tools are generally more intrusive than ANS activity measurement tools. Thus, the use of ANS activity measurement tools typically allows studies to be conducted in (more) natural research settings or contexts. This has a positive effect on verisimilitude and thus on EV. However, the analysis also shows that there are significant tool differences both within brain imaging tools and within ANS activity measurement tools. For example, a direct comparison of fMRI and EEG (research-grade) shows that both tools have a high degree of invasiveness. However, EEG allows for a much higher degree of movement freedom and natural position, as indicated by a statement in Léger et al. (2014, p. 653): “Electroencephalography (EEG) unlike other more obtrusive methods (e.g., fMRI) offers NeuroIS researchers the possibility of measuring human brain activity during ecologically valid interactions with IT; that is, while users interact with an IT in a more natural setting.”

When interpreting our analysis in Table 3, it is important to remember that there is often no alternative to the use of brain imaging tools. Thus, tools related to measuring ANS activity cannot simply replace brain imaging tools. For example, Dimoka (2010) used fMRI to investigate whether trust and distrust in online shopping are different constructs and thus reside in different brain areas. This and several other research questions addressed by NeuroIS researchers cannot be investigated based on tools related to the measurement of ANS activity or other brain imaging tools—as they either do not provide comparable spatial resolution (e.g., EEG) or are limited to measuring activity only in specific brain areas (e.g., prefrontal regions, fNIRS) (Riedl & Léger, 2016). With this in mind, we conclude that while the use of ANS activity measurement tools has a positive impact on the naturalness of research settings or contexts in which IS studies can take place, further strategies are needed to increase EV when using brain imaging tools.<sup>12</sup>

One important strategy has been introduced by Dimoka (2010). Essentially, she suggests that an fMRI study should be complemented by a behavioral study outside the scanner using the same research design. Based on such a procedure, it is possible to compare the behavioral (self-reported) data collected inside the scanner with the data collected outside the scanner, and if there is high agreement, then the artificiality of the research setting or context should not be a serious problem. This strategy can also be applied to the use of other neurophysiological instruments. It is clear that such a strategy is powerful for demonstrating EV. However, it comes at a “cost,” namely the effort of conducting a complementary behavioral study. Moreover, the strategy carries the risk of non-correlation between the behavioral (self-reported) data collected inside and outside the MRI machine (or other neuroscience tools), implying a low EV of the results collected in the neuroscience tool environment. In general, however, we recommend Dimoka’s (2010) strategy. Support for this strategy can also be found in other NeuroIS papers (e.g., Anderson et al., 2016b; Reeck et al., 2023; Vance et al., 2018).

Another important strategy that we observe in NeuroIS research to increase EV is the use of less intrusive tools within a tool category. For example, in the recent past, consumer-grade EEG tools have become increasingly important. These are portable and wireless EEG tools that were not originally developed for research purposes but are used for scientific research (e.g., the Emotiv EPOC, a 14-channel EEG headset originally developed for gaming purposes). A recent NeuroIS literature review found that 11 out of the 27 completed EEG studies used the Emotiv EPOC system (Riedl et al., 2020a). Table 3 shows that the degree of invasiveness of consumer-grade EEG devices is lower than that of research-grade EEG devices (e.g., Neuroscan, Brainvision). A main reason for this assessment is that research-grade instruments typically use many more electrodes (32, 64, 128, or even 256; Mueller-Putz et al., 2015) and the montage is more complex than consumergrade instruments (even if water-based or dry electrodes are used in a stretch lycra cap). A recent application example of the Emotiv EPOC system in our sample of analyzed articles is Moravec et al. (2022).

In addition to EEG, a similar development can be observed with respect to heart rate as a physiological signal. The gold standard for measuring heart rate is the ECG, where electrodes are attached to the chest (multichannel ECG uses 3, 6, or even 12 electrodes) (Nadj et al., 2023, p. 1626). However, if a NeuroIS researcher uses heart rate or heart rate variability (HRV) as a metric, either a chest strap or photoplethysmography (PPG, usually as a built-in function in smartwatches) can also be used as a measurement instrument. Table 3 shows that the degree of intrusiveness differs among the three options for assessing HR and HRV, in ascending order: PPG (smartwatch) < chest strap < multichannel ECG.

There is a tendency in academic research, in general, and in NeuroIS, in particular, to use the less intrusive option within a tool category, as evidenced by the increasing use of consumer-grade EEG devices (rather than research-grade devices), smartwatches with PPG (rather than ECG), face analysis software such as Noldus’s FaceReader (rather than fEMG), or built-in galvanometers in wearables such as Empatica’s E4 wearable device (rather than traditional galvanometers with sensors attached to the non-dominant hand) (Riedl et al., 2020a). However, while this trend is positive for EV, there is an ongoing scientific debate about the measurement quality of such “lightweight” devices. Recent debates in the NeuroIS literature include but are not limited to Riedl et al. (2020b), discussing the measurement properties of consumer-grade EEG, and Stangl and Riedl (2022), discussing the reliability and validity of consumer-grade HR and HRV measurement devices. The current state of the discussion is that there is no general rule. Rather, NeuroIS researchers discuss why their measurement tool or approach is justified in their specific research situation. For example, Moravec et al. (2022, p. 896) wrote:

EEG data were collected using an Emotiv EPOC … In the NeuroIS literature, using consumer-grade systems in analysis of timefrequency data and event-related spectral perturbation (ERSP) analysis has gained broader acceptance as many studies show, for this type of analysis, the consumer-grade systems provide data that is as accurate as larger density, medical-grade systems (albeit with lower spatial resolution from the lower number of electrodes).<sup>13</sup>

## 4.2 Nature of Stimuli

Our analysis (see Appendix for details) shows that most NeuroIS studies have used static images as stimuli— often screenshots of user interfaces, such as eBay websites (Riedl et al., 2010b) or security warnings (Jenkins et al., 2016; Vance et al., 2018), but also some other forms of static stimuli, such as solutions to programming problems in the form of short source code snippets (Fadel et al., 2015), pictures of avatar faces (Riedl et al., 2014b), or three-dimensional surface graphs and bubble graphs (Walden et al., 2018), among others. Thus, the nature of the stimuli typically does not allow for real interaction with digital technologies (only 10 out of 42 papers used real interaction). Rather, subjects were typically required to passively view the stimuli, and the corresponding physiological correlates were recorded and analyzed during this passive viewing phase. While this approach is also dominant in cognitive neuroscience research, it has a negative impact on EV; as Parsons (2015, p. 1) tellingly wrote:

An essential tension can be found between researchers interested in ecological validity and those concerned with maintaining experimental control. Research in the human neurosciences often involves the use of simple and static stimuli lacking many of the potentially important aspects of real world activities and interactions … there is a growing interest in the human neurosciences to use cues about target states in the real world via multimodal scenarios that involve visual, semantic, and prosodic information. These scenarios should include dynamic stimuli presented concurrently or serially in a manner that allows researchers to assess the integrative processes carried out by perceivers over time. Furthermore, there is growing interest in contextually embedded stimuli.

It is obvious that most IS phenomena are not static but represent unfolding, evolving, and waning phenomena (e.g., stress, trust, or attention to IT security warnings). In other words, the world is made up of processes, and everything is thus in constant flux rather than in a stable state (vom Brocke et al., 2021). It follows that the use of (1) multimodal (visual, semantic, and prosodic information), (2) dynamic (serially or simultaneously presented information over time), and (3) contextually embedded stimuli in neuroscience research positively affect EV (Parsons 2015), a fact that also applies to NeuroIS research. Unfortunately, it is neither always desirable nor easy to design and present stimuli with these three properties in NeuroIS laboratory studies. First, isolating a specific effect may require tight control of the stimulus material. Tight control may be easier to achieve with static stimuli than with multimodal, dynamic, and contextually embedded stimuli. Second, studying a construct dynamically as it unfolds over time is a complex endeavor, as task and stimulus development typically involve a great deal of effort. However, the use of virtual reality (VR) as a stimulus presentation technology, or as a task context in general, is a promising way to increase EV.

Parsons (2015) published a seminal paper on VR for enhancing EV and experimental control in clinical, affective, and social neuroscience, arguing:

A virtual environment provides the researcher with an ecologically valid platform for presenting dynamic stimuli in a manner that allows for both the veridical control of laboratory measures and the verisimilitude of naturalistic observation of real life situations … Virtual environmentbased assessments can provide a balance between naturalistic observation and the need for exacting control over key variables. (p. 7)

Although not described in Parsons (2015), we make a call for VR-based studies in combination with neurophysiological measures.

Most VR goggles specialize in simulating real-world activities in a virtual environment, allowing the user to freely interact with the environment as they would outside the virtual environment (Slater, 1999). Some important examples of VR applications in neuroscience that are also highly relevant to NeuroIS are virtual offices (Jansari et al., 2013; Lamberts et al., 2010; Montgomery et al., 2011), virtual apartments or houses (Sweeney et al., 2010, imagining smart home applications), virtual street crossing (Clancy et al., 2006, imagining the possible fatal consequences of looking at the smartphone), and virtual parks (Buxbaum et al., 2012, imagining their potential as a countermeasure to technostress). In addition, distractors based on the natural environment can be implemented. For example, if a person is in a virtual office, they might hear a phone ringing, or if they are on a virtual street, they might see cars passing by. There are also a number of studies on virtual classroom environments with the implementation of distractors (Díaz-Orueta et al., 2014; Lalonde et al., 2013). Such environments could be used to study several IS constructs, including attention, stress, multitasking, among others, and the corresponding effects on outcome variables such as user satisfaction or work performance. For example, Stangl et al.’s (2023) pilot study compared fatigue and stress levels (based on HRV measurement) in digital collaboration, comparing videoconferencing (using MS Teams) and the metaverse (based on the Meta Quest 2 virtual reality headset (https://www.meta.com/quest/products/quest-2).

Another important argument for VR-based stimulus presentation in NeuroIS research is based on the evidence that VR is effective in inducing realistic emotions. For example, it has been shown that interacting with “anxious” or “relaxing” virtual environments actually induces anxiety or relaxation (Riva et al., 2007). This finding provides an important basis for our call to integrate VR-based stimulus presentation into future NeuroIS research since a major argument for the potential of neuroscience in IS research is that it can capture hidden or unconscious processes that “may not readily be uncovered with self reports or observation because such patterns may not be available for introspection” (Dimoka et al., 2012, p. 687). Obviously, affective states, feelings, and emotions—rather than deliberate thought processes— are often not easily accessible through introspection.

As a possible starting point for future NeuroIS studies using VR, we recommend Pfeiffer et al. (2020). They used eye-tracking at the point of sale to investigate two customers’ shopping motives: goal-directed and exploratory information search behaviors. In their study, Pfeiffer et al. conducted two experiments: the first in an immersive VR and the second as a field study in a real supermarket. Importantly, Pfeiffer et al. argued that they “conducted a virtual reality study, because recently launched virtual shopping environments suggest that there is great interest in using this technology as a retail channel” (p. 675). Thus, despite the fact that Pfeiffer et al. conducted a complementary field study (thereby increasing EV), their VR study alone allowed for multimodal, dynamic, and contextually embedded stimulus perception, thereby positively affecting EV. However, their study was the only one of 42 articles analyzed that used VR. Thus, future NeuroIS research should address this important gap.

## 4.3 Nature of Task and Required Participant Response

To improve EV in future NeuroIS studies, researchers should ask the following question: Is the task chosen for the laboratory study what people do in the real world? Asking this question requires consideration of participant behavior and the response required in the study. There are studies that select a task that exists in the real world (or at least closely resembles it), while there are other studies that design tasks to fit the research environment (e.g., the fMRI environment).

Suppose a researcher selects a task that is a simplified version of what an employee might encounter in a realworld context, such as analyzing data using PowerBI in Microsoft Excel. An example of simplification would be if a subject had to passively view some analytical graphs and answer some questions about them instead of actually interacting with the software program. In this case, a question that could undermine verisimilitude is whether subjects would behave similarly when performing this task to the way they would when performing full-scale data plotting in PowerBI in an organizational context.

We emphasize that we are not undermining the findings of such studies—they are indeed informative. However, the question that we raise is the relevance and applicability of such research to real-world problems (Mohajeri et al., 2020). Thus, one major recommendation for future NeuroIS research would be for researchers to explicitly discuss whether and to what extent the research task resembles activities that subjects encounter in real life, even in artificial environments such as fMRI scanners (Parsons, 2016). Such a discussion could be included in the methods or limitations sections. As our analyses show (see Appendix), such discussions are scarce in the existing NeuroIS literature. However, there are notable exceptions, such as Anderson et al. (2016b),<sup>14</sup> Fadel et al. (2015),<sup>15</sup> and Twyman et al. (2020).<sup>16</sup>

In this context, we also recommend using already existing tasks whenever possible, particularly when their relevance to real-life situations is obvious. For example, Minas et al. (2014) used a preexisting task, namely the hidden profile decision task, to select student applicants for admission to a university. In their paper, Minas et al. indicated that the task “was validated by the admissions office at the university where the experiment was conducted to ensure that its information was appropriate” (p. 60). Such validations increase EV and are therefore recommended for future studies. Furthermore, the use of an existing task has a positive effect on the replication of results, thus promoting a cumulative research tradition (Berthon et al., 2002). Another recommended strategy was used by Turel et al. (2021), who explicitly asked the participants about the perceived realism of the task after they had completed the experimental task. Turel et al. commented in their paper: “Their feedback indicated that the scenarios’ realism and understandability were high and that the expected low and high positive valence of the outcomes of control and ISP [information security policy] violation scenarios was realistic” (p. 1739).

## 4.4 Nature of Subjects

Nine out of 42 articles reported studies based on nonstudent samples (Fadel et al., 2015: programmers; Fadel et al., 2022: software developers; Jaeger & Eckhardt, 2021: employees; Li et al., 2014: working professionals; Meservy et al., 2014: software developers; Meservy et al., 2019: programming professionals; Nunamaker et al., 2011: city residents; Ortiz de Guinea & Webster, 2013: employees; Pfeiffer et al., 2020: supermarket shoppers) (see the “nature of subjects” column in Table 2). While a student sample is not problematic per se, especially when students are the primary target population (e.g., social networking site use), in other situations, the use of a student sample is likely to reduce veridicality and hence EV. This is consistent with the existing literature on the use of student subjects in behavioral IS studies. For example, Compeau et al. (2012) indicated that the extent to which research findings based on student samples can be generalized depends, among other things, on “meaningful parallel conditions or key attributes … [and they suggest that a] clear and specific justification for the use of student samples” (p. 1101) should be provided in a research article. Our analyses of the 42 articles also revealed that some papers (or specific studies reported in these papers) did not report sample characteristics in detail; rather, they simply characterized their samples as “subjects” or “participants” without revealing whether the participating persons were students or not (for details, see the entries “subjects” and “participants” in the “nature of subjects” column in Table 2).

Another important aspect is neurodivergence within a study sample, which can have a significant impact on the results of NeuroIS studies. For example, there is a widespread perception that careers in technology and software development may attract a higher proportion of neurodivergent individuals, particularly those with characteristics associated with autism spectrum disorder or attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Indeed, a 2019 survey of the software industry asked questions including the following, to which the following percentage of the 15,922 respondents answered affirmatively: “I have a concentration and/or memory disorder (e.g. ADHD)” (6.4%) and “I have autism / an autism spectrum disorder (e.g. Asperger’s)” (2.6%) (Stack Overflow, 2019). Furthermore, Lesiuk et al. (2011) reported that in the population of system developers, “there are twice as many individuals with introversion as with extraversion [and the] ratio of introverts to extraverts showed an opposite trend to the general population, with an almost two-to-one ratio of extraverts to introverts” (p. 63). The implication is that the unique cognitive styles and perspectives of neurodivergent individuals may result in different patterns of brain and ANS activation, as well as different behavioral patterns, compared to their neurotypical counterparts.

This recognition underscores the increased importance of a careful and comprehensive description of the study population in neurodivergent research, beyond the level of detail typically required in more traditional IS research. The recognition of (possible) neurodivergence within the sample prompts researchers to delve deeper into the intricacies of individual differences, thereby contributing to a more nuanced understanding of the phenomena under study. Furthermore, exploring neurodivergent perspectives within the fields of engineering and software development opens up avenues for promoting inclusivity and innovation. The diverse cognitive strengths and problem-solving approaches of neurodivergent individuals can be harnessed to enhance creativity and productivity within these sectors. As we explore the complexities of neurodivergence and its potential impact on study outcomes, it is imperative that researchers not only acknowledge these differences but also embrace them as valuable contributors to the ever-evolving landscape of NeuroIS research. Thus, as the field of NeuroIS continues to evolve, an inclusive and comprehensive approach to research design is likely to lead to more robust and meaningful findings.

Based on our analyses, we recommend the following for future NeuroIS studies. First, researchers should explicitly state whether students are a target population. Second, if a student sample is used when students are not the target population, researchers should state this fact in the limitations section. We highlight a positive example in this context: Tams et al. (2014, p. 742) wrote that their “study relied on student data and, thus, it is not clear to what extent the results can generalize to the workplace. Yet, much like corporate employees, students are human beings and users of technology who experience technostress.” Third, if a student sample is used because students are a target population, or even the target population, this fact should be indicated in the paper. Ye et al. (2022, p. 944) wrote in their study based on e-commerce pages that “because our experiment stimuli were either selected from or a simulation of real-world websites, the use of a student sample should not present a serious threat to the external validity of this study.” Fourth, if a sample of non-students (e.g., software developers, managers) is used, this fact should be highlighted as a strength of the study. For example, Meservy et al. (2014, p. 21) wrote that “because our target population includes experienced knowledge workers who search for solutions to problems online, we solicited experienced software developers from four organizations.” Table 4 summarizes the strategies to increase EV in NeuroIS research as discussed in Section 4.

## 5 Concluding Comments

Establishing EV is a major challenge in NeuroIS research. This study’s literature review revealed that many NeuroIS articles in the existing literature have not effectively addressed EV or have neglected discussions of its influence on the generalizability of their findings to the real world. We argue that there are two main threats to EV. The first threat is verisimilitude, which refers to the extent to which the laboratory situation resembles the situation in everyday life. The second threat is veridicality, which refers to the extent to which subject behavior in the laboratory can predict the phenomenon under study in everyday life. It is likely that most NeuroIS researchers do not have a clear concept of EV in mind. Therefore, we developed a conceptualization based on existing literature published in neuroscience and psychology (Figure 1). Verisimilitude has three dimensions: the nature of the research setting or context, the nature of the stimuli, and the nature of the task and required participant response. Veridicality is influenced by verisimilitude and thus by its three dimensions; in addition, the nature of the subjects is an important dimension of veridicality. Based on this conceptualization, we reviewed all 42 empirical NeuroIS articles published in the AIS basket-of-eight journals in the period 2007-2023 to document the status of EV in the NeuroIS literature. Based on this analysis, we outline possible strategies for future studies to increase EV. However, while we consider our EV conceptualization, the review, and the outlined strategies to be a useful theoretical and empirical foundation, it is hoped that our paper will stimulate further research and thus be more of a “starting point” rather than the “final word.” It will be rewarding to see what insights future research will reveal.

## About the Authors

Ali Balapour is an assistant professor of business information systems at Northern Kentucky University. His research focuses on the impact of immersive systems, games, and mobile apps on individuals and organizations. His work has been published in leading information systems journals such as Computers in Human Behavior and International Journal of Information Management. He has also presented at major information systems conferences, including the Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences, the Decision Sciences Institute Annual Conference, and the Americas Conference on Information Systems.

René Riedl is a professor of digital business & innovation and the head of the Digital Business Management study program at the University of Applied Sciences Upper Austria, where he also served as vice dean of the School of Business and Management. He is also an associate professor of business informatics at the Johannes Kepler University Linz, Austria, the current academic director of the Neuro-Information-Systems Society (www.NeuroIS.org), and a coinitiator and co-organizer of the Transdisciplinary Research Union for the Study of Trust Initiative (www.t-r-u-s-t.org). He has published in such journals as MIS Quarterly, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Journal of Management Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, Journal of Information Technology, Computers in Human Behavior, Scientific Reports, and Journal of Systems & Software. He has also served as a senior editor, associate editor, and reviewer for many academic journals, including Business & Information Systems Engineering and MIS Quarterly (associate editor), and Journal of the Association for Information Systems (senior editor). His research has been funded by various institutions, including FWF—Austrian Science Fund, FFG—Austrian Research Promotion Agency, and the European Union, as well as private organizations.
