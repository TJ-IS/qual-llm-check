## a b s t r a c t

This study considers the competition between an inferior search engine and a superior search engine with the option to introduce a knowledge-sharing service. This study focuses on the pure strategy Nash equilibrium of the competition between inferior and superior search engines attempting to maximize their either pro<sup>fi</sup>ts or market shares. If one search engine introduces a knowledge-sharing service, it decides whether to make its answer database accessible by the other competing search engine. This compatibility decision of each search engine is shown to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by whether it maximizes its pro<sup>fi</sup>t or market share. The superior search engine should keep its answer database closed to maximize its market share, but may make its answer database open to maximize its pro<sup>fi</sup>t unless the amount of information available on the Internet is small. The inferior search engine should keep its answer database open to maximize its market share if its search technology is far behind that of the superior search engine. Both the inferior and superior search engines should make their answer databases open to maximize their pro<sup>fi</sup>ts if the amount of information available on the Internet is large. This study also shows that equilibrium strategies for inferior and superior search engines depend primarily on the amount of information available on the Internet, the degree of searchers' patience to wait for answers, and the search quality difference.

## 1. Introduction

Google has been a leading search engine in the United States for several years, and its proprietary search technology is a major contributor to its huge success. Previous research on consumer choices in search engines has con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that most users choose a favorite search engine based on its search quality, not its additional non-search services [19,20]. Although brand awareness can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the searchers' choices between search engines [8], this study focuses on the competition between two major search engines with only a minimal difference in brand awareness.

Yahoo, Google's main competitor, has a well-developed knowledgesharing service called Yahoo Answers, which it introduced in December 2005. This online knowledge-sharing service connects people who have failed to <sup>fi</sup>nd answers to their questions via search to answerers who have some knowledge about the topic in question [10]. In this way, Yahoo may complement its search quality by inducing its users to visit Yahoo Answers when their initial search on the Internet is unsatisfactory. Yahoo Answers had a 74% market share in the US online knowledge-sharing market as of March 2008.<sup>1</sup> This suggests that Yahoo sites including Yahoo Search and Yahoo Answers may have increased Yahoo's overall market share. Despite the success of Yahoo Answers, the market share of Yahoo sites in the United States has not increased at all; Google increased its market share in 2008.<sup>2</sup>

Naver, a dominant leader in the South Korean search engine market, uses its successful knowledge-sharing service to maintain its leading position.<sup>3</sup> According to the New York Times, about 80% of the information available on the Internet is written in English.<sup>4</sup> Compared to other popular languages such as Chinese, Spanish, Russian, German, and Japanese, the amount of online information in Korean is relatively small. Based on the limited amount of online information available in Korean, Korean searchers may use a knowledge-sharing service more frequently than searchers who use English to search the Internet. According to the Financial Times, Naver attracts an average of 44,000 questions and 100,000 answers each day, making it the world's <sup>fi</sup>fthlargest portal.<sup>5</sup> As a result of this huge amount of knowledge-sharing,

Naver has accumulated a large number of already-answered questions in its knowledge database. One critical policy of Naver is that its answer database is only available to users visiting Naver; other search engines cannot access the information stored in the database.

In contrast to the Naver's closed answer database, Yahoo has kept its answer database open; search engines such as Google and Microsoft Bing can access the Yahoo Answers database. For instance, Fig. 1 shows the Google search results for “Good Restaurant in Palo Alto”. Google provides a link to relevant answers stored in the Yahoo Answers database.

When a search engine opens its answer database, it encounters a trade-off between attracting additional searchers who originally visited a competing search engine but were indirectly led to the search engine itself and helping a competing search engine access more information.

When a search engine introduces a knowledge-sharing service, it chooses its compatibility with the answer database created by the knowledge-sharing service. If the search engine makes the answer database open (or compatible), other search engines can access the answer database; if the search engine keeps the answer database closed (or incompatible), other search engines cannot access the answer database. When two search engines (called SE1 and SE2) introduce their knowledge-sharing services, Fig. 2 shows the possible compatibility choices made by SE1 and SE2.

When both SE1 and SE2 keep their answer databases closed, they compete on the basis of their search technology and proprietary answer database. When both SE1 and SE2 make their answer databases open, their answer databases will not increase their competitiveness as search engines. When only one search engine keeps its answer database closed, its proprietary answer database increases its competitiveness (especially when information available on the Internet is not abundant); however, it loses the traf<sup>fi</sup>c of searchers who originally visited the other search engine but are indirectly led to its answer database.

The different policies of Naver and Yahoo regarding their answer databases could be the results of the different online search environments in the United States and South Korean search engine markets. The amount of information available on the Internet in English is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly larger than that in Korean. As a result of this information asymmetry, searchers in South Korea may be more willing to use a knowledgesharing service than users in the United States. Additionally, South Korea and the United States may have different search quality variations in major search engines. Considering these search environment factors, this study investigates pro<sup>fi</sup>t-maximizing equilibrium strategies for inferior and superior search engines if they can introduce a knowledgesharing service with an open or a closed answer database. Table 1 shows the major search engines' compatibility choices in the United States and South Korea.

To <sup>fi</sup>nd the pro<sup>fi</sup>t-maximizing equilibrium strategies for the inferior and superior search engines, we <sup>fi</sup>nd four different types of ful<sup>fi</sup>lled expectations equilibria (FEEs) according to which search engine and knowledge-sharing service are expected to the <sup>fi</sup>rst choice of searchers. The equilibrium concept we employ in this paper is similar to that applied in some two-sided-market research [7]. We also provide the equilibrium analysis if they focus on maximizing their market shares.

## 2. Literature review

This study makes a contribution to the literature on search engine competition by analyzing search engine competition with the option of introducing a knowledge-sharing service. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents probably the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to analyze the static competition between an inferior search engine and a superior search engine when they have the option to introduce a knowledgesharing service. Kim and Tse [11] consider the dynamic competition between an inferior search engine with a knowledge-sharing service and a superior search engine without a knowledge-sharing service. They show that the inferior search engine will bene<sup>fi</sup>t more from its knowledge-sharing service if the amount of online content decreases. They also suggest that an inferior search engine should generally close its answer database to increase its market share. However, they do not provide an analytical condition under which the inferior search engine should keep its answer database closed. Telang et al. [18] provide a static model of competition between an incumbent search engine and a new search engine. Their study shows that multiple search engines with different search qualities can coexist because search engines do not charge a fee to users and their performance is limited; as a result, searchers tend to multihome, i.e., they visit multiple search engines. Telang et al. [18] do not consider the amount of information as a part of search quality, but the proposed model explicitly includes the amount of information in the search engine's decisions regarding the development of a knowledge-sharing service and the optimal search quality. Pollock [17] suggests that Google's dominant position in many countries is likely to be strengthened when each search is performed on a single search engine; the strong assumption that each searcher visits only one search engine leads to the latter conclusion. In contrast, this study is based on the assumption that searchers may visit another search engine when they fail to <sup>fi</sup>nd answers to their questions. Argenton and Prüfer [1] also assert that a leading search engine will continue to be dominant unless it shares its search logs with other search engines. In addition, Lianos and Motchenkova [12] indicate that a dominant search engine may not improve its search quality well and its advertisers may be charged higher fees. This study shows that when online content is limited, even a superior search engine may lose its dominant position to an inferior search engine if the knowledge-sharing service of the inferior search engine offers better results than that of the superior search engine.

This study also makes a contribution to the literature of two-sided markets. An online knowledge-sharing service is a platform that connects questioners and answerers [10]. The decision of whether to introduce a knowledge-sharing service is analogous to that of a general service provider regarding whether to introduce a service-related platform business. From this perspective, this study provides a strategic framework through which the manager of a search engine can decide whether to complement its search quality by introducing a platformbased knowledge-sharing service. Hagiu[7] provides the decision of a market intermediary regarding whether to be a merchant (e.g., buying goods from sellers and reselling those goods to buyers) or to become a platform for the interaction between buyers and sellers. Whereas that study focuses on analyzing the conditions under which a market intermediary as a monopoly should be a platform, we analyze the conditions under which a search engine should provide searchers with a platform for knowledge-sharing when it competes in a duopoly setting.

The decision of a search engine regarding whether to keep its answer database closed is related to the concept of the degree of singlehoming<sup>6</sup> in two-sided-market literature [2,3,4,15,16]. As indicated by Kim and Tse [11], a search engine can be viewed as a two-sided platform that helps searchers <sup>fi</sup>nd information on the Internet. The information on the Internet can be accessed by any search engine; we may say information on the Internet multihomes. A search engine can increase the degree of singlehoming on the information side by keeping its answer database closed. This study shows that when the amount of information available on the Internet is small, in equilibrium, inferior and superior search engines keep their answer databases closed, which increases the degree of singlehoming on the information side.

We also show that when the amount of information on the Internet is not small, in contrast to Doganoglu and Wright[5], the superior search engine may choose to make its answer database open to maximize its pro<sup>fi</sup>t.

## 3. Base model: demands for two competing pure search engines

This section introduces the base model, which describes the searchers' demands for two competing pure search engines with heterogeneous search qualities. Throughout the remaining part of this paper, the inferior search engine is called SE1 and the superior search engine is called SE2. To focus on the strategic effect of a search engine's development of a knowledge-sharing service on the competition, we assume that both SE1 and SE2 have a <sup>fi</sup>xed search quality. The base model is similar to the search engine competition model employed by Telang et al. [21], but also incorporates the effect of the amount of information available on the Internet in search quality.

The search quality of a search engine is speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by the parameter s ∈ [0, 1], which represents how well the search engine provides answers to searchers' questions. The search quality can generally be decomposed into how much information a search engine can index and how well the search engine can extract relevant answers to search queries from its indexed information. The parameter s in the abstract represents the combination of these two factors: s can be viewed as q × t, in which t ∈ [0, 1] measures the amount of online information that a search engine can index and q ∈ [0, 1] measures the quality of search algorithm. If the normalized amount of information available on the Internet in a particular language is $\mathcal { T } \in ( 0 , 1 ]$ , searchers using <sup>I</sup>this language will <sup>fi</sup>nd answers to their questions with a probability of sI if they visit a search engine with search quality s.

## 4. Competition with a knowledge-sharing service

In the proposed model of search engine competition with the option of a knowledge-sharing service, SE1 and SE2 simultaneously determine whether to introduce a closed or open knowledge-sharing service. Therefore, each search engine has the following possible strategies:

• N: Do not introduce a knowledge-sharing service (the search engine uses only the Internet resources, without an answer database);

• C: Introduce a knowledge-sharing service and keep its answer database inaccessible by the other competing search engine (i.e., closed);

• O: Introduce a knowledge-sharing service and make its answer database accessible by the other competing search engine (i.e., open).

To obtain the pure strategy Nash equilibrium of the proposed mode of competition with a knowledge-sharing service, it is <sup>fi</sup>rst necessary to derive the possible FEEs for each strategy pro<sup>fi</sup>le. When only one search engine introduces a knowledge-sharing service, we derive the FEE that satis<sup>fi</sup>es searchers' expectations about how successful the knowledgesharing service will be. When both SE1 and SE2 introduce knowledgesharing services, searchers will have two expectations: one regarding which search engine will be better and another regarding which knowledge-sharing service will be better. We provide all the possible FEEs that satisfy these expectations.

Before explaining how to determine a ful<sup>fi</sup>lled expectations equilibrium, it is necessary to specify how searchers use a search engine and a knowledge-sharing service to <sup>fi</sup>nd answers to their questions. Fig. 3 shows the process that searchers follow when SE1 and SE2 introduce their knowledge-sharing services (KS1 and KS2, respectively). Searchers <sup>fi</sup>rst compare the expected utilities of SE1 and SE2. If searchers are not satis<sup>fi</sup>ed after visiting the search engine with higher expected utility, they may visit the other search engine. At the same time, a d-portion of them may use the knowledge-sharing service with more answerers expected. If their <sup>fi</sup>rst-visited knowledge-sharing service is not satisfactory, they may use the other knowledge-sharing service. The reason why searchers who are not satis<sup>fi</sup>ed from their <sup>fi</sup>rst search may use the other search service as well as the knowledge-sharing services is that the use of knowledge-sharing services generally needs a certain amount of waiting time, during which another search can be reasonably performed.

When SE1 and SE2 introduce KS1 and KS2, SEi can access the answer database of KSj if SEj makes it open. Because SEi can access the whole answer database of KSi, the expected utilities of SE1 and SE2 are speci<sup>fi</sup>ed as follows:

$$

As the search technology difference between SE1 and SE2 decreases, the equilibrium regions (C,C) and (C,O) expand, but the equilibrium region (O,O) shrinks. This is because the strategic importance of the answer database of SE1 increases as the technology gap shortens; as SE1 becomes more competitive, SE1 is more likely to keep its answer database closed to increase its utility to searchers.

## 4.2. Market share-maximizing equilibrium

A search engine may focus on increasing its market share if potential advertisers tend to use only the search engine with the largest market share for their search-advertising platform. According to Nielsen KoreanClick,<sup>10</sup> as of April 2012, 32.4% of advertisers in the South Korean search advertising market used only Naver as their online advertising platform.

To derive the market share-maximizing equilibrium, the cost of introducing a knowledge-sharing service is assumed to be negligible, so that both SE1 and SE2 essentially have two strategies: introducing a closed knowledge-sharing service (C), and introducing an open knowledge-sharing service (O). The market share of each search engine can be derived by considering all the demands for SEi and KSi for i = 1, 2. Thus, for each possible ful<sup>fi</sup>lled expectations equilibrium, the market share of SEi is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as follows:

$$
\text { Market   share   of   SEi } = \frac {\text { Demand   for   SEi   and   KSi }}{\text { Demand   for   SE1 } + \text { KS1 } + \text { demand   for   SE2 } + \text { KS2 }}.
$$

Fig. 9 shows the market share-maximizing equilibria for different values of $\dot { s } _ { 1 }$ and $s _ { 2 }$ when KS1 is better than KS2. Note that the overall shape of equilibrium regions (C, C) and (O, C) does not change signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly if KS2 is better than KS1.

The market share-maximizing equilibrium depends on the amount of information available on the Internet when there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant search technology gap between SE1 and SE2, . For small amounts of information, both SE1 and SE2 choose to provide a closed knowledge-sharing service in equilibrium. If the amount of information available on the Internet is large, SE1 chooses to provide an open knowledge-sharing service and SE2 chooses to provide a closed knowledge-sharing service.

When the search technology gap between SE1 and SE2 is small, in equilibrium, both SE1 and SE2 introduce a closed knowledge-sharing service regardless of the amount of information. Given the same search technology gap between SE1 and SE2, the (C, C) equilibrium is more likely to hold when SE2's search technology is not high. In general, whenever a search engine competes with a slightly better competitor, in equilibrium, the search engine chooses to keep its answer database closed.

## 4.5. Google and Yahoo in the United States

Given that Google has search technology superior to that of Yahoo, the pro<sup>fi</sup>t-maximizing equilibrium in Fig. 7 suggests that Google may increase its pro<sup>fi</sup>t by introducing a knowledge-sharing service if the degree of patience is not very low. ## 5. Concluding remarks

This study investigates the competition between two search engines with different search qualities, and focuses on the effects of a knowledgesharing service on the competitiveness of two competing search engines. If they introduce a knowledge-sharing service, the search engines must determine whether to adopt a closed answer database. Search engines that adopt an open answer database can indirectly attract some searchers who originally visited a competing search engine, but also help their competitor access more information. Search engines' compatibility choices may change depending on whether to maximize their pro<sup>fi</sup>ts or market shares.

To some degree, this result is in agreement with Google's current strategy of not developing its own knowledgesharing service.

This study also presents the market share-maximizing equilibrium strategies for the inferior and superior search engines. These market share-maximizing equilibrium strategies generally depend on the search quality difference and the amount of information on the Internet. When the amount of information on the Internet is not large, in equilibrium, both the inferior and superior search engines should keep their answer databases closed. When the amount of information is large, if the search technology difference is small, both the inferior and superior search engines should keep their answer databases closed. If the search technology difference is not small, the inferior search engine should open its answer database, and the superior search engine should keep its answer database closed.
