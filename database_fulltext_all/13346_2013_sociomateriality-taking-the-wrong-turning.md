## a b s t r a c t

The claims for sociomateriality are reviewed and two key problems are isolated: a failure to be speci<sup>fi</sup>c about technology and a neglect of broader social structures. These problems are located in the formulations of Barad. Her notion of agential realism is contrasted to an alternative perspective constructed using the resources of critical realism. The potential of the latter to contribute to the refreshing of the socio-technical tradition is outlined. This tradition offers more resources for the consideration of the important role of the material in contemporary organizing than sociomateriality, which is argued to be a wrong turning.

## 1. Introduction

A welcome focus on the importance of the material in social and organizational life has seen considerable interest in ‘sociomateriality’ (Leonardi, Nardi, & Kallinikos, 2012). This umbrella term covers a wide variety of approaches to the relationship between the social and the material. It seeks to redress what is perceived as the neglect of the material in broader social and organizational theories. This article welcomes this ambition. but joins others in doubting the value of certain aspects of the formulation as opposed to more traditional approaches such as socio-technical systems (Faulkner & Runde, 2012; Leonardi. 2012). In particular. I will argue that some approaches. particularly those grounding their work in the philosophy of science of Karen Barad, have some key weaknesses which make them less useful in approaching some of the combinations of the social and the material that characterise contemporary organizational life. I am thinking in particular of the large data intensive systems coming under the general label of ‘enterprise systems’ (Boudreau & Robey, 2005). I argue that some approaches tend towards a perverse (given the promise of the concept) neglect of the speci<sup>fi</sup>city of the systems involved and an inability to deal adequately with the broader context of practice.

Orlikowski (2010) develops, in a review of practice-based theorising, a useful threefold distinction between phenomenon, perspective and philosophy. These distinctions are recognised to be overlapping, but they can valuably be applied to the range of work that has been conducted under the broad label of ‘sociomateriality’. A summary of what Orlikowski (2010: 23) terms ‘three modes of engaging practice in research’ is provided in Table 1. At the level of the phenomenon, we have the welcome revival of interest in the material shaped in large measure by the growing importance of internet-enabled applications. However, this is not the only reason. Approaches such as strategy-as-practice have also rekindled interest in the use of artefacts such as whiteboards in the particular arrangements of time and space that enable the production of particular forms of performance (Fayard & Weeks, 2007; Whittington, 2006). Much of the work that has been carried out under the general heading of ‘sociomateriality’ is, therefore, concerned with novel phenomena, with the underlying philosophy being of lesser interest. However, each approach mobilises a particular perspective, or set of perspectives. The perspective adopted can render certain aspects of the focal phenomenon more or less visible. Thus, the work of Scott and Orlikowski (2012) on TripAdvisor, adopting the lens of sociomateriality, draws our attention to the importance of the algorithm that produces the rankings. This rather opaque but crucial mechanism is of more signi<sup>fi</sup>cance than the obvious artefacts deployed and is a valuable reminder that materiality is not a synonym for the physical, nor is it restricted to information and communication technology (ICT) artefacts. Similar concerns are also provided by a focus on architecture (Bygstad, 2010; Clark, McLoughlin, Rose, & King, 1988; Mutch, 2010) where it is the arrangements of particular constellations of materiality that are consequential, or the focus on classi<sup>fi</sup>cation and measuring criteria (Bowker, 2000; Pollock, 2012). However, a variety of perspectives can address such features. Although associated with technology and perhaps rather dated in some of its terminology, there is still value in returning to socio-technical approaches (Robey, Raymond, & Anderson, 2012). Similarly, approaches such as actor–network theory have yielded valuable insights with a focus on the need to be speci<sup>fi</sup>c about particular aspects of the material (Monteiro & Hanseth, 1995). Whilst both might be encompassed under the broad heading of sociomateriality, there is something more speci<sup>fi</sup>c about the philosophical position that is the concern of the argument presented in this article.

Whilst it is important to note that the term sociomateriality (without any intervening hyphen) was developed in the work of Suchman (2002), a high proportion of work coming under this heading makes particular use of the work of Barad. A physicist working with the resources of feminist critique of science, whose work is explored in more detail later in the article, Barad has developed the notion of ‘agential realism’. This is drawn upon, explicitly by some in the sociomaterial approach, rather more indirectly by others, to argue for the ‘constitutive entanglement’ of the social and the material. This is a particular ontological claim which has consequences for the perspective we adopt and so the phenomena that are brought to our attention. It is my contention that there are large questions to be asked of this philosophical approach, which for present purposes we might term ‘strong’ sociomateriality, questions which I ask using the resources of another philosophical tradition, that of critical realism. Others in this tradition have also raised similar concerns, although they have tended to rest at the level of perspective (Faulkner & Runde, 2012). My argument is that it is important to treat these arguments with the respect and seriousness that they deserve and, accordingly, my focus in this article is largely on those, such as Orlikowski, Scott and Nyberg, whose work displays a detailed engagement with aspects of Barad's work. In raising some concerns about what might be lost by such an engagement I suggest that critical realism offers an alternative set of resources, one which can encompass the interesting insights that have been gained through much of the work that goes under the banner of sociomateriality. However, I suggest that a more productive route is through revisiting and refreshing the insights from the socio-technical tradition, with its emphasis on a non-con<sup>fl</sup>ationary approach, in which the social and the material are held apart for the purpose of exploring their interplay.

## 2. Sociomateriality: promise and problems

The lack of attention to IS in the mainstream journals concerned with business, management and organizations has been commented on in review articles by Orlikowski and Scott (2008) and Leonardi and Barley (2010). Their concerns echo long standing complaints from within IS research that analysts fail to be speci<sup>fi</sup>c about technology (Monteiro & Hanseth, 1995; Orlikowski & Iaconno, 2001). Such complaints note that the impacts of technology can vary depending on the affordances which they offer, enabling some activities but constraining others. Failure to recognise this speci<sup>fi</sup>city means that accounts fail to convince. In addition, there is concern that many of the accounts produced which do consider IS focus on speci<sup>fi</sup>c ‘impacts’, most notably at the point of implementation. In so doing, they fail to trace the changing use, adaption of and accommodation to such technologies. So we have the position that many accounts of IS seem to tell tales of resistance and failure, whilst anecdotally organizations, however imperfectly, seem to have IS-enabled systems embedded at the heart of their activities. These concerns have considerable validity and they form the backdrop to the advocacy of sociomateriality as a way of better exploring the mutual entanglement of organization and technology.

In a review article, Orlikowski and Scott (2008) outline a number of approaches to the study of technology and organizations. They distinguish between two ways in which analysts have treated the relationship, in both of which the organization and the technology have been treated as discrete entities. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst, technology and organization are treated as variables, with the focus being on specifying the relationship between the two. This has been subjected to critique by the processual school of studies, which sees technology and organization being shaped through mutual inter-action. However, this view sees organization and technology as very distinct entities. This is contrasted to approaches, the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant of which are those drawn from actor–network theory, which advocate symmetry of analysis between humans and non-humans. Whilst seeing this as something of an advance, Orlikowski, drawing on the work of Barad, suggests that these approaches fail to take into account the ontological entanglement of the social and the material. In her words,

materiality is integral to organizing, positing that the social and the material are constitutively entangled in everyday life. A position of constitutive entanglement does not privilege either humans or technology (in one-way interactions), nor does it link them through a form of mutual reciprocation (in two-way interactions). Instead, the social and the material are considered to be inextricably related — there is no social that is not also material, and no material that is not also social (Orlikowski, 2007: 1437).

This idea has been drawn upon by a number of scholars, and a review of how they have used the concept gives us some sense of the problems which are encountered in practice, problems which, it will be argued, raise serious concerns about the value of the concept. It should be emphasised that this review preserves the distinction that is drawn between sociomateriality and approaches such as actor–network theory. The claims for the latter as an effective way of approaching the relationship between organizations and technology are not at issue here, although an approach founded on critical realism, as espoused here, <sup>fi</sup>nds some aspects problematic (Elder-Vass, 2008; Mutch, 2002).

As noted above, the degree to which those under the broad heading of sociomateriality engage with the work of Barad in any detail is open to question. For many, the attention is on the focal phenomenon and sociomateriality is a useful way of expressing a desire to take the mutual relationship between the social and the material seriously (Baptista, Newell, & Currie, 2010; Greenhalgh & Stones, 2010; Wagner, Newell, & Piccoli. 2010). One could even extend this observation to the fascinating exploration of the ways in which the internet application TripAdvisor has changed the dynamics in the hotel industry (Scott & Orlikowski, 2012). Whilst there is a review of Barad's formulations in Scott and Orlikowski's article, it is not easy to see how this is used to generate insights that would not be gained through other approaches. By contrast, Nyberg's consideration of call centre work is constructed around a detailed engagement with ideas of not only Barad (including not only the widely cited Signs article, but also her 2007 book) but also concepts drawn from the work of Iedama (2007) and his use of Barad's work. So, for example, he notes

Barad (2007) thus uses quantum physics to illustrate that there are no clear boundaries between subject/object or object/object. It is the form of observation that makes particular delineations or cuts. For example, in micro-physics there is no clear boundary between a coffee mug and the hand that holds it; the atoms of the hand and the mug become ‘entangled’ (Nyberg, 2009: 1185).

This approach is then married to an ethnographic form of inquiry to draw conclusions about the material in call centre work. The consequences of this are taken up in a little more detail below.

It could be argued that this difference in the degree of engagement with and application of Barad's ideas has to do with the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of developing the appropriate language to express very complex ideas. The ideas are at an early stage of development and require to be put into practice in order to develop ways of expressing complex phenomena. This is a legitimate argument, but it does appear that many who seek to apply the ideas <sup>fi</sup>nd them dif<sup>fi</sup>cult in practice. Thus, Wagner, Newell and Piccoli note that ‘We found it quite challenging to keep the material in the storyline without falling from one side to another — either leaving the material realm unexamined, or emphasizing the agency of the material to the detriment of understanding the entangled practice’ (Wagner et al., 2010: 292–3). In their discussion of the impacts of an enterprise system, Wagner et al. (2010) use examples which seem both to stress a human-centred perspective and to underplay the materiality involved. Thus, they cite one respondent as saying ‘I hope you understand that it's not [the ES] itself that's the issue. It's the lack of understanding and regard for the people bringing in the money and the people doing the work that's so frustrating’ (Wagner et al., 2010: 285; italics in original). So this sounds not to be a tale of sociomaterial entanglement but one of, say, failed organizational communication. Of course, this could be down to dif<sup>fi</sup>culties with expressing the ideas in language, but this attention to the social might point to more underlving difficulties. There are two which seem to emerge from a more detailed consideration of the work which engages in more detail with Barad: a lack of speci<sup>fi</sup>city about the material and a neglect of broader social structures.

In his discussion of call centre work, Nyberg (2009) is very dependent upon his informants for his view of the material factors which come into play. As he asserts

during the performance of a customer service call, the computer system, the keyboard, the screen, the telephone and the CSO all became one <sup>fi</sup>gure in relation to the customer. No boundaries were experienced between the actors. The orientation was towards the engagement in the practice, rather than the parts performing it (Nyberg, 2009: 1193).

From this what we get are the perspectives of the customer service operator and our knowledge of the sociomaterial assemblage is limited to their understanding. So we might be conscious that in a call centre a web of interlinked technologies are brought to bear to produce the interaction (Chin & Kotak, 2006; Day, 2003). There will be an Automatic Call Distribution system which manages telephone traf<sup>fi</sup>c which might be combined with an intelligent call routing system which means that this call has ended up on the screen of this operative rather than another. The screen may be displaying results combined from one or a number of databases, in <sup>fi</sup>elds which have been established and which are displayed in a fashion, perhaps accompanied by a script, which might be determined by arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence. There are a few conditional terms in that series of sentences, because Nyberg gives us none of this detail. One might say that he is unlikely to, because it could be asserted that most call centre operators are unaware of it. Hence their tendency, as Nyberg discusses, to attribute different forms of agency to ‘the system’ and to discuss it in anthropomorphic terms. Such descriptions are important and interesting, but they are far from an adequate account of the socio-technical entanglements at the heart of a call centre. Perversely. an approach which is advocated in order to bring matter to the centre pushes it to the margins, producing what is a human-centred account underneath the super<sup>fi</sup>cial rhetoric.

Nyberg is dealing in this case with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c articulation of material components, components which are more distinctive and proprietary than those which feature in TripAdvisor (Scott & Orlikowski, 2012). Here the problem, one beyond the control of the researchers, is that speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the key component, the ranking algorithm. is hidden behind barriers of commercial confidentiality. It is therefore something of an unfair criticism to argue that the material is not speci<sup>fi</sup>ed here, but a fairer observation is that to do with the treatment of the broader social context. Here the authors would appear to draw, although not explicitly, on the conventional resources of sociological theory in their consideration of roles such as ‘guest’ and ‘hotelier’. Their account supplies us with interesting details of how the materiality of TripAdviser is changing the performance of these roles, but not in a fashion one might expect from a full-blown sociomaterial perspective

Such a perspective would seem to suggest both that roles are only constituted through their performance and that they are performed by the particular socialmaterial assemblage. Such would seem to be the conclusion from Nyberg's approach. Drawing not only on Barad but also, via Iedama (2007), on the ethnomethodological tradition, he presents an account in which structure is reduced to its performance in practice. So, he argues that the main implication of his account is. counter to other approaches to call centre work which focuses on worker resistance and accommodation,

that, in order to understand the production of control and resistance in call centres, we have to abandon pre-established roles and look at the communities as continuously evolving through the con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of human and non-human actors. … This means that we cannot separate the roles of actors into managers/workers or people/things before analysing activities and practices (Nyberg, 2009: 1194–5).

Social life here is reduced to the practice re<sup>fl</sup>ected on by particular informants and contingently observed by an external party. That such approaches can yield rich material is not in question, but social structures are not necessarily transparent to participants. The approach above ignores the role of time in producing particular constellations of position-practices that emerge from the activity of persons, but are not reducible to that activity. Most crucially, the conditions in which such practice occurs were not produced by those now here present.

A full account, that is, has consider the structure of the organization, a structure which gives some particular resources upon which they can draw, especially in their negotiation of their relationship to technology. So, for example, Wagner et al. (2010) supply two examples of the positions and resources which prevent some from negotiating a satisfactory accommodation with the enterprise system they studied. In one they note, ‘the hiring process was far more complex in the ES than in the old HR system, but the VP for HR rejected clerks' requests to simplify the user interface’ (Wagner et al., 2010: 290). In a second, ‘very little support was found for changing the procurement process at OldU to involve less data input by purchasing clerks, despite those clerks complaining about an inef<sup>fi</sup>cient ERP-enabled work<sup>fl</sup>ow at go-live’ (Wagner et al., 2010 291). They contrast this to the capacity of academics to contest a particular materialisation of the system and win some compromises. The attempt to apply a sociomaterial approach seems to throw up questions of power and the command of resources which are addressed by ‘traditional’ approaches, such as the socio-technical tradition, where concepts such as ‘role’ and ‘structure’ help us account for what these investigations throw up.

I am going to argue in the next section that these problems are inherent in not just in the concept as developed by proponents of strong sociomateriality but also in the foundational ideas of Barad. I seek to take her ideas seriously, but <sup>fi</sup>rst a summary of some of the concerns that the discussion has raised so far. The application of sociomateriality appears to be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult in practice, with accounts tending towards a stress on the human side of the ‘intra-actions’ that are supposed to constitute sociomaterial entanglements. This produces accounts that, perversely, seem not to be speci<sup>fi</sup>c about technology, which tend to be coy about just which combinations of the social and the material are performing subjects in particular situations and how. They also neglect the broader context in which practices are situated, which renders it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for analyses to take full account of factors such as power.

We could add to this the particular emphasis that is placed on the application of these ideas on the context of novel applications, as in the following: ‘[a]s these are multiple, distributed, emergent, dynamic, and unprecedented phenomena, we believe a range of different and <sup>fl</sup>exible lenses and tools is appropriate for studying them’ (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008: 466). The examples used tend to downplay the type of large data intensive systems that characterise the activities of companies such as Wal-Mart which have been suggested as the ‘template for 21st century capitalism’ (Lichtenstein, 2006). I am going to argue that our theoretical systems need to be able to cope with the full range of technology-enabled applications. In doing so they need to take account of the ways in which position-practices articulate with technology. It is surely ironic that at the time when such approaches are being rejected, some operating within the tradition of the ‘strong programme’ of science and technology studies are questioning the focus of approaches such as actor–network theory on local practice and the injunction to ‘follow the actor’. Which actor should we follow, ask Pollock and Williams in their ‘biography’ of the SAP enterprise system, suggesting that ‘this emphasis on local processes and actors may be at the expense of paying less attention to more generalised and long-term shaping processes’ (Pollock & Williams, 2009: 82). Accordingly, in the next section I engage with some of the ideas to be found in Barad, reading her approach against the alternative realist account to be found in the tradition of critical realism.

## 3. Sociomateriality and critical realism: contrasting perspectives

Both these sets of ideas are complex and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult; I cannot provide either a full critique of Barad or a complete account of critical realist approaches in this limited space. Accordingly, my account focuses on those aspects which are particularly pertinent to the agenda which strong sociomateriality has set. Barad's ideas, in particular, are developed at considerable length and in a way which is often not particularly accessible. I seek to provide as clear a reading as I can, without engaging in any detail with her account of quantum mechanics, which is beyond my area of knowledge. In doing this, however, I do draw upon accounts in the critical realist tradition which cast at least some doubt on her approach. These ideas are deserving of a more thorough engagement, but this article is not the place for that. I select two areas for particular discussion: ideas of emergence and strati<sup>fi</sup>cation as a counter to claims about coffee mugs and molecules; and a defence of a traditional approach to structure and agency. Before that discussion, however, I seek to position Barad in her particular intellectual con<sup>fi</sup>guration and to consider the consequences of such a positioning.

Barad gives us a clear indication of the in<sup>fl</sup>uences that have shaped her work:

Agential realism is an account of technoscienti<sup>fi</sup>c and other practices that takes feminist, antiracist poststructuralist, queer, Marxist, science studies, and scienti<sup>fi</sup>c insights seriously, building speci<sup>fi</sup>cally on important insights from Niels Bohr, Judith Butler, Michel Foucault, Donna Haraway, Vicki Kirby Joseph Rouse, and others (Barad, 2003: 810).

Out of this list we can see two major intellectual projects that have shaped Barad's approach. One is feminism and queer studies, where Barad applauds many of the insights but is concerned by the neglect of the material. As she memorably formulates it ‘Language matters. Discourse matters. Culture matters. There is an important sense in which the only thing that does not seem to matter anymore is matter’ (Barad, 2003: 801). To counter this lack she draws upon her engagement with theoretical physics, most notably with the work of the quantum theorist Nils Bohr. Here her concern is that Bohr did not bring out the full ontological implications of his approach and that physicists who have built on his insights subsequently have taken an instrumentalist approach to quantum theory. That is, they have taken that theory's undoubted powers of prediction as read and not concerned themselves with what might be considered profound implications for our understanding of the nature of the world. For Barad this is unsatisfactory. She wishes to hold to a conception of matter as real, as against some of the social constructivism associated with parts of feminism, but to resist the instrumentalism and representationalism of the majority of physicists. This leads her to formulate her notion of ‘agential realism’, seeking to provide a counterpoint to ‘the naïveté of empiricism or the same old narcissistic bedtime stories’ (Barad, 2003: 827). The rejection of these two poles and the provision of an alternative is also the ambition of critical realism. Barad does not recognise the resources of this tradition at all, but in what follows I seek to read her work against some aspects of that tradition and seek to show that critical realism offers more to students of information systems.

Critical realism is a philosophical current not only of growing signi<sup>fi</sup>cance but also of considerable complexity (Sayer, 1992, 2000). This article is not the place to supply a detailed account and in what follows I will be drawing upon only a few elements that seem pertinent. But it seems important to point out two key factors. One is that critical realism as a philosophical tradition has its origins in an engagement with scienti<sup>fi</sup>c practice, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally through Bhaskar (1979) formulation of the question, ‘what must be the world be like for science to be possible'? The second is that critical realism sees itself as a philosophical 'under labourer' for other disciplines. That is, there is not a ‘critical realist’ theory of information systems, or of any other phenomenon for that matter. Rather, critical realism is valuable in helping us gain some conceptual clarity about the nature of the world in order for us to build or amend substantive theories of particular domains. With these two thoughts in mind, the following account <sup>fi</sup>rst counter-poses critical realist concepts of emergence and strati<sup>fi</sup>cation to Barad's agential realism. It then draws upon some work, informed by critical realism, at the level of social theory, in order to relate these ideas to information systems.

Christopher Norris (2000), in work drawing directly on critical realism, has supplied an account of quantum physics, not used by Barad, which presents a view quite different to her formulations. He shares with Barad a recognition of the predictive power of quantum physics and of the puzzles that it poses for conceptions of the world. Along with Barad he recognises some of the paradoxes of quantum physics, such as the measurement problem and wave-particle duality. As with critical realists generally, he too rejects instrumentalist approaches to theory and allows the value of gedanken or thought experiments as valid tools in the search for better knowledge. These facets are shared with Barad, as is the ambition to build an alternative to empiricism and social constructivism. However, the account he provides differs sharply from that which Barad builds. Let us <sup>fi</sup>rst try to outline what that is.

For Barad the referent when we are talking about reality ‘is the phenomenon, not some presumably preexisting, determinately bounded and propertied object’ (Barad, 2007: 202). Phenomena are what our theories address and a ‘phenomenon is a speci<sup>fi</sup>c intra-action of an “object” and the “measuring agencies”; the object and the measuring agencies emerge from, rather than precede, the intra-action that produces them (Barad, 2007: 128). Objects are not ‘primitive’ on this account but always produced by the intra-actions that constitute the phenomena. But in turn the ‘apparatus’ which is involved in these intra-actions is not a simple passive laboratory instrument but ‘productive of (and part of) phenomena’ (Barad, 2007: 142). In more detail, they are ‘speci<sup>fi</sup>c material-discursive practices’, ‘boundary-making practices that are formative of matter and meaning, productive of, and part of, the phenomena produced’ (Barad, 2007: 146). They are themselves phenomena and are ‘open-ended practices’ (Barad, 2007: 146). This conception of phenomena applies not just to the laboratory but to all matter. So, for example,

If “humans” refers to phenomena, not independent entities with inherent properties but rather beings in their differential becoming, particular material (re)con<sup>fi</sup>gurings of the world with shifting boundaries and properties that stabilize and destabilize along with speci<sup>fi</sup>c material changes in what it means to be human, then the notion of discursivity cannot be founded on an inherent distinction between humans and nonhumans (Barad, 2003: 818).

What gives us when we apply this to the social world is a focus on practice, in which large numbers of elements are mutually entangled. So when Barad approaches the analysis of the social world she gives us long lists of factors which produce particular results, as in, for example, her 106 word long sentence of the factors involved in scanning tunnelling microscopes (Barad, 2007: 360–1). (A still more complicated diagram is to be found on page 389 which contains literally hundreds of items.) It casts some light on the approach that we have seen Nyberg adopt and it is one in which no sense of priority is accorded to any of the many factors, as the traditional resort of social theorists to the agency–structure distinction is rejected. For Barad what is important, and what is carried forward into strong sociomateriality, is the notion of the ‘agential cut’. As she argues ‘the agential cut enacts a local resolution within the phenomenon of the inherent ontological indeterminacy’ (Barad, 2003: 815). The problem here is, as Faulkner and Runde argue, that the implications of this are problematic in the social domain, for they appear to suggest that categories are always the product of particular local contingency. As they note, ‘many if not most of the boundaries and categories we live by in our day-to-day lives are generally quite stable, at least relative to our life-histories. and that the same is true of most of the obiects classified within them'(Faulkner & Runde, 2012: 60). Indeed, one could argue that if they were not then the enterprise of social science, to say nothing of organizational practices such as market research would be implausible.

So it is that when one examines Barad's work in detail it gives us few indications as to how to carry out concrete social analysis. There is, for example, a chapter which takes as its focal example the work of Fernandes on working conditions in Calcutta jute mills. Fernandes (1997) uses the work of Bourdieu in particular to explore the complex intersection between gender, class and culture in the production of identities in this context. (Bourdieu being a social theorist whose work, Fowler (1997) has argued, can be seen as consistent with the ontology proposed by critical realism). Her focus on practice, to the relative neglect of broader social structures, may explain why Barad is drawn to her discussion rather than to, say, the earlier discussion of the role of gender in another manufacturing context, Pollert's (1981) examination of the production of identities in cigarette manufacture. Pollert's work provides an alternative discussion of such issues set in the intellectual tradition shared by many critical realist approaches (Pollert, 1996; Walby. 2001). By contrast. Barad suggests that her agential realism will provide new theoretical tools for enhancing this work but these are not provided, with the admission that ‘I have not delved into the speci<sup>fi</sup>c details of this example and much more work is needed to grasp some of the other relevant entanglements and how they matter and for whom’ (Barad, 2007: 242). It is instructive that this chapter is only 24 pages long, set against the 106 pages of the one which follows and which presents aspects of quantum physics in considerable detail. Of course, one might argue that it is for social theorists to take these ideas and develop them for application to speci<sup>fi</sup>c domains, such as information systems. However, I want to suggest, using the resources of realism, that Barad supplies a shaky foundation for such endeavours. I would also suggest that there are resources at the level of social analysis that are much more pertinent to the study of IS in organizational life.

It is interesting to observe Norris's conclusion that

it is preposterous in the strict sense of that term – an inversion of the rational order of priorities – when thinkers claim to draw far-reaching ontological or epistemological lessons from a <sup>fi</sup>eld of thought [such as quantum mechanics] so rife with paradox and lacking (as yet) any adequate grasp of its own operative concepts (Norris, 2000: 5).

Norris reaches this conclusion from a review of work in theoretical physics, much of which is not drawn upon by Barad. In particular, Norris places particular emphasis on Bohm's ‘hidden variables’ theory as a plausible alternative to the orthodox ‘Copenhagen’ approach. This is not to reach any de<sup>fi</sup>nite conclusions, but rather to suggest that the status of theory in this area is open and contested. Drawing on the history of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c discovery he argues that ‘there exist many features of reality that lie beyond our knowledge or present-best powers of understanding, but which none the less obtain quite apart from what we happen to think or believe’ (Norris, 2000: 63). In particular, he takes a very different perspective on Bohr's work, <sup>fi</sup>nding it confused and contradictory. For example, he suggests that ‘One fairly obvious sign of that confusion is Bohr's habit of constantly veering about – often within a few sentences – between different senses of the term ‘phenomenon’ as applied to quantum mechanics’ (Norris, 2000: 232). Barad herself acknowledges that her interpretation of Bohr as a realist is a minority position (Barad, 2007: 122). Clearly, Barad's work on phenomena deserves a more detailed critique than is appropriate or possible here, but Norris' work at least suggests that there are other interpretations of quantum physics and that there are alternative formulations of a realist approach. One might suggest that Barad's approach is in part strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by her location in the world of theoretical physics with the majority commitment to an instrumentalist approach, especially in the United States. This might lead to her presenting rather stark views of the positions she seeks to contest. So, for example, she argues that

If we no longer believe that the world is teeming with inherent resemblances whose signatures are inscribed on the face of the world, things already emblazoned with signs, words lying in wait like so many pebbles of sand on a beach there to be discovered, but rather that the knowing subject is enmeshed in a thick web of representations such that the mind cannot see its way to objects that are now forever out of reach and all that is visible is the sticky problem of humanity's own captivity within language, then it becomes apparent that representationalism is a prisoner of the problematic metaphysics it postulates (Barad, 2007: 137).

One could argue that this is something of a caricature and that critical realism represents a tradition which is attentive to the nature of our representations in its distinction between the intransitive world, which exists regardless of our knowledge of it and the transitive formulations by which we seek imperfect and provisional knowledge of that world. Of importance in that conception is the notion of the world as stratified and emergent and it is to this that I turn next.

I have outlined the ways in which the puzzles that quantum physics has presented are a matter for debate. Those debates, as Norris argues, are a shaky foundation on which to extrapolate to the domain of the social. But even if matters were settled here, critical realism would suggest that it is an illicit move to jump from the level of microscopic physics to the social without taking into account the notions of strati<sup>fi</sup>cation and emergence. As we have seen, Nyberg (2009) uses the example of the atomic composition of coffee mugs to argue for the mutual entanglement of the material and the social. The coffee mug features, albeit in passing, in Barad when she argues against the notion that ‘A coffee mug ends at its outside surface just as surely as people end at their skins’ (Barad, 2007: 155). This is as far as she pursues the example, but Nyberg uses it to suggest that there are no distinct boundaries between the elements that make up a customer service interaction. However, I would suggest that this is confusing levels of analysis; what is useful and appropriate at the level of micro physics is inappropriate at the level of the social. Here the arrangement and behaviour of atoms are beside the point; it is the inter-relations between speci<sup>fi</sup>c con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of the material and the social, and the perceptions that these give rise to and shape them, that are the object of analysis. This assertion is based on the strati<sup>fi</sup>ed nature of reality.

For the neuro-biological scientist Stephen Rose, exploration of the mind and learning shows how memory emerges from a particular biological con<sup>fi</sup>guration but cannot be reduced to that con<sup>fi</sup>guration (Rose, 1993). So, for example, memory is seen to be a self-organizing system that can survive the removal of particular regions of the brain. In this way the biological person emerges from particular constellations of the chemical and, in turn, the physical, but, once emergent, is not reducible to those levels. From the biological emerge the psychological properties of beings and, in turn, the social. But social structures, once emergent, are not to be reduced to matters of individual psychology. This means that critical realists defend, for example, the sex/gender distinction which is denied by Judith Butler and others upon whom Barad draws. In defending this distinction from a critical realist perspective, New (1998, 2005) argues for the notion of human beings as being natural kinds, part of and emergent from the natural world, but with speci<sup>fi</sup>c arrays of properties that give them distinctive capacities for particular types of action. Primary amongst these is the capacity for language, which once emergent is produced and reproduced by humans, but is not reducible to individuals. In this way social structures emerge which then escape their conditions of production and form the context for future action. Against Barad, such structures include the realm of ideas, where theories become important causal factors in social life.

I have argued that the notion of strong sociomateriality draws on work, significant elements of which can be shown to be open to considerable debate. It is based on a particular interpretation of the implications of quantum physics, a <sup>fi</sup>eld which is still rife with debate and contention. These ideas are stretched to draw conclusions about the social world without recognising the importance of emergence. The ideas that are presented about the social world contain commitments, such as those about the nature of human beings, that are contentious and perhaps not in line with the thinking of all those who seek to apply sociomateriality. I have suggested that the tradition of critical realism can supply resources which enable an alternative to be produced. For the purpose of the present article, I seek to turn to those resources to help in the ambition of refreshing the socio-technical tradition, one in which emphasis is placed on the centrality of the material to social life but which holds the items apart in order to examine the interaction between the two. In order to do this, I outline brie<sup>fl</sup>y the morphogenetic approach of Margaret Archer (1995) and seek to show how consideration of material factors, such as the technologies that enable information systems, can be incorporated.

## 4. Taking the material seriously: the resources of realism

I am now turning from the level of philosophy to that of perspective, That is, a number of authors share a common commitment to notions such as strati<sup>fi</sup>cation and emergence from the philosophy of critical realism, but develop different approaches to the social world and to the place of the material within it. So, for example, the work of Tony Lawson (1997) in economics has in its turn provided resources for the approach to the material supplied by Faulkner and Runde (2009, 2012). Mingers' (2006) work on the conceptual foundations of information systems is based on a detailed engagement with aspects of Bhaskar's philosophy. The work of Rob Stones (2005) seeks to revise and extend the insights of Giddens, and as such is drawn upon in the context of health information systems by Greenhalgh and Stones (2010). There is, that is, a healthy level of debate amongst critical realists about how the fundamental concepts are to be applied to the social world and this has led to growing interest in the domain of information systems (Bygstad, 2010; Dobson, 2002; Iannacci & Hatzaras, 2012; Smith, 2006; Volkoff, Strong, & Elmes, 2007). However, it is arguable that the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential perspective amongst these is the morphogenetic approach of Margaret Archer and hence the following discussion focuses on the resources this can supply us with. Archer is a social theorist and a sociologist of education who has developed over a series of books what she terms a morphogenetic approach to the study of society (Archer, 1995, 1996, 2000, 2003, 2007).

Building on notions of strati<sup>fi</sup>cation and emergence, Archer argues that all agencies takes place in conditions that predate action, forming logics that condition and shape what is possible. Time is therefore important in our analyses, as certain conditions are more enduring and resistant to change. Whilst her attention in the early part of her project was on formulating the relationship of agency to both social structures and culture, her more recent work has been concerned with elucidating the nature of agency. She has a particular focus on the nature of re<sup>fl</sup>exivity, arguing that this is the key mediating term between action and structures. This work was developed in part through a critique of the ideas of Giddens and his structuration theory. Structuration theory has been particularly in<sup>fl</sup>uential in the IS domain, informing, for example, the development of Orlikowski's (2000) practice-based perspective. For Archer, Giddens' formulation is guilty of what she terms ‘con<sup>fl</sup>ationism’, that is, of eliding the distinction between agency and structure. Because of his formulation of structures as ‘memory traces’, only instantiated in action, notions such as the inscription of structures into material artefact is problematic (Jones & Karsten, 2008). It might be seen that Giddens' con<sup>fl</sup>ation of agency and structure sits more comfortably with the ‘constitutive entanglement’ of sociomateriality, Archer's temporal separation of the two with the tradition of socio-technical studies.

For the purpose of the current discussion, the important point is that her formulations do not contain much discussion of the impact of technology, but drawing on the resources of critical realism more generally and engaging with the work produced by currents such as actor–network theory, we can suggest that material properties matter and that technology can be seen as inscribing certain aspects of social structure. In specifying the nature of these material properties, I (Mutch, 2010) have suggested that we need to pay attention to level and features. This enables us to be speci<sup>fi</sup>c about the materiality we are engaging with and to enable our approach to cover the full range of instantiations. Importantly, I suggest that different aspects of structure are inscribed, with more or less enduring impacts, at different levels. Drawing on the work of Bowker (2000) on the importance of data classi<sup>fi</sup>cations I suggest that the inscription of such classi<sup>fi</sup>cations into material form might be the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor, as with the inscription of ranking systems into the TripAdvisor algorithm.

Such a formulation draws upon earlier work in a broadly socio-technical vein which suggests that what is important is the constellation or arrangement of particular combinations of levels and features (Clark et al., 1988). We can term this ‘information architecture’, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the nature of much work in contemporary practice (Bygstad, 2010). What a morphogenetic approach can offer is a means of relating the formation and impacts of such architectural arrangements to the broader cultural and structural context. If we return to the questions we raised about the nature of the systems deployed in a call centre that we explored in the context of Nyberg's work we can see that an architectural approach would suggest that our research strategy needs to be broadened beyond the call centre operatives themselves. This is because the view of the customer that is presented to them is likely to be the consequence of a connected series of organizational actors and systems, not all of which are present at the immediate point of contact. The call itself, for example, is likely to have been routed through an automatic call distribution system. Such systems often handle a large number of virtual telephone numbers which are mapped onto a smaller number of physical telephone lines. At a point distant in both time and space from the call centre a decision will have been taken, perhaps in the marketing and sales function, to map a particular telephone number onto a particular offering or service, When called, opague to the customer, a particular routing is activated which in turn retrieves particular details from a database and a corresponding script to be presented to the operator. Thus, our research needs to address this collaborative network of systems and actors, in which some of our sources may not be human ones at all, but rather system generated logs of patterns which suggest key nodes and patterns for investigation.

In turn, such constellations need to be placed, as Pollock and Williams (2009) argue, in the context of broader considerations. The ability to use telephone numbers to route particular calls, for example, is not necessarily an obvious one and may be shaped by the networks in which some organizational actors are embedded. As Pollock and Williams point out, consultancies can be particularly important in<sup>fl</sup>uence mechanisms here. However, the implementation of such capabilities can also depend on the balance of power within organizations. The attractions of organizational innovations enabled by ICT are by no means either obvious to or welcomed by all organizational actors, as indicated by the differing responses to the use of analytics in organizations. The experience of the UK retailer, Tesco, indicates how the detailed analysis of data collected by customer loyalty cards was resisted by those operating with traditional models of intuitive decision-making (Humby, Hunt, & Phillips, 2003). What is important here is the power of some to make their classi<sup>fi</sup>cations stick, power which rests on both the position they occupy in the organization and the ways in which they perform those roles. What we lose in a strong sociomaterial approach is this ability to draw on concepts such as ‘roles’, which, as we have seen in Nyberg's account, cannot be separated out from practice

## 5. Conclusion

Whilst one can agree that there is a need to bring the material to the centre of organizational analysis, strong sociomateriality marks a wrong turning. If we trace the concept back to its origins, then we can see that the problems that are experienced with its application in the domain of information systems have their roots in the original formulations. There are implications associated with the use of the term, if we are to be true to its original conceptualisation, which might not be welcomed by those who are deploying the term to draw attention to the importance of the material. These implications rest at the heart of the concept and this article has sought to show some of those implications. At several levels we can note points at which sociomateriality is contestable. At the level of its origins in the study of the implications of quantum physics it is built on one possible interpretation of what remains a contested and paradoxical domain. That interpretation might require challenge on its own terms, but that is not a task for theorists of the social domain. However, it is important for such theorists to be aware of the status of the argument.

A further problem is that even if we bracket the debate about the ontological implications of quantum physics, then there is an illicit move in stretching the characteristics of the microphysical to the realm of the social. A description of the behaviour of particular properties of some objects may have validity at one level of analysis that doesn't apply at another level. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the blurring of boundaries that occur at the micro level does not invalidate tools of analysis such as the structure agency division that are operating at a more macro-level. As we move between the levels of analysis, then other problems with and implications of sociomateriality present themselves. The notion of human beings as a natural kind is resisted with a form of analysis which downplays what many may see as de<sup>fi</sup>ning characteristics of intentionality. Again, this is a matter of considerable debate, but the implications need to be recognised.

These are important implications which produce, it has been argued, some of the problems that we have encountered. The focus on practice means that our analyses cannot be sensitive to those features of situations which are part of the objective context of action, features which have emerged over time from the actions of others but which cannot be reduced to those actions. The tools of sociological analysis, such as the way in which Bourdieu (2004) uses notions of the <sup>fi</sup>eld and reputational capital to argue that objective knowledge is not a product of individual ethics but of the competitive struggle for reputation in a structured <sup>fi</sup>eld of activity, are no longer available to us. A consequence is that we become fully dependent on our informants to tell us about material arrangements, meaning that, counter to our ambitions, the material aspects are strangely underspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed.

The resources of critical realism are an alternative means of drawing our attention to the importance of the relationship between the social and the material and of exploring that relationship. Holding to an ontology of properties and powers, critical realism suggests that we live in a strati<sup>fi</sup>ed world, where what is real at a particular level is the product of tendencies which it is the task of the analyst to explore, always with the imperfect and provisional tools to hand. One of those tools is the morphogenetic approach to the study of the relationship between structure and agency. To this framework we can add the notion of technology as inscribed structure. However, these insights need to be combined with the resources supplied by existing perspectives. There can be value, that is, in refreshing existing perspectives rather than seeking new ones (Robey et al., 2012) One such perspective is the tradition of socio-technical systems (Leonardi, 2012). This has become associated with technological determinism, often in ways which are a caricature of the original approaches. The formative work of Trist and his collaborators, for example, shows a sensitivity to both local roles and broader social and cultural structures (Trist, 1963). We now have a broader understanding of the material and our concepts and language for dealing with the social have been re<sup>fi</sup>ned over time, but one suggestion of this article is that there is considerable value in adopting a non-con<sup>fl</sup>ationary approach at the level of the perspective, informed by the philosophical resources of critical realism, to the novel phenomena represented by socio-material systems.
