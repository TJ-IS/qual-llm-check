# Applying a critical approach to investigate barriers to digital inclusion and online social networking among young people with disabilities

Lareen Newman,\*<sup>,</sup>\*\* Kathryn Browne-Yung,\* Parimala Raghavendra,<sup>†</sup> Denise Wood<sup>‡</sup> & Emma Grace<sup>†</sup>

\*Southgate Institute for Health, Society and Equity, Flinders University, Level 2 Health Sciences Building, GPO Box 2100, Adelaide, SA 5001, Australia, email: lareen. newman@unisa.edu.au, <sup>†</sup>Disability & Community Inclusion Unit, Flinders University, GPO Box 2100, Adelaide, SA 5001, Australia, email: parimala.raghavendra@<sup>fl</sup>inders.edu.au, <sup>‡</sup>Central Queensland University, 44 Greenhill Road, Wayville SA5034, Australia, email: d. wood@cqu.edu.au, and

Abstract. Despite the seeming ubiquity of young people’s Internet use, there are still many for whom access to the Internet and online social networking remains inequitable and patterned by disadvantage. The connection between information technology and young people with disabilities is particularly under-researched. This article contributes to the <sup>fi</sup>eld of critical information systems research by exposing signi<sup>fi</sup>cant barriers and facilitators to Internet accessibility for young people with disabilities. It uses Bourdieu’s critical theory to explore how the unequal distribution of resources shapes processes of digital inclusion for young people with disabilities. It highlights access needs and experiences that are both disability and non-disability related. The article draws on interviews in South Australia with 18 young people aged 10–18 years with a physical disability (such as cerebral palsy) or acquired brain injury and with 17 of their family members. Interviews evaluated participants’ and parents’ re<sup>fl</sup>ections on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of a home-based, goaloriented intervention to increase the young person’s Internet use for social participation purposes. The Bourdieuian analysis demonstrated how varying levels of accrued individual and family of<sup>fl</sup>ine capital resources are related to digital/online resources and disability-speci<sup>fi</sup>c online resources. This revealed how unequal resources of capital can in<sup>fl</sup>uence technology use and hence digital inclusion for young people with disabilities. Our study demonstrates that young people with particular types of disabilities require intensive, personalised and long-term support from within and beyond the family to ‘get online’. We conclude that Internet studies need to more frequently adopt critical approaches to investigate the needs of users and barriers to information technology use within sub-groups, such as young people with disabilities. © 2016 Blackwell Publishing Ltd

## INTRODUCTION

The Internet’s potential reach is everyone in a population, yet there is less research about what proportion of any given population is willing and able to get online (Bennett & Glasgow, 2009). Internet access and use among minority groups such as young people with disabilities are a particularly under-researched area. In general, data show that higher proportions of certain groups are less likely to be online, and demographic and geographic factors continue to shape inequalities (National Telecommunications & Information Administration (NTIA), 2013). In the USA, only 37% of households use the Internet where they are headed by someone with less than a high school diploma, compared with 90% of households headed by someone with a college degree. Looking by ethnicity, 83% of Asian-American households use the Internet compared with 76% of White households, and only 57% of African-American households (NTIA, 2013). In Australia, 86% of households have Internet access, but this is lower for those who have only completed high school or a vocational quali<sup>fi</sup>cation (73%) and for low-income households (67%) (Ewing & Thomas, 2012). Key factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing other groups not being online, such as older people and those in rural areas, also include lower education level, lower income, poor English pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency and single-person household arrangements (Dane et al., 2013; Goodall et al., 2014). A recent report by two well-known non-government organisations notes that Internet access is a key component of the ‘bundle’ of factors that constitute multiple disad vantage – other factors include disability support, low Year 9 reading levels and lack of social engagement as young adults (Vinson & Rawsthorne, 2015). In the USA, households headed by a person with a disability are less likely to use the Internet (48%) than households headed by a person without a disability (76%) (NTIA, 2013). The once popular rhetoric that all young people are innate users of digital technologies (as espoused, e.g. by Prensky, 2001; Tapscott, 2008) has failed to account for the considerable inequalities that exist between different youth populations in terms of access to IT (information technology) and digital literacy. The data show that not all young people are online. Some face a range of barriers to Internet access, while others, once online, face other barriers to more complex, frequent or creative use (Dobransky & Hargittai, 2006; Helsper, 2012a; Raghavendra et al., 2012, 2013a; Smith et al., 2013; Correa, 2015). A recent national UK survey, for example, found that 9% of people aged 16–24 years still do not use the Internet for social networking and 32% do not use it for playing or downloading games (Of<sup>fi</sup>ce for National Statistics, 2014).

In the context of these broader socioeconomic inequalities, this article makes an important contribution to the literature by conceptualising digital inclusion for young people with a disabil ity through a critical Bourdieuian approach. This highlights how digital exclusion may be mate rially experienced by individuals. The digital inclusion discourse tends to either ignore disability as a separate category or place it in a category together with other disadvantaged groups, which may obscure the speci<sup>fi</sup>c Internet needs of those with disabilities (Adam & Kreps, 2009:1045). This also includes the needs of subgroups within the overall disability group, such as young people.

In this paper, we highlight the Internet and technology access and use experiences of a group of 18 young people with physical disabilities, including cerebral palsy, or acquired brain injury. They were initially recruited via an invitation to participate in home-based training in the use of computing devices and the Internet for online social networking in order to increase their so cial participation. The paper thereby seeks to address the challenge set by Selwyn (2004:352) to understand differential levels of engagement with the Internet by distinguishing between the different mediating forms of Bourdieu’s capitals and the resources they generate that intersect at the individual level of experiencing IT use. The following questions guide this paper:

1 What are the barriers and facilitators to IT engagement experienced by this group of young people with physical disabilities or acquired brain injury?

2 How does the level of a young person’s of<sup>fl</sup>ine individual and familial resources of economic, cultural and social capital impact on their ability to access and use the Internet for online social networking?

## BACKGROUND

To better understand IT use within particular sub-groups, it is useful to <sup>fi</sup>rst consider the literature on IT use among young people in general and then speci<sup>fi</sup>cally among young people with various disabilities. The degree of research focus on the former group is, so far, not mirrored by an equal level of attention on the latter group, highlighting the need for the empirical research presented in this paper.

## Young people and digital technology use

The amount of research on IT use among children and young people has grown dramatically in the last decade (Livingstone & Bulger, 2014). However, there is little research addressing different levels of young people’s Internet use (on computers, mobile phones or other devices) despite a comprehensive body of research on their general IT use (Livingstone & Helsper, 2007; Soderstrom, 2009, 2013; Helsper, 2012a; Wood et al., 2010). In addition, inequalities in Internet use are now being linked to offline social inegualities: for example. people with disabilities who are not online are less well educated and more <sup>fi</sup>nancially disadvantaged than those who are online (Dobransky & Hargittai, 2006). Selwyn (2004) argues that young people’s digital engagement is often ‘unspectacular’, with a ‘misplaced technological and biological determinism’ underpinning current portrayals of young people as innate talented users of digital technologies. Some studies conclude that breadth of IT use, experience, gender and education levels can be more important than generational differences (Helsper & Eynon, 2009; Golder et al., 2010; Newman et al., 2010). Studies of Internet use have also moved from describing static characteristics of the ‘digital divide’ to investigating processes of digital inclusion, i.e. what it is about how people live or the resources that they have that leads to them being online or not (Parsons & Hick, 2008).

Helsper (2011) argues that if digital inclusion is linked to of<sup>fl</sup>ine social inequalities, then a digital underclass will persist even when young people grow up. This is possible because those without the necessary of<sup>fl</sup>ine resources may never get online, or may only ever get online at a basic level or a level below that of the majority of others. Inequalities exist both in online activities and device ownership. A recent UK survey found that only 61% of children aged 5–10 years report being able to use the Internet without assistance and, of those aged 11–15 years, only 70% claim they can do all the tasks they want online (Ipsos MediaCT, 2012). The easiest tasks for 11 to 14-year-olds were using social networking sites, sending/receiving email and watching online video. The easiest tasks for 5- to 10-year-olds were playing website games, with ‘watching videos’ and ‘using social networking’ being somewhat easy to do alone. By comparison, sending/receiving emails and making online calls (e.g. via Skype) were regarded as considerably harder. For support, 53% of children aged 11–14 years said they would ask family or friends to teach them a new online skill, and 33% said they would learn by trial and error. The ‘basic skills’ for Internet use were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as communicating (sending/receiving emails), <sup>fi</sup>nding things (Internet browsing/using a search engine), sharing personal information (e.g. registering on a social website and making a job application) and staying safe (e.g. identifying spam and setting privacy settings) (Go-On UK, 2013).

There are also differences in the types of device that young people use for Internet access. These are important because physical and cognitive abilities in<sup>fl</sup>uence IT consumption at the individual level (Kvasny & Keil, 2006). Internet access using a mobile phone is increasing for all ages: in the UK. it more than doubled between 2010 and 2014. from 24% to 58% (ONS 2014), and in Australia sat at 37% in 2011 (Ewing & Thomas, 2012). In the USA, 85% of mobile phone users under age 30 years use their phones to send text messages, and 57% to browse the Web (NTIA 2014). Among children aged 9–16 years in Australia, only 14% go online via their mobile phone, but 46% go online via another handheld device such as an iPod Touch, iPhone or Blackberry; in the European Union (EU), 22% go online via their mobile phone and 12% on a handheld device (Green et al., 2013). Nevertheless, recent research suggests that a ‘mobile Internet underclass’ may be emerging who are limited by functionality and content availability, which does not suit their needs (Napoli & Obar, 2014).

## Disability and digital technology use

While there has been a considerable amount of research published on IT use in the general population and for certain sub-groups, less is known about ITuse among people with disabilities and those at younger ages in this group. We also know little about how their IT use may in turn support increased social participation. We use the International Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of Functioning, Disability and Health (World Health Organization, 2001) de<sup>fi</sup>nition of disability as the interaction between a person, his or her functional ability and the environment. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition includes the importance of a person’s communication and information environment, together with the physical, social and policy environment, which are relevant to this study. However, studies of digital technology use vary in their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of disability. Two major quantitative studies in the EU and

USA provide their own de<sup>fi</sup>nition of disability broadly as ‘any long-standing illness, disability or in<sup>fi</sup>rmity that limits activities in any way’ (Vicente & Lopez, 2010). Other studies have explored IT use among people with a wide range of speci<sup>fi</sup>ed disabilities, including those who have hear ing and/or visual impairments, have dif<sup>fi</sup>culty typing or have mobility limitations (Dobransky & Hargittai, 2006).

For a long time, disability was rarely considered in studies of Internet use and the digital divide (Adam & Kreps, 2006). Today, when investigating disability and Internet use, much of the data are provided for adults rather than separately for young people. The European E-User study, for example, had 9807 respondents across 10 member states and found signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower Internet use among adults with a disability than the non-disabled population (Vicente & Lopez, 2010). A recent analysis in Nigeria, using national census data, also shows within-group differences: Those classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as ‘blind’, ‘autistic’, ‘mentally disabled’ or with ‘hearing dif<sup>fi</sup>culties had worse access to computers, Internet and/or mobile phones than people with other disabil ities (Indongo & Mufune, 2015). There is also less information about some groups. For example, little evaluation exists about social media use among those who develop communication disabilities after an acquired brain injury and how this might support their social participation (Brunner et al., 2015). Nevertheless, one recent study has looked in depth at Twitter use among adults with severe communication disabilities who use assistive technologies (AT) (Hemsley et al., 2015). Pinchevksi and Peters’ (2015: 2) work proposes that for people with autistic spectrum disorders, the Internet provides ‘an alternate mode of texting, typing and mediated talk, [which is] stripped of both verbal and non-verbal complexities’. Nevertheless, while adults with disability often focus their Internet use on disability-related activities (Seymour & Lupton, 2004), young people with disabilities use the Internet differently, suggesting that age-speci<sup>fi</sup>c disability IT studies are needed (Soderstrom, 2009).

Australia has approximately 41 000 children aged 10–18 years with a disability or who have a need for assistance in core activities (Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) 2006). Among 15- to 24-year-old Australians with a disability, Internet use at least once in the 12 months prior to survey was only slightly lower than for all Australians of this age (90% compared with 92%) (ABS 2012). However, use in this age group differed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly by severity of disability, with only 59% of those with a profound disability recording Internet use, compared with 83% with a severe disability, 85% with a mild disability and 90% with moderate disability (ABS 2012). Most young US adults with disabilities use electronic communication such as email, but although 32% use it daily, 46% use it less than once a week, and use also differs signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly by disability type (Newman et al., 2011). For example, hearing-impaired young people use the Internet more than their hearing peers and more than those with other types of disability (Barak & Sadovsky, 2008; Newman et al., 2011). A comparative study of Internet use among Dutch teenagers with and without physical disabilities such as cerebral palsy or traumatic brain injury and who were living in residential care found that both groups had high levels of Internet access (Lathouwers et al., 2009). This study also found that online activities were similar for both groups, yet parents of the teens with a disability were more concerned about Internet safety (Lathouwers et al., 2009). However, a British study of 25 adolescents and young people with communication disabilities found that Internet and social media use were limited more by literacy skills and the availability of AT (Hynan et al., 2014).

There are few studies on Internet use or online activities that speci<sup>fi</sup>cally identify the factors determining choice of device for young people with disabilities (e.g. smartphone vs. standard mobile phone and tablets and other handheld or laptop devices). By comparison, there are studies about use among adults with disabilities (see, e.g., Fox, 2011). Hewett et al.’s (2014) pilot study with 12 partially sighted adults found that iPads provided some improved technical accessibility over a standard computer, although some aspects undermined access for those with more severe sight impairments. One Japanese survey found high mobile phone use among visu ally impaired teens (Watanabe et al., 2008). Napoli and Obar’s (2014) critical analysis of mobile vs. PC-based Internet access concludes that mobile Internet access may be a cheaper solution but may not be useful for some individuals; the main barriers were the limitations of lower levels of functionality and content availability relative to needs. Nevertheless, compared with studies about Internet use and device choice among young people in general, the ways in which young people with disabilities use various devices remain largely underexplored (Tsaliki & Kontogianni, 2014). In particular, there is only a small body of research examining the social media use of young people with communication disabilities (Brunner et al., 2015).

## BOURDIEU’S THEORETICAL APPROACH

In social science research, a range of epistemological positions falls under the ‘umbrella’ of the critical approach (or critical information systems – critical IS – research). Although these draw upon a variety of social theories, three major critical research streams have emerged as foundations for Critical IS research based on the work of Foucault, Habermas and Bourdieu (Howcroft & Trauth. 2005: Mvers & Klein. 2011). Encouraging a breadth of critical approaches is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial in strengthening the <sup>fi</sup>eld of critical IS research and in connecting theory and practice (McGrath, 2005). Information systems (IS) research that consciously adopts a critical perspective emerged in the early 1990s and expanded further from the early 2000s (Howcroft & Trauth 2005; Richardson & Robinson, 2007). Critical theory perspectives encourage inquiry into both understanding what drives the unequal distribution of resources across a population and use of that understanding to bring about change (Crotty, 1998; Baum, 2008). This is important because this unequal distribution shapes opportunities for well-being and hence contributes to inequalities in a variety of socioeconomic status positions and outcomes (Crotty, 1998; Baum, 2008). Because these ‘inequalities’ are not a given but are structurally determined, they are unfair, avoidable and remediable (Dahlgren & Whitehead 1991). This means that research can be motivated by an intention to change social realities and promote emancipation, which introduces a moral and ethical dimension (Stahl, 2008). Critical IS research is therefore concerned with social issues such as freedom, power, social control and values with respect to the development, use and impact of information technology and can help challenge prevailing assumptions (Myers & Klein, 2011). Recently, Walsham (2012) has argued the need to advance an IS research agenda that emphasises the focus on ethical goals and increases the use of critical approaches.

Bourdieu’s critical theory is concerned with understanding how inequalities in society are created and maintained (Bourdieu, 1977). It is a useful response to Walsham’s (2005) call fo an explicit critical examination of IT use. A Bourdieuian approach allows us to consider how the unequal distribution of resources may shape processes of digital inclusion for young people with disabilities. Bourdieu’s approach introduces the conceptual tools of economic, social and cultural capitals and social <sup>fi</sup>elds to help explain an individual’s social position and the social processes that reproduce and maintain societal inequalities (Bourdieu, 1977, 1986). Economic capital refers to wealth and assets while social capital refers to resources generated through group membership, which can be positive or negative (Portes, 2000). Cultural capital takes three forms: institutionalised, through educational quali<sup>fi</sup>cations; objecti<sup>fi</sup>ed, through an appreciation of material goods; and embodied, through dispositions and practices (Bourdieu, 1977). Overall, cultural capital refers to the collective value of adaptive knowledge, skills, competencies and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of family background and investment in education that may shape success (Kvasny & Keil, 2006). Bourdieu viewed all social processes as operating within a network of social <sup>fi</sup>elds. Within each <sup>fi</sup>eld, a dominant form of capital based on the values and norms of that <sup>fi</sup>eld prevails, shaping relations and practices (Bourdieu, 1977). An individual’s resource of the capital dominant in a particular <sup>fi</sup>eld determines that person’s social position in that <sup>fi</sup>eld. Of relevance to this study is the way that embodied cultural capital focuses on how values and norms are embedded in everyday practice in an individual’s social, physical and personal environment; for example, tastes, dispositions and symbolic value obtain their meaning only in relation to others (Bourdieu, 1984).

Exploring how capital resources are distributed can reveal non-economic hierarchies and power inequalities, which are often obscured (Bourdieu, 1984). According to Bourdieu, all forms of capital are relational, although economic capital is most readily convertible and transformed into cultural or social capital. For example, high levels of economic capital can be invested in education to produce cultural capital that can enhance job prospects and provide opportunities for greater social capital in one’s social networks. As networks become broader and more conducive to opportunity, they further enhance other capital stocks and thus reproduce socia position (Edgerton & Roberts, 2014: 195).

Researchers including Selwyn (2004) and Warschauer et al. (2004) have argued for the need to recognise the mediating role of economic, cultural and social resources (capitals) in individ ual IT access and use. Selwyn (2004:353) applied Bourdieu’s approach to recognise the medi ating role of unequal capital resources in IT use, noting that ‘what an individual (or group of individuals) can do with IT is intertwined with their corresponding levels of cultural capital'. He also suggests (2004) that there are ‘speci<sup>fi</sup>c technological forms of capital that are useful to the information age’ and which make the difference between having technology access or ownership and making meaningful use of it. Selwyn (2004:354) points out that such factors have led some authors to identify ‘the fundamental importance of technological capital as a subset of, and an addition to, Bourdieu’s cultural, economic and social forms of capital in the information age’; individuals and communities can command these capitals when engaging with technology. Helsper (2012b) theorises the need to compare the impacts on IT use of, for example, ‘of<sup>fl</sup>ine economic resources’ and ‘digital economic resources’.

A previous study using Bourdieu’s theories to explore IT engagement for disadvantaged adults revealed how unequal distributions of capital resources can shape digital access and limit potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of IT use, thereby reinforcing and amplifying existing disadvantage (Baum et al., 2014). Such applications of Bourdieu’s theory highlight societal digital exclusion

However, they do not address the social contexts, speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT preferences or daily practices where physical and cognitive abilities in<sup>fl</sup>uence individual-level IT use (Kvasny & Keil, 2006: 32). This is of particular relevance for young people with disabilities.

The emerging literature on digital inclusion emphasises the need to look beyond individuals to investigate how the social context and daily practices of speci<sup>fi</sup>c groups shape resources fo Internet use (Baum, Newman & Biedrzycki 2014; Geniets & Eynon, 2011). Previous qualitative research with adults from lower socioeconomic and non-English speaking backgrounds has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed barriers to Internet use beyond low income and poor literacy to include lack of social and family resources for IT use (Newman et al., 2010; Goodall et al., 2014). Providing IT hard ware in schools is often seen as an equaliser to socioeconomic barriers to IT use, but home IT use is often impacted by broader family factors (Warschauer et al., 2004).

Most services available on networked computers have been designed for the able bodied (Adam & Kreps, 2009). For adults with a disability, the relatively high cost of AT can discourage Internet use (Vicente & Lopez, 2010). Many young adults with cerebral palsy have described how slow text input and poor interface features of AT act as barriers to Internet use (Lewis, 2010). However, students with a physical disability can experience restricted participation in computer-based educational activities regardless of whether they use AT (Lidström et al., 2012).

## METHODS

This case study analyses interviews with young people with disabilities and their parents following participation in a tailored one-on-one home-based intervention to support the young person’s increased Internet use for social purposes. The study re<sup>fl</sup>ects the set of principles proposed by Myers and Klein (2011) for conducting critical IS research as follows. Firstly, data collection and analysis should be organised around core concepts from one or more critical theorists (in this case Bourdieu’s theories). Secondly, values such as equal opportunity should be advocated – we do this through investigating the needs of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c group of young people to identify what it takes for them to have equal opportunity to get online. Thirdly, beliefs and social practices should be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and challenged with potentially con<sup>fl</sup>icting arguments and evidence, which we do by identifying barriers to this group getting online (whether disability related or not). Finally, research should suggest how to improve society for these purposes, which we address in the Discussion section. These principles are important because they enable us to challenge prevailing assumptions about high levels of Internet use across the youth population.

The paper applies Bourdieu’s concepts to identify ways in which Internet use by young people with a disability is shaped by personal and familial resources of capitals. These resources and social processes are often hidden ‘below the iceberg’ (Newman & Freeman, 2011). This means that we see quantitative data patterns in access, ownership and usage, while ‘the deeper cultural causes behind this inequality (in IT use) have rarely been described’ (Tondeur et al., 2010: 163). Bourdieu’s approach calls attention to the often ‘taken-for-granted’ ways that dominant values and ways of seeing the world are accepted as the Doxa or ‘natural order of things’, which helps reproduce inequalities (Bourdieu, 1977). Applying this notion to how technology is designed and created against a background of taken-for-granted ablebodiedness reveals how adaptive technologies are often an additional prerequisite for those with disabilities to gain accessibility (Adam & Kreps, 2009). Taking this critical approach in data analysis also offers a way to turn ‘the spotlight’ onto the issue of Web (in)accessibility for people with a disability (Adam & Kreps, 2006). Qualitative research methods can particularly provide insights into these aspects of IT use in individual lives (Bennett & Maton, 2010: 320). They can then inform more nuanced strategies to promote digital inclusion. The following sections describe the study methods in detail.

## Sample recruitment

Invitations were sent to 123 eligible clients of an organisation providing state-wide services in South Australia to children with physical disabilities from birth to age 18. The invitation was di rected to parents for their child to participate in an intervention that would provide home-based training in the use of computing devices, Internet use and online social networking sites. It was explained that the intention was for the child to begin participating, or to participate more, in online social networking as a way to increase their level of social participation. Twenty-two clients consented while four subsequently withdrew, resulting in 18 young people with cerebra palsy or acquired brain injury participating in the study. The participants were 12 boys and 6 girls aged 10–18 years (mean age 13.7 years) (15% of the 123 invited).

Most of the 18 participants had mild to moderate physical disabilities and good communication abilities. However, <sup>fi</sup>ve with severe communication disabilities had mild to severe physica disabilities. The study was approved by the Children, Youth & Women’s Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee (South Australia). Parents were provided with a project information sheet and consent form, and parents gave consent to the child’s participation as their legal guardian. Consent was also explained verbally to the young people, who assented to participation in the study before the intervention commenced.

## The intervention

The home-based intervention was conducted between March 2011 and June 2012. A research of<sup>fi</sup>cer worked with participants to identify what they would like to start doing on the Internet or what problems they faced with current use. Using this information, the research of<sup>fi</sup>cer helped participants to set their own goals, with the focus on increasing social participation. The goals which participants set mainly related to the following key activities:

• Writing/sending/receiving emails to interact with friends and family.

• Using Skype (Internet video calls) Livewire or Facebook to interact with friends and family.

• Sharing links to, and watching, YouTube videos as a way to share common interests and to interact with friends and family.

• Increased independence in reading and writing online and capacity to interact with others online in these ways.

• Learning to be safe online and increasing awareness and sense of responsibility for personal safety when interacting with others online.

Some participants had goals for online gaming using a PC or gaming over the Internet using a console or virtual worlds: One participant who later withdrew from the intervention used Club Penguin (a Disney online virtual world for children using cartoon penguin avatars), and one participant used Habbo Hotel (an online virtual world/game aimed at teenagers) and Second Life (a 3D virtual world). However, subsequently, this latter participant decided that he did not like these worlds. Snapchat did not exist at the time of our study and, because Instagram was only new, our participants mostly did not have devices that would install these apps and so would have had to use the online versions. To comply with usage requirements, Facebook was only permitted as a goal choice for participants aged 13 years or over, and if their parent consented. One UK study found that parents often assist their children to circumvent such age restrictions because they want the children to be able to access educational and communication sites that the children want as part of their online experiences (Boyd et al., 2011).

Participants and or parents sometimes felt it was not possible for the participant to use the Internet for social networking in any form. In such cases, the researcher tried to promote online games or Facebook (if age-appropriate). Nevertheless, options such as Skype and email were often more agreeable goals that families felt held less risk than a more networked platform. In one case, the young person really wanted Facebook, but the parents felt she would embarrass herself by sending inappropriate messages, given her limited literacy skills and other dif<sup>fi</sup>culties.

Goal attainment was variously successful: 70% of goals were achieved (35/50), with seven achieved at the expected level and 28 above the expected level; all participants achieved at least one goal (Raghavendra et al., 2013a). Parents were also included in the training. Each participant received a mean of 11.3 visits over an average 7-month period, with each visit lasting an average of 75 min. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant time was spent preparing and developing visual resources, troubleshooting and integrating complex speech-generating devices with the Internet, and travelling to participants’ homes. The research of<sup>fi</sup>cer also spent considerable time liaising with AT suppliers, online forums and IT specialists to resolve technical issues on participants’ behalf.

## Qualitative interviews

Between 4 and 14 days after the intervention’s completion, the 18 participants and the parents of 16 of them were interviewed about their participation in the project. For one participant, an older sister had provided computer support prior to and during the project and, hence, she was interviewed instead of the parents; another participant lived away from home and no family was interviewed. A pre-set interview schedule was used to direct the questioning. This allowed room for interviewees to provide additional information that they felt was relevant to mention. In line with the original study design, questions were formulated to generally explore views about participating in the project. Identifying the impacts of relational resources was not an aim of the investigation, and these resources were only identi<sup>fi</sup>ed retrospectively during analysis as hav ing impacted on technology use. Hence, the interviews were subsequently reanalysed for this paper using a critical Bourdieuian approach (as explained in the subsequent text).

The young people’s interviews were generally short, averaging 11 min, but they ranged from 5 to 30 min, and conversation was not complex. This was in some cases disability related; fo example, those using speech-generating devices spoke fewer words. However, it may also be a re<sup>fl</sup>ection of their age, because Newman et al. (2009: 147) also reported short and noncomplex conversations with a group of children of similar age (8–18 years) without disabilities in an earlier study. The parent interviews lasted an average of 13 min and ranged from 6 to 34 min. These were shorter than expected for individual adult interviews for several reasons Firstly, the researcher had come to know the parents and participants well during the project (visiting each family an average of 11 times over 7 months), so rapport was already established and time was not needed for this at the interview’s commencement. Secondly, this familiarity meant that parents did not need to provide background information about their child’s disability or goals, which is necessary with a new interviewer. Finally, some parents’ answers were brief and others detailed; the number of goals varied for the participants and parents commented on each goal, resulting in some having longer interviews.

## Data analysis

Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim by a professional transcriber. Data were thematically coded, drawing on critical realist approaches that acknowledge the ways in which individuals make meaning of their experiences (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Of the paper’s authors, the second author (KBY) conducted the initial open coding, and the <sup>fi</sup>rst author (LN) conducted the cross-check. The data were interrogated using Bourdieu’s relational concepts to identify levels of capital resources and how they shaped experiences within social <sup>fi</sup>elds. Interviews were analysed to identify challenges to Internet use by applying framework analysis (Ritchie & Spencer, 1994), i.e. through reading and re-reading transcripts, we explored how levels of resources, physical limitations and everyday contexts in<sup>fl</sup>uenced IT use. We then allocated text to coding categories of economic, social and cultural capital. These were subdivided by whether the capital was accumulated as basic of<sup>fl</sup>ine capital (e.g. family income), or was related to getting online, i.e. ‘digital capitals’ (e.g. suf<sup>fi</sup>cient disposable income to purchase IT). During the coding, we also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed text that <sup>fi</sup>tted an emergent sub-category of ‘disability-speci<sup>fi</sup>c digital capital’. The content of each code was developed recursively to identify and gather all the data relevant to it. Codes were then sorted by creating charts and mapping responses. Data were allocated to the digital capital codes based on the concepts developed by Selwyn (2004), Seale (2013) and Helsper (2012b), as shown in Table 1.

The open coding process and recursive development of codes also supported identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the emergent theme of Disability-Speci<sup>fi</sup>c Digital Capital.

## Social capital and social digital capita

As previously mentioned, social capital refers to the resources accrued from membership of networks or groups (Bourdieu, 1977). Bonding networks comprise close ties with family and friends and are often referred to as generating ‘getting by’ resources (Putnam, 1993). Most of the children’s social networks in our study were bonding, consisting of family and a small number of friends who were demographically similar; much of their socialising with friends was performed at school. The small network groups were re<sup>fl</sup>ected in similarly low numbers of social digital networks. However, these low levels of social digital networks may constitute one reason why families chose to participate in the project. Most participants relied on family know-how and social support for assistance in IT use, which highlights how economic digital capital alone is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for digital engagement. It also shows that children and young people with disabilities may be more dependent than so-called able-bodied peers on family assistance to get online for prolonged periods during childhood. Most of the children in our study required assistance in daily living domains, and this extended to their digital engagement.

Most Internet use during the study maintained and reinforced existing of<sup>fl</sup>ine social networks. This provided an additional cyber social space to consolidate friendships for children who have limited opportunities for social engagement outside the school environment. Participants commented, for example: ‘I can keep talking to them [school friends] after school’ and ‘Because now I’ve got Facebook I can talk to my sisters [who live elsewhere] and I could never do that before’.

For many of the young people, their level of of<sup>fl</sup>ine social interaction skills determined their level of engagement in online social networks, which was mediated by their cognitive and literary skills (institutional cultural capital) and in many cases their physical limitations. The following excerpt from Sophie’s mother highlights the relational nature of capital resources and how they link with cultural digital capital, as well as where support can enhance interaction:

‘[Sophie] probably could spend a bit more time on Livewire, I think, and I think she probably needs to respond to what other people are saying on Livewire. She’s getting quite good at telling them about herself, I think, but maybe more practice at responding to what other people are doing’. (Mother of Sophie, age 11 years 2 months).

A few families mentioned social digital capital resources in the form of contacts (e.g. a sibling, uncle or family friend) who could provide practical computer advice if problems arose, who could lend them IT equipment or help them get onto the Internet. Bridging social capital is generated through diverse networks of people who are different across demographic factors and can create more resources for ‘getting on’ in life (Putnam, 1993). These networks include peer mentors to help further develop their IT abilities and their online social networks.

Arguably, all the families had bridging social capital resources through their study participation as clients of a major disability support organisation. This enabled them to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the research of<sup>fi</sup>cer’s combined disability and technical expertise that provided a form of cultural digital capital that they otherwise would not have available. It also provided disabilityspeci<sup>fi</sup>c digital capital in the form of the research of<sup>fi</sup>cer identifying, and where necessary obtaining, equipment to meet the child’s and family’s IT and AT-for-IT requirements. Other social digital capital bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of study participation for the families included information and advocacy via the research of<sup>fi</sup>cer who liaised with AT companies and IT helpdesk experts to purchase equipment on the families’ behalf. The research of<sup>fi</sup>cer also linked the equip ment to the participant’s home computer and instructed them in its use. Overwhelmingly, opportunities to access diverse networks were curtailed by families’ existing knowledge and the young people’s cultural capital resources, such as their limited conversation and communication skills.

## Cultural capital and cultural digital capital

## The parents and family context

As already noted, parental cultural capital shapes children’s tastes and attitudes through famil socialisation (Bourdieu, 1977). However, in terms of cultural digital capital, most study parents were not con<sup>fi</sup>dent ITusers. Many were unaware of how to access programmes and were often unacquainted with the available online choices for children and young people. One mother’s response is fairly typical and highlighted the desire for support:

‘I think there were so many things to do with the Internet that I don’t know that it would have been good to have… maybe a few more visits [from the project] so that as Sophie came across things that she didn’t know about she could have asked somebody that does know. It’s one area that I can’t help her with at all because I’ve got absolutely no idea. In fact, she will be teaching me about blogging and sites like Livewire and that sort of thing… I just wasn’t brought up in the technology era’. (Mother of Sophie, age 11 years 2 months)

Parental levels of cultural digital capital may be more in<sup>fl</sup>uential for those children with disabilities who are more dependent on family members to establish and maintain their digita engagement. As another mother said:

‘It has been important for me to be part of the learning [in this project]… because he’s not had the motivation to want to learn about it. So I’ve had to learn about it to then be able to encour age him and help him practice and explain to him why it would be even a good thing to know about’. (Mother of Jeremy, age 17 years 4 months)

We noted previously that parental motivation for participating in the intervention may have been a desire to increase their own cultural digital capital or their child’s social digital capital. This may also be converted into of<sup>fl</sup>ine social capital through strengthened relationships with family, school and broader community members. The home-based intervention provided an opportunity for parents to learn with their child to develop cultural digital capital resources together, as Jeremy’s mother pointed out: ‘It was a good opportunity to learn about some things that we didn’t know anything about – the blogging, the Skyping, that sort of thing’.

It could be argued that a degree of disability-speci<sup>fi</sup>c cultural digital capital was also demonstrated. This means that young people with a disability cannot get online just by having the same of<sup>fl</sup>ine resources as their so-called able-bodied peers but require some group-speci<sup>fi</sup>c additional resources (AT-for-IT is one example). Furthermore, at the intervention’s commencement, we found that most parents had limited awareness of the ATavailable to help their child to get online or to support them while they were online. An example is the use of speech recognition software to reduce frustration with using a keyboard, or software that supports online reading and writing. During early home visits, the research of<sup>fi</sup>cer assessed each participant’s AT needs and recommended and purchased items such as adaptive mouses, AT software and hardware that support wireless Internet access for disability communication devices. Some participants required basic support, such as to be shown how to use a prepaid mobile Internet connection, Web camera, iPad apps or to instal browsers or Skype. Others needed help to set up desktop shortcuts o to set up online accounts such as for email. So although parents may be successfully dealing with ‘disability of<sup>fl</sup>ine’ for their children, in knowing about and accessing disability support, it appeared that this had not translated into similar levels of disability-related digital cultural capital.

This boosted self-con<sup>fi</sup>dence and their embodied cultural capital, as illustrated by 16-year-old Ashleigh’s experience in receiving an iPad through the project: ‘[The iPad] helped me socialise more with people. The parent interviews revealed pre-existing low levels of cultural capital resources among the children. One mother noted that her son (age 10.5 years) required considerable assistance creating sentences because of his poor basic literacy skills, and she was unsure if he understood the concept of email. On the othe hand, the sister of Dan (age 13 years) indicated that being supported to build up his cultural digita capital had resulted in his regular ITengagement. These are tasks which to others may seem simple and, for similar aged children (11–14 years) in the UK, were reported among the easiest to do without assistance (Ipsos MediaCT, 2012). ## DISCUSSION

This paper was guided by research questions related to barriers and facilitators to ITuse among young people with physical disabilities or acquired brain injury. It also investigated how their IT access and use are impacted by their of<sup>fl</sup>ine familial resources of economic, cultural and social capitals. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings augment the scant literature on IT use among young people with disabilities by providing qualitative insights into often-hidden barriers and facilitators that affect Internet use in one particular sub-group of young people. The study provides a nuanced understanding of how to increase digital inclusion in this sub-group. It goes beyond description of their Internet use patterns to apply and analyse the impact of an intensive home-based intervention and the different levels of resources required to make this successful. Against the context of the avail able literature on IT use among young people with a disability, our study provides a new contribution by making visible the wide range and types of resources required to support this group to get online or increase their IT use for social purposes.

The paper is contextualised within the <sup>fi</sup>eld of critical IS studies. This aims to understand the unequal distribution of resources across a population and how this distribution contributes to inequalities in outcomes. Our approach, drawing on Bourdieu’s theories of capital, has allowed us to identify how young people with disabilities differ from their peers who do not have disabilities in the nature and extent of Internet use. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that they face many barriers and facilita tors: varying functional abilities in areas of physical, cognitive and literacy skills and limited of<sup>fl</sup>ine social networks; they also face environmental factors such as limited access to appropri ate assistive technology, parents’ fear of their children being online and parents’ low expectations of their children’s online potential (Raghavendra et al., 2012, 2013a, 2013b; Grace et al., 2014). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also con<sup>fi</sup>rm previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings among children without disabilities that home/family resources can impact on young people’s IT use. A key barrier that our study identi<sup>fi</sup>ed was that parents who are less IT con<sup>fi</sup>dent do not encourage their child with a disability into IT use without outside encouragement and support. They may also be unaware of the social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for their child in being online.

Figure 1 summarises the capitals shaping digital inclusion that were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in our study. It shows that a combination of three levels of capitals was necessary for the young people in our study to get online or improve their use of online social media. Firstly, of<sup>fl</sup>ine capitals need to be present, which can be translated into digital capitals, and in turn into disability-speci<sup>fi</sup>c capitals. The three levels are (a) Basic Of<sup>fl</sup>ine Capitals that are often invisible or ‘taken-forgranted’ in access and use of information technology (IT) – these include income (economic capital), the ability to read and write (cultural capital) and knowing how to converse and socialise (social capital). Secondly, (b) there are Digital Capitals, including the economic capital to purchase and maintain hardware and software, cultural capitals that support cybersafety and positive attitudes to children being online and social capital such as having people to connec with online. Thirdly, (c) there are Disability-Speci<sup>fi</sup>c Digital Capitals. These include the economic and cultural capacity to know about and purchase AT-for-IT, having positive attitudes to young people with disabilities being online and knowing about disability-related social media sites. These capitals were also shaped to some extent by Symbolic Capital, such as attitudes to using AT-for-IT and recognition of which hardware is ‘prestigious’. Most other studies of digital inclusion have looked mainly at Digital Capitals (Row 2), although Helsper has recently explored links between of<sup>fl</sup>ine and online capitals (Rows 1 and 2). Our study additionally identi<sup>fi</sup>es Disability-Speci<sup>fi</sup>c Digital Capitals (Row 3) and how these are underpinned by, and interact in relation with, both Basic Of<sup>fl</sup>ine Capitals and Digital Capitals among the young person, their family and other people whom they know.

All human agency requires resources of capital to maintain a position in society (Bourdieu, 1977). For successful universal Internet engagement, varying levels of economic capital are necessary to purchase and maintain hardware. In addition, some cultural capital skills are essential to navigate the Internet. Furthermore, social capital resources are essential to create and maintain networks of engagement that may also provide help to solve technical problems. Our study demonstrates that young people with disabilities and their families can require inten sive support to augment the resources needed for ITuse. It also shows that a considerable proportion of the barriers to increased ITuse are not disability related. This echoes <sup>fi</sup>ndings from an Australian survey with adults that found that among those Internet non-users who reported a physical or mental disability or signi<sup>fi</sup>cant health condition, disability was only a key reason for non-use for 20% (Dane et al., 2013). Our participants only started using or increasing their Internet use through the intensive home-based intervention that augmented a range of capita resources for them and their families – economic, social and cultural capitals – as well as corresponding digital capitals.

However, our study suggests that group-speci<sup>fi</sup>c digital capitals are an additional layer of resources that need to exist in order for certain groups to be digitally included. We found that an additional level of technical expertise (and, for some, additional social connection) was required to be aware that disability AT are available to facilitate Internet use for young people with a disability. We suggest this is disability-speci<sup>fi</sup>c digital social and cultural capital. Low levels of knowledge and expertise at the start of the study resulted in many participants using the Internet without ATs. AT costs were not speci<sup>fi</sup>cally mentioned as a barrier in this study but have elsewhere been found to be a barrier for adults with a disability (Vicente & Lopez, 2010; Jaeger, 2012). Our results indicated that it was more likely that parental lack of awareness was the issue. This was overcome through the families bene<sup>fi</sup>ting from their bridging social capital as clients of an ‘organisation of power’ (i.e. the children’s disability organisation), which made them aware of the opportunity to participate in the study. This <sup>fi</sup>nding does not support Helsper’s (2012b) Corresponding Fields model, which would suggest that parents who have the capitals to manage their child’s disability of<sup>fl</sup>ine should be able to manage it online. Indeed, we found that this was complicated by parents’ lack of digital cultural capital in general and their lack of disability-speci<sup>fi</sup>c digital cultural capital in particular.

Our study shows that group-speci<sup>fi</sup>c digital capitals, such as AT-for-IT, are often additional requirements for young people with a disability to get online because Internet technologies are mostly created for people without disabilities. While AT-for-IT may enable greater access for young people with disabilities, it adds a level of complexity that can also bring limitations. Furthermore, participants and their families may need additional assistance in accessing capital resources to mediate disability challenges to achieve digital engagement. This means that they do not have the same resources as many able-bodied children. A study in the UK, for example, showed that 70% of 11- to 14-year-olds claim that they can completely do the tasks they want online without help and 53% say they would ask a family member or friend to teach them new skills (Ipsos MediaCT, 2012). Furthermore, a recent study in Sweden found that people with intellectual and multiple disabilities (young people aged 15–20 years and adults) may re quire several years of training to become independent in their ITuse (Naslund & Gardelli, 2013).

Another emerging <sup>fi</sup>nding was that some devices with high levels of mainstream symbolic capital (such as an iPad) did encourage ITuse in this group. Nevertheless, use of AT (to connect with IT) may undermine embodied cultural capital for young people with a disability and be stigmatising. One suggestion for further research is for designers to collaborate with disability organisations, young people and disability experts in the design of AT-for-IT to make it more attractive and useable for users in this age group. There is as yet scant literature about device use and IT-AT device interaction among young people with disabilities, other than for young people who are visually impaired or deaf. Our study identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that some devices are a support to some and a hindrance to others (although our intervention occurred in 2011 and 2012 when tablets and smartphones were less common). Use of a touch screen, such as on an iPad, may help or hinder activities for some. On the other hand, a small device could improve accuracy for those with peripheral weakness (e.g. for some individuals with spinal muscular atrophy or muscular dystrophy). Other studies show that mobile phones can be useful for people with deafness. However, the small buttons or online keyboard may make these hard to use for people with other physical disabilities, especially those with severe disabilities who have limited <sup>fi</sup>nger or hand movements. This re<sup>fl</sup>ects recent <sup>fi</sup>ndings in Napoli and Obar’s (2014) study that mobile phones are not bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for everyone. It suggests the need for further research on the interactions between, and facilitators of, Internet use, device use and ATuse among young people with different disabilities. Another limitation identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by our study is that tablets and smartphones that do not run a full operating system make it impossible to use the same AT software that young people are used to on a laptop or PC in order to be compatible with their communication device. Such issues could be considered at the design stage of AT in terms of accessibility and compatibility with mainstream devices. Their use in practice could be another valuable subject for further research.

Our study demonstrates the interrelatedness of stocks of the various capitals both of<sup>fl</sup>ine and online, such as showing that physical and communication functional challenges can be compounded by lack of social skills. In turn, these limit progress in computing and Internet use. Reduced relational digital cultural capital combined with poor (of<sup>fl</sup>ine) literacy and cognitive skills further limited digital social networks and engagement and required ongoing support from the project. The intervention’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the recommendation of Lidstrom et al. (2012) that an individual plan can be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to young people with a disability; this should focus on ITuse, examine the need for AT support and ensure that digital skills can be fully utilised both at home and school. However, limitations may be placed on young people’s access to online social networking both in the home and school environments, for ethical, legal, privacy or safety reasons (Boyd et al., 2011). These may or may not be seen as justi<sup>fi</sup>ed by either children or parents (Boyd et al., 2011) and in turn limit the children’s ability to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from online social contact with others.

At the theoretical level, this paper demonstrates bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of conducting research within a critical information studies approach. It has uncovered and critically appraised the barriers and facilitators shaping inequalities in IT use among young people with disabilities and the extent to which these rely on resources. These are often assumed to already be in place and hence are often invisible for successful ITusers. The type of intensive IT support provided by our study is not currently available through existing services. This is predominantly because these focus on disability supports around activities of daily living such as walking/enhancing independent mobility, self-care activities and communication. In contrast, they do not provide support for IT use or AT-for-IT supports. We hypothesise that parents lack the time and energy, which we found are required to help their children access the Internet. Many mothers of children aged

6–17 years with disabilities already spend 6 h per weekday caring for their child’s daily needs and 8 h on weekends (Sawyer et al., 2011). In addition, our study showed that some parents are unaware of any bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of their child going online. Refusals to the original study invitation and comments during parent interviews suggest that some parents of a child with a disability believe that their child has limited or no ability to use the Internet or to use it for social network ing purposes.

Craft (2013) recommends a focus on how to support, negotiate and open up the positive aspects of social media so as to enhance its use among young people. Disaggregating the forms of capital into the three different levels (of<sup>fl</sup>ine, digital and disability-speci<sup>fi</sup>c digital), as we have performed, makes it easier to identify practical intervention points (i.e. any of the box areas and their constituent parts). We suggest this can assist IT use by young people with disabilities for improved social participation and digital inclusion. For example, increasing of<sup>fl</sup>ine cultural capita in the form of improved basic reading and writing ability could be the role of schools. At the same time, increasing digital social capital could be improved through youth services providing IT training. Furthermore, disability-speci<sup>fi</sup>c digital economic capital could be addressed by agencies that fund disability support. Alongside challenging prevailing assumptions about what young people need to ‘get online’, critical IS studies also aim to bring about change (Crotty, 1998; Baum, 2008; Myers & Klein, 2011). We found that providing IT support facilitated both the young person and parents to be con<sup>fi</sup>dent and safe online. Disability services could therefore be changed so that they identify to parents the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of IT use for their children. They could also identify ways to fund home-based IT training for the child and parents together. This would constitute a way of increasing the family’s cultural capital and the child’s social participation.

Because know-how' is acguired through socialisation. finding ways for children with disabilities and/or their families to share online experiences may also be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial. This could build levels of digital capitals speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the needs of young people with disabilities and reduce barriers to their ongoing use. Seale (2013) similarly recommended that increased contact among university students with disabilities could ‘marshal shared support’. While all participants in our study improved their Internet use, with some reaching their goal(s), longer-term support could minimise frustrations and circumvent IT abandonment. Our latest research has extended the intervention to young people with disabilities in rural and regional areas (Raghavendra et al., 2015). It is also trialling the use of peer e-mentors who also have a disability to support more sustainable Internet use. Providing workshops and home-based or school-based child-and family training may also be useful new developments alongside the existing plethora of general cybersafety information for parents. The New Zealand ‘Computers in Homes’ programme provides one successful model that bridges the gap between school and home (Communications Trust, 2013). It has also increased a variety of capitals for parents and children from disadvan taged backgrounds (Communications Trust, 2013). Children with a disability tend to be overprotected compared with their able-bodied peers (Holmbeck et al., 2002; Sanders, 2006) so that parents may limit their child's IT use as a safeguard. This may result in a double disadvantage for young people with disabilities who cannot independently use the Internet. According to Bourdieu (1977), possession of capital resources provides a means to gain a ‘voice’ and <sup>fi</sup>nd a place in modern society. The alternative is social exclusion or membership of the growing ‘digital underclass’ (Helsper, 2011) or the ‘mobile Internet underclass’ (Napoli & Obar, 2014).

Finally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support Soderstrom’s (2009) argument for age-speci<sup>fi</sup>c studies of IT use among people with disabilities because young people with disabilities use the Internet differently to adults with disabilities. Others also call for researching among children of different age groups. Swist et al. (2015) particularly recommend a focus on children under age 12 years because their IT use has signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increased in recent years, yet research attention has not matched this. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also support Selwyn’s (2009) critique that not all children are ‘innate talented users of digital technologies’. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings identify the need for future research to look within disability’ to closely examine the often-hidden relational capitals that underpin young people’s Internet use. They suggest the need to investigate the impact of different degrees of physical and cognitive disability on degree of IT use and quality of use. Research should also investigate differences within the group of young people with disabilities by socioeconomic status, gender, ethnicity and rurality, because the literature shows that these are key factors that also shape Internet use (e.g. Dane et al., 2013; Ewing & Thomas, 2012; Golder et al., 2010; Goodall et al., 2014; Helsper & Eynon, 2009; Newman et al., 2010; NTIA National Telecommunications & Information Administration 2013; Vinson & Rawsthorne, 2015).

Finally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings add weight to the conclusions of Van Meeteren et al. (2009), from research with adult migrants, that individuals not only need the appropriate forms of capita but in the right combinations if they are to realise their online goals.

## CONCLUSION

The results presented in this paper highlight the need for more nuanced interpretations of variations in IT use within populations of young people and within populations with disabilities. From a critical IS studies perspective, it has provided new information about the range of resources required. It also identi<sup>fi</sup>es ways in which policymakers and service providers migh support resource provision in order to increase digital inclusion for this group of young people. Despite some study limitations, such as the small sample size, the study highlights the challenges faced by young people with disabilities when seeking to begin or improve their Internet access and use. In particular, taking a critical approach by applying Bourdieu’s theory of practice provided a lens through which to better understand the complexity of resources at the individual and family level that shape Internet access and use. This paper provides important new insights into how individual and family-level resources shape IT access and use in this group. It particularly identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the need to develop skills that are considered ‘basic’ by many peers without a disability. The study identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three levels of capitals supporting IT use in this group in terms of of<sup>fl</sup>ine social, economic and cultural capitals and digital capitals, as well as disability-speci<sup>fi</sup>c digital capitals. It demonstrates that some young people with particular types of disabilities require intensive, personalised and long-term support from beyond the family in order to ‘get online’. Further research is warranted to identify what contributes to, and can overcome, inequalities in IT use within groups with different disabilities and of different ages. In particular, the study identi<sup>fi</sup>es speci<sup>fi</sup>c resources that are needed and demonstrates practical, albeit intensive and often time-consuming, processes that can increase digital inclusion for young people with disabilities.
