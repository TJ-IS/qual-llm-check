# Waiting in line for online services: a qualitative study of the user’s perspective

Gerard Ryan\* & Mireia Valverde<sup>†</sup>

\*Marketing Area, Department of Business Management, Faculty of Economics & Business, Universitat Rovira i Virgili, Avda. Universitat, 1,43204 REUS, Tarragona, Spain, email: gerard.ryan@urv.net, and <sup>†</sup>Management Area, Department of Business Management, Faculty of Economics & Business, Universitat Rovira i Virgili, Avda. Universitat, 1,43204 REUS, Tarragona, Spain, email: mireia.valverde@urv.net

Abstract. This manuscript is conceptually based in the areas of e-consumer behaviour and e-services marketing. Specifically, it examines the phenomenon of waiting in terms of the role it plays in the functionality of service delivery over the internet and the effect waiting has on how consumers behave online. A number of previous studies have identified a link between the ‘quickness’ of web sites and the site’s overall functionality. The existing knowledge on consumer waiting behaviour in the general context of traditional or ‘off-line’ services and in the specific context of online services is reviewed. A qualitative empirical study of consumer behaviour in online waiting situations is undertaken. The factors that determine how e-consumers behave when faced with online waiting situations are identified and explored.

Keywords: waiting, e-services, e-consumer behaviour, functionality

## INTRODUCTION

Often referred to as the ‘world wide wait’ (Weinberg, 2000; Weinberg et al., 2003), the slowness of the internet directly affects the functionality of the medium as a commercial channel for e-service delivery. Slowness leads to poor functionality, which in turn leads to delays and waiting in electronic service delivery. Indeed, it is widely accepted among academics and practitioners that delays and waiting on the internet are a significant impediment to growth in online commerce (Nielsen, 1999; Rose et al., 2001; 2003) and a contributing factor in e-commerce failures (Sorkin, 2000; Stockport et al., 2001).

This manuscript examines the phenomenon of waiting on the internet from the perspective of the online consumer (the e-consumer). Although waiting on the internet has been traditionally treated solely as a technical problem, the approach taken in this article is based on the broader view of e-services as a combination of marketing (and consumer behaviour) and management information systems (MIS) (Hoffman, 2003). In this sense, waiting on the internet is not solely a technical issue. It is also a marketing problem akin to making customers wait for service in a traditional (or off-line) environment. In this sense, the sources of slowness in online service delivery are many, including technical, logistical, marketing, service quality and customer care issues. This article reports on a qualitative study of internet users that identifies the factors that affect how e-consumers react behaviourally and affectively to poor web site functionality related to waiting on the internet.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

This section outlines the literature which was examined in order to provide a conceptual basis for the empirical analysis of waiting on the internet. The areas examined are mostly based in services marketing and consumer research, such as service design and quality, waiting in traditional services and e-consumer behaviour. The review has been organized into two subsections, the first on waiting for e-services and the second on waiting for traditional (of off-line) services.

## Waiting for e-services

A number of authors have referred to the ‘quickness’ or ‘agility’ of a web site as a significant feature of the functionality and usability of a web site. Pitt et al. (1999) suggest that just as consumers in traditional services will not wait endlessly in line, e-consumers faced with a slow web site will simply move on to another. Mohammed et al. (2001) refer to ‘quickness’ in terms of (1) download speed, as measured by the time required to download each page to the user’s screen; and (2) reliability, in terms of lack of ‘down times’ (for maintenance and other reasons) and the extent to which the site downloads without errors to the user’s screen. These features seem to have a direct effect on the bottom-line of e-commerce. Indeed, research suggests that navigation features that reduce the time required to purchase products have a significant positive effect on sales (Lohse & Spiller, 1999). Similarly, Stafford et al. (2004) suggest that internet management may profit from improvements in the process dimension, such as making things easier (and therefore quicker) for customers to find in web sites (e.g. by enhancing searching capabilities).

However, in practice the internet is often frustratingly slow (Rose & Straub, 2001; Ryan & Valverde 2003; Weinberg et al., 2003), so much so that the term ‘web rage’ (a negative reaction to the slowness of the internet akin to ‘road rage’) has moved into the popular vocabulary (Ananova, 2002; Eisenberg, 2002; Ewalt, 2002). This reflects the fact that e-consumers are continually being forced to wait for online services and products (Ryan & Valverde 2005).

The fact that consumers are being forced to wait in virtual queues on the internet is all the more surprising in light of the fact that two of the main motivations why consumers use the internet are for its potential time-savings and convenience (Foucault & Scheufele, 2002; Rodríguez, 2002; Chen & Chang, 2003; Rose et al., 2003). Marketers need to understand the particular requirements of online consumers and provide for their needs (Vrechopoulos et al., 2003). Yet, delays caused by the slowness of the internet may be neutralizing the motivations of these virtual consumers (Dellaert & Kahn, 1999; Rodríguez, 2002). In a similar vein, if the ‘rason d’être’ of employing information systems in e-services is to create value through improved service (Piccoli et al., 2004), then the slowness of the internet presents an important barrier to achieving this by reducing the functionality of web sites.

The slowness of the internet has traditionally been treated purely as a technical problem, which in time, should be overcome by the spread of broadband and a faster internet. It is not surprising then that most of the existing research on the topic is in the areas of human computer interaction (Selvidge et al., 2000; Perfetti, 2001; Meyer & Hildebrandt, 2002; Nah, 2004) and internet quality of service (Bouch & Sasse, 1999; Bhatti et al., 2000). Although the slowness of the internet has been referred to in many ways in this literature [download delay (Rose & Straub, 2001), internet latency (Bhatti et al., 2000), feedback delay (DiClemente & Hantula, 2003)] the conceptualization of the phenomenon is the same across the board. All of these labels refer to the time delay involved in clicking on a web page and the page fully downloading and appearing on the user’s computer screen.

The overriding objectives of previous research have been centred on the following:

1 To identify how long internet users are willing to wait for web pages to download before abandoning the web site. It is generally accepted that internet users have an objective maximum time they are willing to wait for a web site to download before giving up and abandoning the site. However, the precise limit in terms of time varies across different studies between 2 s and half a minute. (Nielsen, 1996; 1999 – 10 s; Tedeschi, 1999 – 28; Accounting Web, 2000 – 8 s; Nah, 2004 – 2 s).

2 To identify the factors that influence how long users will wait for downloading web pages, such as the online experience of the user (Borella et al., 1997; Weinberg, 2000), cumulative session time (the time online in one single internet session) (Johnson, 1998; Bhatti et al., 2000), personal attitude to delay (Rose et al., 2001), the position of the wait (Weinberg, 2000), expectations of the wait (Bhatti et al., 2000; Bailey & Schaffer, 2001), the specific task involved (Dellaert & Kahn, 1999) etc.

3 A number of authors address the need to find ways to decrease web site download times (Nielsen, 1996; 1999; Dellaert & Kahn, 1999; Nah, 2004).

4 Some other questions have received minor attention in the literature, such as how waiting affects return visits to the web site (Rose & Straub, 2001), how waiting affects attitudes to the web site (Borella et al., 1997; Dellaert & Kahn, 1999; Weinberg, 2000; Galletta et al., 2004), and attitudes to the online retailer (Rose & Straub, 2001; Rose et al., 2001). A detailed review of the literature on waiting on the internet and a research agenda is proposed by Ryan & Valverde (2003).

In recent times, a number of authors have questioned the concept of ‘download delay’ as a too narrow approach to the conceptualization of the slowness of the internet (Perfetti, 2001; Ryan & Valverde, 2003; 2005). Indeed, recent studies based on e-consumers’ perceptions of the internet, suggest that the slowness of the internet is much more than just slow downloading web pages. Based on an e-services approach, Ryan & Valverde (2005) suggest a much broader conceptualization of the problem rooted in e-service quality and referred to as ‘waiting on the internet’. They identify 14 separate types of situations in which e-consumers are forced to wait for service on the internet. However, the vast majority of empirical studies on waiting on the internet examine just one type of waiting situation, i.e. ‘download delay’ (or waiting for web pages to download to the user’s screen) and just one aspect of the waiting experience, i.e. ‘How long will the user wait?’ Yet the waiting situations identified by Ryan & Valverde (2005) and the unanswered questions surrounding the behaviour of users in waiting situations are wide and varied. Some of the waiting situations identified by Ryan & Valverde (2005) include: waiting for replies to enquiries to online customer service, waiting caused by intrusive (and often animated) online screen advertising; waiting for password or username reminders to be received, waiting for confirmation of online transactions, waiting because of web site outage or server saturation, waiting caused by long, complex purchase and checkout processes, waiting caused by having to download and install ‘plug-ins’ or updated versions of software, waiting caused by web site time-outs (see Ryan & Valverde 2005) for a complete explanation of the types of waiting situations). All of these situations directly affect the functionality of web sites by hindering the user from achieving the task they set out to achieve on the internet in the most efficient timescale.

## Waiting for traditional services

As is the case in traditional or off-line services (East, 1997), consumers of online services (or e-consumers) do not like to wait (Weinberg, 2000). Although waiting on the internet has received little attention among researchers beyond its immediate effect as a specific dimension of functionality, waiting in the context of traditional or off-line services has been a major focus of research in the wider area of services marketing for over 20 years (Hornik, 1984; Maister, 1985). Research on this topic has been carried out in a wide range of service contexts such as restaurants (Davis & Vollmann, 1990), retail banking (Sarel & Marmorstein, 1998), supermarkets (East et al., 1994), telephone services (Antonides et al., 2002). It is widely accepted that the longer the consumer has to wait the more negative the reaction to the wait (Tom & Lucey, 1995; Davis & Heineke, 1998; Durrande-Moreau, 1999). This reaction may include a perception of poor service quality and feelings of frustration and irritation (Katz et al., 1991; Davis & Heineke, 1994; Taylor, 1994).

If the central issue is how consumers react to waiting for service delivery (whether the context is off-line or online) the existing literature on waiting in services may have much to offer in terms of understanding how e-consumers behave while waiting on the internet. For instance, we know that in off-line services, there are a number of factors that influence consumers’ reactions to delays. These include the consumer’s expected waiting time (East et al., 1991; Chebat & Filiatrault, 1993), the importance of the service encounter (Davis & Heineke, 1994; Jones & Peppiatt, 1996), the mood of the consumer (Davis & Heineke, 1994; Chebat et al., 1995; Hui et al., 1997) and so on. In addition, there is evidence that a number of actions may be taken in order to reduce the negative effects of delays in services. This includes informing consumers of the expected duration of the delay (Hui & Tse, 1996), providing facilities to occupy the consumer during the wait (Taylor, 1994; Jones & Peppiatt, 1996; Durrande-Moreau, 1999), apologizing (Sarel & Marmorstein, 1998) etc.

However, until now, researchers in marketing have made little effort to apply the knowledge and know-how gained from 20 years of research in traditional services to waiting in the online context. Indeed, while on other aspects of e-consumer behaviour, researchers are already moving towards developing specific models for online consumers (O’Keefe et al., 2000), research on online waiting behaviour is still in its infancy. The little research which exists focuses almost entirely on measuring how long e-consumers will wait for web pages to download before giving up and clicking on the competition (Dellaert & Kahn, 1999; Weinberg, 2000; Rose & Straub, 2001; Rose et al., 2003; Weinberg et al., 2003). This lack of research interest on the specific topic of waiting on the internet is surprising given the importance of quickness, speed and time savings in choosing online service over more ‘traditional’ off-line forms of service delivery (Nua, 2001; Pastore, 2001; McMahon, 2002; Strauss et al., 2003). In addition, the existing research, on the whole, does not take into account the wealth of knowledge we have concerning waiting in traditional services from the marketing literature.

## PURPOSE

The preceding sections outline the general research climate in which the current research project was undertaken. This climate is characterized by an almost myopic focus on measuring web page download time tolerance levels. As a result, there has been little attention given to other forms of waiting on the internet (Ryan & Valverde, 2005) and there has been little effort dedicated to incorporating the existing knowledge on waiting in traditional services to assist in the analysis of waiting on the internet. But now that we know that waiting on the internet is much more than simply waiting for web pages to download to the computer screen (Ryan & Valverde, 2003), it is of great importance for e-marketers and web sites designers to identify how users react when faced with a broad range of waiting situations and what factors determine these reactions.

The remainder of the manuscript reports on a study that was undertaken in order to answer the following research question: What are the factors that determine how e-consumers react when they are forced to wait for service delivery on the internet?

The following sections describe the research methodology, the research methods and the sampling strategy employed. This is followed by the results of the analysis, discussion and conclusions.

## THE RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

## Methodologies in previous research

It is surprising that all of the previous studies that focus specifically on waiting on the internet have been based on laboratory experiments (Ryan & Valverde, 2003). It is not the intention of the authors to question the validity of the experiment as a research method, but it would seem that the total reliance on this method may inhibit the advancement of our knowledge and understanding of this phenomenon. This may be especially significant as the topic is still at an exploratory stage of research, when a variety of different methodologies are generally required in order to examine a variety of questions surrounding an issue (Boyd et al., 1989).

Although the experimental design provides absolute control over the variables, the true reactions and feelings of participants in consumer research may be more suitably examined in naturalistic settings. Indeed, the ecological validity of the previous experimental research on the topic is debatable as the experiments are not based on the normal activities e-consumers carry out in their everyday use of the internet. Many of the experiments are based on just one visit to one web site even though the effects of waiting may be cumulative (Rose & Straub, 2001). Moreover, the web pages are chosen by the researchers (often simulated or mock-up web sites) (Weinberg, 2000) and not by the participants, and the tasks participants are asked to perform tend to be pre-chosen by the researchers and very limited in scope.

## Methodology

As the authors were not equipped with existing testable theories on waiting on the internet, a constructivist viewpoint based on an inductive approach was adopted in the present research project (Gill & Johnson, 1991; Richards & Richards, 1994). Given this inductive approach, it was decided that a qualitative methodology would be more suitable as it would allow greater flexibility and adaptability in the research project (Carson et al., 2001). In addition, an intensive rather than a superficial (and extensive) approach was considered more suitable (Shayo et al., 1999), given that in recent times a number of commentators have suggested the use of a wider range of research methodologies (Mingers, 2003; Lings et al., 2004) and a greater emphasis on qualitative techniques (Parasuraman & Zinkhan, 2002) in future research on issues related to online consumer behaviour and electronic services. For the current research project, qualitative research may offer valuable insights into how people think and feel about phenomena and why this is so (De Ruyter & Scholl, 1998; Silverman, 1998). Indeed, the specific individuals that the researchers were interested in for this research were internet users (or e-consumers). As commented by Watson et al. (1998), it is precisely these very same users that have the most pertinent information and opinions in order to judge the features and quality of web sites.

## Methods: the personal diary method

Non-intrusive qualitative research techniques were chosen for the present study because of their ability to allow the researcher access to the users’ daily internet sessions. Personal diaries are often used as a research method to gain insights into the reactions, descriptions, feelings and behaviours of the individual ‘within the context of their own [subjective] world’ (Symon, 1998). Diaries are especially useful when the research moves outside the laboratory environment into the natural environment of the participants. This facilitates an increase in the ecological validity of the research (DeLongis et al., 1992). If maintained on a regular basis or on an ‘after-occurrence’ basis, the diary descriptions may be fresh and rich as they are still clear in the mind of the individual (DeLongis et al., 1992; Symon, 1998; Conway & Briner, 2002;

Toms & Duff, 2002). Therefore, the personal diary method was employed to examine how consumers behave when they experience waiting on the internet.

A template structure was used in the design of the diary, with each informant asked to fill out one template for each online waiting situation. This would facilitate comparison during the analysis stage of the project.

The instructions for the diary research placed much emphasis on the degree of richness and the contextualization of the situations. The participants were informed that the researchers were most interested in rich descriptions of waiting situations. In this sense respondents were not asked to record every single waiting situation during the 9 week research period. Instead the emphasis was clearly on recording a wide range of situations in rich (in-depth) detail, rather than simply recording a large number of repeated or similar waiting situations.

It should be noted that a series of focus groups were carried out before the diary research began (with the same participants). The purpose of the focus groups was twofold, to determine whether waiting for service on the internet was a concern for internet users and to identify the situations in which users felt that they were waiting for e-services (see Ryan & Valverde, 2005). The focus groups lead to the development of a definition of what constitutes an internet waiting situation. This definition was then adopted in the diary research. However, it should be stressed that the exploratory and qualitative natures of the research meant that users were encouraged to report on their own subjective perceptions when they felt that they were experiencing a waiting situation on the internet.

Although the diary was designed in the form of a template in order to facilitate comparison during the analysis, the format of the template was very ‘open’ to allow participants to record the depth and richness of information which was required for the research. A copy of the diary template is included in Appendix 1. For the purposes of the present manuscript, the most relevant question in the diary template is the question ‘Why do you think you did what you did and felt what you felt?’ (. . . during the waiting situation).

The diaries were designed with the principal goal of allowing the informants to describe in as much detail as they preferred, in their own words, their thoughts and feelings during the waiting situation, and to identify the factors and reasons that provoked their affective and behavioural responses to the waiting situation.

To avoid drop-outs, to maintain contact with the participants during the research period and to ensure the quality of the diary entries, all respondents were asked to send the researchers their diaries (in-progress) on two occasions during the 9-week period of the field research and to send in the full completed diary at the end of the period. A personalized feedback email message was sent to each individual by the researchers each time they sent in their diary inprogress.

## Sampling strategy

In light of the adoption of a qualitative approach to the research, an intensity sampling strategy was deemed the most appropriate (purposeful sampling) (Miles & Huberman, 1994). Intensity sampling is one of various strategies employed to purposefully select information-rich informants. This approach seeks to identify informants that ‘manifest the phenomenon of interest intensely (but not extremely)’ (Patton, 1990). In other words, the researchers sought internet users who use the internet in an intensive manner, on a very regular, everyday basis. It was felt that this type of internet user might potentially experience waiting on the internet many times and in many different situations during the period of the fieldwork, and would provide the depth and richness of information required for the present study.

The participants were chosen for their regular use of the internet (mostly daily and often many times in 1 day) and the wide variety of tasks, professional, personal, educational and entertainment (Jayawardhena, 2002; Jayawardhena et al., 2003) that they carry out online. The typical profile was of a university graduate, professionally employed with a regular income, between 26 and 50 years old. The fieldwork involved 92 participants who kept personal diaries of their online waiting experiences over a 9-week period. They were asked to record their diary entries during or as soon as possible after the actual waiting event.

## Qualitative analysis

At the end of the 9-week period a total of 1141 entries (running into many thousands of pages of descriptions) were examined by two researchers using computer assisted qualitative analysis software Nvivo. Although the use of computer software does not offer any conceptual advance over manual methods of data analysis, the software facilitates a range of complex operations which would be very difficult (but not impossible) to perform by hand (Bourdon, 2002). The decision to use Computer Assisted Qualitative Data Analysis Software (CAQDAS) was based on the sheer volume of data that were produced from the personal diaries.

Strauss & Corbin (1990) formalized the approach to the coding task in qualitative research in three consecutive operations in order to progressively analyse and achieve a deeper understanding of qualitative data. However, in practice the coding of data is not merely a three-stage linear process. In practice the analysis of the diary data is an iterative process which involves many phases in which any of the three types of coding might be required at different times. These coding procedures (open coding, axial coding and selective coding) are explained in detail in Strauss & Corbin (1990; 1998), and a useful summary is available in Charmaz (1983). In the case of the current study a complete open coding was performed on the data. This was followed by an iterative process of axial and selective coding.

In line with this coding procedure and in order to gain new insights into waiting on the internet and to advance our understanding of the phenomenon, a data-driven approach was deemed appropriate. The data-driven approach implies that the data are approached with an open mind, without preconceptions, with the objective of allowing the categories (nodes) to emerge from the reading of the qualitative data (Gibbs, 2002). Nevertheless, the authors did not approach the coding of the data with a totally blank mind (tabula rasa) as the project was designed on the premise that previous knowledge on the phenomenon of interest (however, scarce) should be used to inform the choices and developments in the qualitative data analysis.

It should be noted that the initial phase of data analysis of the diaries actually took a conceptdriven approach, thus adopting a form of template analysis (following the terminology by Crabtree & Miller, 1992), which is analogous to codebook analysis or thematic coding) based on the a priori categories which emerged from the review of the literature (King, 1998). However, it soon became evident that this approach was unsuitable as the nodes which were produced by the automatic coding procedure merely provided the researcher with a decontextualized list of informants’ responses. These responses could not be appreciated in isolation, nor could relationships be identified between nodes. It was evident that each category needed to be examined in the context of the ‘whole’ waiting event to which it was related. This form of automatic coding lead to a decontextualization of the diary entries, so much so that it was decided to modify the coding scheme to one based on the analysis of each individual waiting situation as a whole. Indeed, this is a clear example of how the process of qualitative data analysis must be open and flexible (Miller & Crabtree, 1994). Thus, the researchers began to analyse the data in sequence, on a line by line basis, beginning with diary number 1, waiting situation 1 and moving sequentially through the data. This process reflects the first coding procedure of open coding in grounded theory as suggested by Strauss & Corbin (1990). The information from this open coding process was organized around each of the types of waiting situations. This change of approach facilitated a move from a decontextualized to a contextualized coding of the data.

Regarding the issue of maintaining rigour in qualitative research, it is important to highlight that qualitative research does not rely on the traditional tenets of positivist enquiry which measure the reliability and validity of the research process once the results have been obtained (Charmaz, 1983). Many researchers suggest that these are simply not appropriate measures of qualitative research (Janesick, 2000; Welsh, 2002), preferring instead to use terms such as ‘rigour’ and ‘quality’ of the research. In qualitative research design, measures of validity of the data must be built into the design of the research process and the analysis of the data, as there are no statistical techniques to perform this task. These so-called ‘canons of verification’ are based on systematic checks and procedures which take place throughout the research process. A number of actions were taken throughout the research process of the present study in order to guarantee the highest levels of rigour. For instance ‘adequacy’ (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Morse, 1994) was achieved by ensuring that sufficient data were collected on a sufficient number and variety of waiting situations. A detailed audit trail (Morse, 1994) was maintained throughout the research project. A teamwork approach (Morse, 1994) was taken to the entire data analysis process. The teamwork approach (as apposed to ‘multiple raters’) implies that the two (or more) researchers are actually involved in the entire analytical process and share its development and insights. Fit (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) was ensured by seeking the consumer/user perspective of waiting on the internet, through the unobtrusive method of diary research, with sufficient detail to allow the merging theory to ‘fit’ the daily reality of internet use. Every effort was made to use the language used by the informants in the construction of the theory in order to maintain a high degree of understandability (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) for users, consumer behaviour analysts and marketers alike.

Finally, it should be noted that there are a number of potential operational problems associated with diary research. For example, some informants tend to concentrate on only one dimension of the phenomenon that is being examined. Others might not record in their diaries on a regular basis. This can cause recall error if the informants wait too long to write in their diaries. Another frequent difficulty is that there tends to be high drop-out rates in diary research. In order to avoid such problems two rounds of work-in-progress were successfully carried out during the diary research period as outlined earlier. Where necessary, participants were asked to provide more details on the contextualization of the waiting situation or more developed descriptions of their thoughts and actions in the waiting situations. Some individuals who had little variety in the type of waiting situations in their diaries were asked to attempt to be aware of other possible forms of waiting online.

## RESULTS

This section outlines the results of the analysis of the diary entries. It briefly explains each of the factors that were identified as having an impact on how e-consumers react behaviourally and affectively to waiting situations on the internet. Examples from the diaries are included that illustrate how each factor affects how e-consumers react to waiting on the internet. (Note: Because of limitations in space, only a limited number of example quotations from the data set are provided. Further examples are available directly from the authors.)

The analysis of the qualitative data suggests that the factors that have an effect on how econsumers react to waiting on the internet are as follows:

## 1. Availability of alternatives

This factor deals with whether or not there are alternatives available to the user, in order to achieve the task he/she is trying to undertake on the internet. Other online alternatives may exist in the form of other web sites with similar contents, products or services etc. Unsurprisingly, when faced with waiting on the internet, the existence of alternatives generally alleviates the situation.

I felt indifferent because I found the information in another web site.

However, when there are no alternatives available, frustration and anger are often felt on the part of the user. The extent or intensity of these feelings tends to be mediated by other intervening variables such as how essential or important the task is, whether the user is under time pressure or not (these are also mediating factors which shall be commented later in the article) and so on.

Above all I feel really annoyed because I can’t access the part of the web site that I want. In the end you get sick of trying and you just leave it. It always gives me a sense of powerlessness because you can’t complain to anyone or do anything else about it.

Some of the strongest negative reactions are produced in situations where users must carry out an essential task within a certain limited time period and where no alternatives exist, either online or off-line.

I couldn’t do anything, I became extremely anxious, apart from being really desperate, as I wanted to go to bed and I couldn’t connect to the internet. I can’t remember any other occasion being so desperate. I was so angry I felt like kicking the computer . . . I got so frustrated for not being able to do what I wanted to do.

## 2. Comparison of alternatives

This variable refers to how users compare the web site to alternatives (i.e. other web sites or the off-line version of the task). Users tend to compare the outcome of the waiting situation with the alternative means of achieving the same task. If the alternative is considered to be quicker, then the resulting feelings are negative. In contrast, when the user considers that the alternative would have taken longer, then the resulting feeling is positive.

Although I think I spent too much time reading the contract and in the prepurchase phase, I don’t think I spent any more time than I would in a real supermarket. Also, I would [normally] have to leave home to do the shopping but in this way [online], they deliver the products to my home.

## 3. Connection speed

This refers to the actual speed of the user’s connection to the internet. The faster the connection speed, the more negative the reaction of the user when faced with delay. On the other hand, users with a slow connection tend to be resigned to waiting. It should also be noted that the negative reaction tends to be amplified with when factor 3 is combined with factor 13. In other words, the reaction is stronger when despite having a good connection speed, the user attributes the cause of the wait to other parties.

So much hype about broadband only to find that [name of company]’s web site is painfully slow. The search facility takes AGES (!!!) and gives back ‘O matches’ all the time!

## 4. Importance of the task

The importance of the task at hand was one of the key factors to emerge from the analysis of the data. When waiting occurs in situations where the user is carrying out an important or essential task, the negative reaction of the user is amplified. When the task is not so important, the reaction of the user is less. Likewise, the feelings associated with a successfully achieved task are determined by the importance of the task.

I received a call from a client who told me that he had sent me a budget proposal to which I would need to make some modifications . . . I connected with [name of internet portal] . . . the connection was long and tense because the web page wouldn’t download . . . [I felt] frustrated and anxious because the budget was important.

As I wasn’t looking for anything in particular and I was just passing the time, I wasn’t bothered.

## 5. Fill the wait

Users who fill the wait (take advantage of the waiting time to do something else) are less negative in their perception of the wait.

During the wait, as I was playing with my son I wasn’t very bothered.

I think that I knew how to take advantage of the waiting time. and therefore I think that I didn’t waste much time. I took advantage of the time to do something else I had to do sooner or later anyway.

However, when it is not possible to fill the wait, the effect on how users feel tends to be more negative.

A total waste of time. On top of having to wait for the files to download, I couldn’t even take advantage of the connection by reading my email.

## 6. Free service

This factor was exclusively related to the waiting situation ‘dealing with online advertising’. When faced with waiting because of online advertising, some users comment that the wait is a small price to pay seeing as the service or information they are accessing is otherwise free.

I was a little bothered because of so much advertising, although I realised that this is a free service and nowadays there’s nothing free.

## 7. Availability of feedback

This factor refers to the extent to which the user is informed or uninformed, by whatever means, of how long he/she is going to have to wait. The uncertainty provoked by a lack of information can provoke a negative reaction.

[I felt] angry, not just because I couldn’t access [the web site] because I had lost my password, but for the vagueness and insufficiency of the information which I got from customer service.

Uncertainty also is evident in terms of not knowing for sure whether an online process has been successfully completed or not.

The sensation I had was a general sense of uncertainty/insecurity (have I done it properly? Have I actually sent it [the enquiry].

Another sensation I experienced was the fear of whether I had done something wrong and I wouldn’t be able to change the [airline] ticket.

## 8. Achievement of objective

This factor refers to whether or not the internet user achieves his/her goal or completes the task he/she set out to achieve. In many cases this is the fundamental and key factor in determining the users’ evaluation of the wait. Users who achieved their goal feel less negative and even sometimes express feelings of satisfaction at the (eventual) positive outcome.

Even though it took a long time, the sensation I have is that this will be a very useful tool . . . when I had finished the process I found that it wasn’t really as difficult as I had thought.

[During the wait] I felt discouraged because I couldn’t find such simple information straight away. After the wait I felt very satisfied because I found the product I was looking for.

Just as a positive outcome is a determining factor of the users’ evaluation of the wait, a negative outcome leads to strong negative responses.

I was extremely angry. I felt this way because the only thing I could do was close [pop-up] windows and I didn’t even get to see the web site in question.

During the slow process in the web site I was patient in order to advance the task at hand. Afterwards, having realised that I was wasting my time I was very frustrated.

## 9. Once-off waiting saves time later

This factor is associated with waiting situations such as ‘installing plug-in software’ and ‘registering for access’, in which the user must carry out some form of intermediate task before he/ she can continue with his/her main task. However, this extra time will not be necessary on future visits to the web site.

The online presentation of the tax declaration is supposed to be a way of saving time but in this case it was not so, although now that I have the software it won’t be necessary [to wait] on future occasions.

For future [online supermarket] purchases, once you have the list of products prepared, you simply have to repeat it. It’s quicker and easier.

## 10. Opportunity cost of time

A number of informants considered the opportunity cost of the time they were wasting while waiting on the internet and how they could be better dedicating this time to a more beneficial activity. This variable negatively affects how users react to the wait.

When the situation began to change and I had to keep retrying, I felt like a real idiot because instead of enjoying my day off I was here doing things that I should be doing at work during work hours.

And the truth is that when you keep trying and you see that time is passing by while you could have been doing other things and taking greater advantage of the time, your blood begins to boil.

## 11. Outcome worth the wait?

In situations where the outcome is worth the wait, there is a general positive effect on users.

I thought that to listen to the radio [on the internet] was a simple matter of connecting and listening, so it took me a long preparation time before I could listen to the radio online. But in the end it was well worth it.

The only problem is the fact that you have to register first, which really takes up a lot of time . . . once the process is finished you can pay by credit card . . . it’s really quick and cheap (I would recommend it to others) . . . [I felt] satisfied . . .

In contrast, in situations where the user considers that the outcome is not worth the wait, there is a negative effect, thus increasing the intensity of the other possible negative effects that the wait might already have provoked.

I got an error message telling me that I had to download a specific program [to read the file] but that I could do it from the same web site . . . I felt so frustrated when I opened the file and I realised what it was and that I already had this plug-in on my computer but the web site hadn’t detected it!

What bothered me most was the fact of having to register with them as a user. I think to make a simple enquiry it shouldn’t be necessary to go through this long rigmarole . . . it annoyed me.

## 12. Previous experiences

This variable mediates users’ expectations of how long the wait will be. This variable also means that users form mental explanations of the causes or circumstances that lead to the wait. In turn, these imagined causes may have a positive or negative effect on users.

Users who encounter unexpected waiting in web sites where they have had previous positive experiences may have either a negative or a positive affective reaction. Thus, the direction of this relationship is not clear according to the data analysed. The first example shows how a user reacts positively, while the second example shows how under similar circumstances, the user reacts negatively. Hence, the difference in affective responses may be explained by other intervening factors. For instance, in the first example, the objective was achieved, whereas in the second example the objective was not achieved.

This is an operation which I repeat more than once a month with the same company and without problems. For sure you have to keep in mind that it’s normal that from time to time this happens. Banks are not perfect either.

I felt anxious because I didn’t know if the service would be fixed. Also keeping in mind that there are very few days left until the 1st of May deadline. Disappointed because until now I have never had problems when reserving tickets with this system.

## 13. Attributing blame for the wait

In the present study, this variable is extremely dense in the data. It refers to who (or what) the user blames for the waiting situation. The data suggest that, on the whole, users place the blame for the wait on the web site owners/service providers. However, there were also cases in which the users blamed themselves for the waiting situation.

I never actually felt anything in particular, because the problem was my own fault.

I was really disappointed, because a company like [name of company] should take more care of its presence on the web. At least it should answer the emails of its clients.

## 14. Apology provided

Interestingly, there was evidence in the data that specific actions on the part of the service provider, such as apologizing for the delay can reduce the severity of users’ negative feelings to waiting.

I liked the fact that on the one hand they replied to my message and on the other hand they apologised for not having done so earlier. That’s a point in favour of their customer service.

## 15. Expectations of time required to achieve task

One of the most significant intervening factors was the expectation of the user of the time it would take to complete a specific task online.

[I] didn’t feel anything particular because I understood that the cause of the wait is a common occurrence.

It surprised me. I thought that I could finish it on the same day . . . and I realised that I had to wait, at least one full extra day. It pissed me off a little.

## 16. Time pressure

When the user is under time pressure to achieve his/her task on the internet, there is a clear negative effect on how users feel. On many occasions this negativity manifests itself in feelings of anxiousness or frustration.

If you are in a hurry the last thing you need is that these stupid things [daughter windows with advertising] start appearing on your screen!

I needed a reply in 2/3 days . . . I have the sensation that they are taking the piss . . . I’ve been waiting for a WEEK!

The presence of time pressure also affects what users do but there is no clear pattern as to the exact behavioural reactions.

I decided not to continue with the process because I had little time to spare.

I prayed, and waited for the reservation.

In situations where the user is not under any time pressure, the overall effect on how users feel tends to be less negative. Many users clearly state that the fact that they are not in a hurry has lead to indifference or resignation.

In terms of the wait, it annoyed me a little, but the truth is that I had nothing else to do that afternoon, so I thought ‘I’ll wait’.

There was no urgency involved . . . , that is, it wasn’t an urgent situation . . . I took it quite well, with resignation . . .

## DISCUSSION

Having briefly outlined each of the factors identified in the analysis of the data, we extend the analysis in this section, highlighting the most relevant outcomes and comparing these with previous research on waiting in traditional and in electronic services.

On a first reading some of these factors may appear quite alike. For instance, factors 1 (Availability of alternatives) and 2 (Comparison of alternatives) may seem quite similar but they are in fact very different. Whereas the first factor deals with whether alternative methods of achieving the task are available, the second factor deals with how the individual compares their internet experience with how long it would have taken to achieve the task by other means. Likewise factors 8 (Achievement of objective) and 11 (Outcome worth the wait) may seem related but are in fact distinct. Whether the outcome is worth the wait (11) may on some occasions be measured in terms of achieving the objective (8) but it may also be measured in many other ways (such as investing time now saves time in the future, the importance of the task implies that the wait was worth it etc.). In addition, factor 11 (Outcome worth the wait) takes place (temporarily) after factor 8 (Achievement of objective). Finally, factor 11 is a subjective perception, whereas factor 8 is an objective fact.

## Grouping the factors

The fact that the data produced 16 separate factors is not surprising given the qualitative nature of analysis, whose aim is to generate a deep and rich understanding of a phenomenon. Nevertheless, it is sometimes difficult to work with such a broad range of factors making some form of conceptual grouping useful (especially for the reader). Indeed, in the past some authors have attempted to group the factors related to waiting for traditional services. For example, Durrande-Moreau (1999), in her literature review of research on waiting in services, groups the factors which affect how individuals react to waiting in terms of six categories: (1) real time; (2) personal expectations; (3) individual factors before the wait; (4) situational factors before the wait; (5) individual factors during the wait; and (6) situational factors during the wait. Likewise, the meta-analysis carried out by Ryan & Valverde (2003) groups the factors that affect how internet users react to waiting into eight groups. These are (1) time online in one single session; (2) online experience of user; (3) economic cost; (4) attitude to delay; (5) position of the wait; (6) user’s expectations; (7) type of task; and (8) uncertainty.

In order to further the conceptual analysis of the factors identified in the present article, the 16 factors may be grouped in the following four conceptual categories;

1 Individual factors (connection speed of user’s computer, previous experience; outcome worth the wait, opportunity cost of time, comparison of alternatives, free service, expectations of time required). Individual factors are defined as those determined by the individual user (his/ her own internet connection speed, previous experience on the Net, as well as his/her subjective perception of the outcome, the (lack of) economic and opportunity cost and the relative comparison with alternatives and his/her own expectations of how much time is required to complete the task online).

2 Situational factors (time pressure; fill the wait). Situational factors are those that are determined by the individual, but change with the specific circumstances surrounding the actual waiting situation.

3 Web site-related (availability of feedback, attributing the blame for the wait, apology provided). Web site factors are those related to the specific web site with which the waiting situation is taking place.

4 Task-related (importance of task, achievement of objective; availability of alternatives, once off wait saves time later). Task related factors are those connected with the specific task being carried out by the user when the wait takes place.

## Making inferences and weighing-up the factors

It should be emphasized that it is not the intention of the authors to quantify the importance of the various factors identified in the data, but to identify them from the analysis of actual user experiences. Neither is it possible to identify significant causal relationships from the data (as this was not the purpose of the research). Indeed, the inductive nature of the research design of this project implies that the authors do not avail of tools such as regression coefficients to deduce definitive conclusions regarding the weight of factor. Deductive (or conclusive) research on this topic will be the objective of future research.

However, qualitative research avails of the concept of data ‘density’ in order to help the most salient issues of a study to emerge from the analysis of the data. By density we refer not only to the proportion of the total number of occurrences represented by each individual type of factor (the frequency of the factor in the data), but also the precision and variety of the examples of each factor as found in the qualitative data (Strauss & Corbin, 1998).

Therefore, the evidence from the data that suggest that certain factors are more salient when they:

• occur more times (frequency);

• occur across more individuals;

• occur more precisely (the factor is similar across occurrences and individuals); and

• occur in a wide range of situations and circumstances.

In this sense, the coding schemes used in the analysis of the data develop throughout the coding process, from the initial open (or line by line) coding where many categories appear, to the axial coding where some categories are collapsed (or combined) into stronger, more refined categories and other categories are discarded. and finally onto selective coding where the density of each category is evaluated (see Appendix 2 for an example of a work-inprogress coding scheme of the categories related to download delay. May of these categories were collapsed or discounted later in the analysis).

In terms of density, the key factors, that is, the factors that seem to have the greatest influence on how users’ react to waiting on the internet are;

• importance of the task;

• achievement of objective;

• ‘time pressure; and

• attributing the blame for the wait.

In fact, of these, by far the overriding factor was ‘achievement of objective’. In other words, whether or not the user eventually achieves the task he/she sets out to achieve is the factor that seems to have the greatest impact on the reactions of e-consumers to waiting in the electronic delivery of services. Although all waiting is generally negative, if the individual is eventually successful, the frustration of the wait is replaced by the positive reaction associated with a positive outcome.

It proved extremely difficult to measure the precise effect of each individual variable in each waiting event. This is because in most waiting events, a number of factors tend to affect the situation simultaneously. The presence of a group or cluster of intervening factors jointly impacts on users’ reactions. This characteristic of waiting events tends to make the study of the phenomenon ‘messy’ or untidy, but this is a general characteristic of qualitative research, which is precisely the feature which makes the analysis richer and more grounded in reality (since the data have been obtained in naturalistic environments). Further research should study the precise intensity of the relationships outlined and the cumulative effect of the presence of a number of different intervening variables (or clusters of variables) in the same waiting event.

The following subsections examine some of the key findings of the present study in relationship to previous research on waiting on the internet and waiting in off-line services.

## The other main differences are:

1 While previous studies (Dellaert & Kahn, 1999; Bhatti et al., 2000) suggest a relationship between the type of task and the users’ tolerance of download delay, the present study suggests that it is not the type of task but the importance attributed to the task by the user (often determined by presence or absence of time pressures) that determines their behavioural and affective reactions to waiting. This was shown by the fact that no pattern of affective or behavioural reactions was found when subjects were undertaking similar tasks. Therefore, codings based on grouping types of tasks were discarded once the results of the initial open coding were analysed.

The thing is, I wasn’t really in a hurry to buy the book, I mean, it wasn’t urgent (if it was I would have gone crazy). I took it [the wait] quite well. I though that I could just leave it for now and come back to check again later or tomorrow.

Taking into account the importance of the [financial] operation and seeing that the system, for some reason, was not responding, I started to get anxious, especially as I didn’t have much time left.

2 Two of the factors previously identified are related to the actual or perceived length of the waiting time, i.e. time online in one single session and the real and perceived waiting time. However, in the present study users placed more importance on the achievement of the objective rather than the actual time this took. In fact, there was no evidence of a maximum time users are willing to wait on the internet. This time varied greatly (from a few seconds when waiting for a page to download, to weeks waiting for an answer of confirmation of a transaction) depending on a range of other factors such as the actual type of waiting situation and other intervening factors (for example, the importance of the task and the time pressure).

I knew it would take at least a day for my payment to show [as credit] on my account balance and I didn’t mind waiting at first. When 24 h had passed I started to get worried that my payment hadn’t been registered and I went crazy when I found out that my credit card had already been charged. I sent an email every hour to customer service until they must have got tired of reading my mails and fixed my balance.

After waiting for more than 15 min for the confirmation screen to appear, which felt like an eternity, because I was fighting against the clock, I got a screen message saying that because today was a bank holiday, I couldn’t make on-line reservations. A little more and I’d have a heart attack on the spot. The worst thing was I still hadn’t managed to book a flight.

3 The question of the position of the wait is considered in a number of previous studies (Sears et al., 1997; Dellaert & Kahn, 1999; Weinberg, 2000). However, in the present study there is no evidence of any noteworthy differences between users’ reaction to pre-process or in-process waiting (pre-process referring to ‘waiting to enter’ a web site and in-process referring to waiting which takes place once the user has entered the web site). Although pre-process and in-process are clearly two separate waiting situations, the position of the wait did not show effects on users’ behavioural or affective responses to waiting in the context of the internet. Users did not mention the position of the wait in their diaries, so it was not a conscious issue to them. However, it could be that the position of the wait produces different reactions without the participants being aware or conscious of this. Nevertheless, when the reactions of participants to waits with different positions are analysed, no trends or patterns in the data were found. The following examples illustrate how waiting at different parts of the process (pre-process, before entering the web site and in-process, at the end of the purchase) provoke a similar feeling of desperation in both participants. This demonstrates how the position of the wait is not a determining factor in the participant’s reactions.

Pre-process wait (trying to access a web site):

My utter desperation reached its high point when after 30 min I still couldn’t enter the web site, and on top of that, it was Saturday. What was I doing wasting my time in front of the computer when I could have been doing so many other things.

In-process wait (trying to book theatre seats):

I didn’t want to give up because I had already been at it for so long. I had found the theatre, checked availability, chosen my seats, entered all my information [. . .] obviously I was totally desperate at not being able make the booking. After everything I had done, I was left with such a sense of wasted time and effort. Finally, finally, I tried entering my password in block capital letters and believe it or not, it worked!

Despite the differences in intervening factors as highlighted above, there are also a number of similarities between previous research and the present study, as shown in Table 1. In particular, these factors are information on the wait (availability of feedback), expectations of waiting time (Expectations of time required) and attributing the blame for the wait (same).

The lack of Availability of feedback clearly provokes negative feelings on the part of the user. That is to say, when users are not informed of how long they will have to wait, they tend to become frustrated and impatient. However, the present study suggests that users place little confidence on the accuracy of the download indicators, especially those provided by web sites. Neither do users place much confidence in the indicators built into the internet browsers. Therefore, the absence of information on the waiting time leads to negative feelings. However, even when these indicators are present, in many cases, the participants in this study tended not to trust this information. Indeed, many users placed more trust in checking that their modem was flashing or that their internet connection indicated that data were being sent or received.

I checked to make sure that the little light on my modem was flashing on and off, an obvious sign that I was receiving information. It was flickering on and off so something was happening.

I could see that the little bar at the bottom of the screen was moving but I knew from before that this didn’t really mean much; it could stop at any time and give me an error. The screen was telling me not to hit ‘resend’ or I would end up paying twice, but five minutes had passed and nothing was happening.

Furthermore, while previous studies exclusively examine the provision of information on the wait in download delay, the present study examines the question of providing information in a wider range of waiting situations. The findings suggest that the provision of information on the length of the wait for a response to an enquiry to be received and for an online process to be confirmed is as important as indicating how long a web page will take to download.

It is important to emphasize that these differences may be partly explained by the fact that a very different perspective of the phenomenon of waiting has been taken in the present project. In this article, as mentioned earlier, the definition of waiting on the internet is much more than simply waiting for web pages to download (i.e. the definition employed in previous research). Furthermore, a different research methodology has been employed in this study. All previous research has been based on laboratory experiments. In addition, the sampling strategy of examining intensive internet users may further explain some of the differences between the present study and previous research. Therefore, the authors suggest that the approach taken in this study contributes to our knowledge and understanding of the phenomenon of waiting on the internet.

## Comparing the findings with previous research on waiting in services marketing

The section outlines the similarities and differences between the results of the current study and previous research on waiting in off-line services.

## Differences (waiting in traditional services)

1 In research in traditional services, the overriding conclusion is that the length of the wait in real time is the key determinant of how the user perceives the wait. The longer the wait, the more negative the perception of the wait by the consumer (Tom & Lucey, 1995; Davis & Heineke, 1998; Durrande-Moreau, 1999). However, the present study suggests that in waiting on the internet, the achievement of the user’s task or objective is more important than the actua waiting time in determining the affective response of the user.

When I had finished having supper I went back to check the computer. I didn’t have much hope that the booking would be made, so when I saw that it was actually done I felt a great sense of relief.

2 In traditional services, the reaction to the wait is significantly influenced by how long the individual expects to wait (Carmon, 1991; East et al., 1991; Chebat & Filiatrault, 1993). The present study suggests a similar relationship. However, the expectations of time required, i.e. the time the user considers necessary, reasonable, suitable or appropriate to achieve the task played a more important role than the time expected to spend at the task. In other words, even if a participant expected to wait some time in order to achieve his/her objective, he/she still felt that they were waiting if the expected time exceeded the ‘required’, suitable or desired time.

Disappointed. [name of company] is one of the most well-known online companies and I believe that they cannot allow themselves the luxury of having their servers saturated. They should have systems so that this doesn’t happen because it gives a very negative image to the user.

3 According to research in traditional services, the greater the importance the individual assigns to the service the less negative the reaction to the wait (Davis & Heineke, 1994; Jones & Peppiatt, 1996). However, the present study suggests that the greater the importance the task, the more likely the user will continue to try repeatedly to achieve his/her task, but also the greater the negative reaction to waiting.

I needed to register [with the web site] to get the information I needed. I kept at it because I thought it would be worth it to be able to impress my boss at our meeting. It mad me so angry when it didn’t work. I tried to calm down and keep trying but I was ready to chuck the PC out the window in rage.

4 In traditional services, the general assumption of authors is that in-process waiting is preferred to pre-process waiting, although research results are inconsistent. As mentioned earlier, the present study found no preference for either pre-process or in-process waiting (the position of the wait).

5 According to previous research, announcements about the duration of the wait before the wait begins may reduce the negative effects of the wait (Hui & Tse, 1996). However, the present study found no relationship between the provision of download time indicators and the reactions to the waiting situation, probably because, as mentioned earlier, participants placed little if any trust in the preciseness of download indicators. In addition, the analyses identified similar types of reactions to the wait in both types of situations, i.e. when information about the duration of the wait was and was not provided to the user.

In a sense I felt cheated because after paying, the screen message said that I would receive the tickets by post in three days, THREE DAYS! I was so pissed off because I thought I could go collect them the same day.

It’s been a week since I filled out the questionnaire in order to have access to the restricted contents, and I still haven’t received the username or password, even though it said that I would have access within 24–48 h. It’s really annoying but it’s nothing new. It’s what you come to expect.

## Similarities (waiting in traditional services)

1 Research in services marketing suggests that the mood of the customer before the wait will positively affect the reaction to the wait (Davis & Heineke, 1994; Chebat et al., 1995; Hui et al., 1997). That is, a customer in a good mood will not mind so much if he/she has to wait. Although the present study does not directly observe mood, the data suggest that the more relaxed and less time constrained the users, the more positive is their reaction to waiting.

2 Research in traditional services suggests that the time pressures of the customer will negatively affect the reaction to the wait (Mattson, 1982; Gross, 1987; East, 1997; Bennett, 1998). The same relationship was found in the present research project. Time pressure clearly increases the intensity of negative feelings towards waiting.

3 Previous research suggests that individuals who fill the wait or occupy themselves during the wait generally have a more agreeable wait (Taylor, 1994; Jones & Peppiatt, 1996; Durrande-Moreau, 1999). The present study found similar results in that users who fill the wait tend to have a less negative affective reaction to the wait. However, in many cases of waiting on the internet, the length of the wait is difficult to predict, therefore users do not always know if they will have sufficient time to fill the wait with another task.

4 In traditional services, individuals tend to imagine what is causing the delay and construct self-explanations (Durrande-Moreau, 1999). The present study clearly indicates that internet users also imagine the causes of the wait and even seek possible solutions (such as checking whether the modem is working, resending an email enquiry, coming back when they feel there will be less ‘traffic’ on the internet etc.).

5 In waiting in services, consumers who attribute the delay to causes other than the service provider are more likely to experience a positive waiting experience. (Folkes et al., 1987; East et al., 1991; Taylor, 1994; Tom & Lucey, 1995). The present study suggests that when the user attributes the wait to the service provider, the affective reaction will be negative (disappointment) and the behavioural action may include abandoning the web site and clicking on an alternative web site.

6 In traditional services, the negative effects of the wait may be alleviated by providing an apology (Sarel & Marmorstein, 1998). Although this category was very low in density in the present study, there is some evidence that apologies may decrease the negative effect of waiting on the internet.

7 Research in traditional services suggests that when the service is performed, the perception of the consumer of how it has been carried out will affect his/her subsequent perception of the wait (Katz et al., 1991; Davis & Heineke, 1994; Taylor, 1994). The present study found a similar result in that when the user successfully achieves his/her task, the outcome may influence his her affective response.

## CONCLUSIONS

As outlined in the previous section, a wide range of factors have an impact on how users react to waiting on the internet. A number of possible relationships between these variables and users’ behavioural and affective reactions have been suggested. Nonetheless, as mentioned, further research should examine the precise intensity of these relationships and the cumulative effect of the presence of a number of different intervening variables in the same waiting situation (as is the reality of online waiting).

Indeed, in most cases, a number of intervening factors are acting on the waiting situation at the same time. In other words, groups or ‘bundles’ of intervening variables impact upon feelings and behaviour. This makes the study of the phenomenon ‘messier’ but at the same time it is precisely this feature which makes the qualitative analysis richer.

Further research would benefit from the development of an integrated theoretical framework of the ‘whole’ phenomenon of waiting on the internet. This framework could incorporate the entire range of types of internet waiting situations (Ryan & Valverde, 2005), the behavioural and affective reactions of internet users in waiting situations (Ryan, 2004) and the factors which determine these reactions, as explained in this article. In addition, the development of this framework would benefit from the inclusion of different types of internet users (in terms of intensity of usage, level of experience with the internet etc.) as they may display different types of online behaviours.

There are a number of implications for internet practitioners and e-service management from this research project. Probably most striking is the need to understand the e-service experience from the e-consumer’s point of view. It is important to identify and understand the issues that really matter to e-consumers. If the achievement of objectives (online) is of greater importance than the length of the time online then efforts should be focused on improving a range of aspects related to web site functionality and not just simply on making web pages download more quickly (although this is also a priority).

E-service managers should be aware of the factors that determine how their consumers behave online. They should also identify which of these factors can be managed or controlled in order to improve the quality of the e-service. For example, it is unlikely that e-retailers can affect the ‘time pressures’ of their customers, but they can work with web site designers to speed-up purchase and check out processes and to avoid time-outs or server saturation. They may also work with customer services to increase the efficiency in replying to customer enquiries.

Finally, it is important to point out that waiting on the internet deals with just one specific aspect of the functionality of the electronic delivery of services on the internet. Nevertheless, as illustrated in the introductory paragraphs, waiting has a significant effect on the commercial development of the web, and especially so, given that in this competitive environment the competition is but ‘a click away’. Indeed, as the overall speed of internet accessibility improves over time (e.g. high-speed wireless technology, Chau & Lai, 2003), it is likely that delays caused by technical and functional limitations will be less evident than online waiting caused by limitations in other areas of the business such as the design of the online service, online customer response systems, physical product delivery and logistic systems and the overall functionality of the web site. In this sense, Marketing and MIS must work together in order to identify the issues which matter to e-consumers and combine marketing and MIS know-how in order to provide quality e-services.
