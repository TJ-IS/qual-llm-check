## Abstract

In this study, we investigate information system (IS) failures by leveraging a novel construct— dissonant relational multiplexity (RM)—to develop a unique perspective of these failures. Dissonant RM exists when two organizational stakeholders have multiple types of relationships that are in conflict. To investigate the salience of dissonant RM in IS failures, we use a case study combined with the analysis procedures of the grounded theory methodology (GTM) to examine a major failure in enterprise resource planning (ERP) implementation. Our analysis and theorization highlight that RM became increasingly dissonant in the relationships between key organizational stakeholders because of a shift in technological frames that represent cognitive perceptions about technology. Further, a key insight from our findings is that the move to dissonant RM occurred through a process that we term relational unbalancing. In addition, we also found evidence of an opposing relational balancing process that was used by stakeholders to address dissonant RM. Such stakeholder efforts were often undermined by inherent constraints in the implemented technology. The relational balancing efforts were not productive, and the dissonant RM continued to exist, ultimately contributing to the failure of the ERP implementation. Our study shows that IS failures are characterized by elements of both determinism and indeterminism, are undoubtedly sociotechnical in nature, and are shaped by technological constraints and stakeholder perceptions of those constraints. From a practical standpoint, our study highlights the importance of managing multiplex stakeholder relationships in an IS implementation process, especially when the multiplexity is shaped by the technology.

Keywords: IS Failures, Relational Multiplexity, Sociotechnical Perspective, Grounded Theory Methodology (GTM)

## 1 Introduction

Information systems (IS) implementations often fail to deliver their promised benefits, causing substantial organizational losses (Lei et al., 2022; Triche & Walden, 2018). These failures occur when a system being implemented does not meet the expected requirements and is incapable of furthering organizational objectives (Dwivedi et al., 2015). Obviously, there is a need to better understand the phenomenon of IS implementation failures (henceforth, “IS failures”) so they can be effectively prevented. Researchers have called for the incorporation of diverse and novel perspectives within the IS failures literature (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014) and have highlighted the need for new theorizing on this phenomenon (Baghizadeh et al., 2020).

Our study answers such calls for novel perspectives on IS failures. To chart our contributory path, we first develop an in-depth understanding of the limitations in the IS failures literature and use it to problematize our study (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011; Chatterjee & Davison, 2021). A review of the IS failures literature (summarized in Table 1) allowed us to understand three dominant assumptions characterizing this stream of work. Assumption 1 is that it is possible to understand causal factors that can contribute to IS failure (or success). Studies based on this assumption tend to have a rather singular focus on understanding these factors (Shao et al., 2016; Tan et al., 2016). For example, Benaroch and Chernobai (2017) showcase how IT governance factors cause IS failure. In addition, studies subscribing to Assumption 1 often use the well-known Delone and McLean (2003) model of IS success to understand IS success/failure, such as the study by Scott et al. (2016), who measure egovernance success using the concept of public value integrated with the Delone and McLean model.

Assumption 2 is that social constructions of technology are relevant in shaping and understanding IS failures/successes. There is substantial work on this theme, often using qualitative approaches. For example, Lim et al. (2011) discussed the social construction of project risk through successive crises that emerged in IT implementation. Conboy (2010) represented the challenge of tight budgetary control in IS projects using project control theory and Bartis and Mitev (2008) showed how stakeholders’ ascribing various meanings to an IT system contributed to IS failure.

Assumption 3, which is often not explicitly stated but inferable nonetheless, is that IS failures are primarily social phenomena where sociopolitical factors (e.g., decision-making and organizational culture) play a predominant role. For example, Gengatharen and Standing (2005) discussed a framework of such factors influencing the success or failure of small and medium enterprises receiving government support. Lesca and Caron-Fasan (2008) highlighted socio-organizational factors (e.g., stakeholder experience) contributing to IS failure, and Ravishankar et al. (2011) discussed the role of subcultural alignment in IS success.

However, each of these three dominant assumptions in the literature can be challenged on multiple grounds (please see Table 1 for a summary). For example, Assumption 1 can be critiqued for being mechanistic and deterministic (Bartis & Mitev, 2008). It also generally treats the mechanisms of failure as a “black box” (Dwivedi et al., 2015) and has a limited focus on only the outcomes of success or failure—thereby often ignoring the advice that IS phenomena (including IS implementations) need processual unpacking for finer insights (Orlikowski, 1993). Assumption 2 can be critiqued for being a primarily representational perspective, where “the essential purpose of information systems is to provide representations of individuals’ or groups’ perceptions of real-world phenomena” (Burton-Jones et al., 2017, p. 1308).<sup>1</sup> The key drawback of the representational approach is that it is “premised on essential properties that are deemed to exist independently of human action or interaction” (Schultze & Orlikowski, 2010, p. 814). Therefore, human action/interaction is an important factor that often escapes the representational perspective of IS failures (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014). Given that an organization is ultimately a “relational setting” in which “the pattern of relationships within an institutional context” shapes their actions (Raffaelli & Glynn, 2014, p. 541), a purely representational approach can be found wanting in the study of organizational phenomena, including IS failures. Finally, Assumption 3 practically eschews the role of technology in shaping IS successes/failures (Mueller et al., 2019; Shimada et al., 2019). By primarily focusing on the socioorganizational angle, this assumption overlooks the enduring legacy of the sociotechnical perspective in IS research and practice (Sarker et al., 2019).

The critiques of the three dominant perspectives in the IS failure literature led us to consider and leverage a novel construct, relational multiplexity (RM), which addresses many of the issues raised in the critiques (see Table 1). Formally, RM is defined as always existing between two entities (i.e., in a dyad) and “is the number of [coexisting] diverse types of ties between these [two entities]” (Tuli et al., 2010, p. 38). It exists when the relationships between two entities include multiple types—not merely multiple instances—of activities or conversations (Mesch & Talmud, 2006).<sup>2</sup> Multiplex relationships can be aligned or conflicting and may accordingly lead to different outcomes (Sytch & Tatarynowicz, 2014).

Specifically, an aligned set of multiplex relationships showcases resonant RM (Howe & Jin, 2022); in contrast, a misaligned set, where both positive and negative relationships coexist, indicates dissonant RM (Brass, 2022; Brennecke, 2020). We primarily concentrate on dissonant RM as it is relevant to IS failures.<sup>3</sup>

RM allows us to contribute because it is central to the development of a theory of IS failures that addresses each of the three critiques discussed earlier. It allows us to dismantle Assumption 1, informing us that due to changing stakeholder relationships, the real causes of IS failures cannot be ascertained a priori, and they often emerge due to the volatility of such relationships. Therefore, the IS implementation process is neither purely deterministic nor can it be purely “blackboxed.” RM allows us to comprehend and theorize about this emergent IS failure by allowing us to focus on the changing stakeholder relationships. To elaborate, by using RM, we highlight how most aspects of an IS implementation are not “given” and arise only because there are unique and largely unpredictable actions and interactions undertaken by the stakeholders along the way. These actions and interactions continuously recalibrate the relationships between the stakeholders, revise their mental models about technology and other stakeholders, and induce new and often unpredictable action. Thus, RM allowed us to capture the continuously evolving and often unpredictable causal landscape between action and cognition in our case study.

RM addresses Assumption 2 by refocusing on the organization as a relational setting. In contrast to the representational perspective, RM embraces a performative perspective that subscribes to a relational ontology (Keevers et al., 2012) where relations between entities, whether human or nonhuman, are the primary focus and what the relations objectively represent (i.e., a formal supervisor-supervisee relationship), or are subjectively construed to represent (i.e., an imagined ally), take a relative back seat. In a performative perspective, “the world is always in flux and transformation; identities, functionalities, theories, and practices are materialized and transformed along the way” (Garud & Gehman, 2019, p. 681). It is a perspective that “view[s] reality not as composed of fixed and independent entities, but as constituted by fluid, dynamic, multiple, and emergent phenomena” (Schultze & Orlikowski, 2010, p. 814). Consistent with performativity, RM highlights that human/nonhuman entities are meaningful primarily in a relational context of enactment and action (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014):

…everyday doings and sayings [i.e., actions] … are constitutive of social life. Of interest here is the set of activities and interactions engaged in by various actors and how these are related and mobilized to produce certain effects. Such actions and interactions are not independent or abstract, but deeply connected and grounded, only attaining meaning and significance in the situations at hand. Performative definitions thus shift the focus away from presuming the existence of independent objects with fixed or given properties and boundaries, to a focus on practices that enact particular relational and situated boundaries, entities, identities, trajectories, and effects. (Schultze & Orlikowski, 2010, p. 814, emphasis added)

In fact, RM allows us to address documented problems of studying IS failures with a primarily representational perspective (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014):

The problem with representationalism, as science studies scholars have long argued, is the assumption that representations and the objects they represent are independently existing entities… That is, assessments of IS success as facts or perceptions exist as entities separate from IS as objects of assessment. What is particularly striking is the faith in representations, either as measures or subjective interpretations of IS success, as opposed to that which is represented. What seems to be hidden from view is that IS projects and implemented systems as objects of assessment are not given and fixed but are themselves performed as objects by the processes of assessments (p. 562, emphases added).

Specifically, RM allowed us to develop a process theory that links continuously changing cognition and mental models in a relational setting (e.g., technology frames, representing cognitive perceptions about technology, perceived coercion in relationships, etc.)

with dynamically evolving action (e.g., initial actions in favor of technology, then resistance, followed by actions achieving temporary alignment, and finally the substantial degeneration of the process). “Viewing a process in terms of multiple actors with mutual influence [i.e., considering the actors in a relational setting] creates an understanding of the dynamic phenomenon” (Niederman, 2021, p. 79), and RM allowed us to develop such an understanding.

Regarding Assumption 3, our study, anchored in RM, allowed us to deliver a sociotechnical perspective of IS failures. RM arose in our study not only due to the sociopolitical factors (e.g., power and trust issues) but also due to the technology constraints and the consequent cognitive understanding of technology in the form of technological frames. Using RM and conceptually pinning it to the interaction between sociopolitical factors and technology created a completely different contributory space and a sociotechnical theory of IS failures. This is in marked contrast to the received, primarily social understanding of IS failures. By using RM in conjunction with technology frames and technology constraints, we move the understood sociopolitical space of IS failures in the existing literature into a predominantly sociotechnical space.

Informed by our problematization and arguments thus far, our research objective was to understand the salience of dissonant RM to IS implementation failures. Specifically, we investigated three research questions:

RQ1: Where and under what circumstances does dissonant RM (a) not arise and (b) arise in IS implementations?

RQ2: When is dissonant RM accentuated, sustained, or resolved?

RQ3: Why and how does dissonant RM lead to IS failures?

To better understand the rise, evolution, and consequences of dissonant RM in IS failures, we investigated the implementation of an enterprise resource planning (ERP) system. Empirically, we used an analysis based on grounded theory methodology (GTM) (e.g., Strauss & Corbin, 1990) within a casebased research framework (Walsham, 2006; Yin, 1994). Our insights revealed how dissonant RM emerges through stakeholder actions and how technological perceptions and constraints play a crucial role in its shaping and reshaping, ultimately leading to IS failure. We developed propositions capturing our insights to accentuate the usefulness of our study, especially in terms of developing constructive knowledge for a generalized understanding (Sarker et al., 2018a, 2018b) of IS implementation failure.

## 2 Dissonant RM: Its Relevance to IS Failures and Its Conceptual Underpinnings

## 2.1 Dissonant RM: An Appropriate Concept to Understand IS Failures

Dissonant RM allows researchers to unpack some of the key relational problems that occur in IS implementations. <sup>4</sup> It provides a lens through which multiple conflicting relationships between the same organizational stakeholders can be understood, thereby providing a nuanced view of the relational spillover effect (Rogan, 2014)—in which one relationship between a dyad of organizational stakeholders affects other relationships between the same (or sometimes, different) stakeholders. In the context of IS implementations, it is necessary to account for technology-induced conflicting relationships to understand how two entities can jointly shape outcomes. Dissonant RM thus allows researchers to move beyond the limited relational approach of analyzing only a single type of stakeholder relationship (Ansari et al., 2011).

Dissonant RM highlights that an agent has varying relationships with other agents and recognizes stakeholder dependencies and their domino effects— thereby providing a premise that goes beyond a purely representational perspective. RM’s focus on changing relationships also allows for investigations into the varying role of technology, particularly in terms of how it impacts stakeholder relationships. As IS are defined within relationships among stakeholders (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014), problems emanating from these relationships are crucial to developing knowledge about why IS failures occur. In fact, stakeholders involved in an IS implementation can participate in multiple, often conflicting relationships, largely due to the nature of the central technology (Sarker et al., 2006). In this context, dissonant RM fosters a better understanding of the relational perspective of IS implementation by factoring in unique and co-present tensions and conflicts that can exist between two organizational stakeholders (Brennecke, 2020). Dissonant RM thus facilitates a sociotechnical understanding of the phenomenon of IS failures, as discussed earlier.

Our leveraging of dissonant RM is consistent with the acknowledgment that stakeholder relationships are intricately linked to IS implementations (Gebauer et al., 2023; Venkatesh et al., 2011). Such relationships are often played out through power struggles (Pouloudi et al., 2016), stakeholder convergence/divergence (Hu et al., 2024), stakeholder politics and resistance (Müller et al., 2017), or stakeholder control of resources (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019). Inspired by this body of work, we use RM to forward a unique performative take on the evolution of stakeholder relationships and their centrality to the IS implementation process.

## 2.2 Conceptual Underpinnings in the Emergence of RM: Balanced/Unbalanced Dyads

Dissonant RM is often characterized by unbalanced dyads, which are pairs of actors that exhibit both enabling and constraining relationships (Sytch & Tatarynowicz, 2014). In contrast, a balanced overall relationship is one in which either enabling or constraining relationships exist but not both (Shipilov et al., 2014). Past research has expanded on the role of RM in shaping balanced/unbalanced dyads—delineating the structural properties and mechanisms that lead to evolving relationships (Ahuja et al., 2012; Zaheer et al., 2010). In this study, we draw on Zaheer et al. (2010) to understand the concise set of mechanisms potentially relevant to RM evolution. Prominent among these are power/control, trust, and signaling. Specifically, power/control is a fundamental issue in dyads and is thus closely linked to RM. Power differentials are crucial because they can be both formal and informal, thus giving rise to multiplex relationships (Ibarra & Andrews, 1993), often through stakeholders’ negative reactions to the exercise of power. Moreover, power is crucial to understanding how negotiation patterns evolve between organizational actors (Yan & Gray, 1994), and how these patterns subsequently shape multiplexity (Lee & Lee, 2015). In fact, IS failures are often the setting for intense power struggles (Silva & Fulk, 2012)—introducing multiplexity into existing relationship dyads.

Trust between organizational actors has an impact on multiplexity because it is an outcome of existing relationships and further modifies them (Ahuja et al., 2012). An additional trusting relationship between two actors who already share other organizational ties (e.g., as colleagues) can introduce relational multiplexity (Isett & Provan, 2005). Here, the actors have two relationships: professional and trusting. Over time, if their commitments are not fulfilled, it can cause the creation of a distrusting relationship (Moody et al., 2017), thus resulting in dissonant RM.

Finally, mutual signals between organizational actors can change their relationships and, depending on the signals, reinforce multiplexity. Similarly, signaling shapes interaction patterns between actors (Leibbrandt & List, 2015), thus reinforcing, weakening, or modifying existing relationships and possibly giving rise to multiplexity. In this context, an organizational change is often a negotiation process between actors in an organizational structure (Volkoff et al., 2007). Depending on whether actors generate positive or negative signals during the negotiation process, this signaling can foster multiplexity.<sup>5</sup>

## 2.3 Technological Perceptions and Their Role in Shaping RM

Given our focus on the role of dissonant RM in IS implementation and our use of a sociotechnical perspective, we need to explain how perceptions about the technology being implemented evolve, interact with, and ultimately shape RM. Understanding changing perceptions about technology requires a sensemaking perspective, according to which organizational actors try to understand organizational reality in terms of the technology and the institutional courses of action related to it.

Sensemaking regarding technology in IS implementation is powerfully captured by the concept of technological frames (Davidson, 2002; Orlikowski & Gash, 1994), which comprise the assumptions, expectations, and knowledge actors employ to understand the role of technology and its use (Mishra & Agarwal, 2010). Organizational actors’ perceptions can vary, thereby shaping and being shaped by the ways with which the actors relate to technology (Lin & Silva, 2005). In turn, technological frames evolve through these interactions and lead to the ultimate success or failure of the IS implementation (Mishra & Agarwal, 2010). Thus, these frames are cognitively constructed perceptions of technology that are directly affected by its objective performance (or underperformance) (Luna-Reyes et al., 2005).

We argue that one of the most important factors in understanding how organizations adopt and use technology lies in organizational stakeholders’ perceptions of technology—i.e., technological frames. Technological frames are an important ingredient in our conception of RM. Literature on technological frames has multiple traditions. Some of the early work following the tradition of Bijker (1995) considers technological frames largely from the social angle (Davidson, 2006), while other traditions, such as the sociocognitive tradition, capture and understand how organizational stakeholders interpret IT and act accordingly (Chiasson & Davidson, 2005).

The same technology can invoke varying frames among different stakeholders. If the technological frames of two or more stakeholders match, then they are congruent; otherwise, they are incongruent. Formally, frame congruence implies that similar expectations exist regarding the role of technology in organizations, how it is used, and how frequently it is used. In contrast, frame incongruence implies salient differences in expectations, assumptions, or knowledge (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). When technological frames are incongruent, organizations encounter challenges in IS implementation (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994) due to changing stakeholder relationships contributing to multiplexity. Due to the salience of technological frames, organizations often focus on addressing frame incongruence and moving toward congruence; however, this may not be possible, leading to undesirable outcomes.

In this context, a parsimonious set of technological frames that can exist (Mishra & Agarwal, 2010) are the “benefit” frame, which views the technology to be implemented as a valuable addition; the “threat” frame, which views it as a challenge to existing organizational processes; and the “adjustment” frame, which captures the mutual adjustments between the technology and the organization’s social structures. Consequently, if one stakeholder (in a dyad) embraces, for example, the “benefit” frame and the other embraces the “threat” frame, then frame incongruence arises.

These frames allow us to incorporate the fact that frames may be different across organizational stakeholders (e.g., “benefit” vs. “threat” frames) and that stakeholders can also “modify [i.e., adjust] their frames [if different] toward a common frame” (Azad & Faraj, 2013, p. 122). Very simply, technology frames allow us to focus on what organizational stakeholders consider when thinking about technology—for example, whether the technology is valuable for the organization (a “benefit” frame) or not (a “threat” frame). These differing perceptions create new relationships between organizational stakeholders. Coupled with other preexisting (and continuing) relationships, such emerging perceptions can create RM.

## 3 The Case Study: Failure of the ERP-Plus Implementation

We followed a case-based research framework (Yin, 1994) guided by our conceptual underpinnings. Our case study investigates dissonant RM in the implementation of an ERP system called “ERP-Plus” in a large public university system. Our study focuses on the ERP implementation failure using dissonant RM as the theoretical lens.

The implementation of ERP-Plus took place within the “Diverse University System” (DUS; we use pseudonyms for anonymity). Four universities comprise the DUS: Alpha, Beta, Gamma, and Delta. Alpha is a comprehensive undergraduate, doctoral, and professional degree-granting institution. Beta is an upper-level institution with more limited graduate programs. Gamma is an open-enrollment institution with preparatory, undergraduate, and graduate programs. Finally, Delta has undergraduate and graduate programs. DUS is headed by the Board of Regents (BOR); The BOR is responsible for making high-level decisions, such as approving funding. The DUS chancellor reports to the BOR, serves as Alpha’s president and, together with the vice chancellors, comprises the University System Administration (UA), which manages system-wide operations. Each remaining university is headed by a president who oversees a group of faculty-and-staff (FS) managers. The presidents report to the BOR and are largely autonomous in managing their universities, although they are subject to DUS policies.

## 3.1 Case History and Justification for Studying Implementation Failure

In 1995, the BOR issued a mandate requiring the UA to select and implement one integrated IS, which was meant to replace the existing systems to achieve better functionality and cost-effectiveness. Following a selection process, the DUS entered into a contract with a vendor, ERPCorp, for the purchase of applications to support various university functions. The BOR then charged the UA with overseeing the implementation. To fulfill the BOR’s mandate, the UA assembled an implementation team consisting of FS managers, developers, analysts, and others. The implementation began in 1999 with the first step being planning. However, eventually, the Beta administration had problems with the implementation (e.g., difficulties accessing the training version of ERP-Plus) and issued an ultimatum to the UA. The FS would stop performing their critical role in the implementation (e.g., testing results of data conversions) indefinitely unless the UA responded to their list of concerns. This brought a stop to the implementations so that these concerns could be addressed, which also resulted in a warning from the chancellor against the use of future ultimatums. Finally, by late 2001, the implementations of all ERP-Plus applications had taken place. While those for the finance and HR components went relatively smoothly, the ERP-Plus student administration component (hereinafter, simply referred to as ERP-Plus) was unstable. Many embarrassing system-related problems—such as million-dollar student bills and junk enrollment data— surfaced, leading to budget overruns. Such problems concerned the BOR, and by late 2002, the UA was ordered to present a status report, for which a team of its members and the FS managers were assembled to give this presentation. In the end, they convinced the BOR to continue funding the implementation. By 2004, upgraded versions of the ERP-Plus applications had been installed at most DUS universities. However, problems surfaced again, and while some were fixed, many ERP-Plus components, such as student financial aid functionality, remained unstable.

Nevertheless, the UA remained committed to the ERP-Plus implementation and hired consultants to evaluate the process around 2004. They recommended that it continue with key modifications to system rollout and change management. However, administrations at Alpha and Gamma, where the ERP-Plus applications were to be implemented next, did not share the UA’s commitment to ERP-Plus. Specifically, the Alpha administration continued to improve the functionality of its legacy system, ManageR, while the Gamma administration gained sufficient support to implement a competing student administration system, Flag. Later, in 2007, DUS began the “go live” of the latest version of ERP-Plus but continued to face serious problems (such as nonfunctional interfaces for student inquiries) that could not be fully fixed. By 2008, the students and FS managers remained largely unsupportive of ERP-Plus, and the implementation project was primarily viewed by major stakeholder groups as a failure.

## 3.2 Data Collection

During the study, 33 initial interviews and 34 followup interviews were conducted (see Appendix A, Table A1). Our initial data collection was conducted in 2003, and two follow-ups were conducted in 2005 and 2007. We selected interview participants using the popular snowballing approach to qualitative research, which affords the benefits of leveraging networks of interviewees and also possible interviewee referrals (Parker et al., 2019), to increase the variety of interviewed stakeholders. Subsequently, we used semi-structured interviews to allow participants the freedom to offer their own interpretations. Follow-up interviews were then used to clarify the responses to questions that were asked or to ask additional questions prompted by the initial interviews. When permitted by the participants, interviews were recorded to facilitate verbatim transcriptions. Written notes were taken to record the interviews and supplement the recordings. To complement the interviews, we collected relevant documents and artifacts suggested by the interviewees (newspaper articles, minutes of meetings, emails, etc.) (see Appendix A, Table A2).

## 3.3 Data Analysis

For our data analysis, we considered the relationships between collective entities that were stakeholders in the ERP-Plus implementation.<sup>6</sup> The level of analysis is the “relationship” between the entities, primarily categorized as being supportive or dissenting, which is where the dissonance (conflict) between them acquires salience.

## 3.3.1 Analysis Procedures

There are two leading approaches to analyzing data collected from case-based research. The first is an open subjective approach, as described by Walsham (2006), and the second involves the adoption of a more structured procedure (Bligh et al., 2004). We chose the latter and used GTM, because it is essentially grounded in empirical analysis and also provides structured guidelines that enhance the richness of conceptual development (Wiesche et al., 2017). Additionally, it embraces an abductive research logic, which is used to develop research concepts not merely through a mechanistic coding process but also through the intertwined cognitive processes of discovery and justification (e.g., Reichertz, 2007).

Birks et al. (2013) identified some key characteristics of GTM-based analysis, which we followed to ensure the appropriateness of our data collection and analyses (Appendix B, Table B1). In view of these criteria, our study is best characterized as following the philosophical and procedural GTM guidelines for data analysis. Notably, there are variants of GTM that embody differing assumptions and perspectives about its practice (Wiesche et al., 2017). Thus, researchers should choose the GTM variant that is most appropriate for their study. In this study, we used the GTM variant known as the “Straussian approach” (Corbin & Strauss, 2008; Strauss & Corbin, 1990), which analyzes data using three different coding steps: open, axial, and selective coding. Appendix B presents the extensive procedural details of this analysis, as well as a discussion and illustrative examples of our coding journey through open coding, axial coding, and selective coding, allowing us to depict a transparent trace of the analysis process and the logic underlying it.

## 3.3.2 Analytical Considerations in Applying GTM

GTM analysis procedures are guided by two basic considerations essential to theory generation: theoretical sensitivity and constant comparison (Sarker et al., 2018a, 2018b). The first refers to the ability of researchers to develop insights and thus be “able to pick up on relevant issues, events, and happenings in the data” (Corbin & Strauss, 2008, p. 32). Further, theoretical sensitivity helps researchers to be immersed in the investigated phenomena, be knowledgeable of general ideas related to the phenomena, and understand the context of the theory development (Urquhart et al., 2010)—all of which help in identifying subtle nuances in the data (Corbin & Strauss, 2008).

GTM procedures recommend coupling theoretical sensitivity with comparative analysis. Strauss and Corbin’s approach to coding recommends two types of comparisons: constant and theoretical. Constant comparison involves comparing different incidents, events, and behavioral patterns evidenced in the data; that is, researchers search for similarities and differences in the data to develop classifications of conceptual elements within the themes capturing the phenomenon (Zahedi et al., 2006). Such comparison is essential to the formulation of both higher-order categories as well as their properties and dimensions (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). Meanwhile, theoretical comparison enables researchers to understand the significance of particular incidents or patterns within the data (Corbin & Strauss, 2008). Such comparison is aided by developing memos, which are artifacts linking researchers’ understanding of the data to the analysis process. Appendix B provides examples of such memo-taking and constant comparison (see Tables B2, B3; Figure B3). In our study, memo-taking was often supplemented by developing tabular representations of theoretical relationships and insights that emerged from the memomaking process, particularly during selective coding. This aided in the final emergence of our theory. We provide illustrative examples of such emergence in Appendix Tables C1 and C2.

Consistent with recommendations for GTM-based analysis, our theoretical sensitivity took the form of a pre-understanding of the literature and the conceptual underpinnings of RM. Used in conjunction with constant comparison, our efforts allowed us to explore and identify salient concepts over multiple rounds of data analysis, culminating in our theory of IS failures (Figure 1). During our analysis, we ensured that our use of theoretical perspectives did not contravene the core values of GTM-based analysis by avoiding steps, such as forcing preexisting theory on the data for the purpose of proving or disproving it (Suddaby, 2006). For example, although the concepts of mechanisms of RM evolution and technological frames sensitized us to how multiplexity is shaped by stakeholders’ perceptions of technology, the specifics of the relationships emerged from our data and were not theoretically forced.

## 4 Findings: The Salience of Dissonant RM in IS Implementation Failure

We developed a narrative-based process theory that captures the unfolding and implications of RM related to IS failure (see Figure 1). The analysis of the empirical data helped us understand the dynamics and salience of dissonant RM within the IS implementation. Table 2 depicts the stakeholders and the salient technologies in the case study. Overall, our analysis hinges upon the identification of two primary relational states: (1) resonant and (2) dissonant. These states represent stable descriptions of social collectives (e.g., project stakeholders) for a particular period of time (Chakraborty et al., 2010).

The resonant and dissonant relational states differ with respect to the nature of dyadic relationships and the congruence of technology frames in each state. The former is a state where the relational dyads are balanced and their technology frames are congruent. Meanwhile, the latter represents a state where the relational dyads are unbalanced because of dissonant RM and the technology frames are incongruent. In addition, we found evidence of a more dynamic quasistate where local balanced dyads and technology frame congruence existed amid a general environment of unbalanced dyads and incongruent technology frames. We call this the transitory relational state.

Our understanding of the implementation captures the transitions between the states at various stages of the implementation process. The transitions took place for various reasons, most notably due to the variations in the technological frames among different organizational actors. This variation led to the emergence and evolution of dissonant RM. Key factors that shaped the emergence and evolution of RM were the mechanisms of trust, power/control, and signaling, especially as enforced by the salient actor (UA) during the implementation. Overall, most of the mechanisms contributing to dissonant RM had two organizational stakeholders in multiple (conflicting) co-present relationships between them. <sup>7</sup> Coupled with incongruent technological frames, such conflicts created further dissonance in the multiplex relationships, thus compromising the implementation.

## 4.1 The Emergence of the Resonant Relational State: Balancing Dyads and Aligning Frames

The resonant relational state represents a stable configuration of dyadic relationships between the stakeholders and aligned technology frames that emerged in the initial stages of the ERP-Plus implementation. This emergence was heavily dependent on how the key stakeholders formed dyadic relationships with the other stakeholders and how these relationships evolved. Thus, here, we elaborate on the dynamics that led to the emergence of the resonant relational state.

The key stakeholders and perhaps the most influential actors in the beginning stages of the ERP-Plus implementations were the BOR and the UA. The idea of initiating such a DUS-wide implementation project stemmed from the BOR’s identification of key problems in the existing legacy systems in 1995:

We … understand that, in order for all the universities to be on the same system, the way that we keep our books and the way that we register and enroll students will have to change.

This mandate, which symbolizes the BOR’s authority, was the first step in defining the strategic vision of the DUS actors regarding technology—the need for a consolidated, standardized IS that spanned the DUS.

To further propagate this vision, the BOR leveraged their hierarchical relationship with the UA by tasking it with garnering support from the member universities and developing a climate of enthusiasm about the proposed system. This relationship between the BOR and the UA was aptly summed up by an FS member: “The regents say what we [member universities] will do. The [UA] says how we will do it.”

At this point, the UA appeared to be loyal to and enthusiastic about the BOR’s vision, and they enacted their vision in two ways. First, the UA engaged with the leadership of the member universities, obtained their support, and co-opted them into the BOR’s strategic technology vision. Second, they tried to extend this alignment beyond the member-university leadership by co-opting the FS and the students of the DUS. This was done by formally involving the FS in the implementation; they would either help implement ERP-Plus or be trained in its use while performing their regular duties.

At this juncture, the UA attempted to align another key constituent, the DUS students, with their strategic vision.

To this end, the UA first aggressively marketed the capabilities of the proposed system—comparing it favorably with the existing ManageR system—to students, and then co-opting the FS by promoting the benefits of the new system (as shown in a newsletter):

ERP-Plus … provides a virtual campus for students … allowing the institution to broaden its reach, communicate more effectively, and cultivate relationships with … its constituents.

To provide students with a sense of ease and to inform them about the impending move to ERP-Plus, the UA instituted policies on how phone calls from them were to be answered by the FS members: “Hello, this is ERP-Plus…” (to familiarize students with the system). Additionally, they also projected ERP-Plus as the technology of the future for DUS: “Everyone who currently uses the … [old] system will change over to ERP-Plus.” The UA also commissioned promotional materials, which provided a rosy picture projecting ERP-Plus as a solution to problems that were currently compromising DUS objectives:

ERP-Plus is the primary technology tool being implemented to support … studentrelated functions, outstanding customer service delivery, and effective use of modern technology.

In the end, the intensive efforts of the UA convinced the major stakeholders and they were able to successfully instill a “benefit” frame for ERP-Plus. For example, during initial training sessions, the FS perceived ERP-Plus to be a simple-to-use but effective tool: “It’s just a web browser.” Further, as a UA member recalled, the FS were initially favorable to ERP-Plus and had accepted the “benefit” frame: “The faculty … were very positive about ERP-Plus.”

Upon examining the evolution of dyadic relationships during this time period, a few observations stand out. First, the dyadic relationships and their alignment with the objectives of the project were imbued with expressions of power embedded in the DUS authority structure. This is evident from the BOR’s mandate and the UA’s subsequent attempt at onboarding other key stakeholders. Thus, the dyadic relationships were characterized by a top-down initiation (e.g., the UA and other universities, the UA and FS). Here, it is important to note that the key stakeholders readily aligned themselves with the overall strategic vision. Thus, although there was asymmetry in the dyadic relationships due to the power differences, there were hints of the development of trust, which leads to positive and balanced relationships. This is similar to the “swift trust” that is often seen at the initial stages of many organizational projects (Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999).

Notably, expressions of power were characterized by instances of positive signaling in the dyadic relationships. For example, the UA’s approach to coopting the other universities into the system is an example of the positive signaling that hinted at equal partnerships within the relationships despite the inherent power differences. Additionally, both the UA and the BOR leveraged the general positivity of the long-term relationships that existed between the stakeholders. This is not surprising, as the literature has previously identified the positive effect of good historical relationships of aligning stakeholders and developing shared frames about a technology project (Chakraborty et al., 2010).

Consequently, by this time in the implementation, all the key stakeholders had quickly developed congruent technology frames, which were mainly characterized by a “benefit” frame. From a relational state perspective, there were balanced dyadic relationships and congruent technology frames at this point. Further, there was a sense of resonance and harmony within the dyads, and this was reflected in the steady and onschedule progress of the project in terms of the technology solution chosen and the initiation of the implementation. ## 4.2 The Shift to a Dissonant Relational State

As noted, the initial period of the ERP-Plus implementation was characterized by a balanced relational state, which exhibited two aspects of dyadic relationships. First, they were balanced, and second, the dyads had a shared frame of reference through congruences in terms of individual technology frames—all stakeholders subscribed to the “benefit” frame for ERP-Plus. However, as the implementation progressed, this initial harmony and resonance toward shared technology objectives changed. These were often reactions to certain key episodes, such as disagreement within the dyads and obstacles that delayed implementation. These emerging challenges led the transition to an unbalanced or dissonant relational state, with unbalanced dyads and misaligned technology frames (please see Figure 3).

## 4.2.1 Relational Unbalancing

A key event in the implementation project was the first rollout of ERP-Plus, which was disappointing in several different ways: reduced scope, difficulties in working with implemented functionalities, and the possibility of expensive delays. An immediate outcome of this was a shift in the role played by the BOR, as the BOR became concerned that the implementation was becoming too expensive and was failing to deliver on its promised benefits. They thus threatened to withdraw their support, which represented a shift in their technological frame from a “benefit” to a “threat” frame. More specifically, their perceptions of the benefits of ERP-Plus were now tinged with skepticism and concern that the technology could be detrimental to the DUS. For example, it was observed that: “The regents expressed a certain concern and a desire to clearly see the benefits of the new system” (Alpha faculty member).

The BOR also began to suspect that the UA’s assumptions about ERP-Plus were naive and that they were not being proactive in their responses to the system falling below expectations. This preliminary skepticism was exacerbated by the UA’s response to them, which was sprinkled with obfuscations about the implementation; the following was mentioned by an FS interviewee: “The [BOR] was lied to by Vice President … on what ERP-Plus could do, how much it would cost, and who supported it.”

As a result, dissonant multiplexity arose in the BOR-UA relationship because the existing relationship was modified by the BOR’s explicit unhappiness, possibly because the UA had begun to keep the BOR in the dark. Thus, the relationship between the BOR and the UA, who were initially collaborators in the implementation, evolved to one of duplicity, negativity, and distrust. However, their supervisorsupervisee relationship remained as before. Due to two opposing relationships (supervisor-supervisee and the new negative and distrustful relationship, that replaced the earlier positive collaborative relationship), the BOR-UA relationship became less balanced and exhibited dissonant RM. Clearly, the BOR began to view ERP-Plus with what was primarily a “threat” frame, whereas the UA continued to view it with a “benefit” frame, leading to an increasingly adversarial, multiplex, and unbalanced relationship.

Another example of relational unbalancing was in the relationship between the UA and Gamma. As problems with ERP-Plus surfaced, Gamma began to advocate for the implementation of Flag, a competing ERP system. As a part of this breakaway move, Gamma made a presentation in 2003 to garner the BOR’s support for Flag as an alternative solution to the problems that the ERP-Plus was intended to solve. In this context, a Beta administrator who attended this presentation recalled:

## The [Gamma] administration had students demonstrate … Flag …, and it was extremely successful.

By demonstrating the success of Flag, the Gamma administration secured the BOR’s permission to allow them to use Flag instead of ERP-Plus in 2004. This undermined the UA’s aims and represented a shift in the relationship between Gamma and the UA. Gamma now represented direct opposition to the UA’s strategic vision, even though it was still structurally under the UA. Moreover, the UA was continuing to view ERP-Plus from a “benefit” frame, whereas Gamma now viewed it predominantly from a “threat” frame and switched to viewing Flag from a “benefit” frame. Thus, Gamma’s position shifted to that of an adversary. Specifically, multiplexity emerged in their relationship with the UA when they essentially signaled a significantly reduced trust in the UA (while still in a continuing reporting relationship to UA). This was illustrated by their bypassing of the UA in presenting their case directly to the BOR. Thus, the dyadic relationship between Gamma and the UA became multiplex: the UA was still in the “benefit” frame and had supervisory control over Gamma, but Gamma was prepared by the “threat” frame to become an adversary to ERP-Plus and thus to the UA, who were championing ERP-Plus.

Upon examining the evolution of dyadic relationships during the ERP-Plus implementation, it became clear that the deterioration of the dyadic relationships was largely consistent across the dyads. In each of these relationships, this deterioration was accompanied by the emergence of multiplexity. This occurred when there were shifts in relationships due to project issues, resulting in antagonistic relational perspectives being added to the prevailing positive relationships in the dyads (e.g., the BOR-UA’s positive supervisorsupervisee relationship in addition to the separate trusting relationship later acquiring distrust, evasion, and subterfuge). Thus, such dyadic relationships were clearly unbalanced and exhibited dissonant multiplexity. We term this process of the unbalancing of dyadic relationships as relational unbalancing, which leads to the emergence of dissonant multiplexity in stakeholder dyads. Therefore, we propose:

P2a. A shift to a dissonant relational state occurs when dissonant RM arises through a process of relational unbalancing.

## 4.2.2 Roles of Shifting Technology Frames and Constraints in Relational Unbalancing

Problems within dyadic relationships were apparent across the board as the ERP-Plus project evolved. Further, a multitude of relationships that were dissonant emerged along with technology frames that were incongruent. This dissonance was often triggered by a number of factors, such as the problematic rollout of ERP-Plus, the endemic failures of such rollouts, and constraints inherent within the IT.

More specifically, the frustrations with the preliminary rollout started causing shifts in the technology frames that were initially subscribed to by the stakeholders. Previously, while discussing relational unbalancing, we described how the BOR shifted to a skeptical “threat” frame. A similar pattern was also evident in another dyadic relationship: that between the UA and Beta. While the latter had been a significant supporter of the former during the initiation and preliminary stages of the ERP-Plus project, cracks started developing after the first phase of the rollout. This was because Beta had been chosen as the rollout site and was quickly unimpressed by the capabilities of the system, as its functioning was plagued by continuing failures. Such failures included a massive rate of errors, leading to a distrust of both the data produced and the system itself. In this context, an associate dean at Beta remarked:

The error rate [of data] was in the neighborhood of 70% … it was such drastically poor data that set the perception that one could not trust ERP-Plus.

Another key grievance was related to the continual outages within the system, which began with the preliminary rollout but continued far into the project, as noted in the IT Availability Center report:

Databases went down for over two hours due to problems with [the] core system … [modules] all reported alerts of critical severity … failed transactions.

These failures started taking a toll, and just like with the BOR, there was a distinct shift in Beta’s technology frame. There was a significant lessening of trust in ERP-Plus with a simultaneous move toward a “threat” frame regarding the system, given the system outages. Moreover, as the UA continued to be a champion for the implementation project, a distinct misalignment of technology frames arose in this dyad (UA-Beta). Another consequence of the failures in the first phase of the rollout was that Beta became increasingly vocal in its opposition to ERP-Plus. This created a change in the dyadic relationship—the preliminary mutually supportive relationship became adversarial (while the separate supervisor-supervisee relationship between UA and Beta continued).

Aside from failures, technology constraints also played a big role in the shifting of technology frames within the dyadic relationships. A Beta vice president (member of the FS) noted the difficulty that ERP-Plus presented in terms of inputting prospect data (i.e., data on students considering attending Beta):

You don’t have it in the prospect database, because there’s no way to upload the thing, you know, fairly easily, without having to manually enter all those names.

Thus, ERP-Plus was riddled with issues, and these started affecting the dyadic relationships. This is perhaps best illustrated by the evolution of the UA-FS dyadic relationship. Here, it should be recalled that the FS were successfully co-opted by UA and played a crucial role in advocating the system to the student community. The FS also reported to the UA, which was a separate relationship which existed before, during, and after the ERP-Plus implementation. However, a number of issues with ERP-Plus during the implementation process began to influence the FS’s additional perceptions about the system, and by extension, the UA. First, the system was unreliable, which contributed to the inability of the FS to serve students effectively, such as the inconsistency in canceling students’ enrollment:

One day, the enrollment cancelation process works. The next day, it doesn’t … We never know from one day to the next if it will even run, much less run correctly. (FS member)

A second issue arose from the fact that ERP-Plus was a prepackaged enterprise solution that was not explicitly designed for an academic organization. Thus, there was often the need for users, such as the

FS, to significantly change their work practices to only those processes that were allowed in ERP-Plus:

They won’t change the way the system is programmed to accommodate me, so the only way that I’m going to get what I want is … [to] … change my business practice. (FS member)

Whereas the FS initially agreed to such workarounds, unintended and difficult-to-solve consequences began emerging. Here, the rigid processes and lack of customizability caused a shift in the FS’s technology frame. ERP-Plus’s mandated processes were seen as poorly aligned with those at the DUS universities and led to the belief and frustration that it was inherently unsuitable for universities:

We [faculty members] talked about how obtuse the system is and how difficult it is… It [ERP-Plus] also requires that users deal with terminology not used in higher education. (FS member)

Thus, the FS began to view ERP-Plus with a “threat” frame that was both detrimental to higher education and unreliable. However, because the UA was utterly committed to ERP-Plus, they were now also viewed by the FS as a coercive enforcer of ERP-Plus, which added relational multiplexity given the other relationship that they shared in terms of the FS reporting to the UA.

Although the situation with the FS exemplifies the impediments posed by technology constraints, they were not the only ones frustrated by ERP-Plus. When the implementation began, the FS generally enjoyed the trust of the students, and the UA had already made efforts to co-opt them through the FS. However, the required workarounds to deal with the rigidity and unreliability of ERP-Plus started to negatively influence students as well, with activities like routine scheduling being affected:

## The big problem with ERP-Plus … is [that it is] so rigid that it makes liars out of us all. We kind of had to figure out how to schedule our classes. (FS member)

The rigidity of the ERP-Plus process is exemplified by the fact that to reserve classrooms before enrollment, the FS needed to create fictitious enrollment data for each class and enter them into the system. This workaround often led to downstream issues, such as the dropping of students who had been enrolled only for the sake of setting up the administrative processes. Thus, students also began to lose trust in the system. In addition, the continual issues with regular university procedures because of this system caused them to compare it unfavorably to the existing ManageR system, and students voiced their displeasure:

It’s just less user friendly and more cumbersome [to use than ManageR] … ERP-Plus runs like outdated technology, and it’s just not very efficient by design. (Student interview at local newspaper)

The aforementioned realizations led to two events. First, students started becoming distrusting of the FS, who they believed still supported the UA’s mission to enforce ERP-Plus. This resulted in the development of multiplexity in the FS-student relationship, as an opposing relationship driven by local IS implementation issues was added to the already existing collaborative relationship (where the FS and the students were also working together to ensure that ERP-Plus could be used productively). In fact, the students were so frustrated that they made their anger public. An FS member said this about a student: “She’s very upset with us [FS]. Says she’s going to call Channel 3 News. You may see us on TV!”

Overall, ERP-Plus seriously impeded work within DUS, with continual failures and constraints. Such issues significantly hampered the key users of the systems, such as the FS and the students, and resulted in consequential shifts in technology frames and ultimately incongruent frames within the stakeholder dyads (e.g., UA-BOR, UA-FS, and FS-students). Such incongruent frames eventually led to relational unbalancing and ultimately to the rise of dissonant RM. Therefore, we propose the following:

P2b: Relational unbalancing is triggered by incongruent technology frames due to shifts in technology frames within stakeholder dyads.

P2c: Technological constraints or systemic failures result in potential shifts of technology frames, creating incongruence in technology frames within stakeholder dyads.

## 4.3 The Transitory Relational State: Temporary Balancing and Frame Alignments

There was some attempt to restore temporary order, and it was partially successful, at least initially. The main actor in this effort was the UA, and its efforts resulted in a shift of the ERP-Plus project to a relational state marked by the partial balancing of dyads and frame alignments. That is, this balancing and alignment occurred with only a subset of the stakeholder dyads. Further, this relational state exhibited lower degrees of dissonant RM, but some nonetheless remained. Another aspect of this state was that it was less permanent than the initial resonant or later dissonant states. The dyadic relationships were more unstable and the shifts in technology frames more dynamic, perhaps reflecting the more turbulent project environment. We call this state the transitory relational state, reflecting its more transient nature. The transitions to this state and the reverse transition to a dissonant relational state that took place during this time were influenced as before by several events, such as stakeholder machinations (using mechanisms of power, trust, and signaling) as well as technology failure and constraints.

The efforts to revert back to a more balanced relational state transpired through a process of relational balancing, which was led by the UA. After the BOR became increasingly reticent about the implementation, the UA replaced the BOR, becoming the implementation’s champion. In this role, the UA’s first course of action was to attempt to move the BOR to an “adjustment” frame to encourage the BOR to reconsider the value of ERP-Plus. The UA used Beta, where ERP-Plus was first implemented, to help persuade the BOR. To this end, Beta was directed by the UA to participate in an elaborate presentation, which was strategically manipulated to invoke an “adjustment” frame in the BOR by only highlighting the parts of ERP-Plus regarded as reliable. Two Beta administration members described the approach:

When the [UA] wanted [Beta] to demo ERP-Plus to the [BOR] in January, the [UA] carefully orchestrated more than 30 [DUS] and [Beta] administrators for over a month to make sure only positive things were presented about ERP-Plus.<sup>8</sup>

This one-sided effort had two major outcomes. First, the BOR was successfully moved to a more supportive role and a more positive adjustment frame:

They [the BOR] came away, saying, “Thank you. This was very helpful.” So, they [the BOR] had a more complete understanding of this [the implementation] than they had … in the past. (UA member)

Following their regaining of the BOR’s support, the UA started using BOR as a resource to continue with the ERP-Plus implementation, thus temporarily moving the system to a somewhat “benefit” frame in the eye of some of the stakeholders.

The second outcome was that the involvement of Beta in the UA’s attempt to win back the BOR also created a shift in their role, which was now perceived as an active ally and supporter of the UA. Thus, this effort resulted in the UA and Beta sharing a benefit frame, at least publicly. Because the UA, Beta, and the BOR were able to temporarily converge into a “benefit” frame, their relationship became somewhat balanced after the initial dissonance.

The other strategy that the UA undertook was to oversee workarounds so that other stakeholders, notably the FS, could continue to work with ERP-Plus by “tricking the system.” A member of the FS offered an example with a positive orientation, signaling a move away from a “threat” frame:

We have a workaround…So, we’ve had to, like, think of little ways to trick the system to get out … [what we need].

These workarounds were temporarily successful, as noted by a member of the FS:

We’ve pretty well calmed down with Finance. I mean … we’re pretty much able to do what we need to do [i.e., the workarounds were temporarily working and able to meet the needs of the FS]

The previous examples represent some of the small temporary “wins” for UA during the implementation and highlight that the successful realignment of technology frames can move an unbalanced dyadic relationship back to one that is balanced. Such relational balancing, of course, occurred through a combination of maneuvering involving the more successful and selected aspects of ERP-Plus and forcing others to engage in workarounds. Whereas there was some restoration of balanced dyads (e.g., UA-BOR, UA-FS), this remained local, as dissonance about the usefulness of ERP-Plus festered elsewhere, especially with Gamma and the students.

In fact, the alliance and a show of public harmony by the UA and Beta and the UA and the BOR had negative consequences down the road in terms of other dyadic relationships. Over time, these stakeholders began to see through the nontransparent efforts of the UA to artificially align the technological frames regarding ERP-Plus. This caused them to view the UA with even greater suspicion and lack of trust. Thus, after the temporary realignment, the pendulum began to swing back heavily to the other side. Arguably, it was the UA that spawned this negativity by continuously signaling their coercive intent by enforcing the use of ERP-Plus in the face of rising dissent from the member universities:

“The chancellor [UA head] said that we would delay, but that we would be going forward and don’t do that again. (FS member)

Consequently, there were criticisms of the UA’s efforts to present an artificially “optimistic” view of ERP-Plus. Stakeholders gradually became skeptical about how trustworthy this entire effort had been:

It was a “dog and pony show,” one in which the regents were given the clear impression that all was right and good with ERP-Plus. (FS member’s critical appraisal of the presentation)

Although the UA perceived the discontent in all their dyadic relationships, they decided they could reestablish harmony by using the same tactics as before—continuing to enforce their institutional control and power. This was further supplemented by unjustified signaling about ERP-Plus’s success and usefulness. This lack of change in direction or tactic on the UA’s part led to the progressive deterioration of their dyadic relationships with other stakeholders, especially the FS. In fact, the UA tried to deflect the FS’s negativity regarding ERP-Plus to some agitators:

You had people, some [faculty and staff] people … [they] would tell the users that it wasn’t a good system and that it doesn’t work … When people who are in a position of influence say things such as this, they can poison the opinions of other users.

Meanwhile, the UA continued to overlook all the deficiencies in the ERP-Plus project, which had to be dealt with by the FS. Even the training they mandated was inadequate. As revealed by an FS member:

I am really frustrated in the sense that when we go through this training, it’s kinda like just a robotic kind of “Okay, this is what you do in order to do this.” But when we ask a “Why?” … but just for me, I need to understand it in order to be able to memorize that process.

Thus, the FS started to view the training and, by extension, ERP-Plus, with a “threat” frame. Further, the UA was now definitely viewed by the FS as a coercive enforcer of ERP-Plus, which added multiplexity to the reporting relationship they had with the UA at the start of the implementation (or even after the temporary alignment). Additionally, at one point, the UA threatened to cut off access to prior systems and resorted to threats to make others comply, worsening the situation.

The UA’s preoccupation with relational balancing and frame alignment also led to them having a tenuous grasp on the realities of the project. They also failed to adequately address the testing issues with data processing that arose during the implementation process. This resulted in further delays in delivering effective functionalities to end users, such as the FS and the students:

We tested that [cancelation for the nonpayment process in ERP-Plus] before we ran it, but because we didn’t test it on the whole population, we missed some things... It didn’t cancel some students—or it did cancel some students that we didn’t want canceled. (FS member)

At this point, the UA and the FS had clearly progressed into a fully dissonant multiplex relation in which the latter resisted the former while being subjected to their control due to the DUS administrative structure. More specifically, the UA used its administrative power to compel the FS to forcibly fit their work practices to ERP-Plus by signaling that they had made the decision to continue with ERP-Plus and would not tolerate any opposition. As noted by a Beta administrator: “The decision to stay with ERP-Plus had already been made [by the UA]” (i.e., the UA was not going back on the decision to implement ERP-Plus). This signaling of unmitigated support for ERP-Plus was an attempt to address the dissonant RM and reconcile the FS with ERP-Plus’s benefits. However, this move further alienated the FS from the UA due to what the former perceived as an unsympathetic and coercive signal.

Although we focused on the UA-FS stakeholder dyads in describing the unbalancing of relationships, similar events took place across the board. Thus, the shift to the transitory state turned out to be temporary, and the project shifted back to a dissonant relational state. The localized relational balancing in some stakeholder dyads was eventually dominated by the relational unbalancing occurring in others. However, this preliminary shift from and reversion back to the dissonant relational state revealed a few theoretically interesting insights. For example, attempts at reducing dissonant RM and relational balancing resulted in a transitory relational state where there were partially balanced dyads and frame congruence. This relational balancing was further marked by actions/machinations by important stakeholders to modify other actors’ technological frames (Olesen, 2014). Research suggests that such mechanisms are pivotal for organizational stakeholders seeking to achieve congruence in technological frames (Yeow & Sia, 2008). The frames themselves evolve when various mechanisms of alignment are invoked. As a case in point, the power/control mechanisms or signaling can be used by the more powerful actor in the dyad to attempt to achieve frame congruence. However, if this effort is viewed as unreasonable and coercive, then the frames remain misaligned. Likewise, one of the actors in the unbalanced dyad may invoke mechanisms to gain trust, but if that fails, then the technological frames revert to being misaligned. In this context, our evidence suggests that perceptions of coercion and unjustified signaling lead to only temporary relational balancing. In addition, such coercive mechanisms can have ripple effects on other stakeholder dyads as well, as shown in this section, causing a rise in dissonant RM and a shift back to the dissonant state. Based on this understanding, we propose:

P3: Stakeholder dyads in a dissonant relational state may attempt to achieve relational balancing by activating mechanisms to successfully realign technology frames.

P4: Stakeholders will shift to the transitory relational state when incomplete and temporary relational balancing results in partially balanced dyads and partial frame congruence.

P5: Stakeholders will shift from a transitory relational state to a dissonant relational state if mechanisms of realignment are perceived to be coercive and relational balancing is impeded.

## 4.4 Relationship between Dissonant RM and IS Implementation Failure: Psychosocial Alienation, Resource Silos, and Information Asymmetry

An IS implementation can be termed a failure when it fails to meet the user demands of a functioning system (Ewusi-Mensah, 2003). From this perspective, the project was a failure, as it did not address the stakeholder needs. To understand how failure was ultimately shaped, we present three emergent fallouts of dissonant RM: psychosocial alienation (where stakeholders are isolated from each other, both socially and psychologically), resource silos (where organizational resources are disjointed and do not work in tandem), and information asymmetry (where different stakeholders have different levels of information about organizational phenomena). While research has shown that resonant RM improves collaborative spirit, resource flow, and information sharing (Howe & Jin, 2022), dissonant RM arguably has the opposite ramifications, likely leading to psychosocial alienation (opposite of collaborative spirit), resource silos (opposite of resource flow), and information asymmetry (opposite of information sharing).

As expected, we found that the persistence of dissonant RM resulted in the emergence of psychosocial alienation within stakeholders, and situations of resource silos and information asymmetry. In other words, the consistent dissonant multiplexity ultimately inhibited the stakeholders’ ability to meaningfully work together to address sociotechnical issues in order to ensure positive outcomes of the ERP-Plus implementation project. Ultimately, dissonant RM and its ramifications ensured that the system was below stakeholder expectations and thus unacceptable to them, thereby consigning it to failure. These fallouts are elaborated upon in the subsequent sections.

## 4.4.1 Psychosocial Alienation of Stakeholders Leading to IS Failure

The first ramification of dissonant RM was the consistent psychosocial alienation of important stakeholders during the process. For example, an FS member observed the following—“A trend on campus is becoming increasingly more evident and many might say it is a standing policy: Students do not matter.” The FS member also noted that “the University seems bent on casting students aside.”

Due to the dissonant RM, UA became progressively unbothered by the students’ concerns about ERP-Plus. Initially, students were co-opted and were willing participants in the ERP-Plus implementation. However, as time progressed and dissonant RM arose and was reinforced, the UA began to demonstrate a lack of concern about what students or other stakeholders thought about ERP-Plus and treated them like they were a “nuisance” for voicing their concerns, which, unsurprisingly, was alienating to them. As one FS member noted: “Administrators appear to have overlooked [continuing] problems [with ERP-Plus]” and because they did so, UA found it convenient to take advantage of the dissonant RM and alienate stakeholders having problems with ERP-Plus. For example, students were treated as if they were irrelevant, and their repeated pleas were continuously overlooked.

It was not just the students whose concerns were being summarily and continuously overlooked by the UA, the FS also voiced their frustration regarding the errors that needed to be corrected (which was not done):

## Our inventory of facilities was all wrong, and we didn’t know it was all wrong. The biggest thing that needs to happen … is to correct that. (FS member)

By overlooking the concerns of the FS and the students regarding ERP-Plus, the UA’s continued tone-deaf support for ERP-Plus signaled to the stakeholders that they were only focused on their agenda and did not care about dissent. In this way, UA damaged the collaborative spirit of the implementation and left the FS and the students to fend for themselves. As an FS member acknowledged, “[they] had nowhere else to go for direction.” In effect, the UA threw the member universities, the FS, and the students under the bus and alienated them. They could conveniently do that because, by now, they were quite aware of the dissonance between them and the other stakeholders. They also knew that their superficial efforts at reconciliation and resonance had failed. Therefore, given the dissonance, it was a strategic (though shortsighted and detrimental) action on UA’s part to alienate these stakeholders.

If stakeholders are alienated, an IS implementation is most likely to fail—one of the benchmarks of a successful IS implementation is stakeholder consensus on its benefits (Serrano et al., 2020). In our case, the students, the FS, and other stakeholders, who were supposed to be the key users benefiting from the ERP-Plus system received the signal that they did not matter. If the key stakeholders are alienated when they voice problems with the system, then obviously, the system will never meet their expectations and consequently will be a failure (Dwivedi et al., 2015). Therefore, we propose:

P6a: The inability to shift from a dissonant relational state and the continued existence of pervasive dissonant RM lead to emerging stakeholder psychosocial alienation, which can move an IS project toward failure.

## 4.4.2 Resource Silos

The second fallout of the consistent presence of dissonant RM was the emergence of siloed resources. Due to the consistent presence of dissonant RM, there was no clear agreement on what resources to share and how to share them. Both students and the FS fell prey to the siloed resources where various modules of ERP-Plus started functioning independently. This led to the ERP-Plus not being able to assist them in any way due to the presence of silos and lack of overall integration:

The [ERP-Plus] system won’t update, so it shows that I’m failing courses and that I have a bad (grade point average) … I can’t even begin to start the financial aid process until they update the system. (A student’s frustration, reported in the student newspaper)

The continuous emergence of silos affected not only the students but the FS as well. The data produced and hosted through ERP-Plus, was severely fragmented. Further, even when resources were shared, there were no clear ownership rules that were set up—due to the dissonance—with the effect that each stakeholder group was working on its own rather than synchronizing their data access and manipulation:

Well, what that means is the data, our data, doesn’t reside here. It resides there. OK? So, any time we need to make changes … we have to basically get them to do it. (FS member)

Further examples of consistent silos were provided by an FS member, who recalled how despite the problems, there was no collaborative attempt at resolution, an immediate fallout of dissonant RM:

Nobody likes it [ERP-Plus]. And I don’t know if it’s really being addressed from a corporate standpoint. I don’t think it is really being addressed … It’s just, people are trying to deal with it individually in isolation.

This quote reinforces the idea that the UA was not really interested in developing any centralized resources for addressing the problems, as it was experiencing substantial dissonance with other stakeholders. Thus, each stakeholder group ended up working in isolation and not sharing any resources for collaborative problem solving. UA was partly culpable for the state of siloed resources due to their lack of effort:

In my opinion, these resources are inadequately funded by the institution, are the last place where new resources are allocated, and have not kept pace with the changes in the mission of the institution. (FS member)

The continuous resource silos in the ERP-Plus components ultimately led to catastrophic results:

The result has been a nightmare. . .. The impact … includes, among other things, an undetermined, but substantial amount of lost SCHs [semester credit hours] for the semester. (FS member)

There were other examples where the resource silos caused systemic disasters. As ERP-Plus modules were not synchronized properly, technological fixes often failed miserably. For example, a patch installed in a module resulted in ERP-Plus malfunctioning on a registration day in 2007:

We put in the one [the patch] that broke it on like a Thursday night … This happened on a registration day. It was horrible and was referred to by some as Black Friday. (UA member)

This ultimate catastrophic failure can be attributed to the fact that the ERP-Plus modules (which were under different stakeholders) functioned as independent modules rather than as part of a well-integrated enterprise system. Because there were siloed resources due to the dissonant RM, there was inconsistency and confusion, leading to serious errors that undermined the whole purpose of ERP-Plus. Therefore:

P6b: The inability to shift from a dissonant relational state and the continued existence of pervasive dissonant RM lead to the emergence of resource silos, which can move an IS project toward failure.

## 4.4.3 Information Asymmetry

The consistently existing dissonant RM also led to extensive information asymmetry within stakeholder dyads. Due to the dissonant relationships, information was either not shared or there were no organizational mechanisms put in place for information sharing. An FS member noted:

It seemed disjointed. It was like—one of the things from a project perspective that I didn’t see—there didn’t seem to be one person who was the project leader that you could go to. There were a lot of people out there, and everybody was scrambling trying to figure out, “Who is the one person who is responsible for getting me the answer?”

What this quote suggests is that not only was there information asymmetry about the implementation itself (the process), but there was also information asymmetry on who to approach (the people) for the resolution of any roadblocks that stakeholders experienced. This is quite debilitating for a project.

Other examples of information asymmetry abounded. For example, due to the information asymmetry, the organizers of training programs did not know what the trainees were expecting, with the result that the trainings did not provide adequate information on how to navigate ERP-Plus and test it:

We had a training class for the basics, but…the training class didn’t cover everything necessarily that my test was going to end up having. (Frustrated FS member)

This quote shows a serious lack of knowledge of trainee needs, reflecting the ever-increasing information asymmetry during the process. This ever-increasing asymmetry followed from dissonant RM, where stakeholders—due to the dissonance—could not converge and share information adequately.

There were other issues characterizing the state of information asymmetry; it was, in fact, observed that information often did not flow across the stakeholders properly. An FS member recounted frustration about information not being shared properly and the downstream effects of information asymmetry:

But the information didn’t get down the pipe to say. So we had to make some calls, and we are still dealing with new personnel not necessarily always getting that information.

The continuous information asymmetry was exacerbated by the fact that the UA, the key overseer of the implementation process, did not enjoy a harmonious relationship with stakeholders such as the FS and the students. The UA never made efforts to make information flow transparent and, in fact, was culpable for orchestrating deceptive communications to keep other stakeholders from receiving adequate knowledge of ERP-Plus. Thus, due to the dissonance they faced with other stakeholders, they seemed to regard it as the best strategy to keep the stakeholders in the dark. The FS (and other stakeholders) finally realized that UA was culpable of hiding the fact that ERP-Plus was not appropriate as a university-wide system:

“We’ve got a software product that was made for HR. It’s not made to run a university.”

Indeed, in many ways, the UA took advantage of the dissonant RM and information asymmetry that continued to pervade, by co-opting the vendor of ERP-Plus to engage in deceptive tactics about ERP-Plus— adding more information asymmetry:

[The vendor] came in and did a dog and pony show…And they are trying to jury-rig this thing. And so it’s being jury-rigged … it was a lot of smoke and mirrors. (FS member)

Clearly, due to the dissonant RM, information asymmetry rose; in fact, it has been observed that information asymmetry is associated with dissonant relationships (Barile et al., 2014). According to prior research, when they experience cognitive dissonance, entities engage in behaviors that exacerbate information asymmetry (Gong et al., 2021). Recalling that conflicting technological frames (an example of cognitive dissonance) caused the dissonant RM to arise, we can infer that once the dissonant RM emerged and could not be mitigated, serious issues of information asymmetry developed.

When there is information asymmetry among stakeholders, they often make incorrect decisions (Brodbeck et al., 2007), leading to misunderstandings among IS project stakeholders (Wachnik, 2015). In such a state of information asymmetry, there is no convergent understanding as to whether the IS implementation actually delivered the expected benefits, and the general acceptability of the implemented system is vastly reduced (Cihon et al., 2021). As argued before, if there is a general low-level acceptability of an implemented IS, then it is deemed a failure. Therefore, we propose:

P6c. The inability to shift from a dissonant relational state and the continued existence of pervasive dissonant RM lead to the emergence of information asymmetry within stakeholder dyads, which can move an IS project toward failure.

## 5 Discussion: Contribution and Future Implications

## 5.1 The Centrality of RM to Understanding IS Failures

Our study shows that RM can be central to understanding IS failures, a position which is unique when compared to prior IS failures literature. To the best of our knowledge, no study has connected RM to IS failures, even though, as this paper shows, it can be an extremely insightful connection.

The centrality of RM to IS failures is summarized in Table 4. RM shows that dysfunctional relationships between stakeholders and incongruent frames can be salient mechanisms of IS failures. Our study unpacks the sociotechnical nuances of IS failures, in which technical constraints/challenges and the different ways that individuals react to them (thereby generating multiplexity) contribute to the failure. The existence of technological constraints/challenges does not necessarily mean that the project will fail. We posit that if humans react appropriately to the constraints/challenges, the issues can be resolved. Unfortunately, that did not happen in our case.

In our study, although technological constraints were one of the reasons for dissonance, it is how they were handled (or not handled) that is at the crux of our theoretical story, which RM allowed us to weave. In our study, if the politicking, coverups, and coercion (associated with dissonant RM) had not been there, the technological issues would likely have been quickly resolved, and the system implementation would have likely become successful relatively quickly. Importantly, the incongruence of technological frames contributed to the politicking, coverups, and coercion (e.g., by fostering dissonance), but it is also noteworthy that because the politicking and coverups contributed to dissonant RM, the technological constraints were never resolved and a further incongruence of technological frames emerged, worsening the situation. Thus, it can be safely inferred that technology worked in tandem with the sociopolitical factors to consign this system to failure—a sociotechnical view highlighted by our study using the theoretical umbrella of RM.

## 5.2 Contribution as a Novel and Useful Theory of IS Failures: Offering Concepts and Insights

Our GTM-based study leverages dissonant RM to deliver salient concepts and insights on IS failures (beyond existing IS literature). Informed by our problematization (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011) as summarized in Table 1, RM enabled us to develop a unique process theory to contribute novel and useful findings—a requirement for a strong theoretical contribution (Corley & Gioia, 2011).

## 5.2.1 The Novelty of Our Theory Beyond Existing Literature

First, we deliver concepts not found in extant works. Our study surfaces new constructs (e.g., dissonant RM), events (systemic failures), processes (e.g., relational unbalancing), states (e.g., transitory state), and triggers (e.g., power/coercion) that are mostly absent in the prior literature on IS failures and certainly not assimilated under a common, integrative umbrella such as RM.

Such theoretical components are crucial to developing compelling and novel theories (Weber, 2012) and allowed our study to better unpack the “where,” “when,” “how,” and “why” in relation to dissonant RM and IS failures. Clearly, the overall thrust and findings of our study are quite different from the existing literature, delivering a performative insight into IS failures, which is in stark contrast to the predominantly representational perspective of understanding IS failures, as noted by Cecez-Kecmanovic et al. (2014). In addition, the extant IS failures literature has often articulated concepts like poor planning, unsatisfactory risk management, and the complexity of technical problems as contributing to IS failures (Dwivedi et al., 2015). In contrast to prior literature, our study adds hitherto uninvestigated concepts such as dissonant RM, information asymmetry, and psychosocial alienation, thereby broadening the conceptual canvas to foster a better understanding of IS failures.

The second novel aspect of our theory is that it showcases the unpredictability of IS implementation and thereby adds meaningful extensions to the literature. For example, it embraces the perspective that positive and negative relationships can coexist in organizational dyads (Methot et al., 2017). More specifically, our theory suggests that while IS implementations can begin in a balanced relational state within stakeholder dyads (with positive relationships), dissonant RM may emerge quickly if technological frames diverge among key stakeholders. Thus, in contrast to existing literature, our theory (using RM) shows that a priori causes of IS failures cannot be ascertained, allowing us to address the critique related to Assumption 1 (in our problematization). The study shows that there is often an unpredictable causal evolution in IS failures—from cognition (captured by the technology frames) to action, which often reverts back to recalibrated cognition and further action. In our research, this was a degenerative process, explicated faithfully using RM—leading to a unique process theory that progresses our understanding of IS failures. Prior studies often take a deterministic perspective to understand IS failures/successes (e.g., Hughes et al., 2017; Petter et al., 2013); our study challenges that view and surfaces the inherent indeterminacy in IS implementation contexts.

For example, using RM, we showcased that stakeholder coercion amplified resistance to the technology (i.e., a move to a “threat” frame) and created a breakdown of mutual understanding, where the ways to address the technological constraints were never agreed upon. These events could not have been predicted a priori. Our study also reveals that stakeholder efforts can become unexpectedly counterproductive, a volatility that no prior study has explicitly captured, to the best of our knowledge.

The third novelty of our study is that by using RM, our study allows us to forward a fundamentally alternate performative perspective to understand IS failures, in contrast to the existing representational perspectives, a criticism that has been levied against IS failure studies (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014). <sup>9</sup> Addressing the critique related to Assumption 2, our theorization thus moves beyond considering the social construction of technology (a primarily representational perspective) and reveals a fundamental dysfunctionality in the organizational implementation of technology by offering a novel performative perspective. Our performative study is in contrast to existing literature where the framing of IS failures (or successes) uses a predominantly representational perspective (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014). Such representational studies often focus on objective organizational outcomes such as sales (Petter et al., 2008) or subjective interpretations of IT (Bartis & Mitev, 2008) and bypass discussions on human action and interaction arising from interstakeholder relationships. Our study goes beyond this limited focus.

An example of this performativity is showcased by stakeholder perceptions, actions, and reactions to ERP-Plus. As captured in our study, the continuous assessment of ERP-Plus—captured through the moving technology frames—coupled with the actions by stakeholders, led to revised mental models of ERP-Plus and other stakeholders, causing further degenerative actions and events downstream.

## 5.2.2 The Usefulness of Our Theory

First, our study positions dissonant RM as key to IS failures. Researchers investigating IS implementation have observed that understanding IS failures is likely as crucial as understanding IS successes (Dwivedi et al., 2015). Our delivery of RM is a very useful conceptual addition to the literature because it offers a new lens to studying not only IS failures but also organizational phenomena. RM, by its very nature, presents a relational perspective and allows an inquiry into relational performativity (addressing the critique related to Assumption 2). The relations capturing performativity evolve through a multiplicity of bidirectional relational actions by agents in organizationally embedded dyads.

Ultimately, a performative worldview focuses on action, fluidity, and movement within a relational ontology (Schultze & Orlikowski, 2010)—which are typical organizational characteristics. RM exemplifies itself as a performative construct due to the fact that it allows one to focus on each of these performative demands and positions itself as a lens to not only study IS failures in organizations but also other pressing organizational issues (e.g., decision-making and strategy).

The second useful aspect of our theory is that RM allowed us to develop a sociotechnical view of IS failures, whereas existing literature mostly adopts a sociopolitical view. Addressing the critique related to Assumption 3, and ensuring that the sociotechnical paradigm is embedded within RM, we differentiate our study from the existing IS failures literature and offer a new conceptual anchor to study IS failures. For example, in our study, RM often results from an interaction of technology constraints, sociopolitical coercion of power, and eroding trust, which shape technology frames, generating a sociotechnical space.

Our effort contrasts with prior studies, which have mostly omitted the conceptual richness afforded by the sociotechnical approach, instead treating technologies as mostly contexts rather than an active canvas shaping organizational actions and outcomes. Using dissonant RM allowed us to ascribe a far more meaningful agency to technology than is found in prior IS failure studies, thereby apportioning comparable salience to both technology and humans that is consistent with the sociotechnical paradigm.

The third useful aspect of our study is that though rooted in a particular context, it also abstracts out of that context, following the principle of “transferability” (Sarker, 2016), one of the key tenets of GTM (Sarker et al., 2018a). We thereby deliver an example of what have been called substantive theories (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Urquhart et al., 2010). Such theories are expected to be independent of the data analyzed and incidents observed (Urquhart et al.,

2010), allowing for their application beyond the settings of a particular case (Sarker et al., 2018a, 2018b) and thus improving generalizability.

Fourth, our study can be viewed as a useful addition to the IS implementation literature that draws upon the stakeholder perspective. While understanding stakeholder relationships is an identifiable tradition in IS implementation research (as recounted earlier), a dedicated focus on the relational unpacking of stakeholders—especially where the relationships are dissonant, multiplex, shaped by technology, and evolving—is somewhat absent. We posit that RM, which delivers an insightful perspective of this relational unpacking, can help tease out further nuances of stakeholder relational phenomena such as politicking, power, and resource control, thus informing richer investigations of IS implementations.

## 5.3 Practical Importance

From a practical usefulness standpoint, cognizance of dissonant RM allows organizations to zero in on this fundamental dysfunctionality and address it. RM provides a concrete starting point when implementing organizational technologies. If RM becomes dissonant, then organizational interventions are needed that can appear in various forms, such as changes in technology, organizational structure, or even organizational policies. Therefore, RM offers practical pathways to develop organizational adjustments and interventions during organizational technology implementation.

Our theory of RM has practical utility beyond mere IS implementation. In general, the RM lens can help identify sources of synergy (resonant RM) or discordance (dissonant RM) crucial to setting up collaborative networks for important organizational matters (Liu et al., 2019) such as the determination of organizational policies (Howe & Jin, 2022) and entrepreneurial ventures (Bliemel et al., 2016). Ultimately, dissonant RM allows us to not only identify the sources of dissonance but also effectively address them to create organizational structures and operations conducive to continued success.

## 5.4 Future Research Opportunities

It would be useful to conduct qualitative studies in other organizational settings and different implementation contexts using RM as a theoretical lens to verify whether the findings mirror ours. RM could be applied to associated research areas such as IS project escalation (Lee et al., 2019) and project distress (Baghizadeh et al., 2020) or to other organizational phenomena such as governance and strategy.

Other research opportunities include evaluating the propositional theory presented in the paper by operationalizing it into testable hypotheses and subsequently testing these hypotheses using a positivist approach. Such empirical queries might involve organizational surveys, field experimentation, and longitudinal surveys. Future research could also extend the investigation of dissonant multiplex relationships within dyads to higher orders, such as triads or tetrads. It would be interesting to study whether a dyadic understanding of a phenomenon is different from a triadic or tetradic perspective of the same phenomenon, which could lead to advanced views of organizational and technological phenomena.

Finally, future research could examine IS implementations (as well as other organizational phenomena) both at the macrolevel (as we have done) and the individual level (Lapointe & Rivard, 2007). We believe it would be especially promising to apply RM in such a multilevel manner. Clearly, dissonant RM is a promising lens for investigating troubling issues in IS projects, and we hope that our study energizes future research interested in using this novel perspective.

## Utilizing Theory to Guide Our GTM Analysis

Theory is utilized in GTM through the idea of theoretical sensitivity, one of the core concepts of GTM (Sarker et al., 2018), can be defined as “the ability to recognize what is important in data and to give it meaning” (Strauss & Corbin, 1990, p. 46). It emphasizes that the researcher is immersed in the investigated phenomena, is cognizant of general ideas related to the phenomena, and understands the context of the theory development (Glaser, 1978; Urquhart et al., 2010). Following the concept of theoretical sensitivity, we used our theoretical understanding of RM to develop a preunderstanding of the phenomenon at hand. The use of this pre-understanding allowed us to explore and identify salient concepts related to RM over multiple rounds of data analysis performed by the research team. Of course, we were careful in ensuring that our use of theoretical perspectives did not contravene the core values underlying GTM-based analysis. Specifically, we have been conscious in our efforts to ensure that pre-existing theory was not forced on the data, for the purpose of proving or disproving it, as this would be against the spirit of any GTM variant (Suddaby, 2006). A more detailed account of our use of theory during data analysis is included in the remainder of this appendix, starting with the discussion on the open coding below.

## Open Coding Phase

The open coding focused on fracturing and labeling the study data. The GTM literature has suggested that such coding may be done line by line (recommended in the initial phases of analysis), by sentence, by paragraph, or even by the whole document (Sarker et al., 2000). Following GTM open coding (Sarker et al., 2000; Urquhart et al., 2010), this open coding process was interpretive in nature. We were guided during this generative process by the nature and level of sensitivity we brought to the analysis as researchers immersed within a research discipline. The emergence of concepts and their labels was initially influenced by our assumptions and theoretical understanding of the phenomenon. For example, a fundamental assumption at the beginning of our investigation was that a relational perspective of the actors would provide us with an understanding of the failure of the large-scale ERP implementation. Therefore, during open coding, we were sensitized to look for concepts that would illuminate the dynamics of this failure, informed by our understanding of the theoretical underpinnings of RM. At the end of the generative phase of open coding, we were left with numerous concepts. These concepts emerged from the phrases uttered by the interviewees, the written texts available to us, and the contextual interpretive memos coded by us. The resulting concepts were thus closely linked to the data, and although we could perceive allusions to theoretical concepts underpinning RM at different levels of abstraction, these concepts were not yet completely crystallized. At this point, the phenomena emerged from the empirical text as a fragmented collection of ideas or a “cloud of concepts,” the relationships among which were not quite apparent.

We developed a pictorial representation to make sense of this phase of coding. Inspired by Venn diagrammatic notation (Grünbaum, 1975), we conceptualized each open code as a set of ideas representing the phenomenon. Figure B1 depicts an illustration of the cloud of concepts that resulted from these visualizations. We would hasten to add that the figure is provided as a faithful reproduction of the visualization mechanisms used in the analysis and is in no way an exhaustive representation of all the open codes. The figure is being used to provide an impressionistic understanding of the open codes that emerged. For example, an examination of the figure would show that among other things, the emergent concepts at this point refer to important kinds of technology frames that facilitate the process, as well as trust-related issues that were present during the implementation. Allusions also point to actions taken by stakeholders such as the UA during the process, which had implications for RM.

To develop a more coherent understanding of the phenomenon, we proceeded to the next step of open coding—the organization and aggregation of concepts into categories. The second phase of open coding required us to go back to the text. This time, our analytical approach was somewhat different. Given that our intention was to aggregate concepts and identify conceptual ideas at a higher level of abstraction (i.e., categories), we examined both the data and the contextual memos. In the process, we generated more contextual memos. These contextual memos (see the blue italicized text in Table B2) reflect the difference from the analytical approach of the previous round. This time, our querying of the data was more intimately informed by our theoretical sensitivity.

## Axial Coding Phase

Axial coding is the next step in the theoretical consolidation of empirical data. The objective of this step is to develop a better understanding of the links between the conceptual ideas identified previously. This consolidation is achieved through deeper examination of the relationships between the emerging categories and subcategories in the previous phase and through the development of a hierarchy related to their conceptual underpinning. Crucially, during axial coding, researchers often must return to the empirical data, either to confirm perceived links between categories (and subcategories) or to enhance understanding. This analysis may result in the emergence of new open codes or the identification of new category-subcategory groupings.

The inputs to axial coding were the open codes, category-subcategory groupings, and contextual memos. The starting point of this phase was the groupings of categories and subcategories. We examined these groupings to develop a conceptual explanation. The objective was to see if the links underlying such clusters could be identified and made explicit. For example, an examination of the category groupings led us to identify technology frames as an important category (see Figure B2) and that there were sub-categories under this category such as benefit frames, threat frames, and adjustment frames. This examination and aggregation of clusters continued until we were satisfied that all perceivable links had been established. We facilitated our understanding of this consolidation process and its implications by continuing to use strategies such as developing memos. These artifacts of our coding allowed us to identify and develop theoretical fragments that formed the building blocks of our higher-level process theory. Figure B3 illustrates the hierarchical link diagrams and memos (to simplify the representation, only examples of subcategories are shown in the figure and mentioned in the example memo). Multiple rounds of axial coding allowed us to refine ou understanding represented in such figures and memos.

The analysis performed during axial coding helped us to consolidate conceptual ideas that emerged during open coding and to identify categories that we deemed central to our theoretical narrative. This analytic effort was iterative and was guided by the GTM principles described above. For example, our effort to understand exactly how RM is salient to the large-scale IS implementation process was informed by our decision to understand the actors’ technology perceptions using technology frames. Although we explained the process in terms of these theoretical constructs, we used the principle of theoretical comparison to identify interview sections (e.g., where respondents described their shifting technology frames) that we judged to best illuminate the phenomena.

At the end of axial coding, we were able to identify a set of central categories and their hierarchy of subcategories that were critical to our theoretical understanding of the phenomenon. The main categories that emerged were technology advantages/constraints, technology frames, trust, power, signaling, and relational dyads (see Figure B4). The category relational dyads<sup>.</sup> was chosen as the core or primary category around which the story leading to the process model about RM would revolve, with other categories playing supportive or constitutive roles.

## Selective Coding Phase

The primary objective of the selective coding phase is to develop the final storyline of the theoretical narrative. GTM guidelines suggest that a process theoretical narrative may be crafted by interlinking core categories. Corbin and Strauss (2008) advised that theory development is facilitated by writing integrative memos. They also encouraged researchers to formulate supporting artifacts, such as diagrams. Selective coding is the step by which researchers put together theoretical patterns identified in axial coding into a coherent narrative, starting with the outputs of axial coding. However, researchers may return to previous steps to complete missing parts or develop a more granular understanding.

In our application of selective coding, as a starting point, we used the memos and codes that emerged after axial coding. The theoretical narrative that we began to construct was aimed primarily at explaining RM’s role in a large-scale IS implementation process. Because of our focus on RM, we developed a narrative that centered on an explanation of the change to the key actors’ dyadic relationships, based upon the codes identified in the axial coding phase. We began building our final narrative through the development of integrative memos. These memos initially focused on reporting our responses to the following questions: (1) What characterizes the evolution of the key actors’ dyadic relationships? (2) What is the nature of the multiplexity observed during the evolution of such relationships? (3) How did the patterns of technology-frame shifts relate to the dynamics of such relationships? and (4) What were the relational states that the key stakeholders were in, and how did they transition? While writing the integrative memos, we also focused on abstracting our thoughts from the actual events of the case. Table B3 shows an integrative memo created after the nature of relational states and their evolution had solidified for us

The patterns of the evolution of the dyadic relations and RM allowed us to develop insights into how the multiplexities emerging in dyads affected the progress of the IS implementation. At this stage, we leveraged the principle of theoretical comparison, which sensitized us to return to our empirical data to develop a more granular understanding. These forays back into the data often led us to another iteration of open coding and axial coding. We then constructed additional integrative memos. We also returned to the empirical data to identify quotes that illustrated the narrative. We created the final narrative by combining the integrative memos into a coherent description. We concluded our selective coding phase when we were satisfied that the resulting narrative represented a theoretically dense understanding of the phenomena, without any apparent anomalies between the narrative and the data. At the end of the preliminary selective coding process, we identified important interlinked theoretical components derived from the inter-relationships of our core categories. These formed the basis for our final theorization (Figure B5). The final theoretical narrative emerged from more iterations of integrative memo-making focused on developing more granular insights into these theoretical components and the role they played. A finalized integrated version of this iterative memo-making and selective coding has been presented in the manuscript as the theoretical narrative and theoretical model.

## Table B3. An Example of an Integrative Memo Developed during Selective Coding

The preliminary relational network for the ERP-Plus implementation in DUS was balanced and aligned in terms of technology frames salient within the actors, the apparent harmony started getting compromised quite soon. The Board of Regents became concerned that the implementation was growing too expensive and failing to deliver its promised benefits. Though initially the focal actor of this network, the BOR threatened to withdraw its support for the implementation. This represented a shift in the technology frame of the BOR, from an overwhelmingly benefit frame to an adjustment frame. In other words, their perceptions regarding the benefits of ERP-Plus now became tinged with skepticism. There also seemed to be a perception within the BOR that the UA was not being proactive about its response to the delays and general failure to deliver on initial promises. Therefore, they attempted to bring about a shift in the UA’s technology frame. They did this by expressing their power and signaling that they were becoming impatient with the lack of progress. This led to an emergence of multiplexity in their relationship, where the older relationship was augmented by that of explicit threat.

This change in the BOR’s attitude towards the implementation project created an interesting shift in the network dynamics. While the BOR’s intention was to create a shift in the UA’s technology frame, their threat of withdrawing support had the somewhat unintended consequence of reinforcing its primarily benefit-based frame and escalating their commitment. A consequence of this is that they essentially replaced the BOR as the champion of the implementation process within the network. In this role their first act was to attempt and restore the BOR as an actor supportive of the implementation. In this attempt, the UA decided to employ Beta, where ERP-Plus was being implemented first, in a presentation to regain the BOR’s support. The UA directed Beta to participate in this presentation. This presentation was also strategically manipulated such that only components of ERP-Plus regarded as reliable were to be part of it. This use of Beta in the UA’s attempt to win back the BOR also created a shift in the role of Beta, which now became an active ally and supporter of the UA. The implication was the technology frame became more aligned with the UA and shifted significantly toward a benefit frame.
