# Contextual determinants and performance implications of information systems strategy planning within South African firms

Jason F. Cohen \*

School of Economic and Business Sciences, University of the Witwatersrand, Johannesburg, Private Bag X3, WITS 2050, South Africa

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 9 April 2006 Received in revised form 12 September 2008 Accepted 19 September 2008 Available online 7 November 2008

Keywords: Strategic IS planning Context Environment Strategy Planning expertise Commitment IS performance

## A B S T R A C T

While studies have shown the effect of context on IS planning as important, the incorporation of contextual factors has not been systematic and the categorization of factors has not been made explicit and some factors have only been superficially examined. My study examined the interrelationships amongst four domains of context and considered their influence on IS planning. The corresponding impact of planning on performance was also assessed. Data was collected from 116 South African companies and results revealed that the internal IS context mediated the effects of environmental and organisational level variables on IS planning practice. Results further showed that IS planning intensity and degree of integration with business planning was strongly influenced by managerial planning expertise, and had positive effects on business management’s commitment to IS as well as on IS contribution to business performance. Results have important implications in organisational IS planning and performance.

## 1. Introduction

IS strategy planning (ISSP), or strategic IS planning, is an important part of IS management. Through it, organisations establish effective long-term use of IS and ensure their support of organisational objectives. This entails establishing priorities for implementation of new applications, developing policies and procedures for managing the IS function and organising IS services, and construction of a global, organisation wide, information architecture to facilitate data and application integration.

Many studies have tried to improve ISSP practice by discussing planning problems, identifying factors critical to the success of planning efforts, and providing tools and methodologies for carrying out the process. Others examined the relationship between ISSP and various performance and other outcomes; e.g. [20,25]. In contrast to those context free assessments, others have questioned the appropriateness of formal ISSP under varying contextual circumstances and examined a number of contextual variables for their effects on ISSP practice and its effectiveness. Their work was mainly based on contingency theory and suggested that organisations unhappy with the contribution of IS to organisational performance would need to realign their ISSP to fit their context. The derivation and incorporation of contextual factors has not, however, been systematic: the categorization of factors has not been explicit and results have been mixed.

The IS discipline still lacks an empirically validated theory of ISSP that provides answers to some fundamental questions. What are the determinants of ISSP practice? Which contextual factors are important and how strong are their influences? And how important is planning to improving IS performance?

The purpose of this study was to address some of these gaps. Firstly, I examined the interrelationships amongst variables selected from four domains (the external environment, outside of the organisation’s control; the internal organisation context; the internal IS context, factors that characterise the internal IS/IT; and the managerial context, attitudes, belief systems and experience of managers responsible for making choices) and their influence on ISSP. Secondly, I examined the effect of ISSP on IS performance, including consideration of the effect of business management’s commitment to IS.

Most empirical studies of ISSP have been conducted in developed, high-income economies, particularly in the USA, UK, Singapore, Australia, and Canada. However, issues such as improving IS strategic planning and the alignment of IS and business are growing in importance for IS executives in developing, middle income economies. As IS becomes a strategic resource for firms in developing economies, the focus of IS management should shift from a technology-centric concern to business-relationship issues.

Conducting the study in South Africa provided me with an opportunity to consider how to extend ISSP results from developed economies into the developing nations context.

## 2. Conceptual model and research variables

## 2.1. Conceptual model and theoretical background

This study drew on a number of theoretical perspectives to derive a model (Fig. 1) of how two important dimensions of ISSP practice (planning intensity and planning integration) were affected by the internal IS context (dependence on IS) as well as managerial context (their planning expertise). The model further considered the indirect effects of the external environment context (environmental dynamism) and internal organisational context (strategic orientation) on ISSP Practice through their effects on Dependence on IS. ISSP, was expected to accrue Planning Benefits, including Business commitment to IS and improved IS Performance. The arrows in the model denote hypothesized relationships.

External factors resulting in environmental dynamism invoke a strategic need for IS in an organisation. IS reduces uncertainty by improving both internal and external information flow across the supply chain [22].

Different strategic orientations have different information needs; organisations will depend on their IS to provide this and ensure the successful implementation of business strategy.

The contingency relationship between context, planning and performance lies at the centre of strategic management theory [35]. Planning intervenes between context and performance and accrues benefits. The commitment of business to IS, is considered an internal organisational planning benefit while the higher-order benefit of planning reflects its objective to improve the contribution of IS to business performance.

The development of the model was also influenced by upper echelons theory, which views organisations as reflections of their management team and thus an attempt to understand organisational actions and outcomes must include an assessment of the top managers responsible for making strategic choices.

## 2.2. Research variables

## 2.2.1. Environmental dynamism

An organisation operates in a dynamic environment if external changes occur at such a rapid pace that their products and services, technologies, and practices quickly become obsolete. In prior ISSP research, attempts were made to link environment directly to ISSP practice. However, results were inconclusive; some report that environmental uncertainty will result in less analysis during planning [27], others find that environmental uncertainty leads to increased planning comprehensiveness [28] and improved alignment with business planning [8]. Still, others have not confirmed effects [32] or have reported mixed results [18]. The model offers an alternate perspective: it proposes an intermediary linkage to ISSP through the environment’s effect on organisational dependence on IS. Kearns and Lederer found environmental uncertainty to have a positive direct effect on business dependence on IT.

## 2.2.2. Strategic orientation

Despite the possible effect of other organisational factors such as size and structure, the strategic orientation (business strategy) of an organisation has generally been viewed as a factor that exerts primary influence on IS use and management. It is a planned pattern of organisational response to the marketplace. Miller [16] suggests that it can be measured along the dimensions of innovation-differentiation and conservative cost control, each of which is a continuum; the first captures the extent to which an organisation adopts a proactive, offensive stance, exploiting opportunities, and differentiating its products or services: these are key characteristics of prospector and differentiation [19] oriented organisations. This can be contrasted with defenders or cost leaders who devote considerable attention to cost control, exploiting efficiencies, and maintaining a defensible position in a stable product-market domain. Prior studies [3,26] suggest that strategic orientation influences IS use within organisations and is thus an important contextual factor in the study of ISSP practice.

## 2.2.3. Organisational dependence on IS

Despite IT’s importance, firms will differ in their relative dependence on IS to achieve two key objectives: operational effectiveness and strategic positioning [24]. McFarlan et al.’s [13] strategic grid identifies the different roles played by IS within organisations depending on how critical IS applications are related to business operations and how important IS opportunities drive business strategy. Organisational dependence on IS is the most important of all internal IS context variables in the study of IS management practice [2].

## 2.2.4. Managerial planning expertise

In past ISSP studies, managerial context factors have been limited to the study of the effects of CIO rank; e.g. [12]. This is surprising given the recognition that other managerial factors may be important. Therefore, to better extend our understanding of variations in ISSP practice, the model drew on the upper echelons perspective and included a managerial variable labeled ‘planning expertise’. Following Hopkins and Hopkins [7], managerial planning expertise was defined as managers’ knowledge and expertise to successfully engage in the strategy planning process, and is considered an important managerial determinant of IS planning practice.

## 2.2.5. Information systems strategy planning

While some researchers, e.g. [15], have identified planning dimensions by conceptualising the ISSP process in terms of its phases, others suggest conceptualisation along content and process dimensions (what gets done and how they get done). In my study, two dimensions reflecting the content and process dimensions were included: planning intensity, the emphasis placed on various strategic planning activities [21], and planning integration, to ensure alignment of IS and business objectives and to create an awareness amongst business managers of the ‘‘strategic potential’’ of IS.

## 2.2.6. Commitment to IS

Higher-order financial and market effectiveness benefits of planning appear later than the internal organisational benefits of planning. IS planning can result in internal benefits, such as improved understanding of the potential contribution and role of IS and improved managerial commitment and support for IS. Commitment is important in ensuring successful implementation of IS objectives, and can be conceived as the feelings of business executives that IS objectives are important and relevant to them, that IS contributes to business performance, and therefore that IS objectives are important [23]. Gerbing et al. [6] suggested that planning benefits, including commitment, resulted from planning process characteristics such as the quantity and quality of management participation.

## 2.2.7. IS performance

IS performance, or IT business value, is the extent to which improvements in the operational, managerial and strategic performance of an organisation can be attributed to IS. This is consistent with Sabherwal’s conceptualisation, which focuses on ‘‘the contribution of IS products’’ to organisational success, based on Premkumar and King’s ‘‘contribution of IS to organisational performance’’ variable.

## 3. Hypotheses

## 3.1. Environmental dynamism and organisational dependence on IS

Organisations operating in dynamic environments need IT to help them monitor the changing market needs, coordinate internal responses to change, and maintain profitability by increasing internal efficiency [33]. In dynamic environments, IT plays a role in helping organisations to reduce cycle times, manage inventory and improve customer satisfaction [14]. Prior studies support the effect of environmental dynamism on IT dependence. Thus:

Hypothesis 1. The greater the degree of environmental dynamism, the greater will be organisational dependence on IS.

## 3.2. Strategic orientation and organisational dependence on IS

Das et al. contended that more conservative organisations would require systems that supported cost control and efficiency in operations and that their information processing requirements would usually be routine; on the other hand, innovative differentiators required systems that supported flexibility and diversity in operations, with requirements that varied and were dynamic, and enabled fast responses to changing market conditions. Furthermore, Sabherwal and Chan found that alignment between business and IS was more important for the business performance of prospectors than defenders, thus illustrating greater dependence on IS for prospector oriented organisations. Thus:

Hypothesis 2. The greater the degree of innovation-differentiation in an organisation’s strategic orientation, the greater will be organisational dependence on IS.

## 3.3. Organisational dependence on IS and ISSP

For organisations with a strategic dependence upon IS, formal planning, carefully considered implementation, and meticulous monitoring are required to ensure that strategic advantages from the ISs are achieved [10]. Comprehensiveness in planning will be needed because ad hoc, incremental and disconnected approaches to IS strategy formulation will not ensure a return on the investment [34]. Firms with a strategic role for IS will also need to link IS planning to the business plan. Thus:

Hypothesis 3. The greater the organisational dependence on IS, the greater will be the intensity of ISSP.

Hypothesis 4. The greater the organisational dependence on IS, the greater will be the degree of ISSP-BSP integration.

## 3.4. Managerial planning expertise and ISSP

Lack of knowledge of ISSP and available methodologies can lead to the selection of an inappropriate approach to IS planning [5]. Sufficiently qualified and experienced personnel involved in IS planning are critical to generating a sound IS plan but, without such expertise, organisations are likely to forego IS planning, or leave IS planning to consultants [1]. Experience and familiarity with IS planning will affect the planning team’s ability to overcome a lack of available information during planning, to identify relevant variables, and to ask appropriate questions [29]. Therefore:

Hypothesis 5. The greater the planning expertise of IS managers, the greater will be the intensity of ISSP.

Hypothesis 6. The greater the planning expertise of IS managers, the greater will be the degree of ISSP-BSP integration.

## 3.5. ISSP and commitment

Closely integrated IS and business planning processes bring IS stakeholders together and help establish their mutual understanding of the value of IS and its role in the organisation, resulting in increased top-management commitment to IS. Planning benefits accrue from managerial participation in the planning process. Thus

Hypothesis 7. The greater the intensity of ISSP, the greater will be the business commitment to IS.

Hypothesis 8. The greater the degree of ISSP-BSP integration, the greater will be the business commitment to IS.

## 3.6. Commitment and IS performance

Commitment of the business to IS is characterised by enthusiasm and positive demonstration of support for IS efforts [4]. If commitment is absent, the ability of IS to contribute to performance will be compromised. Managerial hesitancy, negative attitudes and doubts, and delaying initiatives will adversely affect IS performance, while commitment will translate into proactive cooperation [17], the provision of resources necessary for IS plan implementation, and support for corresponding organisational changes. Thus:

Hypothesis 9. The greater the business commitment to IS, the greater will be IS performance.

## 3.7. ISSP and IS performance

ISSP plays a role in helping firms visualise the potential contribution of IT by identifying broad initiatives, specific applications and critical technologies to help the organisation carry out its business strategy more effectively [9]. It also prevents wasted resources and missed opportunities. Sophisticated ISSP improves organisational use of IS by focusing on an analysis of internal processes and patterns of data dispersion, and identifying strategic applications capable of providing competitive advantage [30]. Thus:

Hypothesis 10. The greater the intensity of ISSP, the greater will be IS performance.

Hypothesis 11. The greater the degree of ISSP-BSP integration, the greater will be IS performance.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. The instrument

A structured questionnaire was constructed to capture information from senior IS executives. The instrument used five-point Likert type scales. Planning intensity of ISSP and ISSP-BSP integration were measured using multiple items from the literature. Dynamism measured the extent to which changes in the external business environment made the organisational unit’s products/services, technologies and competitive practices quickly obsolete. Three items consistent with Miller’s innovationdifferentiation dimension of strategic orientation measured the extent to which a firm attempted to innovate with its products/ services. Organisational dependence on IS was measured in terms of the operational and strategic contribution of IS to the organisation. Seven items, that drew on prior measures of the McFarlan grid, were used to reflect the extent to which an organisation is critically dependent on IS to support its operations and the importance of IS and applications under development for competitive success. A four-item scale was developed to measure planning expertise. Three items were used to measure managerial commitment to IS. IS performance was measured as the executive’s perception of the extent to which improvements in five measures of organisational performance could be attributed to IS. In the absence of objective performance measures, perceptual measures of performance have been considered acceptable surrogates in both business planning and IS planning, and have been considered generally consistent with objective measures. The questionnaire was pretested for content validity and clarity by four IS researchers and two strategy consultants. The pretest was followed by a pilot study involving ten senior IS executives. Their suggestions were incorporated into the revised instrument.

## 4.2. The sample

A sampling frame consisting of 615 organisations was developed from the 2002 edition of ‘‘Who Owns Whom in South Africa’’ published by McGregor. This is a comprehensive publication of listed and unlisted companies within South Africa operating in all sectors of the economy and holding prominent positions in their industry. Although not necessarily representative of all South African companies, this list was chosen because the organisations were medium to large firms, and were expected to be concerned with IT management issues and probably using IT for strategic purposes.

Consistent with other studies, the head of IS/IT within each organisation was the targeted respondent. In organisations where the responsibility for IS management practices lay in the individual business units, the questionnaire was mailed to the senior IS executive of the organisation’s primary or core business unit. The questionnaire together with the covering letter was mailed personally, where possible, to the targeted respondent.

## 4.3. Response analysis

121 responses were received, representing a response of 19.7%. However, after removing cases containing large amounts of missing data or those completed by inappropriate respondents, 116 useable responses remained. The industry profile of responses is shown in Table 1. Large organisations were well represented in the sample, with more than half consisting of organisations with more than 1000 employees (see Table 2). Most respondents carried the title of IS manager. Respondent job titles are presented in Table 3. The average IS experience of responding organisations was 18 years (median = 17 years and a range of 4–40 years). A little over 25% of the sample had over 25 years of IS experience.

Three tests were applied for non-response bias analysis. First, chi-squared tests were used to compare responding (n = 121) and non-responding (n = 494) organisations on industry. Results revealed no significant differences across industry. Second, it was found that the response rate was relatively consistent across industry groups. Finally, for the 116 useable responses, early (n = 77) and post-reminder (n = 39) respondents were compared on all scale items using independent sample t-tests and on organisation size using a chi-squared test. The t-tests showed no difference between early and late respondents for all but two (ANAL5 and DYN3). A chi-squared test also confirmed that early and late respondents did not differ with respect to organisation size. On the whole, these tests do not suggest the need for concern over non-response bias.

## 4.4. Common method bias

Since dependent and independent variable data was collected from a single key informant, a test for common method bias was needed. Harman’s one-factor test was used. Unrotated principal components analysis revealed 9 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, which accounted for 71% of the total variance. The first factor did not account for a majority of the variance (24.7%). As no single factor accounted for the majority of variance, common method bias did not appear to be a problem in the study.

## 5. Empirical results

## 5.1. Exploratory analysis

Prior to model testing, initial tests for validity and reliability were performed. Principal components factor analysis (PCFA) was carried out to assess the underlying factor structure of the scale items. Initially, it was carried out on each theoretically defined construct, in order to assess its unidimensionality. For all constructs, single factors emerged, with the exception of organisational dependence on IS: two dimensions emerged reflecting its operational and strategic contributions. To facilitate subsequent analysis, composite scores were calculated for these two dimensions as the aggregate of their items weighted equally. PCFA was then rerun with all the items, which were divided a priori into three groups reflecting (1) exogenous variables (dynamism, strategic orientation and managerial planning expertise), (2) endogenous variables (dependence on IS, ISSP practice, and commitment), and (3) dependent IS performance construct. ## 5.2. Model testing

The PLS approach to SEM was employed to test the study’s hypotheses. PLS-Graph ver 3.00 build 1126 was used. SEM can incorporate latent constructs and their observed indicators into the analysis but it does not provide model fit indices. Instead, R<sup>2</sup> statistics were used to confirm the predictive power of the model.

Analysis of the model proceeded in two phases. First an analysis of the measurement model was undertaken, and then an analysis of the structural model. The measurement model represented the relationships between the latent constructs and their indicators or measures. For the organisational dependence on IS construct, scores representing operational and strategic dependence were modeled. For all other constructs, the original scale items were modeled. All constructs were in the reflective mode. Table 6 presents results of the test.

Individual indicator reliability was established as the measurement model showed that all loadings exceeded 0.65 and were statistically significant. Scale reliability of the constructs was established by calculating Fornell and Larcker’s measure of internal consistency—all were greater than 0.7. Examination of the average variance extracted (AVE) for each latent construct showed that all had AVE’s in excess of 0.5 indicating that the constructs explain more than 50% of the variance in their observed measures. Convergent validity was thus established. Discriminant validity was also established by ensuring that the variance shared between two constructs was less than the variance shared between a construct and it indicators. This was achieved by examining the square root of the construct’s AVE, which was greater than the correlation between that construct and other constructs in the model (see Table 7). This analysis satisfied requirements for reliability, convergent and discriminant validity.

## 6. Discussion

The research supported Hypotheses 1–4. Together, these results may explain the inconsistencies in prior results by finding that dependence on IS mediates the effects of environmental and organisational factors on ISSP suggesting that the internal IS context must be appropriately matched to environmental and organisational factors, which in turn will dictate required IS managerial practices, including planning.

The study also overcame the general lack of tests for the effect of upper echelons by confirming Hypotheses 5 and 6. Firms in developing economies may face unique challenges related to managerial capabilities and expertise to engage in ISSP, and therefore improving knowledge of strategic IS management practices is likely to grow as a key IS management issue.

Because an incomplete and partial set of indicators of the determinism and upper echelon’s based perspectives were examined, it cannot be claimed that the managerial perspective offers a better explanation than the environmental or internal characteristics determinism based perspectives. However, results suggested they should not be ignored in explaining IS management practice.

The relationships between ISSP, commitment, and performance may be the study’s most interesting finding. Hypotheses 7–10 were supported. However, contrary to expectation, Hypothesis 11 found no support. ISSP-BSP integration does not have a direct effect on IS performance; the relationship is fully mediated by commitment. Commitment is an internal organisational benefit of planning that has subsequent implications for IS performance.

## 6.1. Limitations of the study

In interpreting these results it is important to note some limitations. First, data was collected from a single informant per organisation and data collected was largely perceptual; it would have been better to assess environmental and organisational level variables as well as performance using more objective criteria. Furthermore, results may also have been stronger if multiple organisational respondents had been used.

Second, data collected was cross-sectional, and therefore no claims about causality can be made. A longitudinal research design would provide stronger evidence for causality.

Third,thestudywasconductedusingdatacollectedfromasample of medium to large South African organisations. Results therefore may not necessarily be generalizable to organisations in South Africa outside the sample frame. As a result of these limitations the findings should be regarded as indicative of the relationships existing between context, ISSP and performance rather than conclusive.

## 7. Implications for practice

The work confirmed that the internal IS context is strongly influenced by environmental and organisational factors. These dictate the importance of IS, the role it plays within the organisation, and the resources and attention that should be devoted to it by senior business management. IS managers should develop an internal IS context appropriate to the environmental and organisational contexts. Changes in the internal IS context will necessitate a corresponding change in ISSP practice. In particular, IS managers should look for changes in environmental and organisational circumstances which increase information uncertainty and thus reorient the role that IS will have to play within the organisation.

The results also suggest that sophisticated ISSP practices are more likely to prevail when IS managers have more ISSP expertise. It is important that ISSP practice can be improved and appropriately matched to the context.

Finally, IS planning serves to improve the contribution of IS to business performance by steering IS efforts in the right direction. However, business managers must recognise the role they need to play in supporting the IS function. Their commitment to IS serves to create the necessary conditions for aiding IS in improving business performance.

## 8. Conclusion

This paper presented a study of the contextual determinants and performance implications of ISSP practice. The effects of environmental and organisational factors on ISSP were found to be mediated by the internal IS context. Specifically, organisational dependence on IS increased amongst those organisations that saw their environments as dynamic and their strategic success through innovationdifferentiation. Dependence on IS is a significant determinant of ISSP planning intensity and its integration with BSP. Moreover, managerial expertise to engage in planning was a significant determinant. Planning was also foundto add to IS performance directly and also by improving management commitment to IS.
