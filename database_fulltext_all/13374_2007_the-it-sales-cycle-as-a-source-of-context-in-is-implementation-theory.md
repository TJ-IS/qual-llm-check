# The IT sales cycle as a source of context in IS implementation theory

Michael Wybo <sup>\*</sup>

Department of Information Technologies, HEC Montre´al, 3000 Chemin de la Coˆte-Sainte-Catherine, Montre´al, Que´bec H3T 2A7, Canada Received 20 June 2005; received in revised form 4 July 2006; accepted 3 March 200 Available online 25 April 2007

## Abstract

Theories of systems implementation identify contextual factors that influence both management action and implementation outcomes. Building on this, I showed using case studies, how the actions of participants in the IT sales cycle create the context within which implementation takes place. I argue that implementation begins much earlier than current theories have assumed and should be considered to consist of two distinct but related processes: the acquisition of IS and their deployment in the organization. <sup>#</sup> 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: IS implementation; IT sales cycle; Acquisition of IT

## 1. Introduction

The study of IT implementation is a core activity in IS [20] and implementation represents a major component of an organization’s expenditures on new IT. Over time, various theoretical perspectives on implementation have been developed; each focused on some specific construct or set of related constructs to explain implementation outcomes such as systems use, user satisfaction, systems quality, and budget and schedule performance. Some of the better researched models of IT implementation success have proposed technology acceptance [1], user participation [4], management style [3], resource availability [5], tasktechnology fit [12], and project governance mode [10] as key explanatory variables.

Aubert et al. [2] analyzed the IT implementation literature and identified a common architecture across various theories of IT implementation. In their analysis, theories of IT implementation were shown to contain three types of variables:

\- Contextual ‘‘refer to individual, project, project team, and organizational characteristics that can influence the outcome of IT implementations and that are more or less fixed at the beginning of the implementation effort’’.

\- Implementation describe ‘‘management actions and decisions that govern or influence how an IT implementation effort is carried out and executed’’.

\- Outcome represent the results of the implementation effort, generally in terms relevant to management evaluation of the project.

The common architecture of the key contextual, implementation, and outcome variables, and the organization of these variables into four different ‘‘perspectives’’ on implementation by these authors is shown in Fig. 1.

This common architecture is helpful in thinking about and comparing different perspectives on IT implementation. Yet it also begs the question of the origins of context. The context of an IT implementation project includes all those ‘‘individual, project, project team, and organizational characteristics’’ that influence implementation outcomes and management action. While certain of these characteristics may be ‘‘fixed at the beginning of the implementation effort’’, for example the composition of a project team, the fact that certain individuals are members of that team is the result of some decision taken before the project begins. Likewise, to state that the degree of task-technology fit or the level of performance ambiguity are ‘‘contextual’’ is to ignore the process by which the product being implemented has been evaluated and chosen and the contract for implementation services specified.

Fig. 1. Common architecture of theories of IT implementation.

I argue that the IT sales cycle, as an antecedent to most implementations, is an important source of implementation context, that therefore implementation begins much earlier than current theory suggests, and that understanding IT implementation would be better served by considering acquisition activities as part of an overall implementation process.

The question of the origins of context is important for two reasons:

1. Context plays an important role in implementation theory. The greater the extent to which the process of context creation is understood the more it can be managed, which, in turn, increases an implementer’s ability to manage or influence outcomes. Thus, it is important to understand the origins of key components of context and the process that creates them.

![](/api/attachments/J5QGDUJP/fulltext/images/308da5111cc2363509ed69fec09e47a0df16d5a33fee7fae1d5a5469625e4bb5.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Implementation theory causal structure incorporating the sales cycle.

2. Certain contextual variables imply a sales-oriented process or activity prior to deployment of the application. For example, the organizational economics perspective posits performance ambiguity and uncertainty as contextual variables. Reducing the uncertainty surrounding the capabilities of a software application and the ambiguity concerning its performance are the objectives of the demonstration and conference room pilot phases of the IT sales cycle. Reducing the uncertainty relative to who is responsible for which implementation activities and any ambiguity regarding levels of performance that will constitute acceptance by each party are the subject of the negotiations phase of an IT services contract.

It is quite common for organizations to meet their software and IT services requirements through use of an outside vendor. I propose that the IT (software and services) sales cycle is one of the main processes from which the context of IT implementations originates. The IT sales cycle has therefore superseded the internal software development process as a principal arena in which the users and providers of software functionality meet. In a purchased software environment, instead of specifying needed functionality, users must evaluate existing systems (e.g., COTS) to be purchased. Rather than interacting with and managing technical specialists, they interact with a vendor’s sales staff. They must negotiate the system’s characteristics with an external vendor or consultant personnel, who may act as extended staff or managers of the project. Graphically, the architecture of IT implementation theory should therefore be modified to Fig. 2, where the activities of the IT sales cycle are recognized as the source of key contextual variables which influence implementation variables and outcomes.

## 2. The IT sales cycle

An IT purchase is normally expensive, almost always involves operational changes, and often has strategic consequences. As such, the financial stability of the vendor, its R&D and support capabilities, and its understanding of the buyer’s industry are as important as the characteristics of the product. Buyers are obtaining both a product and a relationship with the vendor.

Various authors have described the IT acquisition process and its dynamics [7–9,14–19]. The typical IT sales cycle is:

1. Buyers or their agents (system integrators) solicit vendors with a request for information (RFI).

2. A shorter list of vendors is invited to respond to a request for proposal (RFP). The buyer is more precise about its needs and vendors are required to respond to a set of detailed questions about their solution, implementation experience, and propose a cost and schedule for the project. The proposed solution may include the vendor’s products, third party products, and vendor and third party consulting, deployment, operational, or training services, etc.

3. Two or three vendors are invited to demonstrate their solution to the evaluation team, which normally consists of a project leader, user management, selected users, and representatives from IT. It often does not include the economic decision maker and the project team may not be certain that it has a budget for the project.

4. A preferred solution is identified and the vendor is invited to conduct a conference room pilot (CRP). This is an extended demonstration that can last for several days or even weeks. CRPs are becoming increasingly common since they give the buying company a chance to further evaluate the application’s deliverable functionality, its performance, ease of use, and gauge user reaction and eventual acceptance.

5. Contract negotiations and closing are the culmination of the sales cycle. It is not uncommon to negotiate the size and composition of the deployment team from both the vendor and buyer side, to specify dates and specifications for system modifications to be delivered both before and after deployment, and to negotiate additional work involving integration with other applications. Negotiation rarely includes members of the buyer’s or the vendor’s deployment teams.

Certain characteristics of the IT sales cycle itself reinforce its role as a process from which implementation context originates:

1. The need to evaluate a product’s functionality requires the participation of key actors in the deployment process from both the vendor and the prospect (customer). The corporate and personal vendor–buyer relationships that will be the foundation of the deployment effort have their beginning in the sales process.

2. The contract between the vendor and the purchasing firm will include terms that specify support and implementation activities, determining the governance mode, project structure and project participants, schedule, budget, and resources available. All of these outcomes are identified as important contextual factors in various theories of implementation.

3. The vendor generally benefits from a smooth and successful implementation and will use the sales cycle to help ensure success. This is especially true because successful deployment at one client is a major part of selling a product to other clients.

Characteristics of an application that emerge during deployment in development-oriented implementations (missing functionality, software performance issues, integration requirements, and supplier–user team dynamics) are knowable in purchase-oriented implementations before deployment. Certain techniques for managing around emerging issues may be less important since these issues can be known earlier in the process and measures taken to avoid them. Issues such as acquisition of the vendor by another company or vendor financial difficulties may require different management techniques that do not appear in current implementation theory. Incorporation of the sales cycle in implementation research can help to understand and plan for these circumstances.

## 3. Methodology

My objective is to extend implementation theory to include the IT sales cycle as a source of implementation context. Such conceptual development requires a rich understanding of the phenomenon from which fundamental relationships can be extracted. Qualitative methods are generally viewed as most appropriate for empirical validation during early stages of conceptual development [6,13]. I therefore employed an inductive theory building process based on [11]. Instances of the IT sales cycle over an extended period were observed and evidence of their influence on implementation was determined. The evidence was corroborated using interviews with executives from a software vendor whose activities were not part of the initial observations. The extended observations and interviews together contributed to the development of a conceptual model.

This model was then compared to the wellestablished body of literature. The mapping of the sales cycle conceptual model to existing implementation theory strengthens my principal argument: that implementation theory should be extended to include the sales cycle as a source of identified contextual factors.

## 3.1. Development of the sales cycle conceptual model

As a manager of software development teams, I personally attended approximately 20 sales campaign planning and execution meetings across two software vendors over a period of 4 years. Participants at these meetings generally included sales, services, and general management personnel. In addition, I reviewed RFI and RFQ related to these campaigns, attended numerous product demonstrations, and observed the CRP and the implementation of the two company’s products at five different clients.

From these observations emerged a conceptual understanding of the relationship between sales and implementation in practice. This relationship is shown in Fig. 3; e.g., the conceptual understanding of the sales cycle activity ‘‘response to RFI’’ was that this activity influences both the buyer’s definition of their problem and the nature of acceptable solutions. Likewise, at the conceptual level, the activity ‘‘response to RFP/RFQ’’ serves to influence the buyer’s understanding of their problem and its solution and sets expectations of the cost and level of effort required.

After leaving these companies I conducted a series of interviews with sales, services, and general management personnel from a medium sized software development company. The purpose of the interviews was to validate the relationships observed and stated in the conceptual model. The vendor had been in business for approximately 5 years at the time of the interviews. All the respondents had been in the software business for 15–20 years and all had direct experience in selling and implementing software and/or services in this company and previous large software vendors. The company sold a single product in the North American apparel industry and focused on requirements specific to that industry. Their clients were mid-size to large (\$ 500 million to over \$ 2 billion) companies in the apparel industry.

![](/api/attachments/J5QGDUJP/fulltext/images/914dd8c1e3998187cd9d54ebb0f1800050a3baeb4bf02c94931ae92f42ba44b8.jpg)  
Fig. 3. A conceptual model of the IT sales cycle.

The sales cycle of the company was typical of that employed by the respondents in their previous positions and their clients were familiar with it from other software purchases. Minor variations in the process were associated with different clients, such as the number of prospective solutions included on the short list, the duration and depth of the conference room pilot, etc. These variations were consistent with the observed sales processes.

I conducted interviews with the sales and professional services vice presidents, the CEO, and various sales representatives and professional services staff. The participants were encouraged to discuss their experiences and approaches to the sales cycle and implementation. The five-stage model of the sales cycle was used as a guide for structuring the discussions.

Interview notes were written. These were reviewed and completed immediately after the interviews. Any areas of confusion or missing information were clarified in a follow up phone call to ensure that their meaning had been accurately captured.

The text of the interview notes was analyzed using a coding scheme based on the model. For example, the statement ‘‘Her job is to figure out who would work well with whom and how we should put together our different teams in order to match up skills and personalities and styles’’ was coded as ‘‘vendor exposes buyer to potential vendor team members’’. In this way the relationships that emerged from observations made over a long period at two vendors were corroborated with interview data collected later from a third, unrelated vendor engaged in a similar sales process.

## 4. Extending implementation theory

The mapping of the conceptual model was conducted for four types of contextual variables: fit, size and scope, project team composition, and those related to the contract.

## 4.1. How the sales cycle influences fit

At the heart of any sales (and marketing) effort is the vendor’s attempt to influence the buyer’s perception of fit between its technology and buyer needs. Vendors attempt to influence the perception of the problem to fit their technology, present the aspects of the technology that best fit the task, or both. In many cases buyers use the process as an opportunity to help define the nature of the buyer’s problem.

The RFI from X-Apparel was very open ended; the questions were all over the place. It was clear that they knew they needed to do something in the operations area, especially with the extent of production outsourcing they were involved with, but they weren’t sure just what. . . We found out who else was on the short list. . . They were looking at us (a web-based operations package), an integration middle-ware vendor, and a consulting firm with no package to sell. It was clear they weren’t sure whether they had an applications functionality problem, a systems integration problem, or a business process problem. From that point our strategy changed from convincing them they needed our application to convincing them they needed an application. Later we would worry about selling our product. (VP Sales)

The VP Sales identified his sales objective as convincing the buyer that the nature of the problem was application related. He thus attempted to influence the buyer’s perception of the task in order to be invited to come back to compete on functionality. Through the sales process the vendor’s attempts to change the buyer’s understanding of their task can change their assessment of task-technology fit.

The buyer’s perception of fit is also an object of negotiation in the latter part of the sales cycle. It is common for a buyer to identify new functionality missing in the initial specifications. The degree to which such gaps must be filled is negotiable, with the vendor normally attempting to redefine the buyer’s task to fit the existing functionality rather than investing in the development of specific functionality that may be of little interest to other customers.

When we were negotiating terms, the IT manager who serviced the production group kept bringing up the fact that, in our product, delays recorded for a task did not automatically cascade down the rest of the process and change estimated start dates for downstream tasks. Our product manager was able to convince their IT guy that he should not want some sewing plant operator to affect reserved capacity at the finishing plant because he thought he might be late. The system only allows select users to make schedule changes, and that is the best way to run the business. (VP Sales)

Finally, the buyer may simply accept that the fit between task and technology is not perfect and continue without changes. The definition of an acceptable fit having been agreed as part of the negotiation phase (Table 1).

I received a call from one of the users and he was furious. He was complaining that a particular module kept crashing and losing data. That was one of the modules that we knew had problems and had negotiated to fix in a future release. We had provided a workaround and they weren’t supposed to be using this module. Apparently no one on the deployment team had ever told this guy or showed him the workaround or maybe he missed that day in training. (VP Services)

## 4.2. How the sales cycle influences size and scope

The size and scope of an IT deployment is, of course, related to the type of technology being deployed. However, within the parameters of a particular application, there is an opportunity to influence the project’s size and scope.

The vendor often has conflicting motivations when it comes to project size. A large project means a larger income (license revenues and recurring maintenance revenue). The vendor’s professional services organization will be able to show high utilization rates and margins on a long running deployment. On the other hand, most vendors realize that a dissatisfied client (over-budget, off-schedule, etc.) will not be a positive future reference. In addition, the vendor runs the risk of having internal resources overly committed to a single customer to the detriment of other clients. Thus, a vendor has incentives to influence the size of the project, a key contextual variable from the resource management and organizational economics perspectives, to balance their revenues and staff utilization to ensure that both they and the client can handle the work (Table 2).

## 4.3. How the sales cycle influences project team composition

The makeup of the project team, the skills and personalities of its members, its history of working together on previous projects, the leadership style of the team leader, and the level of top management support accorded the project have all been identified as important contextual variables.

During the course of the sales cycle, both the vendor and the buyer interact with members of each other’s organization. During the demo and CRP phases this interaction can include members of the potential deployment teams, services, support, and training personnel from the vendor, and project managers, IT personnel, user management and users from the buyer. As the sales cycle continues, the skills, personalities, leadership styles and experiences of particular individuals become obvious to both the vendor and the buyer. Vendors may intentionally cultivate personal relationships with influential members of the buyer’s organization, and a set of social relationships are built in a series of lunches, dinners, discussions, and rides to the airport.

We joke about it, we call it ‘‘eating for our country’’, but it is a serious part of our job. We even have a person in our sales group whose role is to get to know the client personally. Her job is to figure out who would work well with whom and how we should put together our different teams in order to match up skills and personalities and styles. We want the client to know that we will be able to work together to complete the project, that there won’t be any people problems. (VP Services)

This information gathering is important at the negotiation phase. The buyer may stipulate that certain members of the vendor team be made available to the project, sometimes even requiring that they be colocated in or close to the buyer’s offices. The vendor may also make suggestions as to the makeup of the buyer’s project team, especially when it comes to removing individuals who have been hostile to the project (Table 3).

We like to hire recent graduates for our services team. They are smart, full of energy, relate well to the client’s junior staff, and what’s really important is that they are unattached. They still think it is fun to get on a plane every Monday morning to spend the week working in another city. And the benefits of providing the customer with a dedicated resource in their offices are huge. (VP Services)

## 4.4. How the sales cycle influences contract terms

Contract terms are the output of the negotiation phase and the culmination of the sales cycle. All the experience and information gained by the vendor and the buyer comes to bear on the negotiation of terms.

We went into the K-company negotiations with some non-negotiable items, mostly around product functionality and how new functionality and enhancements would be requested and delivered. Based on what we learned in the sales cycle there was no way we were going ahead without a clearly agreed upon change request procedure, and we were definitely not going to let them tie our getting paid to the delivery of anything other than the system as it existed at the time of the CRP. (CEO)

These terms are a product of the information gathered during the sales process. As part of the sales process, the dynamics of the negotiation phase also influence terms, especially where one party is a better negotiator.

We knew we were in trouble when they told us we would be negotiating with the manager responsible for their global apparel purchases. That business is brutal and this person is an expert. She had us begging for mercy by the time we finished. We can justify the terms based on the importance of J-Group in the industry and the fact that they agreed in writing to introduce us to a number of their suppliers. But we are not making any money on this project; our payoff is in the long term. We have to ensure that the next few deals provide a sufficient margin and cash flow. (CEO)

The negotiation process not only influences contract terms but can also influence future sales cycles (Table 4).

## 5. Discussion

Including the sales cycle in studies of implementation provides a more complete understanding of the phenomenon. Current theories conceive of implementation context as fixed and static, and different perspectives on implementation posit different contextual variables. Incorporating the sales cycle helps to unify some of these perspectives by identifying a common source for the origin of many contextual variables as well as highlighting the interplay between them.

![](/api/attachments/J5QGDUJP/fulltext/images/8749197ce50790854fe55e74e36f7b2ea4956bc02b432d392d9c97a254331e23.jpg)  
Fig. 4. Example of refinement in the case of the organizational economics perspective.

An organizational economics (OE) perspective provides a concrete example of how inclusion of the sales cycle can refine implementation theory. From the OE perspective, contract type, incentive structure, and appropriate allocation of control are identified as variables that managers manipulate during implementation. By taking into account the sales cycle, these variables might be considered as direct outcomes of contract negotiations between the vendor and the buyer rather than variables that managers manipulate during execution of the project. This would change their place in theory from implementation to contextual variables. Furthermore, managers involved in negotiating the values of these variables may not be the same persons responsible for the implementation, resulting in a need for a new implementation variable concerning the communication of contract type, incentives, and allocation of control to the project execution team. Theoretical refinement would include (1) restructuring the theory by reconsidering certain current implementation variables as context variables, (2) identifying a process by which these contextual variables can be influenced, and (3) identifying new implementation variables that may deepen our understanding and management of implementation. This is shown by the bold text in Fig. 4.

In terms of expanding implementation theory, inclusion of the sales cycle permits us to integrate the acquisition of IT into a more comprehensive understanding of IS practice. Our understanding of implementation might evolve to look more like Fig. 5, where it is understood to consist of two distinct but related processes, the acquisition of IS and their deployment in the organization.

Note that neither social norms nor organizational characteristics are proposed as being influenced by the dynamics of the sales cycle. They remain as part of a static context within which implementation takes place and they would need to be considered in our treatment of IT implementation.

Finally, it is fair to ask why the sales cycle has not been previously included in our theories of IS implementation. It could be that in reality no such relationship exists or that it makes no sense and so has been ignored. I have tried to provide evidence that such a relationship does exist and that it does make sense to consider the sales cycle as a process in which implementation context is created.

## 6. Summary and conclusion

Theories of IS implementation are a core research area. Today’s IS environment is largely focused on purchasing and configuring pre-existing functionality rather than on specifying and building new functionality. I argue that, in practice, the IS sales cycle serves as the origin for many of the contextual factors that are a key part of implementation theory. As such, implementation begins much earlier than suggested in current theory. Context can be influenced and managed with implications for the subsequent implementation.

Operationally, the sales cycle can be defined as beginning at first contact between the vendor and the buyer and ending with the signature of a contract between the parties. The sales cycle is followed by deployment. Accounting for the activities of the sales cycle in an expanded definition of implementation (acquisition + deployment) has two distinct advantages. First, it provides a more complete picture of the dynamics of the implementation process and permits researchers and practitioners to understand and better influence implementation outcomes. Secondly, it can influence conduct in the sales process so that it can be managed to support successful implementations. Understanding the IT implementation process as both acquisition and deployment activities provides a more coherent perspective on implementation outcomes and processes. Such a view can help practice by better management of implementation and by providing both vendors and buyers of IT with greater potential control over the process.
