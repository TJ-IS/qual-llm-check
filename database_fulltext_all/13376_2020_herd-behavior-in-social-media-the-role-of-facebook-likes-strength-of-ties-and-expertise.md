## Abstract

When do social media users click on sponsored content or intend to visit the website at a later time? A qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) using arguments based on herd theory, strength of ties, and social distance shows that only “likes” from socially close and knowledgeable users can consistently generate click-through or view-through intentions. Considering social tie strength in a herd behavior context, the analysis of sufficient configurations for click- and viewthrough intentions provides a nuanced perspective on social media user behavior and social influence. For instance, click-through intention requires observing a “like” from a close person, while view-through intentions can also develop after observing “likes” from less close acquaintances, yet in the last case only if the user assumes the acquaintance is better informed regarding the sponsored content. In addition, a “like” from a close friend deemed better informed can even make a user click on a sponsored content that was not considered valuable before.

Keywords: Social media advertising, social networking sites, fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA), herd behavior, strengths of ties, click-through, view-through

## Introduction

Imagine you are browsing through your Facebook newsfeed after work. Next to pictures and posts disclosed by your Facebook friends, you also see sponsored content about a certain branded product. At first, you do not intend to click on the sponsored content, for instance, to obtain more information about the product. But then you see that others including a close friend have “liked” it, and then you change your mind and click on the content. What made you click on the link after seeing that a close friend liked it, and would you have followed the link if it had only been liked by some remote acquaintance of yours or only by people you do not know? Companies frequently pay for sponsored content on social media platforms such as Facebook, Instagram, or LinkedIn to interact with potential customers while they sit at home in front of their PC or while using smartphones or tablets when traveling. Companies benefit from using sponsored content in two ways [1]. First, the content is clicked by social media users, hereafter referred to as click-through. The click typically redirects users to the product website. Second, users can view the content and intend to visit the company’s website at a later date, hereafter referred to as view-through [1]. While U.S. companies spent around \$38 billion for displaying sponsored content in social media [2], much of it misses its goal. Only a small percentage of sponsored content is actually clicked on and sponsored content does not lead all users to visit the company’s website at a later time [3]. As sponsoring content that does not lead to click-through or view-through behavior is a wasted opportunity for companies to generate website traffic, companies have a strong interest in displaying their sponsored content to selected users who are When a user sees a sponsored content in social media, he/she processes two information simultaneously, which then influence click-through and view-through intention. The user perceives who has “liked” the sponsored content. Beyond that, the user also perceives the sponsored content itself. The literature strand on herd behavior offers some interesting studies showing that the observation of others’ behavior influences one’s own behavior [4–6]. Adapted to sponsored content on social media, we assume observing the “like” of a sponsored ad to influence click-through and view-through intention. Initial research indicates that “likes” are assessed differently depending on the person who has “liked” something [7]. The social relationship with the person whose behavior is observed by the user influences that user’s behavior. More precisely, the persons who have “liked” a sponsored content can be strongly tied to the user (e.g., a close friend), weakly tied (e.g., an acquaintance) or can have no tie (e.g., unknown person) [8] so that different degrees of social relationships can determine individual behavior in different ways [9,10]. Therefore, we theorize that observing the “like” of a strongly, weakly tied, or unknown predecessor influences a user’s click-through and view-through intention. In addition, based on the literature on social media advertising that reveals how processing sponsored content influences a user’s behavior [11], we can theorize that a user’s

overall evaluation of the sponsored content influences a user’s click-through and view-through intention.

In summary, the literature offers good yet separate insights on possible click- or view-through drivers. It is theoretically and empirically unclear, though, which of those is more important and how their interaction influences click- or view-through intention. This leaves many observable cases unexplained. For instance, there might be a user whose click-through or view-through intention is caused by a sponsored content, which is “liked” by a close friend even though the sponsored content itself is not evaluated as relevant. To disclose how such interactions — e.g., how different combinations of observing “likes” from peers of varying social distance and the user’s perception of the sponsored content — influence click-through and view-through intention, we theoretically integrate both research streams. Our research question is:

Under what condition does observing sponsored content that has been “liked” by an individual with a) strong, b) weak, or c) no ties to a focal user lead to that user’s click-through and view-through intention?

To answer this question, we draw on herd theory and adapt it to our context. We conduct a scenario-based study with 168 participants and apply a configurational approach using fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA). The analyses reveal that sponsored content “liked” by others including a strongly tied predecessor leads to click-through and view-through intentions, while sponsored content “liked” by others including a weakly tied and experienced friends only triggers view-through intentions. Sponsored content only “liked” by unknown persons does not explain click-through nor view-through intention, even if the sponsored content is perceived as valuable. There are also interesting gender differences. The influence of observing a “like” from a strongly tied and experienced friend is so strong for female users that it can lead to view-

through intention even if female users perceive the overall value of the sponsored content as low. The influence of a strong tie can influence male users so that they have a click-through or viewthrough intention, even if they do not think the strong tie is better informed regarding the sponsored content. Interviews with a panel of practitioners confirm the practical relevance of our findings or practitioners.

This study contributes to the literature about herding by expanding herd theory with the strength of weak ties theory, thus theorizing that differently tied predecessors are essential to understand herd behavior. A further contribution is the integration of tie strength and a predecessor’s content expertise, highlighting to what extent the observed strongly and weakly tied predecessors need to be perceived as experienced. We also contribute to the literature about sponsored content and social media. Here, we show that observing “likes” leads to click-through and view-through intention, which is also influenced by users’ frequency of using social media. Finally, the paper shows a differentiated perspective into the underlying mechanism of herd behavior; we can show that observing a strongly tied predecessor exerts a stronger influence than observing a weakly tied predecessor.

Online platforms can utilize their user data and apply our findings in designing advertising technology, targeting algorithms to display sponsored content more efficiently, and generate more website traffic and potentially more profit for companies that are sponsoring content. Furthermore, recommendation systems use social recommendations to boost user engagement in online environments. This includes social commerce websites that can optimize traffic for their sponsored search results and sponsored posts.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. We begin by outlining the research context, herd theory, and theorize under what conditions observing a “like” might lead to click-through and view-through intention. Afterward, we describe our methodology, the scenario-based study, our data analysis process, and validity checks. We then present and discuss results of the study and discuss the resulting propositions, its limitations, theoretical implications, and practical contributions.

## Theoretical Background

To answer the research question, we first introduce the concept of sponsored content in social media and then outline herd theory as the guiding theory in this paper. Based on this, we review relevant literature to explain click-through and view-through intention and finally outline our configurational model.

## Sponsored Content in Social Media and “Likes”

Companies in the U.S. spent around \$38 billion for displaying sponsored content in social media in 2020 [2]. Sponsored content in social media refers to persuasive and planned advertising or marketing messages spread through social media and which appear in newsfeed. Therefore, the content is shown to a user while visiting social media platforms. Sponsored content is enriched with social media’s social features so that, for instance, a user can post comments on the sponsored content or share the sponsored content in their newsfeed. The most prominent social media feature used for sponsored content is the “like” function, which enables a user to express positive sentiment through “liking” sponsored content. “Likes” are bound to a specific user and are cumulative and openly viewable for other users [12]. Therefore, sponsored content in social media is enriched with the information of who has “liked” the sponsored content before. This

implies that when a user is exposed to sponsored content, the user does not only see an

advertising or marketing message from a company, rather the user additionally observes whether

others have commented, shared, or “liked” the sponsored content. This means a user can observe

the behavior of other users in response to sponsored content.

There are two major research strands regarding the role of “likes” in social media. One research

strand focuses on why a user “likes” something in social media, while the other focuses on how

“likes” influence the user’s behavior.

Previous research regarding why a user “likes” something in social media shows that a user’s

personality traits and mode have an influence on “liking” posts on Facebook [13]. A user is more

likely to “like” a news article on Facebook that matches his/her point of view [14] and that is

perceived as useful [15]. Posts that use rich media are associated with a higher number of “likes”

[16,17], while lower readability of posts generally reduces the number of “likes” [18].

The research regarding how “likes” influence the user’s behavior shows that most research

focuses on the effect of the number of “likes” on brand pages on social media [12,19,20]. For

instance, observing a friend’s “like” of a brand page can increase a user’s willingness to engage

with this brand [21] and the number of brand page “likes” have a positive influence on offline

customer behavior [19] as well as long-term sales [12]. Only a few studies examine the influence

of “likes” attached to content in social media. Research in that field generally shows that “likes”

received for one’s own posts about an experience can influence a user’s satisfaction with this

experience [22]. The number of “likes” positively influences a user's evaluation of a friend's

posts on Facebook [7]. Sponsored content with a higher number of “likes” is perceived to be

more credible, which in turn can lead to a positive attitude toward the brand [23]. We know that

the number of “likes” that a movie prerelease post receives correlates with the overall gross revenue of movie theatres [24]. In the field of e-commerce, we see that the number of “likes” attached to products drive traffic and increase sales [25]. Similar results are found in the field of group-buying platforms where the number of “likes” attached to deals has a positive influence on purchase intention [26]. In summary, we see that research has devoted a lot of effort into different aspects of “likes” in social media, yet it remains unclear how observing “likes” influences click-through and view-through intention.

## Herd Theory

This research focuses on the context of sponsored content and the influence of observing “likes” on click-through and view-through intention. We posit that herd theory [4,5] is a useful theoretical perspective to offer further insights. Herd theory explains how observing prior behavior of others influences one’s own current behavior and our research focuses on effects of a user observing that others have “liked” sponsored content. Besides, herd theory states that a user only observes the behavior without assessing reasons behind the behavior, which applies to observe that others “liked” a sponsored content.

Herd behavior describes the phenomenon of how a user is influenced by observing others’ prior behavior and imitates this behavior even if their own information supports a different behavior [4,5]. When observing predecessors' behavior, herd behavior can be seen as consisting of two aspects that lead to herd behavior, namely discounting own information initially formed and imitating others [27].

For herd behavior to occur, a user needs to observe the prior behavior of predecessors. If a user needs to decide how to behave and the user observes how someone else has previously behaved, he/she is more likely to either perform the same or a similar behavior [6]. According to the extant herd literature, for herd behavior to occur, a user observes the behavior of unknown persons — the “herd.” The herd can either consist of only unknown persons [27–32] or can include specific persons such as a friend [33,34] or an experienced person [35]. The first aspect of herd behavior is that a user discounts his/her own information, disregard his/her own opinion, and thus rely less on his/her own beliefs than on observed predecessors’ behavior. This may reflect a preference for assuming that predecessors are better informed over investing high cognitive efforts to analyze the decision and make the best possible decision [6]. The second aspect of herd behavior is imitating meaning that a user converges on a behavior similar to the behavior of the observed predecessors [4–6,27]. Thus, a user only performs herd behavior because the predecessor has performed a similar behavior.

In summary, herd theory proposes that the observation of predecessors’ behavior, the discounting of own information, and the imitation of others collectively lead to herd behavior. The theoretical concept of herd theory can be transferred to the context of “liking” sponsored content, as research focused on social media suggest that the observation of “likes” on social media, which are commonly interpreted as signals of quality, may trigger herd behavior [36]. In the case of “liking” sponsored content, herd behavior may extend beyond copying the exact same behavior to other behavior, such as purchasing [36]. In other words, observing that predecessors have “liked” a sponsored content, might not only influence a user’s intention to “like” the content as well, but the influence can go beyond that and influence other behavior, such as clicking the sponsored content or visiting the company’s website at some point. This is in line with herd literature [6] showing that herd behavior not only explains copying the exact same behavior but can also explain similar behavior (for a review of recent herd literature in IS and marketing, see [32]).

## Towards a Configurational Model of Herd Behavior for Sponsored Content

We base on herd theory [4,5] and define herd behavior in the context of sponsored content as a user observing that others have “liked” the sponsored content and imitating this behavior by clicking on the sponsored content (click-through intention) or developing an intention to visit the company’s website later on (view-through intention). Consequently, in the context of sponsored content, herd behavior is manifested by increased click-through and view-through intention.

IS and marketing literature has mainly considered the click-through intention as decisive behavior in response to sponsored content, particularly online ads. In line with prior research, we define click-through intention as the willingness to directly interact with the sponsored content by clicking on it to obtain more information about the company and/or its products or services [1]. The direct click on sponsored content is often the key indicator of so-called performance advertising campaigns, which aim to generate direct clicks on sponsored content [37].

Companies often pay based on the “cost per click” model, meaning that the amount the company pays the social media platform is determined by how often the sponsored content is clicked on [38]. Extant research has identified that personalized sponsored content [39] can lead to higher click-through intention. Research into clicks on social media, in general, has found that users are more likely to click on sponsored content if they have high control over their personal information in social media [40].

A user can also look at the sponsored content and develop the intention to visit the website directly at a later point in time. This allows the user to obtain more information about a company, product and/or service without clicking directly on the sponsored content on the social media site. Recent research has expanded our understanding of decisive responses to sponsored content by defining view-through intention as the willingness to visit a company’s website after being exposed to a sponsored content to obtain more information about the company and/or its products or service [1]. The view-through intention is often the aim of so-called awareness or brand building advertising campaigns, which aim at increasing the awareness of the product or goal-directed browsing mode behavior [1].

When a user browses in social media and sees a sponsored content, he or she evaluates two features simultaneously. On the one hand, he/she perceives who “likes” the sponsored content. On the other hand, the user perceives the sponsored content with its design, colors, and the marketing message. Thus, to explain a user’s click-through and view-through intention from the perspective of a user, we propose that “likes” of a sponsored content, as well as the sponsored content itself, should be both studied simultaneously. For instance, a user might see a sponsored content that has been among others “liked” by a close friend, yet the user might not have a clickthrough or view-through intention when assessing the overall evaluation of the sponsored content as negative. Only when the overall evaluation of the sponsored content is positive the user might have a click-through or view-through intention. Thus, through integrating the influence of

observing “likes” and the influence of the sponsored content, we can provide deep insights into how the interaction of both influence click-through and view-through intention.

To consider how the “like” of a sponsored content influences click-through and view-through intention, we can base on herd theory [4,5] to explain what conditions influence click-through and view-through intention. We can base on social media advertising research [41] to consider how conditions, relevant for sponsored content, influence click-through and view-through intention.

Relevant Conditions for Observing “Likes”

As outlined above, when a user sees a sponsored content in social media, the user can observe the behavior or predecessors, which makes it relevant to consider conditions relevant to explain observing “likes.”

Observing predecessors’ behavior: In this context, we define the herd behavior concept of observing predecessors’ behavior as the observation that others have “liked” sponsored content. Sponsored content is embedded in social media websites, such as Facebook, Instagram, and LinkedIn.

Sponsored content commonly has a “like” button, which enables users to express positive sentiment toward the content through “liking.” A “like” is bound to an individual who is willing to reveal their identity [25], because “likes” attached to sponsored content are publicly viewable by others and enrich the sponsored content with the information of who has “liked” the sponsored content. This means that a “like” is always relational [7] and a “like” is never observed apart from the predecessor who has “liked” the sponsored content. We classify a predecessor who has “liked” the sponsored content based on the strength of ties [8] to the user into three categories: strong, weak, or no tie. For instance, having a strong tie with somebody can be described by spending a lot of time together, or having a high emotional intensity, or emotional intimacy, or doing favors for another [8] . The distinction is reflected in our research model as follows and summarized in Table 1 below.

Observation of “likes” by a herd of an unknown predecessor means that a user observes that people with whom he/she shares no social tie have “liked” a certain sponsored content.

The rationale why the observation of a herd including a strongly tied, a weakly tied predecessor, or of only unknown predecessors is relevant for herding is based on the following. According to the perspective of social distance [42], a user has a small social distance to strongly or weakly tie predecessors and a far social distance to an unknown predecessor. This implies that the social distance is smaller to a herd including a strongly or weakly tied predecessor in comparison to the social distance to a herd with only unknown predecessors. Through the smaller social distance, a “like” from a strongly or weakly tied predecessor provides significantly different quality information about sponsored content as the user has a better context to understand the “like”. As information, which is unlikely for a “like” from only unknown predecessors. In other words, through the small social distance, the user can better interpret a “like” of a strongly or weakly tied predecessor based on their knowledge of these predecessors. Hence, observing a sponsored content “liked” by a herd including a strongly or weakly tied predecessor may contain different information and may differently impact a user’s behavior compared to observing a sponsored content only “liked” by unknown predecessors. This is initially supported by existing herding studies, which show that herd behavior is more likely when known predecessors are in the herd [33,34]. Furthermore, there has been significant research in various fields, such as user behavior in electronic word of mouth [10], viral advertising [43], or user’s resistance behavior [44], showing that the observation of strongly tied, weakly tied persons, or unknown persons have a different influence on individual behavior. For instance, research shows that strongly tied persons are often considered a source of information [45], improve online job search [46], and can have a strong influence on others’ behavior [44]. Furthermore, trust in online settings highly depends on the tie's strengths [47]. Recent social media research indicates that a user also relies on “likes” from weakly tied persons to process information [7], but it remains unclear how the observation of differently tied predecessors influences a user’s herd behavior in the form of click-through and view-through intention.

In summary, we suggest that the observation of a herd including strongly, weakly tied predecessors, or only unknown predecessors exerts a differential influence on a user’s herd behavior.

Predecessor’s expertise: As outlined above, observing a sponsored content “liked” by a herd including a strongly tied predecessor may exert a different kind of influence relative to a “like” by a weakly tied predecessor or by only “likes” of unknown predecessors. This is in line with the basic assumption of herd behavior: User with less accurate information tends to follow predecessors who have more accurate information or expertise [4]. This suggests that if a user can identify a strongly tied predecessor as someone who is known to have expertise about the product, this strongly tied predecessor’s “like” may become more salient. On the other side, when the strongly tied predecessor is not known for the expertise about the product, this friend’s “like” may become less salient [34]. This is supported by recent findings from herding literature [35], which shows that a user can infer whether some predecessors in the herd are experts on the research model that we additionally consider the expertise of the strongly or weakly tied predecessor concerning the advertised product.

Discounting own information: We also adapt the herd theory concept of discounting our own information to fit our research context [27]. When confronted with a situation, a user forms initial beliefs and opinions about that situation. In the context of sponsored content on social media, a user forms beliefs about the sponsored content when they are exposed to it. When a user is exposed to sponsored content that has been “liked” by a herd including a strongly tied, weakly tied, or only unknown predecessor, he/she may discount the own information about the sponsored content. This suggests that a user may disregard his/her personal beliefs about

sponsored content to a varying degree depending on the strength of ties to the person in the herd who “liked” the sponsored content.

Imitating others: We also adapt the herd theory concept of imitating others to fit our research context [27]. In our context, imitating others occurs when a user clicks on the sponsored content or intends to visit the company’s website only because others have “liked” the sponsored content. Such imitation is thus a user’s adaptation in response to observed prior behavior by others [6]; the user shares a strong tie, a weak tie, or no tie.

In summary, we see that observing the “likes” influences click-through and view-through intention.

## Relevant Conditions for Sponsored Content

When a user is browsing in social media and sees a sponsored content that has been “liked” by a herd including a strongly, weakly tied predecessor, or only unknown predecessors, the user’s forms an opinion about the sponsored content. The perception often depends on user characteristics and influences click-through and view-through intention.

Perceived value of the sponsored content: Looking at influencing factors for a user’s clickthrough and view-through intention, extant research on online advertising [48] and research on social media advertising [41] provide valuable knowledge about user’s behavior in response to sponsored content. Common sense is that a user’s click-through and view-through intentions are determined by the perceived value of the sponsored content [49]. Perceived value of the sponsored content is defined as the overall assessment of the sponsored content. Based on the advertising value model [50], social media advertising research shows that the perceived value of sponsored content influences user’s behavior, such as online purchase intention [11]. Similar

studies show that the perceived value influences click-through intention, the attention paid to the sponsored content, and user’s search for related information [51]. This suggests that the perceived value of the sponsored content influences click-through and view-through intention. Thus far, we see that the perceived value of the sponsored content may influence click-through and view-through intention. Social media advertising literature shows that perceptions and responses to sponsored content differ among users. Therefore, it is not enough to consider the perceived value of the sponsored content, but also user characteristics that influence user’s perceptions of sponsored content and a user’s response to sponsored content, such as clickthrough or view-through intention [41,48]. Social media research shows that the behavior of male social media users often differs from the behavior of female social media users [52,53] and that the behavior of a user who frequently uses social media differs from those who rarely use frequency of social media use.

Gender: Research informs us that female and male users process advertising differently [55], which also holds for sponsored content in social media [52]. For instance, a female user’s overall evaluation of sponsored content is strongly influenced by the informativeness and entertainment of the sponsored content, compared to male users [52]. Other studies show that female users react more sensitively to personalized sponsored content [56] and that female and male users differ in how sensitive they are to social influence [57,58]. Therefore, it has become a good practice to study the influence of gender in social media studies, when examining user behavior [53].

Frequency of social media use: Extent social media shows that the frequency of social media use is an important user characteristic so that users who frequently use social media differ from users who rarely use social media. Generally, research shows that individuals who frequently use social media behave differently [48,59], so that users can be classified based on their frequency of using social media and the behavior of frequent social media users differs from users who rarely use social media [60,61]. For instance, users who frequently use social media seek more distractions within social media, while users who rarely use social media tend to seek distractions outside of social media [54]. Also, social media advertising research shows that sponsored content has a higher influence on a user who frequently uses social media [11], and recent research suggests that the experience gained through frequent social media use might facilitate herd behavior [62].

## A Configurational Perspective of Relevant Conditions

In summary, we reveal conditions, that are relevant for observing the “like” as well as conditions that are relevant for sponsored content. Furthermore, the four identified conditions relevant for observing the “like” and the three conditions relevant for sponsored content are perceived simultaneously, and the interaction of those influences user’ click-through and view-through intention. In this context, interaction means that a joint influence of the condition is different from the influence of each condition separately. For instance, the influence of observing a “like” of a strongly tied predecessor might not explain click-through intention, but the interaction of observing a strongly tied predecessor and a perceived valuable content might explain clickthrough intention. The simultaneous perception of features and the interaction is supported by research [63]. Research informs us that when a user sees a sponsored content, the user will recognize different features of that sponsored content and combine those features to assess them holistically. In our context, if a user is exposed to a sponsored content and recognizes the “likes”

as one feature and recognizes the sponsored content as a second feature. In turn, the user combines those two features and sees a sponsored content with “likes”. This shows that the interaction of both, in turn, influences the user’s click-through or view-through intention. In our context, the interaction is not limited to two features, but we consider four identified conditions relevant for observing the “like”, and the three conditions relevant for sponsored content. This implies that a user’s click-through and view-through intention is influenced by the interaction of seven conditions. To reflect this, we consider configurations of the seven conditions. Within these configurations, the presence — e.g., the presence of perceived value of the sponsored — and/or the absence of conditions — e.g., the absence of perceived value of the sponsored content — can be related to a user’s click-through and view-through intention. Whether the presence or the absence of perceived value of the sponsored content is related to a user’s click-through and view-through intention depends on the interaction with the other present and/or absent conditions in configurations. To illustrate this, consider the following example configurations.

A configuration of conditions might reveal that a user has a view-through intention even if the sponsored content is not perceived as valuable but if the sponsored content is “liked” by an experienced strongly tied predecessor. However, it might be that when the same sponsored content is “liked” by an experienced, weakly tied predecessor, the user might not have a view-through intention.

Therefore, we propose that a user’s click-through and view-through intention is formed by the interaction of conditions relevant for observing the “like” and conditions relevant for the sponsored content in terms of a configuration. To reflect this in our method, we apply a configurational approach [64]. Using a configurational approach allows us to explain how

We pursue this configurational approach using a fsQCA and apply a retroductive approach [65]. Taking a retroductive approach — also known as an abductive approach — we treat theoretical concepts of herd behavior as a guiding theory explaining how user behavior is influenced by observing predecessors’ behavior. Then we collect empirical data on click-through and viewthrough intention to test our theoretical model and develop propositions that future research can develop and advance using other approaches, such as a deductive theory testing [65].

## Methodology

To explain how observing sponsored content “liked” by a herd including a strongly tied, weakly tied predecessor, or only unknown predecessors leads to click-through and view-through

intention, we conducted a scenario-based study to examine all three types of observations. In developing our scenarios, we followed recommendations for scenario-based studies and aligned our designs with similar scenario-based studies [66]. We also pretested scenarios to confirm their content validity.

## The Procedure of the Scenario-Based Study

We conducted a between-subject scenario-based study consisting of three different scenarios consistent with three types of observations (observation of a herd including a strongly tied, weakly tied, and unknown predecessor, see Table 1). In each scenario, participants were exposed to a sponsored content on Facebook that was “liked” by 100 people. Participants in the first scenario also saw a “like” from a strongly tied predecessor in addition to 100 “likes”. Participants in the second scenario additionally saw a “like” from a weakly tied predecessor. The study was undertaken in two stages. In the first stage, we asked participants to provide demographic information, and participants in the first scenario were asked to name a close friend who has a Facebook account; while participants in the second scenario were asked to name acquaintances who have a Facebook account. These names were used in the second stage to manipulate “likes”.

In the second stage, we presented the written scenario, which asked participants to imagine that they were on Facebook and saw a sponsored content. After participants read the scenario, a mock-up of sponsored content on Facebook was presented (see Appendix Figure 4 for example mock-ups). Participants assigned to the first scenario saw a sponsored content that had been “liked” by others including the person they had named as a close friend in the first stage. Participants assigned to the second scenario saw a sponsored content that had been “liked” by others including the person they had named as an acquaintance in the first stage. Finally, participants assigned to the third scenario saw a sponsored content that had been “liked” by people they do not know. We used sponsored content about a product of the same imaginary brand in all three scenarios to ensure that attitude toward a brand did not influence our results. After the exposure to the sponsored content, participants were asked to fill out the remaining of the survey. To check whether manipulation with tie strengths was successful, we additionally measured strengths of ties with two items, which are commonly used in IS research [67]. To avoid demand effects, each participant only participated in one scenario, we disguised our treatment variable and our outcome, and participants were not aware of the purpose of the study.

## Data Collection

We used Amazon Mechanical Turk (mTurk) to recruit participants for the scenario-based study. Using mTurk for online advertising topics is a common sampling method [1] because mTurk provides a heterogeneous participant base that is well suited to test online phenomena, such as social media advertising. Furthermore, using mTurk for data collection leads to the same results as with traditional data collection approaches [68]. Our sampling strategy was to limit participation to active Facebook users, which we ensured through using mTurk qualification criteria and multiple screening questions. Overall, 273 mTurk workers clicked on the survey and 55 failed the screening questions, which — in addition to the qualification criteria — ensures that we only have active Facebook users in the sample.

From the remaining 218, we additionally removed 26 participants who failed attention-trap questions, which we used to ensure survey quality. An additional 21 participants were removed because they did not answer the complete survey.

## Measures and Measurement Model

For the survey, we used established measures from existing research (see Appendix Table 4). We adapted three items measuring behavioral intention to use a system [71] to measure click-through intention; for this, we changed the context from intending to use a system to intending to click on a sponsored content. We used two items to measure view-through intention [1]. Based on herd literature [27], we adapted three items for imitating others and three items for discounting own information. We adapted three items to measure the perceived value of the sponsored content [50]. All items were measured on a seven-point Likert scale. To measure the predecessor’s expertise, we used two items from previous research [72]. We measured frequent social media use as the average hours on Facebook per day and we asked for the sex of the participants (see Table 2).

Indicator validity is demonstrated as the loading of each item be at least 0.707, which means that each item explains at least 50 percent of the variance of the latent variable [73]. As displayed in

Appendix Table 5, construct reliability is ensured as the average variance extracted (AVE), which is higher than 0.50 and composite reliability, which is higher than 0.70 [74]. We tested discriminant validity by calculating whether the square root of the AVE (see Appendix Table 5) was greater than the corresponding construct correlations [74,75]. We additionally tested the heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) ratio and used the absolute HTMT<sub>0.85</sub> criterion [76]. The highest HTMT value is 0.80, which is lower than the maximum threshold of 0.85. Given these results, we conclude discriminant validity.

## Data Analysis using QCA

To reveal whether observing a “like” from strongly tied, weakly tied, or unknown predecessors leads to herd behavior, expressed as click-through or view-through intention, we conduct a fsQCA [64], which has successfully been used on individual level data [77,78]. This analysis is based on set theory and allows us to understand, which sufficient configurations of conditions result in the desired outcome. In this study, the conditions are 1) discounting own information and 2) imitating others. Additionally, the three scenarios, “liked” by a herd including a strongly tied, a weakly tied, or an unknown predecessor, are represented by three conditions: 3) observing of a “like” by a herd including a strongly tied predecessor, 4) observing of a “like” by a herd including a weakly tied predecessor, and 5) observing of a “like” by a herd of only unknown predecessors. 6) The expertise of the strongly or weakly tied predecessor is the sixth condition. Additionally, we have 7) the perceived value of the sponsored content, 8) the gender of the participant, and 9) frequent social media use. The outcome of interest is click-through intention and view-through intention. Consequently, fsQCA allows us to analyze click-through and viewthrough intention resulting from different configurations of the nine conditions. In other words, fsQCA enables us to determine how the strength of the tie to the person in the herd, the expertise of the person, and the perceived value of the sponsored content influence click-through and view-through intention. Additionally, we can test whether there are differences based on gender or on the frequency of using social media and the configurational approach, which thereby overcomes simplifications of linear assumptions [79].

In summary, QCA operates on conditions, facilitating the identification of logical combinations of multiple conditions that combine into configurations leading to an outcome. A summary of the important concepts of QCA is shown in Table 3.

Calibration: To apply fsQCA, we calibrated the survey data into fuzzy sets based on an established calibration process [81]. The fuzzy set value of 0.95 was used for full membership, 0.05 was used as full nonmembership, and the fuzzy set value of 0.50 was the point of maximum ambiguity. To calibrate the Likert scale values into fuzzy sets, we used the direct calibration provided by the fsQCA 3.0 software and the three anchors: value 7 for full membership, the value 4 for point of maximum ambiguity, and value 1 for full nonmembership. In this sample, gender was binary, thus we used the value 1 for male users (reflecting the presence of the gender male) and 0 for female users. For frequent social media use, we used the $7 5 ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ quartile of the sample as the value for full membership, the $5 0 ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ quartile of the sample for point of maximum ambiguity, and the $2 5 ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ quartile as the value for full nonmembership. We calibrated the three scenarios into crisp sets, as the observations are a binary condition, e.g., either observing a herd including a strongly tied predecessor friend or not. The condition “observation includes a strongly tied predecessor” was set to the fuzzy value of 1 for participants in the scenario with the sponsored content “liked” by a herd including a strongly tied predecessor. In this case, the conditions “observation includes a weakly tied predecessor” and “observation of unknown predecessors” were set the fuzzy value of 0. The same coding procedure was followed for observing a “like” by a herd including weakly tied predecessors and unknown predecessors. Test for necessary conditions: Using a fsQCA, we tested for necessary conditions first. As described above, a necessary condition is a condition that is always part of a configuration, which leads to the desired outcome. We used the recommended consistency threshold of 0.90 to identify necessary conditions [80]. If a condition has a greater consistency value than the consistency threshold, it depicts a necessary condition. The consistency thereby indicates the degree to which observations with the same conditions share the same outcome [82]. As we have two distinct outcomes in this study, we conducted the test for the necessary conditions for each outcome. Consequently, we tested the necessary conditions for click-through intention and necessary conditions for view-through intention.

Test for sufficient configurations: We then tested for configurations of sufficient configurations. If a sufficient configuration exists, the outcome will exist as well [83]. To conduct the test for sufficient configurations, we followed a three-step approach. First, using the calibrated data, we constructed the truth table of all possible configurations of the nine

conditions. Second, in line with recommendations from the literature [83], we applied a raw consistency threshold of 0.85, which is seen as more reliable than the minimum level of 0.75 [84]. Furthermore, we applied a frequency threshold of three, which is commonly used in QCA studies [78], and a proportional reduction in inconsistency (PRI) threshold of 0.75, which is recommended in QCA literature [65,84]. This means that only configurations with consistency levels greater than the raw consistency and the PRI threshold as well as configurations exceed the frequency threshold were considered sufficient configurations (see reduced truth table in Appendix Table 6 and Table 7). Third, we applied the Quine-McCluskey algorithm to simplify the remaining configurations and to come up with a more parsimonious solution. Again, as we have two distinct outcomes, we first conducted the test for sufficient configurations resulting in click-through intention and then for sufficient configurations resulting in view-through intention.

## Results

As we are analyzing herd behavior as expressed by click-through or view-through intention, we will next present results of the necessary condition analysis and sufficient configurations analysis for click-through intention. Afterward, we will present results of the necessary condition analysis and sufficient configuration analysis for view-through intention.

## Click-Through Intention

Necessary condition analysis reveals no necessary conditions for click-through intention, as each consistency was below the recommended threshold of 0.90 [80]. The test for sufficient configurations revealed two configurations leading to click-through intention, which are illustrated in Figure 3 (C1 and C2). We use black circles to indicate the presence of a condition, while, crossed-out circles to show that a condition is absent and blank spaces refer to a “don’t care situation.” To present the gender in configurations, we use the condition “male social media user” and a black circle indicates male social media users, whereas a white crossed out circle indicates female social media users. The configurations have an overall consistency of 0.92, which is the degree to which all configurations together consistently result in click-through intention. The configuration has a coverage of 0.24, which is the share of observations explained by the solution.

The configuration C1 indicates that only observing sponsored content “liked” by a herd including a strongly tied predecessor leads to click-through intention. The strongly tied predecessor needs to be experienced with the sponsored product and the sponsored content must be perceived as valuable. Imitating others and discounting own information must be present in this configuration. This configuration is the same for male and female social media users who show frequent social media use. The second configuration C2 shows that observing sponsored click-through intention. In this configuration, imitating others is present but discounting own information reflects a “don’t care” situation, meaning that it does not matter whether users discount their own information. This configuration is specific for male users who frequently use social media.

## View-Through Intention

The analysis of necessary conditions did not reveal any necessary conditions. The analysis for sufficient configurations resulting in users’ view-through intention revealed three configurations, which are illustrated in Figure 3 (C3, C4, and C5). The three configurations have a solution consistency of 0.94 and a solution coverage of 0.52.

The first view-through configuration (C3 in Figure 3) shows that observing sponsored content “liked” by a herd including experienced strongly tied predecessor leads to view-through intention. Imitating others is present while discounting own information is a “don’t care” situation, as discounting own information can either be present or absent. In this configuration, the perceived value of the sponsored content is present. This configuration is specific for male users who frequently use social media.

The second view-through configuration (C4) reveals that observing sponsored content “liked” by configuration, imitating others and discounting own information is present and it leads to viewthrough no matter if the value of the sponsored content is present or absent. This configuration is specific for female users who show frequent social media use.

The third view-through configuration (C5) shows that observing an experienced, weakly tied acquaintance also leads to view-through intention, when the perceived value of the sponsored content is present. In this configuration, discounting own information and imitating others are present. This configuration holds for male and female social media users, irrespective of whether they use social media frequently.

## Applicability Check

To evaluate the relevance of our research in practice, we follow recommendations [86] and conducted an applicability check on our results. An applicability check is a recommended additional and independent step in the research process to evaluate the relevance of research results for practitioners [86]. For our applicability check, we collected group feedback with the founder and CEO of a software company specializing in information systems in the advertising industry, the chief product manager of a large e-commerce company and the chief marketing officer of a fashion company. All participants have in-depth practical knowledge of the topic. At the beginning of the group feedback, we discussed the importance of the topic of our research study. All practitioners agreed that the research addresses a timely issue because the advertising intention is important for a broad range of organizations. They reported that a growing number of especially electronic commerce companies advertise heavily on social media and that advertising spending for sponsored content on social media is rising, reflecting companies’ efforts to generate traffic on their websites, and e-commerce shops through sponsored content. Furthermore, the rising trend of social commerce websites and usage of social features, such as “like-buttons” in traditional electronic commerce websites, shows the need to understand how to generate more user engagement. Our discussion of the practical applicability of our results revealed two main perspectives. First, participants see a benefit of results in terms of targeting optimization for sponsored content. The chief product manager stated that the current practice is to target users by demographic data or by their browsing history. Our results indicate that targeting could be further optimized by relational data considering who has “liked” the sponsored content before. Hence, our results point to ways to improve the targeting algorithm for programmatic bidding strategies in social media. Second, participants suggested that these findings could help to increase the performance of low-budget sponsored content campaigns. Normally, for sponsored content campaigns to perform well, a large budget is needed to reach a large audience and generate a high number of “likes.” However, our results indicate that even a smaller number of “likes” including a “like” of a strongly or weakly tied predecessor can increase the performance of a sponsored content campaign.

In summary, the applicability check confirms the relevance of our research topic and suggests that practitioners are particularly interested in using the results to optimize the targeting algorithm for programmatic bidding strategies in social media.

## Discussion

Companies use sponsored content on social media platforms, such as Facebook, to generate traffic on their websites. However, only a small percentage of sponsored content shown to users leads to click-through or view-through intention [3]. Therefore, companies need to understand how observing “liked” sponsored content influences the user’s click-through and view-through intention, to increase their website traffic. Likewise, this understanding is important for social media platforms to provide a more efficient targeting for their customers to be more attractive than other providers.

In this paper, we draw on herd theory [4,5] to explain click-through and view-through intention. We adapt herd theory to our specific research context by considering conditions, relevant to explaining the influence of observing the “likes”, and conditions relevant for sponsored content, to explain how observing sponsored content “liked” by a herd including a predecessor, with whom a user shares a strong, a weak or no tie, influences the user’s intentions to click on sponsored content or to visit the company’s website later on.

## Propositions

We identify two configurations (C1 and C2) to generate direct clicks on the sponsored content and three configurations (C3, C4, and C5) to generate traffic on the website at a later time. Based on those, we next develop and derive propositions.

Users, who frequently use social media, have a click-through or view-through if they see a sponsored content, which is perceived as valuable, with “likes” from a herd including a strongly tied and at the same time experienced predecessor (see Figure 2 C1, C2, and C3). Male as well as female users have this intention when they imitate others and discounting their own information about the sponsored content (C1). Additionally, we see a specific behavior for male social media users (C2 and C3). For male social media users, herd behavior can also arise only because of imitating others, meaning that the user only follows the predecessors by imitating. In other words, when a male user observes an experienced and strongly tied predecessor our results show that only imitating others is required for herd behavior.

The result that observing strongly tied predecessors and experienced predecessor leads to clickthrough and view-through intention is in line with the understanding that a “like” of a strongly tied predecessor in the herd sends more information, and having a different kind of influence than a “like” of a weakly tied predecessor or only unknown predecessors. This means that a user tends to identify better with those socially close and similar to them [87]. Therefore, the “like” of a strongly tied predecessor can be more informative and provide better guidance than a “like” of a weakly tied or unknown predecessor [33]. For example, when seeing a sponsored content “liked” by a strongly tied predecessor, the user can assess that it is more likely to fit his/her own preference than a sponsored content liked by weakly tied predecessors or unknown predecessors. Users can assume that the “like” of the strongly tied predecessor is driven by similar preference to their own. Thus, the higher certainty, that the preference of a strongly tied predecessor matches one’s own preference, makes it easier for the user to infer that clicking on the sponsored content or visiting the website is a good idea. Male users intend to click or to visit the website even if they do not discount their own information. Based on this, we propose the following:

Proposition 1: Users, who frequently use social media, have a click-through intention when observing sponsored content, which is perceived as valuable and has been “liked” by a herd including an experienced and strongly tied predecessor, only when they do it because they imitate the predecessors’ behavior and because users discount their own information about the sponsored content. This is irrespective of the gender of the user.

Proposition 2: Users, who frequently use social media have a click-through or viewthrough intention when observing sponsored content, which is perceived as valuable and has been “liked” by a herd including an experienced and strongly tied predecessor, only when they do it because they imitate the predecessors’ behavior. This is irrespective of whether the users discount their own beliefs about the sponsored content but specific for male users.

Also, when a female user, who frequently uses social media, discount her own information, thus believing that the strongly tied predecessor is better-informed; the user has a view-through intention irrespective of whether the sponsored content was assessed as valuable (C4). This means that when imitating others and discounting their own information are present, a female user will respond with a view-through intention even if they do not see a huge value in the sponsored content. This shows that the influence of observing a “like” of a strongly tied predecessor can be so strong that the perceived value of the sponsored content does not matter. Based on our empirical results, we propose the following:

Proposition 3: Users, who frequently use social media, have a view-through intention when observing a sponsored content, which has been “liked” by a herd including an experienced strongly tied predecessor, only when they do it because they imitate the predecessors’ behavior and because the users discount their own information about the sponsored content. This is irrespective of whether the sponsored content is perceived as valuable but specific for female users.

Our findings differ for observing a herd including an experienced but weakly tied predecessor. We found that when observing sponsored content, which is perceived as valuable, with “likes” from a herd including an experienced and weakly tied predecessor, a user has a view-through behavior only if the user discounts his/her own information and imitating others (C5). Thus, we see that observing weakly tied predecessors exerts less influence on user's behavior because a user only has a view-through intention if they — in contrast to strongly tied predecessors — discount their own information. The perspective that observing weakly tied predecessors exert has a further social distance to a sponsored content “liked” by a herd including a weakly tied predecessor than a strongly tied predecessor [89]. A further social distance is accompanied by a higher degree of mental abstraction of an object [42]. The social distance unconsciously influences a user’s judgment and forms their perception and behavior of a sponsored content. A further social distance can thus be associated with feeling unfamiliar with the sponsored content [89] and research has shown that unfamiliarity has an adverse influence on a user’s engagement with sponsored content [90]. The configuration shows this holds for male and female users irrespective of how frequently they use social media. In summary, a user gets less context

information when observing a “like” from a weakly tied predecessor and the further social distance exerts less influence on the users, therefore we propose the following.

Proposition 4: Users have a view-through intention when observing a sponsored content, which is perceived as valuable and has been “liked” by a herd including an experienced and weakly tied predecessor, only when they do it because they imitate the predecessors’ behavior and because the users discount their own information about the sponsored content. This is irrespective of the gender and the frequency of using social media.

## Theoretical Contributions

With the study, we can explain how — in terms of the specific “like” attached to the sponsored content — and why — in terms of the underlying mechanism of herd behavior and the strength of ties — a user clicks on sponsored content or visits the website later on. Additionally, we provide insights into how gender and frequency of social media use influence click-through and view-through intention.

When a user sees a sponsored content, “liked” by others, extant herd research [4–6] informs us that observing the behavior of others influences a user’s behavior. The social media advertising research [11] additionally shows that conditions relevant for sponsored content also influences a user’s behavior. Thus far, both research streams have only been considered separately and the interaction has been neglected. Our results imply that a user may not blindly follow the herd when he observes the behavior of others, but that the user needs to perceive the sponsored ad as valuable so that a user is influenced by the observation of others “likes”.

Extant herd literature provides information about the behavior of a user observing the prior behavior of people they do not know [27–31] or the prior behavior of a friend [33,34]. In that line, we expand herd theory by the strength of weak ties theory [8] to consider the influence of the strength of social ties on observing predecessors’ behavior, discounting own information, and imitating others. Differentiating friends into strongly and weakly tied persons enabled us to uncover how herd behavior arises. More precisely, we show that only observing strongly tied predecessors lead to click-through intention, which has not been possible when just considering unknown persons and friends. Therefore, considering the strength of ties for predecessors in a herd provides a more nuanced analysis into how the behavior of different friends influences users’ behavior. This means for herd theory that it not only matters that a user observes many predecessors’ behavior, but that it is important whether those predecessors include a strongly or weakly tied predecessor. Additionally, extant herd literature [27–31] has mostly considered that a user observes behavior and copies the exact same behavior. Here, we show that observing a “like” goes beyond influencing that the user also “likes” the sponsored content, but the observing also influences a user’s click-through and view-through intention.

We furthermore contribute by also respecting how the role of experts influences herd behavior. For herd behavior, it is not enough to observe a herd including a strongly or weakly tied predecessor, but this predecessor needs to be considered knowledgeable about the sponsored product or service. The solution shows that there is no situation in which a user has a clickthrough or view-through intention when the observed strongly or weakly tied predecessor is inexperienced. This extends herd theory by showing that a user does not follow any strongly or weakly tied predecessors, but they follow the experienced ones. With this, we shed new light on how experienced predecessors influence herd behavior [35]. Considering the predecessor’s expertise provides a better understanding of the specific role of a weakly or strongly tied

predecessor. In summary, this means that the expertise of the predecessor is an important influence factor.

We extend the literature of sponsored content and “likes” in social media. Previous studies have not considered how “likes” on sponsored content influence user’s behavior. Therefore, we extend the knowledge that not only the number of “likes” is important but that users’ click-through and view-through intention is influenced by persons who have “liked” the sponsored content. The results furthermore show that the user’s frequency influences click-through and view-through intention.

We can get a fine-granular look at the underlying aspects of herd behavior and explain why herd behavior arises. The current understanding of underlying aspects of herd behavior says that when observing others’ behavior, herd behavior comprises two aspects, namely discounting one’s own information and imitating others. Thus, the current understanding assumes that both aspects need to be present for herd behavior to arise, and this has so far only been tested for situations where only a herd of unknown predecessors is observed [27]. We contribute to the understanding of the underlying aspects of herd behavior by showing that both aspects (discounting one’s own information and imitating others) do not need to exist for a male user, who frequently uses social media, and perceives the sponsored content as valuable. Thus, when a male user observes a herd including an experienced strongly tied predecessor, herd behavior occurs irrespective of whether the user assumes the predecessors are better informed. This suggests that the influence of observing a strongly tied predecessor in a herd is gender-specific and for a male user, who frequently uses social media, strong enough that herd behavior arises even if a user does not discount their own information.

In summary, we contribute by developing and testing a configurational model of herd behavior for the specific context of sponsored content in social media. More precisely, we show that herd behavior becomes apparent through click-through and view-through intention. Thus, we show that herding in social media is not limited to imitating the same behavior (e.g., “liking”). Instead, observing predecessors’ “likes” explains why users intend to click on sponsored content or intend to visit a company’s website after observing that people they know “liked” the sponsored content, even if in some cases they do not even see the sponsored content as valuable.

## Practical Implications

Social media platforms such as Facebook, Instagram, or LinkedIn publish sponsored content of companies to increase traffic on companies’ websites. As confirmed by practitioners interviewed for the applicability check, this research makes valuable practical contributions in terms of increasing click-through and view-through intention and, ultimately, generating more website traffic through sponsored content. The findings contribute knowledge to designing targeting algorithms to provide tailored ads to users [91]. More precisely, our findings have design implications for ad tech undertaking both performance campaigns, in terms of increasing clickthrough intention, and awareness-based campaigns, in terms of increasing view-through intention. To improve performance campaigns, sponsored content with “likes” from close friends should be displayed. Likewise, to improve awareness campaigns sponsored content from acquaintances will be useful as well. However, the practical implications are not limited to social media platforms such as Facebook but can be applied to platforms with social networking features, algorithms for ranking content, or recommendation engines. For instance, most social commerce websites can make use of an existing social network structure of their customers to inform customers about products. This might be particularly useful for products that are difficult to evaluate in advance (e.g., restaurants, travel, or services). As stated by the chief marketing officer “promoting services via advertising is always a hurdle, because consumers cannot evaluate the quality of services the same way they evaluate physical products. For promoting services, you need to bring trust to overcome this uncertainty for the consumer, and seeing your buddy having ‘liked’ the service can bring this trust.” Thus the “likes” of friends can be used to overcome uncertainties in the sponsored product or service, and thereby optimize click-through and view-through of sponsored content and create more user engagement on those websites. In summary, platforms with social networking features can leverage our findings to generate more revenue for themselves and more website traffic for companies sponsoring content on their platforms by optimizing their ad tech design and targeting algorithms. For their part, companies sponsoring content on social media should design their sponsored content specifically to motivate users to “like” the sponsored content, which can trigger herd behavior and improve the performance of the campaign. These contributions are thus clearly in line with recent suggestions for IS research to examine topics related to the design, use of information systems and inform other disciplines.

## Limitations and Future Research

This research has some boundary conditions and limitations. The resulting model adapts the definition of herd behavior and its context-specific antecedents to fit this research context, so our findings are only relevant for this research context and may not apply to other contexts. Furthermore, our study focuses on the influence of the presumed “liking” behavior of users who were named and are known by survey participants. This perspective is typical for understanding how herd behavior arises [26] and should be confirmed and extended by future research. As prior research [92] highlights the importance of distinguishing among product types, such as hedonic and utilitarian products or search and experience products, further research needs to take this into account. Studies indicate that a user confronted with a sponsored content is in an information state, i.e., the user is not focused on a purchase decision, but rather on searching for information [66]. This paper focuses on click-through and view-through intention, which future research could extend to understand how and why herd behavior results in conversions, such as purchase [93] or impulse buying [94]. Our results indicate that if an experienced close friend in a herd triggers the herd behavior, discounting their own information about the sponsored content does not matter. In contrast, if the herd behavior is trigger by a distant friend in a herd, the users need to discount their own information. This suggests that herd behavior triggered by close friends in a herd is similar to irrational herd behavior and herd behavior triggered by distant friends in a herd is similar to rational herd behavior [95]. The parallels between rational and irrational herd behavior and discounting own information should be investigated more explicitly by future research.

## Conclusion

This paper builds on herd theory to explain how observing “likes” attached to sponsored content influences users’ intention to click on the sponsored content or to visit the company’s website later. With that, we offer specific takeaways. First, there are different ways of how observing “likes” on sponsored content leads to view-through or click-through intention. However, one specific configuration leads to click-through and view-through intention. Second, click-through intention only occurs when observing a “like” from a strongly tied predecessor. Third, viewthrough intention requires that a strongly or weakly tied predecessor has “liked” the sponsored content. Therefore, no view-through intention occurs when it is only “liked” by unknown predecessors. Fourth, when observing a “like” from a strongly tied predecessor, a male user does not need to discount his own information. Fifth, when a female user observes a “like” from a strongly tied predecessor and additionally discounts her own information, then the user even has a view-through intention for sponsored content they do not see as valuable. These results have practical implications for social media providers in terms of generating more clicks on spon content, in terms of improving targeting algorithms, ad tech design and ultimately, social media marketing success.
