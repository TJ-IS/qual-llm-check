# Social relationship and its role in knowledge management systems usage

Wei He <sup>a,</sup>\*, Qian Qiao <sup>b</sup>, Kwok-Kee Wei <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Management and Marketing, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Kowloon, Hong Kong, PR China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 30 March 2005 Received in revised form 7 September 2007 Accepted 16 November 2007 Available online 7 February 2009

Keywords: Knowledge management (KM) Knowledge management systems (KMS) Usage Social relationship

## A B S T R A C T

The KMS has been widely implemented in organizations. However, its availability does not guarantee that employees have been willing to spend time and effort using it. We explored the use of KMS with emphasis on social relationship. Specifically, social capital theory was employed to establish the social relationship construct and its three dimensions: tie strength, shared norms, and trust. By studying a company that had implemented a KMS, we explored the dimensions of social relationship and its importance in the use of a KMS by employees. A theoretical framework was used to depict the antecedents of employee’s usage behavior. Implications for both researchers and practitioners are discussed, especially for companies expecting to exploit knowledge sharing in the Chinese business environment.

## 1. Introduction

Knowledge management (KM) is considered a strategic and value-added endeavor for improving an organization’s effectiveness in the changing social and business environment [9,23]. IT and IS are frequently used to aid in knowledge creation, storage, sharing and application, and communications to help in enhancing organizational competitiveness in knowledge-intensive work [11]. Knowledge management systems (KMS) are a way of managing organizational knowledge that is widely implemented by organizations. IS literature has extensively investigated issues relating to design, development, and management of KMS. Yet, getting employees to use the KMS effectively to improve organizational performance is still a central question for both researchers and practitioners [15].

The KMS is characterized by a duality of critical relationship: user-to-IT, and user-to-user. Specifically, the KMS needs considerable social interaction involving knowledge sharing when people use a KMS effectively. Although various social factors such as relational capital [24], reciprocal relationships [4] and social ties [18] have been proposed in discussing the importance of interpersonal relationship on knowledge sharing, there is lack of a way aggregate these factors and grasp the mechanism by which the social interactions affect knowledge contribution and seeking behavior. Using Social Capital Theory we considered the social relationship as the heart of social capital and explored social relationship as an important construct in understanding why employees would continue to use a KMS. Therefore, our objective was to develop and validate the concept of social relationship and investigate the role of social relationship in the KMS usage context.

## 2. Definitions of key concepts

In the KM field, much emphasis has been given to understanding tacit knowledge. It is difficult to codify and communicate, and some researchers believe that a computer-based IS has no way to store, display, and transfer it and that this distinguishes KM from IS [25,26]. Alavi and Leidner suggested that KMS may not appear radically different from other forms of IS, but that they can help users to assign meaning to information and capture knowledge, improve its sharing and dissemination, transfer internal best practices, and facilitate interpersonal interaction and collaboration by creating a corporate knowledge directory (or expert map) and knowledge networks. Some literature also has provided evidence that the use of KMS does improve knowledge creation, knowledge transfer, and reuse [16,19]. We adopted Gray and Meister’s definition of knowledge as a mix of expertise, experience, process, conceptual information and insights that provide(s) a framework for decision-making or problem-solving. Also, drawing from [1], we considered a KMS as the IS designed specifically to support and enhance the organizational processes of knowledge creation, storage/ retrieval, transfer, and application. KMS usage, was defined as an employee’s intentional actions of using KMS for knowledge sharing in long-term practice, which could include creating a knowledge document, transferring knowledge to others, requesting knowledge from others, and two-way constructive discussion and communication through the KMS.

## 3. Review of IS usage literature

IS researchers have employed intention-based models to examine how individuals decide to use a particular system. Drawing attention to the difference between IS adoption and sustained usage, Bhattacherjee [2] proposed that the intention to continue using an IS was strongly predicted by the user’s satisfaction, with perceived usefulness as a secondary predictor. However, as ‘‘satisfaction is a transient, experience-specific affect, while attitude is a relatively more enduring affect transcending all prior experiences’’, researchers turn to attitude as a predictor of the user’s intention of continued usage [3]. According to Bhattacherjee and Premkumar’s two-stage model, usefulness (user’s belief regarding IT usage) and attitude (personal affect toward IT usage) determine usage or non-usage, whereas perceived usefulness determines the user’s attitude.

Although these studies can explain a large proportion of variance in IS continued usage, researchers believe that the predictive ability can be improved by including social factors that are context-specific [12]. Information sharing is affected by both rational self-interest and the social and organizational context [5,8]. Therefore, it is wise to view KMS primarily as a social system based on supportive IT. In such a special system for knowledge sharing and collaboration, relationship has been seen as one of the critical issues.

## 4. The role of social relationship: a social capital perspective

Our research was based on social capital theory and focused on a key concept: social relationship. Putman [20] defined social capital as ‘‘features of social organization, such as trust, norms, and networks that can improve the efficiency of society by facilitating coordinated actions.’’ Structural, cognitive, and relational factors are considered as dimensions of social capital [17] embedded within a social relationship; they facilitate knowledge creation and improve knowledge sharing.

## 4.1. Structural capital: tie strength

A fundamental proposition of social capital theory is that networking ties provide access to resources, and thus that interpersonal connections can influence both access to people for knowledge exchange and their anticipation of value in such an exchange [17]. Tie strength characterizes the closeness and interaction frequency of a relationship between two parties [13] (people who contribute knowledge – contributors – and people who seek IT – seekers). Levin and Cross found that strong, trusting ties usually helped improve knowledge transfer within an organization. Strong ties meant that people were more accessible and willing to be helpful in knowledge sharing.

## 4.2. Cognitive capital: shared norms

Employees are members of communities, such as working groups, departments, and organizations. All of these have norms that reflect the commonalities among their members and allow them to coordinate their actions. More specifically, shared norms govern how its members behave, think, make judgments, and even how they perceive the world. Therefore, shared norms generate propositional attitudes that tend to affect the members’ behaviors. Shared language and codes influence the conditions for knowledge exchange, as noted in prior studies [e.g., 10].

## 4.3. Relational capital: trust

Successful cooperation depends on trust. It makes organizational knowledge transfer less costly by reducing conflicts, increasing participant’s willingness to build on other’s perspectives, ideas, and expertise, and therefore facilitates knowledge sharing. McEvily, Peronne, and Zaheer argued that the level of trust influenced the extent of knowledge disclosure, screening, and sharing between the parties. A person’s trust in the other members of a community has been found to be a significant predictor of his/ her intention to exchange knowledge [21]. In an expert-sharing network, Tiwana and Bush found that a user’s trusting relationship with other users provided privileged access to new knowledge: a strong, positive predictor of intention to use.

There have also been some studies showing that a lack of relationship between people is a major barrier to knowledge transfer [22]. Therefore we proposed that social relationship can explain the dominant impact of social capital on successful knowledge sharing.

As an additional aspect, we looked for reasons culled from change management theory, since employees will experience ongoing behavioral changes after adoption of new methods. Researchers in the field of change management have discovered and analyzed the influencing factors that lead to an individual’s behavioral change and have attempted to find how organizations can design interventions to make employees more motivated and prepared for change [6]. When an individual understands the change that is about to occur, he or she believes in the value of the change and intends to change because of the perceived need— employee readiness; the change is then more likely to succeed [14]. Furthermore, a positive social relationship has a significant effect on individual readiness for continuous change.

Therefore, we proposed that social relationship was an emergent factor that contributed to KMS usage in organizations. Due to the difficulty in measuring the true value of social relationship, perceived social relationship was used as pivotal in shaping user’s attitude. We defined perceived social relationship as an individual’s perceptions towards other KMS users (supervisors, subordinates, and peers) with whom the person has social interaction. We proposed that social relationship was formed by three dimensions: tie strength, shared norms, and trust.

## 5. Methodology

## 5.1. Research design

A case study method is valuable when research issues are at their early stage of formulation and the behaviors under examination cannot be manipulated [27]. In our study, we carried out an embedded single case study in the positivist tradition to achieve an in-depth, qualitative understanding of the object of investigation: to investigate why and how social relationships affect employees’ use, or non-use, of KMS in their organizations, and thus its usefulness.

Extra empirical data was collected in order to triangulate the evidence we gained through the interviews: after the face-to-face interviews had been conducted, all KMS users (including those not interviewed) were invited to participate in an internal survey.

The concepts involved in the interview protocol and survey questionnaire were all identified from the literature. The constructs of usefulness, attitude, and system usage have been well defined and tested in previous IS studies, as shown in Table 1, which also shows the newly added constructs.

## 5.2. Company background

Beta Company (a pseudonym) is a retail chemical company located in a large city in China. It has around 1000 employees, most of whom are sales representatives working in branches in different provinces. In 1999, the company initiated a KMS project in order to coordinate the business plans and activities in different sales units, facilitate collaboration and communication, and enhance the organizational competence by sharing knowledge and experiences. This company has been recognized as one of the pioneers in KM practice as well as a successful KMS implementation case in China.

Beta Company initiated a three-phase implementation plan. Phase I (2001) integrated different functional business systems and customary office automation (OA) applications to set up a knowledge portal. The company also advertised the project internally and discussed its importance and necessity of KM using cultural means during this stage.

Phase II (2002–2003) involved the implementation of core modules, such as an online knowledge repository, corporate knowledge map, and online forums to strengthen knowledge creation and collection, sharing, and transmission.

Phase III (2004) was designed to promote a vigorous knowledge network, bringing experts together virtually to deal with vital projects.

## 5.3. Data collection

Observations were made and data were collected at three times. In the first (November to December 2003), we collected a variety of company documents and system statistics, as well as secondary evidence from the public media. In the second (January to February 2004 and February 2006), the first author collected data by conducting semi-structured interviews with 11 members of the organization (1 team leader of the KMS project—the CIO, 2 team member, and 8 end users of the KMS in different functional units). Each individual was informed of the anonymity of their responses before the start of an interview.

In order to ensure construct validity, we used multiple sources of evidence and had a draft case study report, which was viewed by the key informants. In addition to direct observation and informal follow-ups via emails or conversations, the system archives and the company’s evaluation records were used to triangulate our findings. Internal validity was satisfied by adopting a ‘‘patternmatching’’ technique in the data analysis phase. Reliability was achieved by developing a case study protocol.

Finally, in the third part of the study (February to March 2006), a web-based survey was conducted to collect more data for additional empirical testing. The questionnaire mainly covered the employees’ perceptions about system usefulness, social relationship with colleagues, attitudes on KMS usage, and actual behavior. Survey questions were finalized on the basis of the operationalized constructs of Table 1. We identified three dimensions of social relationship as being particularly conducive to knowledge sharing. Backward translation was used to ensure consistency of two versions of questionnaires in different languages. Out of the 200 users we randomly selected from the KMS, 53 (26.5%) returned completed responses.

## 5.4. Data analysis and findings

Detailed data analysis revealed the significant influence of three aspects of social relationship on KMS usage. Besides perceived usefulness, which we had expected to be important, we found a consistent trend of social influence on KMS usage. Active KMS users emphasized the relationships among their colleagues by means of feelings of trust, friendship closeness, and shared norms. Extrinsic motivators from the organization also stimulated the participant’s ultimate behavior.

## 5.4.1. Usefulness of the KMS content

The perceived usefulness was highly influential in determining usage behaviors, as expected [7]. Employees regularly used the system for sharing expertise, processes, and experience. The majority of this usage was from n the R&D division, with the VIP marketing team a close second. One example involved the knowledge repository—people doing similar work in different sites could both access archives and progress reports on past projects and also directly communicate with the document producers via a knowledge map, thus locating relevant persons and exchanging ideas.

Evidence was also found that the most-often downloaded documents in the system were personalized observations, procedures, interpretations, and judgment, such as tips for solving certain problems in product development, project proposals, and documentation such as that on bidding procedures and lessons learned. The usage frequency for such documents was far higher than for marketing research reports, user manuals, etc.

Employees intended to take advantage of KMS in sharing knowledge, but this was not without its challenges. The most severe problem was that the knowledge seekers sometimes lacked the contextual knowledge needed to understand/interpret documents, especially when they were salespeople who were novices in some particular field of expertise. Then direct observation or apprenticeship was a necessity.

## 5.4.2. Trusting relationship

Frequent KMS users said they did not view other individuals in the company as strangers: they understood and accepted people with whom they exchanged information or knowledge as members of ‘‘our company,’’ and thus ‘‘real’’ and trustworthy.

According to one KMS project team member, the staff had developed good relationships through a history of interactions and common experiences, beliefs, and benefits:

The long-term, joint working experiences made them reliable friends. . . and created mutual trust, although quite a lot of them never met personally.

Due to the distance between working positions, salespeople heavily depended on others to help in accomplishing their organizational and personal goals. They needed brainstorming and collaboration with others in the telephone as well as online. This interdependence encouraged further promoted participation in KMS.

‘‘I trust [the people], so I would like to use [the system]; they are my friends and we want to share and discuss [ideas]’’ is a typical representation of active users’ attitudes.

## 5.4.3. Cooperative norms

The organizational culture of Beta Company was intended to encourage people to cooperate with each other and consider their collective interest ahead of their own self-interest. ‘‘Success stories’’ about how some work teams made a positive impact on profitability due to the team members’ good collaboration and sense of responsibility were published in the monthly corporate magazines. In addition, the company had a special award – the President’s Prize – for extraordinary group performance, given twice a year.

However, much user effort was expended in contributing knowledge to the KMS. Employees had tough business goals to meet, and the norms of cooperation and help for each other sometimes proved difficult to achieve. Our interviewees would complain about the burden of packing and disseminating knowledge even though they wanted to make knowledge contributions. This indicated that more careful attention should have been paid to the design of task allocation and time pressures on employees. Alternatively the company might need to consider adding a management position: a new knowledge-managerintermediary.

Clearly, all interviewees reported their commitment to the shared community. However, they felt varying degrees of group identification and obligation. One less active player explained that he went to the system only when he encountered difficulties, but he also agreed that the shared atmosphere and collaboration were crucial to the system maintenance and collective performance of the company.

## 5.4.4. Strong ties

The most common case profile collected in our interviews was one in which more system usage was sustained through the spreading effect from a small group of close colleagues (mostly willing to accept new ideas) to other units of the company. The interviewees admitted they would take the values or standards of close colleague groups as a frame of reference and thereby develop a positive attitude toward knowledge creation through KMS.

## 5.4.5. Rewards, training, and management facilitation

It is unrealistic to expect that all members will like a new system simply because it is adopted by the organization. Indeed, some end users gave euphemistic statements, such as ‘‘We have been told that the new KMS can solve the problems in information retrieval better with knowledge sharing, but I have not seen any difference.’’

The company performed its assessment trials based on individual contributions to the system and the quality of the knowledge documents. Many attempts were made to stimulate interest, such as contests to determine ‘‘the most valuable post in the forum,’’ ‘‘the most influential expert,’’ or ‘‘the most popular KM star,’’ along with corresponding rewards. Economic rewards proved to be beneficial. Of course, this experience of motivating employees might result from the fact that the average income for a worker in China is low compared with that in developed countries, and monetary pursuit is still a primary incentive of work for most Chinese people.<sup>1</sup>

In addition, training was included. The managers and informal ‘‘opinion leaders’’ who had shown doubts about the value of KMS use were selected to attend a four-day, offsite training sessions given by well-known institutions. They delivered KM lectures and showed the business leaders how the application of a KMS could improve communication and performance. The participants completing the course were issued a certificate. All these trainees were later found to have continued to use KMS.

Employees in branches were allocated extra resources for hardware and network configuration. Also five members of the KMS project team were available to support any colleagues having difficulties or to respond to requests and questions on KMS usage. Management commitment and support was also considered a positive factor by all interviewees as strongly determining their behavior (e.g., the CEO himself used KMS).

## 5.4.6. Further analysis

In the final research stage, further empirical data on all KMS users were collected and analyzed. Table 2 shows the descriptive statistics of the dependent constructs in the survey. The demographics of the respondents are shown in Appendix A.

Since the effect of perceived usefulness on user attitude and its effect on IS usage have been widely verified in the IS literature, this survey data were used to test whether the user’s attitude toward KMS usage would depend on the level of social relationship. An independent-samples t-test analysis was conducted. The results showed that the difference in users’ attitude between people with high social relationship and those with low social relationship was significant (t = -2.59). In addition, a bivariate correlation technique was uses to determine the strength and direction of the association between the two variables. Table 3 shows that there was a significant and fairly strong positive correlation between social relationship and user behavior.

The data collection and analysis in the internal survey attempted to identify ways in which the constructs were actually linked in the case. Our data indicated that the strong and positive social relationship acted as a predictor of KMS use by employees, both directly and mediated by their attitude.

## 6. Conclusion and implications

The purpose of our study was to adopt social capital theory to investigate employees’ usage behavior of KMS in organizations. We found that ‘‘hard elements,’’ such as IT, only play a part of the role in KM initiatives: social relationship embedded in human beings is the key factor affecting knowledge sharing behaviors in a KM initiative.

We found that the social relationship could establish positive attitudes toward knowledge sharing and therefore stimulate KMS usage. In this organization, the scattered working conditions (employees working in distant locations) forced people to use electronic media for sharing information and knowledge. Thus, they became more dependent on a trustworthy social relationship based on a set of shared values, norms, and expectations. The relationship evolved into even more communication and interaction. People exhibited a commitment to continue using the KMS.

Although earlier studies have reported various determinants of knowledge sharing or KMS usage, no one to our knowledge has brought forward a synthesized concept deriving from a coherent theoretical perspective and investigated its constitutes in the context of KMS usage. Our study contributed to theory by developing and validating tie strength, shared norms, and trust as three dimensions of embedded social relationship and preliminarily investigating the role of social relationship in KMS usage context.

It is noteworthy that Chinese generally favor informal communication and prefer to transfer knowledge through interpersonal contact rather than through formal means. Therefore, companies expecting to take advantage of knowledge acquisition and sharing in Chinese business environment should devote considerable efforts to the establishment of strong tie, shared norms, and trust.

This work, of course, has inherent limitations that affect the generalization of the results. First, longitudinal data is needed to test the constitution of social relationship. Second, given that the analysis is applied to a single firm, it is hard to justify the findings elsewhere. However, the social interactions, attitudes, and behaviors in use of KMS are typical of those in many organizations that champion KM.
