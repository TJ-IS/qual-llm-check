# AUTOMATING IN HIGH-EXPERTISE, LOW-LABELENVIRONMENTS: EVIDENCE-BASED MEDICINE BY EXPERT-AUGMENTED FEW-SHOT LEARNING<sup>1</sup>

Rong Liu College of Business, Florida International University Miami, Florida, U.S.A. {rong.liu2@fiu.edu}

Jingjing Li Information Technology and Innovation, McIntire School of Commerce University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA, U.S.A. {jl9rf@virginia.edu}

Marko Zivkovic Genesis Research Group Hoboken, NJ, U.S.A. {marko@genesisrg.com}

Ahmed Abbasi Department of IT, Analytics, and Operations, Mendoza College of Business University of Notre Dame, Notre Dame, IN, U.S.A. {aabbasi@nd.edu}

Many real-world process automation environments are rife with high-expertise and limited labeled data. We propose a computational design science artifact to automate systematic review (SR) in such an environment. SR is a manual process that collects and synthesizes data from medical literature to inform medical decisions and improve clinical practice. Existing machine learning solutions for SR automation suffer from a lack of labeled data and a misrepresentation of the high-expertise manual process. Motivated by humans’ impressive capability to learn from limited examples, we propose a principled and generalizable few-shot learning framework—FastSR—to automate the multistep, expertise-intensive SR process using minimal training data. Informed by SR experts’ annotation logic, FastSR extends the traditional few-shot learning framework by including (1) various representations to account for diverse SR knowledge, (2) attention mechanisms to reflect semantic correspondence of medical text fragments, and (3) shared representations to jointly learn interrelated tasks (i.e., sentence classification and sequence tagging). We instantiated and evaluated FastSR on three test beds: full-text articles from Wilson disease (WD) and COVID-19, as well as a public dataset (EBM-NLP) containing clinical trial abstracts on a wide range of diseases. Our experiments demonstrate that FastSR significantly outperforms several benchmarking solutions and expedites the SR project by up to 65%. We critically examine the SR outcomes and practical advantages of FastSR compared to other ML and manual SR solutions and propose a new FastSR-augmented protocol. Overall, our multifaceted evaluation quantitatively and qualitatively underscores the efficacy and applicability of FastSR in expediting SR. Our results have important implications for designing computational artifacts for automating/augmenting processes in highexpertise, low-label environments.

Keywords: Evidence-based clinical practices, systematic review, data extraction, few-shot learning, natural language processing, attention, deep learning, joint learning

## Introduction

Computational design artifacts have garnered considerable attention in recent years as a mechanism for automating/ augmenting manually intensive tasks and processes using machine learning (Abbasi et al., 2016; Padmanabhan et al., 2022; Rai, 2017). However, prior IS work has often focused on contexts involving abundant labeled training data and welldefined machine learning (ML) tasks (Kitchens et al., 2018; J. Li et al., 2020). In this study, we explore the critical and timely problem of designing computational artifacts to automate the systematic review (SR) process crucial to evidence-based medicine (EBM)—a context in which labeled training data is at a premium and routine ML models cannot fully represent the high-expertise nature of the manual tasks.

SR is an explicit, reproducible, and bias-reducing process to extract and summarize medical evidence from prior studies (Higgins et al., 2019). It can enable EBM, an important paradigm in modern medical practice that advocates the conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence for medical decisions (Djulbegovic & Guyatt, 2017; Guyatt et al., 2002). SR has proven instrumental in deriving insights from historical medical successes and failures (Clarke et al., 2007; Higgins et al., 2019) and guiding clinical decisions, such as validating treatment options and determining the best interventions (Jones et al., 2013). For instance, during the COVID-19 pandemic, a government-coordinated crowdsourced SR played a crucial role in accelerating vaccine research and development (Wang et al., 2020a). Overall, it is estimated that SR-enabled EBM could prevent up to a third of deaths and reduce healthcare spending by at least 30% (Djulbegovic & Guyatt, 2017; Pronovost, 2013).

SR is time-consuming, typically performed manually based on a comprehensive protocol, and requires independent work by multiple experts (Higgins et al., 2019; Page et al., 2021). The arduous nature of SR stems from the need to extract highly granular, disease-specific, and constantly changing PICO elements (patient, intervention, comparison, and outcome) <sup>2</sup> from articles (Higgins et al., 2019). For example, the SR for a single disease may involve extracting hundreds of granular PICO elements (See Appendix A), most of which are not applicable to other diseases (Smith et al., 2015). A case in point is that 126 distinct Outcome (O) elements from COVID-19 clinical trials are markedly different from those associated with other coronaviruses (Qiu et al., 2020). Moreover, the constant evolution of medical interventions and the emergence of new symptoms mean that PICO elements, even within a disease, frequently change. For instance, 37% of the O elements reported by Cochrane reviews in 2007 had become outdated in 2011 due to advances in clinical practices or disease progression (Smith et al., 2015). This variability complicates the creation of a standardized database or taxonomy for PICO elements, which could otherwise streamline the SR process.<sup>3</sup> Consequently, the average completion time for a manual SR project is 67.3 weeks (Borah et al., 2017), leading to many studies being outdated by the time they are published (Marshall & Wallace, 2019).

ML and natural language processing (NLP) solutions have been proposed to automate the extraction of sentences annotated with PICO elements from medical publications, often known as data extraction (Marshall & Wallace, 2019; Schmidt et al., 2025). Typically, such solutions are divided into two supervised machine learning tasks: sentence classification to detect sentences with PICO elements in articles, and sequence tagging to identify PICO-related text fragments (or fragments for simplicity) within those sentences (Nye et al., 2018; Schmidt et al., 2020). Due to the sheer number of PICO elements involved and the need for sufficient labeled samples per element (Richter & Khoshgoftaar, 2020), data labeling for ML SR can be as timeconsuming as manual SR (Spasic & Nenadic, 2020). To mitigate labeling efforts, current ML solutions typically limit the number of extracted PICO elements, focus on specific study types such as randomized clinical trials (RCT), or extract from abstracts instead of full texts (Schmidt et al., 2025). Such workarounds can significantly restrict the medical evidence that can be accessed by ML solutions (DeYoung et al., 2020). Moreover, the performance of these ML solutions is also less satisfactory, primarily because they do not adequately incorporate the comprehensive semantic, contextual, and medical knowledge that human experts use in SR (Kiritchenko et al., 2010; Marshall & Wallace, 2019; Schmidt et al., 2020).

In order to address these issues, we propose FastSR, a principled and generalizable machine learning framework. Guided by compositionality theory (Lake et al., 2015; Pelletier, 1994), FastSR incorporates design components to address task complexities stemming from the two aforementioned impediments that have inhibited IT support artifacts for SR data extraction: (1) the need to mitigate extensive manual labeling efforts, and (2) the need to account for rich human expertise in sensemaking. More specifically, FastSR employs a few-shot learning (FSL) framework that leverages compositionality theoretic insights into how humans can efficiently learn and understand language from limited examples by systematically and synergistically constructing complex meanings from simpler elements (namely, parts, subparts, and their interrelations). To represent the expertise-intensive nature of

SR, we extend FSL by adding (1) section, sentence, and fragment representations to account for diverse SR knowledge; (2) attention mechanisms to reflect semantic correspondence of PICO text fragments; and (3) shared representations to jointly learn sentence classification and sequence tagging.

We instantiated and evaluated FastSR on three test beds: fulltext articles on Wilson disease (WD) and COVID-19, and abstracts from a public dataset (EBM-NLP). We acquired the WD dataset from a company that conducted a 1,500-hour SR project to inform a Phase III clinical trial of a new medicine for Wilson disease. The COVID dataset was obtained from an open research challenge (Wang et al., 2020a), and we assembled a team of researchers with a combined 30 years of medical experience to annotate the dataset (see Appendix B). The EBM-NLP dataset, crowd-labeled by experts, included annotated abstracts from randomized clinical trials (RCTs) focused on cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and autism (Nye et al., 2018).

We compared the performance of FastSR with alternative ML solutions and observed a significant performance gain from both sentence classification and sequence tagging across all three datasets. An ablation analysis further demonstrated the utility of our proposed design components. To assess FastSR’s applicability in the real-world SR process, we collaborated with an SR company and integrated FastSR and alternative ML models into their existing manual SR workflow. By comparing the SR reports facilitated by FastSR with those from other ML solutions, we found that FastSR produced results comparable to human-generated reports, whereas other models often had critical errors and omissions due to lower performance. Additionally, we found that FastSR’s superior performance reduces the need for manual validation of ML errors, leading to significant time savings and economic benefits. We also interviewed SR experts to validate FastSR’s applicability and gained insights into integrating FastSR into the SR protocol for a seamless ML-augmented process.

Our study, which follows the computational design genre (Padmanabhan et al., 2022; Rai, 2017; Samtani et al., 2023; Abbasi et al., 2024), makes several contributions to the literature and practice. At a high level, we contribute to the emerging IS body of literature on design research for artificial intelligence (AI) contexts (Abbasi et al., 2024). Our pathway includes developing predictive ML artifacts that follow a theory-guided design abstraction, which involves rich domain adaptation to the SR context, novel and principled integration of ML/deep learning components into an end-to-end architecture, and a sociotechnical design and evaluation process encompassing expert consultations and applicability checks. More specifically, using a compositionality theoretic perspective, we identify four design requirements to satisfy task complexities pertaining to the high-expertise, low-label nature of automated SR. We used these requirements to guide the design of FastSR and demonstrated its effectiveness in three real-world SR contexts. The design proved superior in terms of data quality and accuracy, time-saving economic benefits, and qualitative indicators of usefulness when compared to fine-tuned deep learning and LLM-based FSL methods. Finally, we propose a new SR process augmented by FastSR, which can save up to 65% in time and \$73,500 per project compared to manual SR. With over 4,000 SR reports published annually (Grimshaw et al., 2013), tools like FastSR could yield substantial economic gains. Overall, given the paucity of IS computational design work on high-expertise, low-label problems, our work has important implications for future design research on human-machine process automation/augmentation in such environments.

## SR Automation: Task Complexity, Design Requirements, and Related Literature

Computational design research in IS focuses on creating ML artifacts situated in an application environment, necessitating a profound understanding of task processes and complexities (Li et al., 2020; Samtani et al., 2023). We conducted a comprehensive literature review (e.g., Guyatt et al., 2002; Higgins et al., 2019; Marshall & Wallace, 2019) on the critical steps in SR and their suitability for automation, as in Table 1. We found that the most labor-intensive step in SR is data extraction, yet it is also the least supported by extant IT artifacts. Hence, this paper focuses on automating the data extraction step using ML.

After investigating the SR automation literature and consulting with several SR experts, we discovered that the complexity of data extraction stems from the (1) massive quantity of PICO elements for labeling, (2) need for local and global context of sentences, (3) semantic correspondence of PICO text fragments, and (4) multifaceted knowledge jointly required in an iterative annotation process. Notably, task complexity (1) relates to the importance of mitigating extensive manual labeling efforts, whereas complexities (2)- (4) relate to the need to account for rich human expertise in sensemaking (Li et al., 2020). Theory-guided design is a form of abstraction that can connect gaps/impediments from the application environment to design requirements (Abbasi et al., 2024). We use compositionality theory (Lake et al., 2015; Pelletier, 1994) to guide our artifact design. This theory posits that the meaning of a complex expression is determined by its individual components and the rules for combining them. “Rich concepts can be built ‘compositionally’ from simpler primitives,” such as parts, subparts, and their relations, allowing learning from a few examples (Lake et al., 2015, p. 1333). Sensemaking is the process of searching for a representation and encoding data into that representation in

order to answer task-specific questions (Klein et al., 2006; Russell et al., 1993). SR data extraction involves sensemaking across multiple linguistic components (text fragments, sentences, and paragraphs) and understanding their interrelations—all in a fast-evolving environment (Higgins et al., 2019; Schmidt et al., 2025; Smith et al., 2015). Based on this discussion, we present four identified task complexities, design requirements, existing consideration sets, and adopted design components in FastSR in Table 2 and use the PICO examples in Table 3 to elaborate on these components.

## Task Complexity 1: Massive Amount of PICO Elements for Labeling

Task complexity: A standard SR project could extract hundreds of PICO elements, translating to hundreds of classes in a supervised ML context. Thus, the volume of labels can be daunting. Moreover, labeling is a highly specialized and demanding task, with SR experts needing to painstakingly analyze every sentence in an article, as PICO elements may be scattered throughout. Furthermore, PICO elements are diseasespecific and constantly evolving, preventing the reuse of labels across projects or over time. As such, each new SR project generally starts with labeling from the ground up. Hence, automation solutions must minimize labeling efforts, leading to our first design requirement:

## DR1: The proposed ML automation solution for data extraction should require minimal labeling effort.

Related literature: Existing supervised ML models with minimal labeling effort include large language models (LLMs), semi-supervised learning, and few-shot learning. LLMs include masked language models (MLMs) like BERT (Devlin et al., 2019), which use Transformer encoders (Vaswani et al., 2017) to predict masked tokens with bidirectional self-attention, and autoregressive language models (ALMs) like GPT (Radford et al., 2018), which use Transformer decoders for text generation with unidirectional self-attention. The former excel in extractive tasks (Chen et al., 2024a), while the latter are ideal for generative tasks (Brown et al., 2020). Both models can be pretrained or finetuned with domain-specific data to adapt to particular tasks.

For instance, BioBERT (Lee et al., 2020) and SciBERT (Beltagy et al., 2019) are pretrained on biomedical texts and can be fine-tuned for BioNLP tasks. BioGPT (Luo et al., 2022) and GatorTronGPT (Peng et al., 2023) are pretrained on medical or clinical texts and can be fine-tuned for medical content generation. Fine-tuning large models like GPT-4 (Brown et al., 2020) typically requires extensive, high-quality labeled data. However, SR tasks often have limited labels per class, making fine-tuning less effective (Schmidt et al., 2025). ALMs can also perform tasks without further training through few-shot prompting, but they are highly sensitive to prompt wording, example selection, and domain specificity, making them more challenging for precise tasks (Liu et al., 2023). Additionally, ALMs are more susceptible to data contamination than MLMs —where test data overlaps with pretraining data, inflating performance (Dong et al., 2024). This is because ALMs, with their sequential generation process, are more likely to reuse memorized content, especially in open-domain datasets. To illustrate these limitations, we benchmarked FastSR against fine-tuned BioBERT, fine-tuned and few-shot prompted GPT across both open-domain and closed-domain datasets.

Semi-supervised learning (SSL) leverages unlabeled data to enhance the performance of models trained on a smaller labeled dataset, operating on the principle that similar data points are likely to share the same labels (Oliver et al., 2018; van Engelen & Hoos, 2020; Yang et al., 2022). Among various SSL approaches, such as graph-based, wrapper-based, and deep generative methods, consistency regularization methods, which enforce the model to produce consistent predictions under small perturbations, are more practical for the SR context due to their applicability to limited labeled data and less stringent requirement for data structure. Virtual adversarial training (VAT) (Miyato et al., 2017) emerges as a strong method in this category. However, VAT may struggle when the distributions of labeled and unlabeled are misaligned, a common scenario in the SR context given the diverse distributions across PICO classes (Oliver et al., 2018). To illustrate this limitation, we included VAT as a benchmarking model.

Different from LM and SSL, FSL addresses label scarcity by making more efficient use of labeled samples without requiring a large labeled dataset to be effective. FSL is motivated by the aforementioned compositionality theory in cognitive science, which documents that humans use compositional skills, i.e., combining and contrasting a limited number of samples, to learn in principled and generalizable ways (Lake et al., 2015). FSL accomplishes label efficiency by generating multiple classification tasks comprised of support sets and queries (test instances). These support sets contain small, representative labeled samples from each class, often randomly chosen to ensure representation. In each specific task, FSL classifies queries based on a few labeled samples in each support set (Wang et al., 2020b).

FSL models can be divided into optimization-based and metricbased learning (Yu et al., 2018), each with its own models and strategies, as described in Table 4. The basic idea of optimization-based FSL is to “build an internal representation that is broadly suitable for many tasks” (Finn et al., 2017, p. 1126), allowing new tasks to be trained with just a few gradient updates. However, in the SR context, the classification tasks are highly diverse, each targeting a unique set of PICO elements. For instance, “outcome” and “participant” share less than 1% of tag words. The diversity diminishes the advantage of using a shared initialization. Metric-based models, which construct distinct class embeddings and predict instances by their distances to the embeddings, are better suited to these diverse tasks. Building on ProtoNet (Snell et al., 2017) and matching net (Vinyals et al., 2016), various solutions have been proposed to enhance support set construction (Rios & Kavuluru, 2018b; Xu et al., 2019), embedding networks (Bao et al., 2019; Rios & Kavuluru, 2018b), and prediction functions (Bao et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2019).

Moreover, we chose ProtoNet over matching net due to its computational efficiency, superior performance, and better context alignment. ProtoNet classifies a test instance based on the shortest Euclidean distance to class prototypes, which are the average representation of the support instances (Snell et al., 2017). In contrast, matching net classifies based on a weighted sum of support set labels, with weights determined by cosine similarity between the test instance and each support instance (Vinyals et al., 2016). ProtoNet is more computationally efficient, avoiding the pairwise distance calculations required by matching net (Wang et al., 2020b). ProtoNet’s prototypes provide maximum likelihood estimates of the posterior class distribution (Banerjee et al., 2005), enabling simultaneous prototype calibration and distribution estimation for improved performance (Dopierre et al., 2021; Snell et al., 2017). Prototypes can better represent our multifaceted knowledge, unlike matching net, which spreads knowledge across individual instances. We included FSL benchmarking in Table 4 for justification.

## Task Complexity 2: Need for the Local and Global Context of Sentences

Task complexity: When identifying PICO-related sentences, SR experts need to examine the local and global context of a sentence (Kiritchenko et al., 2010; Marshall & Wallace, 2019; Schmidt et al., 2020). Local context involves word-level or sentence-level meanings, such as understanding that “prevalence” in Sentence 1 from Table 3 indicates a crosssectional study design based on nearby terms like “retrospective study.” Global context concerns the sentence’s location within the article and can be obtained by examining the section heading (e.g., Method), encompassing paragraphs, or adjacent sentences (e.g., “In another study...”). Global context is instrumental in discerning sentence relevance. For example, both Sentences 2 and 5 in Table 3 describe patient groups for the Population element; however, Sentence 5 is omitted because the study is a benchmarking study in the results section. Hence, we propose:

DR2: The proposed ML automation solution for data extraction should consider both the local and global context of sentences, in that a sentence’s relevance to PICO elements is determined by its contextualized meanings and encompassing section.

Related literature: Popular classifiers that can capture the local context of a sentence include support vector machines (SVM), naïve Bayes, recurrent neural network (RNN) (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997), convolutional neural network (CNN), and Transformer-based classifiers (Schmidt et al., 2025). RNNs, which capture word-level context by processing sequences of words, often struggle with long-range dependencies (Cohan et al., 2018). In contrast, BERT’s bidirectional self-attention mechanism allows it to capture contextual relationships across the entire sentence, providing a richer representation of each word (Devlin et al., 2019). Specifically, BioBERT, which fine-tunes BERT on biomedical texts, can effectively grasp biomedical terminologies and contextual nuances (Beltagy et al., 2019). For example, in Sentence 1 from Table 3, BioBERT can link the word “prevalence” to cross-sectional study design. Thus, we prefer BioBERT over RNN for contextualized word embeddings.

The word-level embeddings can be mapped into sentence-level representations using either CNN or RNN layers. We opted for CNNs over RNNs because CNNs are particularly effective at capturing key phrases, such as “characterize the prevalence,” by applying filters that detect local patterns in the text (Kim, 2014). Various CNN architectures, including word-level (Kim, 2014) and character-level CNNs (Zhang et al., 2015), have been developed. Given our small dataset, a word-based CNN with minimal parameters is more appropriate than a character-level CNN, which requires larger datasets to learn language patterns effectively (Zhang et al., 2015). Thus, we used BioBERT with CNN layers to capture the local context of sentences while benchmarking with other alternatives, as in Table 2.

Global context-related NLP methods include documentlevel, cross-sentence, or hierarchical embedding. Documentlevel embeddings are typically generated by summarization models (Xiao & Carenini, 2019). Unsupervised graph-based approaches, which create summaries by selecting sentences with high semantic centrality without relying on large-scale labeled data, are better suited for the SR context (Zheng & Lapata, 2019). However, they often overemphasize dominant classes and may overlook the full diversity of information, a critical issue in SR contexts with class imbalance. Cross-sentence embedding (Xiao & Carenini, 2019) uses a BiLSTM to encode sections but may suffer from memory loss in encoding longer sections (Cohan et al., 2018). Hierarchical embedding (Cohan et al., 2018) mitigates this by using two BiLSTM layers: The first layer embeds individual sentences, and the second encodes the sequence of the sentence embeddings, offering a more robust context representation over longer texts.

Considering these factors, we selected hierarchical embedding as the most suitable method. However, our limited labeled data precludes directly training a hierarchical model. We employed a transfer learning approach (Wang et al., 2020b) for global context representation, comparing it with an unsupervised graph-based summarization model to ensure robustness.

## Task Complexity 3: Semantic Correspondence of Text Fragments

Task complexity: When tagging fragments in a sentence, from a compositionality theoretic perspective, SR experts often engage in semantic sensemaking to assess whether the core concepts of a text fragment relate to PICO elements (Nye et al., 2018). In the SR context, human sensemaking pertains to finding text fragments (data) that are semantically related to a PICO element (representation), via abstraction and disambiguation, to answer questions about whether a sentence contains a PICO element. In essence, the semantic correspondence of a text fragment to a PICO element is more important than exact lexical matching. For instance, in Sentence 5 from Table 3, the core concept that relates to a P (population) element is “pediatric patients,” and other fragments that entail this concept, such as “patients … under 14 years old” or “pediatric patients … under 14 years old,” should be deemed acceptable text fragments. Hence:

DR3: The proposed ML automation solution for data extraction should incorporate ML models capable of mimicking human semantic sensemaking of PICO elements, in that when identifying PICO text fragments, semantic correspondence is prioritized over lexical matching.

Related literature: Many ML solutions approach PICO fragment tagging as a sequence tagging problem, aiming to assign each word an IOB label (i.e., inside, outside, and beginning of a PICO element) in a sequence (Schmidt et al., 2025). This method focuses on precisely delineating text segments that match predefined labels, relying on exact lexical matches to identify PICO elements in sentences. Widely used models in this domain include conditional random fields (CRF) (Lafferty et al., 2001), RNN, and various configurations of Transformer-based models (He et al., 2020; Marshall & Wallace, 2019; Schmidt et al., 2025). Within the FSL framework, the ProtoNER model, derived from the prototypical network, labels each word in a sentence based on its proximity to entity prototypes (Huang et al., 2020). Additionally, the nearest neighbor (NN) model, an adaptation of matching net, determines the label of a word by its distance to each labeled token in support sets (Wiseman & Stratos, 2019).

However, in the context of SR, human sensemaking extends beyond tagging exact matches, involving abstraction and disambiguation to identify semantically relevant text fragments that convey PICO elements, even if they differ lexically. For instance, in Sentence 5 from Table 3, “pediatric patients with WD” might be tagged as a Population (P) element, while “patients with WD who were under 14 years old,” which conveys the same concept, would perform poorly under a rigid IOB tagging system. Consequently, existing models that struggle to abstract core concepts from labeled PICO fragments often result in poor tagging performance in SR contexts (Kiritchenko et al., 2010; Schmidt et al., 2025).

To address these issues, we implemented a global attention mechanism (Luong et al., 2015) to move beyond the limitations of rigid IOB tagging. This mechanism enables the model to focus on semantically relevant words, aligning them with core PICO concepts rather than relying solely on exact matches. We also introduced BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2020) as a supplementary evaluation metric to better capture semantic alignment between fragments. For instance, in the earlier example, “pediatric” could align with “patient” and “under 14 year old” with a BERTScore above 80%, which better reflects expert judgments. To demonstrate the effectiveness of our solution, we benchmarked FastSR against ProtoNER and nearest neighbor.

## Task Complexity 4: Multifaceted Knowledge Jointly Required in an Iterative Process

Task complexity: During data extraction, SR experts utilize semantic, contextual, and medical knowledge in an iterative annotation process. They typically identify a section (e.g., Methods) as a global context, search for sentences whose local contexts may suggest PICO elements (e.g., population), and interpret text fragments within these sentences using semantic correspondence. A sentence is identified as element-bearing only if it contains PICO text fragments and the correctness of the fragments can only be justified by their local and global context. By performing sentence and fragment identification simultaneously, SR experts gradually adjust the annotated text fragments to achieve the desired granularity and completeness for data extraction (Higgins et al., 2019). Therefore, it is essential to permit this synchronous and interrelated knowledge to be shared at each stage of the extraction process. Hence, we propose:

DR4: The proposed ML automation solution for data extraction should jointly identify sentences and text fragments by sharing semantic, contextual, and medical knowledge within an FSL framework.

Related literature: Existing SR ML solutions, on the other hand, divide data extraction into two tasks: identifying PICO element-bearing sentences (T1) and then extracting elements from identified sentences (T2) (Kiritchenko et al., 2010; Schmidt et al., 2020). Errors in T1 can severely compromise T2 performance. If sentences are extracted incorrectly, all subsequent text fragments will be incorrect. Likewise, if any sentences are missed, the corresponding fragments will also be absent, leading to incomplete results. This separation prevents the cross-utilization of insights and leads to error propagation across tasks (Chen et al., 2024b).

Multitask learning (MTL), also known as joint learning when tasks are equally prioritized, is a promising approach to managing the intercorrelation between SR tasks (Chen et al., 2024b). Its success hinges on the careful design of task relatedness, model architecture, and optimization strategies. In SR, sentence classification (T1) and sequence tagging (T2) are related but vary in granularity—T1 addresses broader sentence-level features, while T2 involves detailed word-level analysis. Misalignment between these tasks can lead to one task dominating or negatively influencing the other, resulting in suboptimal performance. Modular architectures (Chen et al., 2024b), where shared modules capture common features and task-specific modules address individual needs, can help reduce competition between tasks. Hierarchical architectures (Song et al., 2020), which process tasks sequentially, align with common workflows but are more prone to error propagation. Optimization strategies, including regularization techniques and dynamic task-specific loss weighting, are crucial in balancing shared learning and task-specific nuances for enhanced model performance (Li & Caragea, 2019).

We adopted a modular architecture in the FSL framework, where a shared prototype for core fragment concepts was utilized across both tasks, alongside additional prototypes tailored to the specific needs of each task. The shared module was trained on data from both tasks to promote alignment and reduce task competition. Additionally, we implemented a regularization mechanism to enforce cross-task consistency— ensuring that if a word is identified as a PICO element in $^ { \mathrm { T 2 , } }$ then the corresponding sentence will be recognized as containing a PICO element in T1. For validation, we included a hierarchical design as a benchmark.

a t t _ {t} = \frac {\exp (a _ {t} ^ {\prime})}{\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {T} \exp (a _ {k} ^ {\prime})}, \text {where} a _ {t} ^ {\prime} = (V _ {Q}) ^ {T} a _ {t}, \text {and} V _ {Q} \in \mathbb {R} ^ {D ^ {\prime}}\tag{10}
$$

$$
F A Q _ {q} = \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} a t t _ {t} w _ {t}\tag{11}
$$

## Joint Learning via Representation Integration

In a prototypical network, prediction is accomplished by comparing the distances between the representation of a query ?? and various prototypes of the support set ??. The predicted class label is determined by the class label from the nearest prototype. In FastSR, multifaceted knowledge of element extraction is captured by the proposed representations and prototypes described in Equations (1)-(11). The two tasks— sentence classification and sequence tagging—are learned jointly via the shared fragment prototypes.

First, sentence classification in FastSR represents a query sentence ?? by sentence contextualized semantics $S E M _ { q } ,$ global context $C o n _ { q }$ and fragment-attended query representations $F A Q _ { q } ,$ and measure the distance of $q$ to $P r o t o _ { s e m } ( S )$ $P r o t o _ { c o n } ( S )$ and $P r o t o _ { f r a g } ( S )$ prototypes. As shown in Figure ${ \mathrm { l e } } ,$ we computed the Euclidean distance between representations and prototypes and took the weighted sum of the distances as the overall distance between ?? and ??, denoted as $d ( q , S )$ (Equation 12). $\alpha _ { s e m } , \alpha _ { c o n } ,$ and $\alpha _ { f r a g }$ are trainable parameters determining the contributions of local and global context of sentences as well as the core concepts of fragments to the sentence classification task. In the final step, we transformed $d ( q , S )$ into a probability such that the shorter the distance, the higher the probability. This was achieved by applying the Softmax function to the negation of $d ( q , S )$ over all ?? support sets (Equation 13). The final prediction for ?? is the class with the highest probability. During testing, a batch of classification tasks with different support set groups for a query will be created, and the final prediction will be the average output of the classification task batch.

$$
\begin{array}{l} d (q, S) = \alpha_ {s e m} * \| S E M _ {q} - P r o t o _ {s e m} (S) \| + \alpha_ {c o n} * \\ \| C O N _ {q} - P r o t o _ {c o n} (S) \| + \alpha_ {f r a g} * \| F A Q _ {q} - P r o t o _ {f r a g} (S) \|, \\ \text { where } \alpha_ {s e m} + \alpha_ {c o n} + \alpha_ {f r a g} = 1 \quad (1 2) \\ p s _ {q} ^ {j} = p \left(c _ {j} | q\right) = \frac {\exp (- d (q , S ^ {j}))}{\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {J} \exp (- d (q , S ^ {k}))} \end{array} \tag {13}
$$

Second, as shown in Figure 1e, fragment tagging in FastSR identifies PICO text fragments by comparing the distance between a word embedding $w _ { t }$ in a query sentence ?? to fragment prototypes $P r o t o _ { f r a g } ( S )$ (Equation 14). Then, as shown in Equation (15), with ?? support sets of PICO element classes, we obtained ?? distances and normalized the distances to obtain the probability of this word being relevant to class ??<sub>??</sub> and then multiplied this probability by $\beta _ { t } ,$ , the emission likelihood based on the alignment between $w _ { t }$ and the fragment (see Equation 9).

$$
d (w _ {t}, S) = \parallel w _ {t} - P r o t o _ {f r a g} (S) \parallel\tag{14}
$$

$$
p w _ {t} ^ {j} = p \big (c _ {j} \big | w _ {t} \big) = \beta_ {t} \frac {\exp (- d (w _ {t} , S ^ {j}))}{\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {J} \exp (- d (w _ {t} , S ^ {k})}\tag{15}
$$

Finally, we computed the loss of this joint learning model. Let $y s _ { q } ^ { j }$ be the ground truth indicating whether ?? belongs to class ?? and $y w _ { t } ^ { j }$ denotes whether the $t ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ word in ?? is indeed a part of a PICO element of class ??. We calculated the cross-entropy losses of sentence classification $L S ( q , j )$ and fragment tagging $L T ( q , j )$ (Equations 16-17). Moreover, we added a regularization term to ensure consistent predictions from the two tasks. Intuitively, once a word in query ?? is predicted to be a part of a PICO element, ?? should be predicted to be PICO element-bearing as well. Thus, we can treat the maximum tagging probability as an imputed classification probability and compute another cross-entropy loss as the regularization term $L R ( q , j )$ (Equation 18). The total loss is the sum of $L S ( q , j )$ ， $L T ( q , j )$ , and $L R ( q , j )$ (Equation 19).

$$
L S (q, j) = y s _ {q} ^ {j} \log p s _ {q} ^ {j} + \left(1 - y s _ {q} ^ {j}\right) \log \left(1 - p s _ {q} ^ {j}\right)\tag{16}
$$

$$
L T (q, j) = \frac {1}{T} \Sigma_ {t = 1} ^ {T} y w _ {t} ^ {j} \log p w _ {t} ^ {j} + (1 - y w _ {t} ^ {j}) \log (1 - p w _ {t} ^ {j})\tag{17}
$$

$$
L R (q, j) = y s _ {q} ^ {j} \log \max _ {t \in q} p w _ {t} ^ {j} + \left(1 - y s _ {q} ^ {j}\right) \log \left(1 - \max _ {t \in q} p w _ {t} ^ {j}\right)\tag{18}
$$

$$
L o s s (q, j) = L S (q, j) + L T (q, j) + L R (q, j)\tag{19}
$$

## Evaluation Strategy and Datasets

## Evaluation Tasks

In computational design science research, it is critical to evaluate how well the proposed design framework satisfies design requirements (Padmanabhan et al., 2022; Rai, 2017; Samtani et al., 2023). For a problem context and design solution (e.g., SR automation) that lies at the intersection of data, theory, and machine learning (Maass et al., 2018), a multifaceted evaluation solution is often needed (Padmanabhan et al., 2022; Rai, 2017). This solution often involves benchmarking, ablation analysis, and downstream value (Padmanabhan et al., 2022).

According to Figure 2, our evaluation method consists of three components: (1) sentence classification, (2) sequence tagging, and (3) SR automation. For the first two, we computed the top-N precision (P@N), recall (R@N), and F1 (F1@N) per article, following the SR automation literature (Kiritchenko et al., 2010). Additionally, we use both lexical- and semantic-based metrics for sequence tagging. Lexical metrics measure the overlap between predicted and ground-truth word fragments (Schmidt et al., 2020), while semantic metrics use the cosine similarity between text fragments’ BERT word embeddings for precision, recall, and F1 score calculation (Zhang et al., 2020). We also calculated the area under the precision-recall curve (PRC), a threshold invariant metric (Davis & Goadrich, 2006) (see Appendix D) for our metric definitions. Finally, we assessed the impact of FastSR on SR automation in terms of report generation, qualitative feedback, time reduction, and SR process.

We applied a default threshold of 0.5 across models to convert predictive probabilities into binary classifications, indicating whether an instance belongs to a particular class. Raising the threshold can increase precision but lower recall. SR experts are best positioned to determine the desired recall level, balancing the retrieval of critical rare evidence with minimizing false positives (O’Mara-Eves et al., 2015). Therefore, evaluating a model solely on precision and recall at a fixed threshold can be misleading. A more balanced assessment should consider metrics like F1 or PRC.

For sentence classification and sequence tagging, we benchmarked FastSR with a number of models summarized in Tables 2 and 4 and also conducted extensive ablation analyses to estimate the impact of each design component. We used three datasets, Wilson disease (WD), COVID, and EBM-NLP, to demonstrate both the effectiveness and generalizability of FastSR. For SR automation, we used the WD datasets due to our access to the SR experts, who manually extracted PICO elements from this dataset. The details for the datasets are presented below.

## Datasets and Model Implementation

WD dataset: The WD dataset came from an SR project for Wilson disease conducted by a research team of seven at a global life sciences company (GLS). This project, which took about 1,500 hours over six months, was initiated in 2018 by a pharmaceutical company planning a Phase III trial for a new drug. The team’s task was to summarize relevant PICO elements for the trial’s design. They screened and assessed 550 articles, ultimately extracting 137 PICO elements (see Appendix A). Out of these, 27 PICO elements grouped into six classes (Table 6) were shared with us for evaluation.<sup>4</sup> Specifically, our training dataset consisted of 222 sentences,<sup>5</sup> each labeled by two SR experts from GLS. We kept no more than 100 labeled sentences in each class to challenge FastSR under labeling constraints, as other researchers have done for FSL research (Snell et al., 2017). We made a special class, NA, for irrelevant sentences. The testing dataset consisted of 110 randomly selected articles that were distinct from the aforementioned labeled data.

COVID dataset: We applied FastSR to a COVID-19 dataset to test its reusability and generalizability. First, we reused the trained WD models to extract sample size, country, and population from this COVID dataset. <sup>6</sup> Second, we trained a new model to extract a new class of outcome elements—risk factors, in order to test the generalizability of FastSR to a different literature domain. In epidemiology, a risk factor is a variable associated with an increased risk of disease or infection measured by metrics such as odds ratio (OR) and hazard ratio (HR). The dataset was labeled by three SR experts, containing a training set of 83 positive sentences from 15 articles and a test set of 62 randomly selected articles that were different from the training set.

EBM-NLP dataset. We used a public dataset to further assess the generalizability of our method. The EBM-NLP dataset contained 5,000 abstracts of RCT studies fully annotated by crowdsourcing and medical experts.<sup>7</sup> Annotations included 17 PICO elements categorized into three classes: participants, interventions, and outcomes. 200 expert-annotated abstracts were reserved for testing and benchmarking. To preserve the flavor of FSL, we applied a stratified strategy<sup>8</sup> to randomly sample 30 training subsets, each comprising 200 sentences. Then, we trained models on each subset and tested them on the designated test set. The global context module was disabled in FastSR since this dataset only consisted of abstracts.

For each dataset, we randomly split the training data into 75% for training and 25% for validation. The summary statistics for the training data are presented in Appendix Table F2. For our 2-way, k-shot FSL models, we conducted a sensitivity analysis for k and set k = 5 for the best model performance. For most of the benchmarking models, we adopted the original implementations provided by the authors. The implementation details can be found in Appendix E. During testing, all models scanned each testing article to extract the top three sentences for each PICO data element class. At least two SR experts checked whether each extracted sentence was associated with the correct PICO element class. The average kappa score between experts was 0.85 for the WD dataset and 0.87 for the COVID dataset, indicating strong agreement. Since the EBM-NLP test dataset was fully annotated with a kappa score of 0.67 (Nye et al., 2018), we were able to evaluate performance on all sentences, as reported next.

## Sentence Classification

We first present the benchmarking analyses for the WD dataset in terms of the top-three metrics and PRC <sup>9</sup> on the 110 testing articles, as shown in Table 7. We also tested the significance of differences in F1@3 and PRC <sup>10</sup> between FastSR and the benchmarking models.

As predicted in the Task Complexity 3 section, CNN emerged as the top traditional classifier, with an average F1@3 score of 0.62. LSTM, when combined with BioBERT embeddings, resulted in a lower performance. Hence, we focused on using CNN for semantic sentence representation. We further conducted VAT on the CNN model with all unlabeled sentences from the full text of the training articles. However, the model performance was barely improved. One possible reason is that the VAT assumes that the distribution of unlabeled data aligns with the labeled data (Oliver et al., 2018), but this may not hold for our diverse full-text unlabeled data.

For the LLM group, fine-tuning BioBERT with the WD dataset resulted in a marginal performance increase with an F1@3 of 64%. Likely, the limited sample size in our SR context restricted the effectiveness of such fine-tuning approaches, as highlighted by Brown et al. (2020). We extensively tested various ALM techniques, including opensource and closed-source models, along with fine-tuning and few-shot prompting (see Appendix G). GPT-4 with few-shot prompting was the best-performing model across all datasets. However, GPT-4 significantly underperformed compared to FastSR, with a 33.77% (F1) and 43.67% (PRC) gap, and fine-tuned BioBERT, with a 23.94% (F1) and 34.83% (PRC) gap. This could be due to ALMs unidirectional self-attention, which lacks the ability to capture bidirectional dependencies among words within sentences, leading to an incomplete understanding of context. Another reason might be data contamination, as we found that when switching to the open-domain EBM-NLP data, the gap between GPT-4 and FastSR was reduced by 21.65% (F1) and 23.62% (PRC). Given GPT-4’s lower sentence performance and high API cost, we did not apply GPT-4 for sequence tagging.

## Sequence Tagging Benchmarking

Building on the FastSR’s promising results in sentence retrieval, we proceeded to evaluate the performance of PICO fragment tagging. This evaluation involved sequence taggers trained in conjunction with the top-performing sentence classifiers from each method group. Specifically, the CNN classifier was teamed with a linear tagger on top of BioBERT embeddings, BiLSTM, or CRF, and ProtoNet was coupled with ProtoNER and nearest neighbors. The linear tagger was used as the base model for VAT. BioBERT was fine-tuned for both tasks. GPT-4 few-shot prompting, the best model for sentence classification, was also used for sequence tagging. However, we omitted evaluation on the WD and COVID datasets due to low sentence classification performance and high API costs. For each combination, we retrieved the top three sentences and measured tagging performance by F1, BERTScore, and PRC. If a sentence was classified incorrectly, no fragments were extracted.

## Ablation Analysis

We now turn to a thorough ablation study on our novel extensions of FSL. As shown in Figure 1e, our novel components (vs. routine FSL) included (1) global context prototypes (Module B) to enhance metric-based learning, (2) attention-based fragment prototypes (Module C) for semantic correspondence of PICO fragments, and (3) a fragmentattended query representation (Module D) to share fragment prototypes across classification and tagging.

We first assessed the impact of each component using the WD dataset. We removed one component from the full model at a time to create three new models (M1-M3). In M1, we excluded the global context prototypes from the sentence prediction by setting $\alpha _ { c o n } = 0$ in Equation (12). In M2, we ignored semantic correspondence by generating fragmentrelated representations using word embedding averaging, as used in ProtoNER (Huang et al., 2020). In M3, we disabled joint learning by removing fragment-attended query representations, i.e., $\alpha _ { f r a g } = 0$ in Equation (12) and eliminating the regularization term in Equation (19). Moreover, we simultaneously removed two components to analyze their potential interactions (M4-M6). In addition, we tested alternative designs for the components. As described in Task Complexity 4 section, we replaced the modular MTL architecture with a hierarchical one in M7, where sequence tagging was enabled only when a sentence ?? was positively classified first, i.e., $p w _ { t } ^ { j } = p \big ( c _ { j } \big | w _ { t } \big ) * p \big ( c _ { j } \big | q \big )$ in Equation (15). In M8, we replaced global context with an unsupervised graph-based summarization model (Zheng & Lapata, 2019), as discussed in Task Complexity 2 section. Finally, we evaluated FastSR with a further reduced dataset. We applied stratified sampling to select 2/3 of the labeled samples to create 10 subsets and train models on each subset.

The ablation performance for the WD dataset is presented in Table 13. Removing context prototypes led to a 3.64% decrease in F1@3, excluding attention produced a 4.30% loss, and disabling joint learning resulted in a 4.74% drop. Additional reductions occurred when two components were simultaneously removed (M5-M6). The attention mechanisms that emphasize the core concepts of PICO fragments generate significant benefits because they make query representations and support set prototypes more meaningful for metric-based learning. Joint learning, which shares this multifaceted knowledge across the classification and tagging tasks, further improves each task. The alternative MTL architecture and global context also led to a 2-4% reduction in the metrics. Finally, a one-third reduction in the training samples resulted in a 3-4% decrease in F1 and only a 1.63% decrease in PRC. This marginal decline underscores the robustness of FastSR in dealing with a more pronounced scarcity of labeled datasets.

Similarly, removing two components simultaneously (M4-M5) resulted in a further reduction in performance. The alternative MTL architecture and global context exhibited inferior performance, compared to our proposed design. Finally, a 1/3 reduction in training samples led to a slight 3- 5% decline across all metrics.

## SR Automation

Computational design science research emphasizes integrating ML models into human decision processes, i.e., creating a human-machine process (Berente et al., 2021). Thus, the goal of our qualitative evaluation is to shed light on when, how, and for whom FastSR can expedite SR. We conducted this evaluation in four aspects: report generation, qualitative feedback, and practical benefits and implications on SR guidelines.

Report generation: We demonstrated FastSR’s efficacy by comparing its SR reports for the WD dataset with those from leading benchmark methods and SR experts. Using top-one sentences from FastSR and ProtoNet and manually extracted PICO elements, we generated four comparative plots (Figure 4). Figure 4a shows the distribution of studies by WD manifestations (P element), and Figure 4b presents the distribution of interventions. These figures reveal FastSR’s closer alignment with SR expert-generated plots, unlike ProtoNet, which missed numerous articles in each category, affecting the completeness, validity, and recency of medical evidence. Figure 4c focuses on WD study sample sizes in China. SR experts found eight studies (sample sizes 1 to 691), shown in blue bubbles. FastSR retrieved seven, while ProtoNet only three, missing the pivotal 2015 study with a sample size of 691. Figure 4d examines trientine studies, an alternative treatment for WD patients intolerant of penicillamine. ProtoNet missed the highestquality evidence from a clinical trial, whereas FastSR identified diverse study types. This comparison illustrates FastSR’s proficiency in retrieving key studies and providing comprehensive, current, and high-quality evidence in specialized SR domains.

Qualitative feedback: We then conducted interviews with SR experts who have interacted with FastSR to assess FastSR’s importance, accessibility, and suitability (Lukyanenko et al., 2019; Rosemann & Vessey, 2008). We summarize part of the qualitative feedback in Appendix Table F1. Specifically, FastSR was considered important and useful, especially for PICO element extraction: “Remarkably, FastSR identified a few sentences that could be overlooked in a condensed synopsis.” They also mentioned its significant impact on evidence synthesis: “With significant time savings in PICO extraction, I can focus more on summarizing and visualizing findings from multiple articles.” In terms of accessibility, they applauded the simplicity of presenting sentences of PICO elements but suggested that a user interface would allow easier navigation across PICO elements and articles. Regarding suitability, the participants felt that FastSR is particularly suitable for time-sensitive SR tasks (e.g., COVID-19). They expressed a desire to test this tool in their upcoming SR projects. They did, however, recommend a thorough revision of the SR protocol to guide the inclusion of automation tools into SR processes.

Economic benefits due to time reduction in SR data extraction: We estimated the economic benefits of FastSRaugmented SR using the WD SR project as a case study. Table 16 outlines the specific extraction steps and time required for four methods: FastSR, the best FSL method (ProtoNet), the top traditional DL method (CNN), and manual SR. Our estimates are based on a project with 550 articles, averaging 120 sentences per article, an annotation time of one hour per article (Nussbaumer-Streit et al., 2021), and an SR expert cost of \$100 per hour. Manual SR typically requires at least two annotators for data extraction, a process referred to as double screening (Higgins et al., 2019). In contrast, for MLaugmented SR, a single screening, where a single expert validates the ML extraction results, is recommended (Shemilt et al., 2016).

In the four-step process in Table 16, data annotation and model training constituted a one-time investment of 100 hours for all three ML-augmented SR methods. The time spent on validation and resolving false predictions varied with model performance. For instance, during validation, while all models retrieved the top three sentences per PICO class, FastSR reduced the number of unique sentences retrieved (1,200 vs. 1,600 sentences) by capturing multiple PICO elements in a single sentence. Time spent on resolving false predictions depends on model performance. We assume that an SR expert would have to screen the full text to see whether the retrieved sentence is incorrect (false positive) or lacks the target PICO element (false negative). With FastSR achieving 81% P@1 and 80% R@1 across 110 test articles, around 43 articles (21 for false positives and 22 for false negatives) would necessitate full-text screening.

![](/api/attachments/PWQB5NVK/fulltext/images/e23a92a5764ddf5b7b5aaa1ed713f22c620ce3f737940256cebdd5d6204bc079.jpg)

Overall, this analysis shows that FastSR significantly reduces time and cost due to its efficient automation and superior performance compared to benchmarking models. With single screening, FastSR achieves a 65% time reduction relative to double-screening manual SR, leading to a cost savings of \$73,500 for a project with 550 articles. Even with double screening, FastSR saves about 45% of the time compared to manual SR. Compared to benchmark models, FastSR saves 20-25% time, irrespective of the screening method. This aligns with research indicating that ML solutions can cut SR project workloads by 30%-70% (O’Mara-Eves et al., 2015; Shemilt et al., 2016). Considering the annual publication of over 4,000 SR reports (Grimshaw et al., 2013), effective tools like FastSR could yield substantial economic gains.

Implications on SR guidelines: Accelerated data extraction has the potential to transform the conventional five-stage SR process (Page et al., 2021) into an iterative, flexible, and more efficient approach. As outlined in Table 1, the screening stage (screening abstracts to determine article eligibility) and the extraction stage (full-text annotation) are labor-intensive and face challenges due to insufficient support of robust IT artifacts. FastSR’s remarkable performance with both abstracts and full-text articles suggests its potential to semiautomate these two stages, as illustrated in Figure 5.

Specifically, during the planning stage, SR experts annotate a limited number of training examples. Utilizing this concise training dataset, FastSR is capable of initially screening abstracts containing PICO elements to determine article eligibility. Subsequently, FastSR can efficiently extract PICO elements from the screened full-text articles. The SR experts can then validate the extracted sentences and text fragments, addressing any false predictions.

While SR experts are essential for validating results and addressing false predictions, this semi-automated approach can significantly reduce the time required for SR because SR experts can focus on the extracted top sentences rather than painstakingly reviewing the full-text articles. Moreover, with the expedited screening and extraction stages, they can further examine research questions and refine search criteria, which are critical for the entire SR project. Finally, this semiautomatic approach also has the potential to mitigate errors and biases that may arise from automation methods. Through validation, SR experts gain insights into the prediction rationale and common errors and biases. This knowledge can be collectively shared with model developers, enabling them to fine-tune FastSR and improve the interpretability of the models.

## Discussion and Conclusion

In this section, we discuss the implications of our design artifacts, the takeaways from designing ML solutions for process automation/augmentation (Padmanabhan et al., 2022), and the generalizability of FastSR.

Computational design for high-expertise and low-label environments—A deep understanding of problem context: Many real-world process automation/augmentation environments are rife with high-expertise and limited training data (Padmanabhan et al., 2022). Following Samtani et al. (2023), we tackled this design challenge by first obtaining a thorough understanding of the application environment and then searching for a knowledge base to guide our design. Specifically, based on our review of the literature and expert interviews, we identified four types of task complexity and proposed design requirements to customize FSL to SR automation. Our design framework, artifact, prototype, and evaluation can guide future IS computational design science researchers to address design in a similar environment.

Making machines learn like humans: Human sensemaking involves synergistically integrating simple components into semantic meaning. Compositionality theory emphasizes the need to find the appropriate components and interrelations to accomplish this synergistic effect. FastSR utilizes a rich theoryguided domain adaptation (Abbasi et al., 2024) to identify design requirements that delineate the necessary components and their interrelations. We designed multiple representations and prototypes within an FSL framework to implement the design requirements, resulting in a synergistic effect where various design components contributed to the overall operational utility. Overall, our design has implications for managing AI and human-in-the-loop work by delineating how to involve human expertise in human-machine process automation/augmentation environments (Berente et al., 2021).

Customized evaluation strategy and generalizability: Evaluation is the cornerstone of design science research. We argue that artifacts geared towards complex sociotechnical environments necessitate a customized and multifaceted evaluation strategy. Accordingly, we developed a comprehensive strategy to assess FastSR’s overall operational utility, including experiments that shed light on the accuracy and quality of data extraction (in comparison with state-of-theart benchmark models), downstream case studies that highlight dashboard decision-making value and potential time savings for experts, and interview-based qualitative feedback on usefulness. Moreover, we conducted detailed ablation analyses on FastSR components to assess the efficacy of satisfying design requirements as an advantageous outcome. Furthermore, by using three datasets, we evaluated the generalizability of

FastSR across multiple disease domains, a wide spectrum of PICO data elements, different text styles (abstract vs. full text), and variations in sample size. Overall, we show that FastSR can substantially reduce SR experts’ workload and shift their cognitive load from mechanical data extraction to high-valueadded evidence synthesis. We are hopeful our evaluation strategy, which emphasizes design efficacy/validity, generalizability, and boundary conditions, may inform future AI/ML-related design research embedded in sociotechnical environments.

We also acknowledge several limitations of this study, thereby identifying future research opportunities. First, we chose to concentrate on demonstrating generalizability within the medical SR context, leaving the exploration of its application to other domains for future work. Second, FastSR assumes predefined PICO classes established by medical professionals, but future work could focus on developing methods to automatically identify new, disease-specific PICO classes. Third, PICO classes often have a hierarchical structure. For example, the Intervention class can include subcategories like surgical and educational interventions. Future work could explore prototype designs that might better reflect this hierarchy to accommodate small or rare subclasses. Fourth, while ALMs underperform in data extraction tasks compared to BERT-based models, they might be useful for downstream tasks like evidence synthesis. Future work could explore integrating both types of LLMs into different stages of the SR process. Finally, whereas our problem-driven design framework remains stable, individual ML components can be replaced as new technologies emerge. Future research could focus on integrating advanced ML components into our design.

In conclusion, we propose a customized FSL solution with several novel extensions to integrate human annotation logic into a computational framework that requires minimal labeling effort. Our comprehensive, multifaceted evaluation showcases FastSR’s advantages in data extraction and SR automation compared to baseline models. Our results have important implications for designing computational artifacts in high-expertise, low-label environments.

## Appendix C

## Pretrained Model for Sentence Global Context

We trained the model (in Figure 2B) with PubMed sections as inputs and their corresponding section titles as targets. There is a total of 13 frequently used section titles. For computation efficiency, we only took up to the first 20 sentences for each section and a maximum of 40 words for each sentence. In total, we kept 50,000 words. ## Performance Metric Calculation

Article retrieval effectiveness by top sentences. The objective of sentence classification is to retrieve relevant articles (containing at least one relevant sentence) for each PICO data element (Kiritchenko, et al., 2010). For each article, once an element-bearing sentence is found for a PICO element $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , sample size), there is no need for SR experts to continue examining other sentences for the same element. Thus, an SR automation tool only needs to recommend a few sentences $( N \leq 3 )$ to retrieve the PICO elements of a class. We adopted top-N precision $( P @ N )$ , recall (??@??), and F1 (??1@??) to evaluate the accuracy of the recommended sentences. Specifically, let $S T _ { N } ^ { j }$ be the top-N sentences by prediction probabilities $p s ^ { j } \left( p s ^ { j } \geq 0 . 5 \right.$ , the default probability threshold), and $S T _ { t r u e } ^ { j }$ be the true sentences for class ?? in an article. We calculate $\begin{array} { r } { P @ N = \frac { | S T _ { N } ^ { j } \cap S T _ { t r u e } ^ { j } | } { \operatorname* { m i n } \left( \mathrm { N } , ~ \left| S T _ { N } ^ { j } \right| \right) } , ~ R @ N = \frac { | S T _ { N } ^ { j } \cap S T _ { t r u e } ^ { j } | } { \operatorname* { m i n } \left( \mathrm { N } , ~ \left| S T _ { t r u e } ^ { j } \right| \right) } , } \end{array}$ and $\begin{array} { r } { F 1 @ N = \frac { 2 * P \ @ N * R @ N } { ( P @ N + R @ N ) } } \end{array}$ for each article.<sup>11</sup> In addition, we also calculate the area under the precision-recall curve (PRC), a threshold invariant metric. We prefer PRC over AUC because each article only has a few element bearing sentences, and AUC can be inflated for highly imbalanced datasets (Davis & Goadrich, 2006).

Sequence Tagging. Once top-N sentences are identified, we proceed to extract PICO text fragments by the probability ???? generated for each word in the sentences (see Equation 15). The widely used metric for text fragment extraction is F1 scores (Schmidt et al., 2020), which measures the word overlap between tagged fragments (??<sup>̂</sup>) in top-N sentences and corresponding ground-truth fragments $( f ) . ^ { 1 2 }$ The precision is defined as $P _ { w o r d } @ N = \frac { | f \cap \hat { f } | } { | \hat { f } | }$ , i.e., the number of overlapping words between ?? and $\hat { f }$ divided by the total number of words in ${ \hat { f } } .$ The recall is calculated as $R _ { w o r d } @ N = \frac { | f \cap \hat { f } | } { | f | }$ , and $\begin{array} { r } { F 1 _ { w o r d } @ N = \frac { 2 * P _ { w o r d } * R _ { w o r d } } { ( P _ { w o r d } + R _ { w o r d } ) } } \end{array}$ . For example, if $f = "$ “pediatric patients with $\mathrm { W D } ^ { \prime \prime }$ and ${ \hat { f } } = { } ^ {  } \operatorname { I }$ Patients with WD who were under 14 years old”, $\begin{array} { r } { P _ { w o r d } @ 1 = \frac { 3 } { 9 } , R _ { w o r d } @ 1 = \frac { 3 } { 4 } , F 1 _ { w o r d } @ 1 = \frac { 6 } { 1 3 } . } \end{array}$ Although ?? and $\hat { f }$ have similar meanings, their F1 score is less than 0.5. Similarly, we also report the overall PRC metric for this task.

As illustrated by the previous example, the lexical-based F1 score is not an appropriate evaluation metric because PICO fragment tagging prioritizes semantic relevance over lexical matching. To address this issue, BERTScore has been proposed to evaluate the semantic equivalence between two sequences (Zhang et al., 2020). Let $f = \{ w _ { 1 } , w _ { 2 } , \dots , w _ { M } \}$ and $\hat { f } = \{ \widehat { w } _ { 1 } , \widehat { w } _ { 2 } , \ldots , \widehat { w } _ { \widehat { M } } \}$ be two sequences of normalized BERT word embeddings. The precision is defined as $\begin{array} { r } { P _ { B E R T } @ N = \frac { 1 } { | \hat { f } | } \sum _ { \widehat { w } _ { j } \in \hat { f } } ( \operatorname* { m a x } _ { w _ { i } \in f } w _ { i } ^ { T } \widehat { w } _ { j } ) } \end{array}$ , recall as $R _ { B E R T } @ N =$ $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { 1 } { | f | } \sum _ { w _ { i } \in f } ( \operatorname* { m a x } _ { \widehat { w } _ { j } \in \widehat { f } } w _ { i } ^ { T } \widehat { w } _ { j } ) } \end{array}$ , and $\begin{array} { r } { F 1 _ { B E R T } @ N = \frac { 2 * P _ { B E R T } * R _ { B E R T } } { ( P _ { B E R T } + R _ { B E R T } ) } . } \end{array}$ , where $w _ { i } ^ { T } \widehat { w } _ { j }$ measures the cosine similarity between $w _ { i }$ and $\widehat { w } _ { j }$ . BERTScore seeks to optimally match each embedding with the most similar one in the other sequence. In the previous example, “pediatric” can be best matched with “patient,” with a cosine similarity of 0.86, and the best match $\mathrm { o f } ^ {  } 1 4 ^ { \cdot \hat { ) } }$ is “pediatric,” with a similarity of 0.66. As a result, we obtain $P _ { B E R T } @ \bar { 1 } = 0 . 8 1 , R _ { B E R T } @ 1 = 0 . 9 4 , F 1 _ { B E R T } @ 1 = 0 . 8 7$ , which are more aligned with SR experts’ annotation. We adopted both wordand semantic-based metrics for tagging evaluation.

## Benchmarking FastSR Against Leading LLMs: A Comparative Analysis

## LLMs Overview

LLMs include masked language models (MLMs) like BERT (Devlin et al., 2019), which use Transformer encoders (Vaswani et al., 2017) to predict masked tokens with bidirectional self-attention, and autoregressive language models (ALMs) like GPT (Radford et al., 2018), which use Transformer decoders for text generation with unidirectional self-attention. The former excel in extractive tasks (Chen et al., 2024), while the latter are ideal for generative tasks (Brown et al., 2020). Both models can be pretrained or fine-tuned with domain-specific data to adapt to particular tasks. For instance, BioBERT (Lee et al., 2020) and SciBERT (Beltagy et al., 2019) are pretrained on biomedical texts and can be fine-tuned for BioNLP tasks. BioGPT (Luo et al., 2022) and GatorTronGPT (Peng et al., 2023) are pretrained on medical or clinical texts and can be fine-tuned for medical content generation. Fine-tuning large models like GPT-4 (Brown et al., 2020) typically requires extensive, high-quality labeled data. However, SR tasks often have limited labels per class, making fine-tuning less effective (Schmidt et al., 2021). ALMs can also perform tasks without further training through few-shot prompting, but they are highly sensitive to prompt wording, example selection, and domain specificity, making them more challenging for precise tasks (Liu et al., 2023). Additionally, ALMs are more susceptible to data contamination than MLMs —where test data overlaps with pre-training data, inflating performance (Dong et al., 2024). This is because ALMs, with their sequential generation process, are more likely to reuse memorized content, especially in open-domain datasets. To illustrate these limitations, we benchmarked FastSR against fine-tuned BioBERT, fine-tuned GPT models, and zero-shot/few-shot prompted GPT across both open-domain and close-domain datasets. Table G1 is an overview of the LLMs included in our benchmarking models. In our previous version, we reported the performance of fine-tuned BioBERT.

## Evaluation Strategy

We explored two prevalent methods— few-shot prompting and fine-tuning—used in the existing literature to adapt ALMs for the SR context (Chen et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2023). Given that GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 are charged based on API calls, with costs directly related to the number of testing examples, we began with the EBM-NLP dataset, which has the fewest testing sentences, as shown in Table G2.

For the EBM-NLP dataset, we tested all possible combinations of models and methods for sentence classification, except for GPT-4, as OpenAI’s fine-tuning service for GPT-4 was not available at the time of the experiments in July 2024. We then conducted sequence tagging. In the SR context, the success of sequence tagging is highly dependent on the performance of sentence classification. If sentences are extracted incorrectly, all subsequent text fragments will be incorrect. Likewise, if any sentences are missed, the corresponding fragments will also be absent, leading to incomplete results. Both BioGPT and GatorTronGPT performed significantly worse, with at least a 46% performance gap in F1 score compared to FastSR. Fine-tuned GPT-3.5 also showed a 9% performance gap compared to FastSR. Therefore, we focused on the top-performing model in sentence classification—GPT-4 with few-shot prompting—for the sequence tagging evaluations.

Data contamination presents a significant challenge when evaluating LLMs (Brown et al., 2020). This occurs when models are exposed to testing data during pretraining, leading to artificially inflated performance. The issue is particularly pronounced in open-domain datasets like EBM-NLP (Lewis et al., 2021) and closed-source models (Dong et al., 2024). To obtain more reliable evaluations, we extended our benchmarking to closed-domain datasets, specifically the WD and COVID datasets, as recommended by existing literature (Gao et al., 2021). These datasets consist of full-text articles from credential-restricted academic platforms, making them less susceptible to web crawling and thus less likely to be included in pretraining datasets.

## Prompting and Fine-Tuning Strategy

## Sentence Classification

As mentioned, we applied few-shot prompting and fine-tuning to all models—BioGPT, GatorTronGPT, GPT-3.5, and GPT-4—for sentence classification tasks on EBM-NLP datasets and selected the top-performing model for the WD and COVID datasets. Following Chen et al. (2024), we adopted a prompt engineering strategy to improve the ALM’s ability to understand the context and generate the expected output. This strategy organizes prompts around components, including Task Description, Examples, Input, and Output specifications (see Table G3). Additionally, to facilitate the calculation of the precision-recall curve (PRC), we generated predictive probabilities for each label (Saito & Rehmsmeier, 2015). In the context of SR, where a sentence may correspond to multiple PICO classes, a multilabel classification scenario is required. Thus, the predictive probabilities for each label are independently assessed and do not need to sum to 1.

Selecting examples for few-shot prompting is essential for reducing biases and preventing overfitting (Gao et al., 2021). Both the order and composition of these examples significantly impact model performance (Liu et al., 2023). We opted to randomly sample five examples for the EBM-NLP dataset per label for three key reasons: (1) more examples can sometimes degrade performance due to the model’s sensitivity to input length and context window size (Brown et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2021); (2) using one to five examples per label aligns with standard practices in few-shot prompting studies, particularly in the medical domain (Chen et al., 2024); and (3) limiting the number of examples helps control API costs, which increase with context window size. For labels with fewer than five examples, we used sampling with replacement to ensure balanced representation and reduce sampling bias. A fixed random seed (42) was chosen to ensure reproducibility. Fo the WD and COVID datasets, we selected one example per class to manage the high API costs associated with large context window.

For fine-tuning, we adapted the pretrained BioGPT (Luo et al., 2022) and GatorTronGPT (Peng et al., 2023) models for sentence classification using labeled examples from each dataset. The dataset was split into 80% for training and 20% for validation. We used the Hugging Face Transformers library (v4.18.0) with PyTorch (v1.10.0) as our framework. Fine-tuning was performed on 4 NVIDIA A100 GPUs for 3 epochs. The AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) was employed with a learning rate of 5e-5, epsilon of 1e-8, and weight decay of 0.01. The batch size was set to 8 per GPU. The time for fine-tuning on one task took approximately 15 to 30 minutes.

We utilized OpenAI’s fine-tuning service for GPT-3.5. As before, the labeled data was split into 80% training and 20% testing. The data was formatted according to OpenAI’s fine-tuning specifications, with each example containing a prompt and a completion. The fine-tuning process was initiated through OpenAI’s API, with default hyperparameters recommended for classification tasks: a learning rate multiplier of 0.1, 3 epochs, and a batch size of 8. The fine-tuning for one task took approximately 10 minutes. After fine-tuning, we applied the same prompting strategies but without examples to obtain the sentence classification results.

## Sequence Tagging

Given that the success of sequence tagging is closely tied to the performance of sentence classification, we selected the top-performing model in sentence classification—GPT-4 few-shot prompting—for our sequence tagging evaluations. Following (Hu et al., 2024), we developed a comprehensive prompting strategy that includes a task description, fragment markup guide, fragment definitions, examples, and input/output specifications. During the selection of few-shot examples, we noticed a significant imbalance in fragment distribution among subtypes (e.g., surgical, physical, and educational interventions). To address this, after performing random sampling per label, we adjusted the examples to ensure that each subtype was represented by at least one example in the few-shot set. To ensure reproducibility, we used a fixed random seed (42).

When applying GPT models to fragment extraction, the performance gap between GPT models and FastSR becomes more pronounced, with a 33.22% difference in word-level F1 and a 20.21% gap in BERTScore. This gap is likely because fragment extraction requires a deep understanding of context in both directions. In contrast, GPT models rely on a unidirectional self-attention mechanism that only considers preceding words, thereby limiting their effectiveness in such tasks.

The comparison between GPT models and BioBERT further highlights this limitation. Despite being a much smaller model in terms of parameters (millions versus GPT-4’s trillions), BioBERT outperforms GPT-4’s best few-shot variant by 14.54% in word-level F1. This underscores the importance of bidirectional context processing in complex NLP tasks like fragment extraction, where the ability to understand the context from both directions is more critical than sheer model size.

Interestingly, compared to BioBERT and FastSR, GPT models demonstrate more significant gains when evaluated with BERTScore versus word-level F1 score. For instance, GPT-4’s few-shot performance improves by 22.42% when evaluated with BERTScore relative to its wordlevel F1 score, whereas BioBERT shows only a 10.34% improvement. This narrows the gap between BioBERT and GPT-4 to just 2.46% in BERTScore, compared to a 14.54% gap in word-level F1. Since BERTScore evaluates fragment matching based on semantic similarity rather than strict word-by-word alignment, this suggests that GPT models are less precise in lexical matching but are more adept at semantic understanding, further supporting the fact that GPT models may be more effective for generative tasks rather than for extractive tasks that require precise identification of lexical boundaries

## Discussion

Based on the benchmarking analyses, we identified the following takeaways:

FastSR’s superiority in complex SR tasks: GPT-4 surpasses its predecessors like GPT-3.5 and domain-specific models due to its advanced architecture and extensive training data. FastSR consistently outperforms GPT models across various datasets, particularly in complex SR tasks requiring precise text comprehension. In our EBM-NLP dataset experiments, FastSR demonstrated a significantly greater advantage in sequence tagging (33.22% higher F1) compared to sentence classification (12.12% higher F1). This disparity likely arises from GPT models’ use of unidirectional self-attention, which generates text sequentially by considering only the preceding context. In contrast, FastSR leverages BioBERT for base word embeddings, effectively capturing context from both directions and giving it a distinct edge in tasks where fullcontext understanding is crucial

GPT’s challenges with closed-domain datasets: GPT models perform poorly on closed-domain datasets. In our experiments with the opendomain EBM-NLP dataset, GPT models’ sentence classification performance was 12% behind FastSR. However, on closed-domain datasets, the performance gap widened significantly, with GPT models showing over 30% degradation. This disparity likely arises from data contamination, where open-domain datasets and their labels may have been included in GPT models’ pretraining, artificially boosting performance on related tasks. This suggests that GPT models without significant customizations, such as retrieval augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020), may struggle in real-world SR tasks, where most datasets are closed-domain, comprising full-text articles from credential restricted academic platforms.

Limited control in GPT models: GPT models offer less direct control over output performance compared to traditional machine learning approaches, often requiring extensive experimentation to achieve desired results. This is due to several factors: the opaque nature of GPT’s complex neural network architecture, especially in proprietary models, which makes it difficult to interpret how specific inputs lead to certain outputs; the autoregressive design, where each token is generated sequentially based on preceding tokens, complicating control over longer outputs; and high sensitivity to prompt formulation, where small wording changes can cause significant output variations. These factors make predicting and controlling GPT models’ behavior challenging, particularly in tasks like SR data extraction, where accuracy and consistency are crucial for evidence-based decision-making. The lack of control in GPT outputs could lead to inconsistencies in data extraction, compromising the integrity of SR.

High cost and low efficiency in GPT models: The cost of using proprietary GPT models, despite their strong performance, can be high, especially compared to FastSR or BERT-based models. GPT models’ costs increase with the number of testing sentences due to API calls, whereas FastSR’s costs are primarily incurred during the initial training phase, with minimal expenses for generating additional content. In our experiments with models like GPT-4, costs ranged from \$1.5 to \$4 per article for generating candidate sentences and fragments for SR experts to review. However, the lack of control over GPT outputs often requires further refinement of these initial results, increasing costs even more. Additionally, GPT’s lower performance in specific tasks means SR experts must review more articles to verify results than they would with FastSR, further raising manual costs. Moreover, GPT models like GPT-4 can have variable response times, sometimes taking several seconds or more to generate a typical response for an SR task. This latency can become more pronounced during high-traffic periods, leading to potential delays in time-sensitive applications. For instance, in our experiments, testing on a large dataset like WD took 25 hours with GPT models, compared to just 2 hours with FastSR. Overall, FastSR emerges as a more cost-effective and time-efficient solution compared to GPT models.
