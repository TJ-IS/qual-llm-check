# Leadership and justice: Increasing non participating users’ assessment of an IT through passive participation

David Gefen <sup>a,</sup>\*, Arik Ragowsky <sup>b</sup>, Catherine Ridings <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> LeBow College of Business, Drexel University, PA 19104, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 19 October 2006 Received in revised form 19 January 2008 Accepted 15 August 2008 Available online 5 October 2008

Keywords: IS implementation User satisfaction Leadership Interactional justice

## A B S T R A C T

Allowing users to actively participate in the development and implementation of a new IS is supposed to increase their satisfaction with it. This type of participation, representing aspects relating to distributive and procedural justice, is increasingly impractical in current organizational settings, because many IS are used by thousands of employees and having them involved is impossible. Nonetheless, there are still benefits to be gained in other ways. Extrapolating from interactional justice, we proposed a passive participation method of engaging users. Its effect was tested in a large company in the USA and proved effective. Interactional justice, the perception of fairness in the way that people were treated at an interpersonal level, was found to increase user assessment of the value of their IS. Implications and expansions to interactional justice theory and how this antecedent affects IS implementation are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

User participation in IS design typically involves granting users responsibility in design and implementation decisions during IS development by making some of them part of the IS team. This active participation ensures productive user–IS team interaction and the users’ full understanding of the problems and issues in the implementation, verifying whether the solutions fit the users’ needs before making changes, and maintaining productive communications within the IS team [2,17]. Although such participation activities apply to managers and those users who are involved in the development process, this participation seldom involves the ‘‘typical’’ user who is the recipient of the resulting IS but who is not personally involved in the decision making process during this implementation process. It is quite rare to have more than a small fraction of the user community actively participate in the implementation of a new IS.

What then should management do to create a positive assessment of the IS among users who are basically passive? After all, the maturity of IS and the growing tendency to install IS as a commodity suggest even less active user participation in future projects.

Addressing this need, we examined an alternative approach; passive participation. We considered user assessment of the environment of the IS during and after the design and implementation and the relationship between employees and the IS team that supported their company’s IS implementation. In answering the question we tried to avoid conceptual confusion by considering user assessment of the IS instead of user satisfaction or approval of software after active participation. The users studied were not actively engaged because there were too many, they were geographically dispersed, and too critical to be removed from their jobs during a large part of the implementation; also many were unaware of the advantage of using such methods.

## 2. Conceptual background

User assessment of an IS is closely related to the behavior of the IS team which can help or hinder users’ approval during the implementation process. The IS team can do this by ensuring that the users are provided with an environment that implies responsiveness and truthfulness, requiring the creation of a sense of fairness among non actively participating users: treating them with respect and keeping them informed of progress and issues during the IS design and implementation.

Based on interactional justice [3,25] and equity theory [1], we introduced the concept of passive participation, to deal with aspects other than distributive and procedural justice. Interactional justice deals with the perception of fairness in the way people are treated. Equity theory deals with the perceived fairness of the rewards that employees receive relative to their inputs compared to those of other employees. Passive participation thus involves the implementation process by making sure that the users feel informed and respected, even though they may not be actively contributing to the process. Users’ positive assessment of the system remains a key issue in IS management, where the project failure rate is still estimated at between 40% and 70% [16].

## 3. Theory and development of hypotheses

## 3.1. Interactional justice

The relationship between passive participation and a positive assessment of an IS can be explained through interactional justice. There are three types of organizational justice:

\- Distributive, dealing with the perceived fairness of outcomes;

\- Procedural, dealing with the perceived fairness of the formal policies and regulations; and

\- Interactional, dealing with the perceived fairness of interpersonal treatment [8,15], such as social aspects of how those in authority interact with employees, over and above the rules of procedural justice [3,8,19]. It reflects employee feelings of being treated fairly at an interpersonal level.

The need to create a sense of fairness among non participating users conceptually parallels the implications of interactional justice in organizational settings as to the need to adhere to an accepted ethical standard. It deals with how information is communicated to employees [5] as a way of creating among them a sense of the quality of interpersonal interaction when new organizational procedures are enacted.

According to fairness theory [9], interactional justice has a stronger effect on outcome evaluation than either distributive or procedural justice. This is because an aggrieved party typically considers the alternative behaviors of another party in alleviating the situation. There is seldom enough information to determine if this was the case when it comes to distributive or procedural justice. In contrast, violations of an accepted ethical code of courtesy and being kept informed are typically taken to be deliberate acts.

Perceptions of interactional justice lead to increased satisfaction: employees expect to be treated in a fair manner. Interactional justice also results in acceptance of authority and an increased sense of fairness of the decision makers. Moreover, interactional justice creates a sense of loyalty that directly affects people’s behavior. In the context of a service provider, interactional justice also builds user satisfaction directly [21]. Generally, perceptions of interactional justice increase positive attitude [7], reduce antisocial behavior, and even create a willingness to accept less favorable situations [20]. In contrast, in organizational settings where interactional justice is perceived to be low, employees retaliate against the organization [24] and increases their disapproval of undesirable outcomes. Perceptions of interactional justice reassure employees faced with uncertainty about organizational polices and their possible adverse effects.

## 3.2. An equity theory perspective of passive participation

The relationship between increased user assessment of an IS and passive participation can also be explained using equity theory. A central pillar of this is the recognition that a perception of fairness is central to social environments. People assess the fairness of their rewards by comparing their perceived return on effort with that of their colleagues. Applying this theory implies that users’ satisfaction with an IS should increase when their apparent gains from the IS compare well to those received by others [6,10,18,23].

Extending this theory to passive participation, we hypothesized that fairness should apply also to non participating users. Although they do not invest personally in the implementation process, they do gain from it indirectly. Specifically, in our study, the newly implemented CRM could benefit its users by providing better work procedures and by making their work faster and more accurate. Since the fairness of the implementation process and the benefits to be reaped from it, as far as these users are concerned, is comparable to how employees are treated in comparable situations, they should have a reasonable basis to assess the fairness of the process. In examining this hypothesis, we relied on the two aspects of interactional justice suggested by theory, namely treating employees with dignity and keeping them informed about what is happening [4,5,8,21]. Thus our hypotheses:

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 A } } .$ Perceived respect and fairness on the part of the IS team during the IS implementation process will increase users’ assessment of their IS.

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 B } } .$ An informative IS team will increase users’ assessment of their IS.

## 3.3. Increasing interactional justice in passive participation

Perceived responsiveness and truthfulness about problems are two trust creating behaviors [11,12]. Certainly, if the IS team does not broadcast respect and share information, the users will not feel that they are being treated fairly. With perceived responsiveness, users feel that the IS team is listening seriously to their reports about possible problems with or errors in the software. Accordingly, we hypothesized that users’ assessment of interactional justice through passive participation would reflect the IS team’s inclusive behavior toward the users.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 \mathbf { A } } .$ Users’ perceptions of the IS team being responsive will increase their assessment of being treated with respect and fairness by the IS team.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 \mathbf { B } } .$ Users’ perceptions of the IS team being responsive will increase their assessment of the IS team being informative.

$\mathbf { H _ { 2 C } } .$ Users’ perceptions of the IS team truthfulness will increase their assessment being treated with respect and fairness by the IS team.

$\mathbf { H _ { 2 D } } .$ Users’ perceptions of the IS team truthfulness will increase their assessment of the IS team being informative.

## 4. Research design

The items in the survey were validated by the IS project manager and her assistant prior to their distribution to verify that they addressed the meaning we assigned them and their appropriateness to the specific setting and population. We also asked a sample of users to read the survey and comment on it before data collection. Our study dealt with non participating users whose passive participation was measured by assessing their perceptions of fairness in two ways: (1) the respect and fairness they felt in their relationship with the IS team and (2) how well they felt they had been informed of the process by the IS team.

The passive participation items dealt with the perceived nature of the relationship, the perceived responsiveness and truthfulness items dealt with the perceived behavior of the IS team that made this relationship possible. Truthfulness measured the IS team’s perceived accuracy in telling users what was happening: this scale had previously been validated in the context of ERP implementation. Users’ assessment of their IS was measured by asking them to state whether they liked the software and were satisfied with it. We opted to adopt this one-dimensional view of user satisfaction based on interviews with the IS project manager. All the items were measured on a five-point Likert scale from 1 = strongly agree to 5 = strongly disagree. The items are shown in Appendix B.

## 5. Data collection

The data were collected from users of a CRM system shortly after it was implemented in a large company in the Mid-Atlantic region of the USA. Collecting the data at this implementation phase ensured that the users were already accustomed to the IS in operation, and so could assess it appropriately. Nevertheless, they still had their encounters with the IS team fresh in their minds, so they could also assess their own passive participation and the IS team’s behavior. The CRM, a complex crucial IS, supported the company’s telephone call support center.

The majority of users, numbering in the thousands in scores of offices throughout the region, did not participate in the design, implementation, or testing of the CRM software. It would have been impractical to have thousands of users actively engaged in these decisions. The users received onsite training by specialized instructors and ongoing support from the implementation team through a help-desk and during visits by the implementation team to provide ongoing technical support and to answer questions about work procedures. During the training sessions and support sessions users were also told what the CRM did, why it handled their activities the way it did, how it contributed to the organization, its importance, and why applying the CRM correctly was important to them and the organization. The users were kept up to date on what was happening to the CRM they were using and how it was designed to support their needs.

Our data were collected from only one organization in order to control for exogenous variance related to the specific IS, its implementation characteristics, and all organization policies. This increased internal validity in our exploratory study. All the users worked for the same company and used the same CRM in the same way. The data were collected through anonymous questionnaires from a representative sample of users. The installation manager and the managers of these users indicated that this sample was representative of their CRM users. The response rate was 94%: the managers had explained the importance of this study and asked the users to complete the surveys anonymously during work time.

One hundred and forty-eight questionnaires were completed. The users were company employees at the customer support center. Their demographics were: 75% female, in their 20s (40%), 30s (29%), and 40s (24%), had a high school (56%) or undergraduate degree (41%), were English speakers, had been working between 2 3 and 37 years for the company (average 12 years, standard deviation 8.9 years) and worked an average of 39 h a week (standard deviation 7.3 h). According to the managers in charge, these were typical customer support employees.

## 6. Data analysis

The data were analyzed using LISREL; we first examined the fit of the entire model to the data, in addition to examining the significance of individual causal paths. In this manner, individual hypotheses could be examined within the context of the model, rather than estimating one path at a time. The analysis applied an holistic approach in which the measurement model (how the measurement items load on their latent variables) and the structural model (how the latent variables are related to each other) were analyzed together because this provides a more accurate assessment of the psychometric properties of the constructs. The analysis is shown in Fig. 2. The items are shown in Appendix B with their standardized loadings and construct reliability. The percent above each box is the degree of explained variance. All the items loaded significantly below the 0.01 level. Appendix A includes the correlation matrix. A principal components factor analysis with a Varimax rotation is shown in Appendix C.

The model fit indices are within the accepted thresholds. The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ with 82 degrees of freedom was 141 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 $ ). RMSEA was 0.070, RMR was 0.045, GFI was 0.90, AGFI was 0.85, NFI was 0.92, CFI was 0.97, and the ratio of $\chi ^ { 2 }$ to degrees of freedom was less than 3 to 1. Discriminant validity and unidimensionality were verified based on [14]. All the hypotheses were supported. Respect and fairness and being informative increased users’ assessment of their IS $( \beta = 0 . 5 0$ and b = 0.26, respectively). In other words, users’ perception of being passive participants was increased when the IS team treated them in a manner that broadcast fairness by including them in the implementation process, supporting H –H . Explained variance degrees were 49% for users’ assessment of their IS, 66% for respect and fairness, and 55% for informative. The path between respect and fairness and informative was insignificant.

## 7. Discussion

## 7.1. Summary

Past research on user involvement dealt with aspects of active participation and the need to consider the users in the process because of the socio-technical as well as the technical, nature of IS [22]. Our study proposed an additional tool that an IS manager can use when major user involvement is impossible: applying lessons related to interpersonal justice. This could prove important, especially with the growing trend to have implementation decisions made by top management who neither disclose them to non participating users nor leave them to the discretion of the IS team. As one manager said: ‘‘I can’t stress enough the importance of clear communication, especially from a users’ perspective. I have found that that is the best way to reduce apprehension.’’

## 7.2. Implications for theory

Our study showed that fairness mattered when a user had no influence but was still affected by its consequences. Such cases are common for non participating users, who are told to utilize an IS without having any influence on its design or implementation. Our study also contributed to IS implementation management by tying passive participation to interactional justice and showing how this increases users’ positive assessment of their IS. We broke new ground by showing that there is a fairness aspect, and that even when rational and social concerns are irrelevant, it can still help users appreciate their IS. This perception of fairness is crucial in many organizational settings and, should be considered in the context of IS implementation. While the importance of engaging the users actively in the decision making process has been widely recognized, especially by Enid Mumford, our study went beyond such involvement by showing that even passive participation (being kept informed in a responsive manner), can do much to improve user assessment of their IS. It is not a matter of better decision making, but it a matter of creating an environment that is perceived to be more just.

The results can also be understood in the light of equity theory. We examined an extension of this theory: what happens when there are no rewards in the scenario? Extending the underlying idea of equity, the study shows that a sense of justice is a major determinant of appreciation of an IS. Equity is important because users who adopt a new IS often need to adapt their work procedures and organizational knowledge to the new environment and procedures as dictated by the new IS. Ignoring users produces a sense of unimportance and is a sure way of creating their resistance and apathy.

## 7.3. Practical implications

Our study showed the importance of IS leadership in creating the correct atmosphere during IS implementation. Keeping users informed may add only marginally to a better designed system, but is a crucial part of IS project management, as it creates a sense of passive participation and increases the perceived fairness of the process. Participation may be one way to overcome user resistance, which is a major reason why IS projects fail. This human side of IS adoption should be part of IS management policy and leadership.

On a more tentative note, some of the conclusions may apply in other contexts such as vendor support and IS outsourcing projects [13]. Emphasizing the need to create perceptions of justice and fairness through responsiveness and truthfulness among users at a customer site might be advantageous to vendors, especially if the result is a better appreciation of the service that vendors need in creating favorable user assessment.

## 7.4. Conclusion

Perceptions of justice and fairness are critical when employees assess their work environment. Good IS leadership can improve this through interactional justice to create perceptions of justice. Active participation in implementation of user systems is becoming increasingly rare with the growing reliance on standardized and outsourced IS. Managing the process effectively could be an alternative to otherwise unobtainable active participation in scenarios where typical users cannot be an active party in the implementation of their IS.
