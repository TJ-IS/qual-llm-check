# Who delivers the bigger bang for the buck: CMO or CIO?

Joseph Taylor<sup>a,⁎,1</sup>, Joseph Vithayathil<sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> California State University, Sacramento, United States

<sup>b</sup> Southern Illinois University Edwardsville, United State

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Chief Marketing O<sup>fi</sup>cer Chief Information O<sup>fi</sup>cer Firm performance Power Managerial power Executive power Information technolog

## A B S T R A C T

There is emerging evidence that the Chief Marketing O<sup>fi</sup>cer is likely to surpass the Chief Information O<sup>fi</sup>cer in IT spending. Hence, it is relevant and timely to understand the relative impact of the CMO and CIO on business value. This study uses the foundation of executive power and in<sup>fl</sup>uence to examine this question and analyze the relative impact of powerful technology and marketing leaders on <sup>fi</sup>rm output. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the inclusion of a powerful technology leader is more predictive of a sustained increase in <sup>fi</sup>rm output than the inclusion of a powerful mar keting leader over a one-year, <sup>fi</sup>ve-year and seven-year period.

## Introduction

“Marketing is from Mars: IT is from Venus,” so proclaims the National Association of Electrical Distributors (NAED, 2014) in trying to understand how these two important C-suite roles are changing with the technological disruption wrought by cloud computing, social media, mobile computing, and analytics. Tomison (Avanade, 2016) asks whether the executive boardroom wil have room for both the Chief Information officer (CIO) and the Chief Marketing Officer (CMO), Others (Krasnikoy and Javachandran. 2008) <sup>fi</sup>nd that marketing delivers greater <sup>fi</sup>rm performance than the functions of Research and Development (R&D) or Operations. Such observations from practice and research coupled with the rapid deployment of radical new technologies raises the question of which executive, the CMO or the CIO, has greater e<sup>f</sup>ect on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance.

In the waning days of the dotcom boom, Information Technology (IT) executives at Amazon recognized an opportunity to leverage the company’s rapidly growing infrastructure to support other companies. The company initiated development of an Application Program Interface (API) based technology infrastructure for internal development. This insightful initiative by Amazon’s technology executives laid the groundwork for Amazon’s subsequent services o<sup>f</sup>ering that became Amazon Web Services, the dominant cloud computing provider with over \$10b in annual revenue (Miller, 2016). This example illustrates a case where a powerful and prescient technology leader recognized a potential shift in the marketplace, and undertook previously unplanned changes that positioned the company to take advantage of future opportunities. Such examples suggest that technology leaders can be uniquely placed to generate <sup>fi</sup>rm value over time.

Scholars have separately analyzed the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the CMO on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance and there is support for this thesis (Eric Boyd et al., 2010; Krasnikov and Jayachandran, 2008; Nath and Mahajan, 2011). There is also ample literature on the e<sup>f</sup>ect of IT investments on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance and business value but with mixed results in research regarding the CIO s impact on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance (Banker et al., 2011). The question of whether the CIO or CMO has greater impact on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance remains unanswered. Therefore, the present study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to shed light on this question and analyze the relative impact of the CMO and CIO on <sup>fi</sup>rm

performance.

Why is this question of the impact of the CMO and CIO of importance today? The answer in a nutshell is: (i) The CIO and the CMO are the biggest spenders on corporate IT (Brinker and McLellan, 2014), (ii) There is mounting evidence that CMO spending on IT may surpass CIO spending on IT (Sareen, 2013; Sorofman, 2017), and (iii) Technology is changing faster than ever in the marketplace, and <sup>fi</sup>rms are increasingly challenged to adapt to new technologies such as mobile, cloud computing, big data, social media among others, to maintain market share and capitalize on new market opportunities.

A secondary motivation for this research study is to shed some light on the perennial feeling of inferiority or irrelevance felt by Information Systems (IS) scholars compared to time-honored disciplines such as Marketing, Accounting, Operations or Finance. These other areas of study do not routinely attempt to justify themselves whereas IS researchers seem to be perennially discussing their relevance and justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for the IS research discipline. One of the manifest obsessions within IS is an obsession with an artifact which seems to function like a security blanket. Whereas an artifact may have been relevant during the era of mainframes, today in the 21st century few would question the pervasiveness of IT in everything we do in society, business and in life. There is no doubt that IT has a<sup>f</sup>ected organizations, people and society and provides tremendous value. Prior research, and more importantly, practice has clearly and indelibly established the value of IT. We add to this research by analyzing the comparative value of the CIO and the CMO.

Technology innovation has long been considered a prominent source of disruptive innovation (Christensen, 2013). While technology disruptions present great challenge to organizations, they also present great opportunities. Technology advancement can be of bene<sup>fi</sup>t to <sup>fi</sup>rms, but internal <sup>fi</sup>rm power struggles and organization dynamics have been found to contribute to resistance (Markus, 1983). The role of power has been found to contribute to the success of organizations. Within leadership teams, one measure of power that researchers have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed is higher levels of relative compensation (Carpenter et al., 2004). Based on prior literature, our research employs a key determinant of power, compensation relative to other executives, to assess the impact that a powerful leader in the c-suite, such as a CIO or CMO has on measures of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. The power of executives in a <sup>fi</sup>rm has been associated with the ability to drive strategic change (Hambrick and Finkelstein, 1987). This prior research leads us to the conceptual model of our empirical study illustrated in Fig. 1

As illustrated in Fig. 1, our analysis considers relative executive compensation of the CIO and the CMO. When these two executives are highly compensated relative to their peers, prior literature informs that they wield executive power and in<sup>fl</sup>uence. A second stream of literature shows that power and in<sup>fl</sup>uence of executives a<sup>f</sup>ect <sup>fi</sup>rm outcomes such as <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. This stream of prior literature on the e<sup>f</sup>ect of executive provides our theoretical foundation is described in the section ‘Theory and literature’.

To answer these research questions, this study examines how <sup>fi</sup>rms with powerful technology leaders can a<sup>f</sup>ect top line performance, or sales output. Technology adoption has been found to contribute to changes in power within the organization over time (Burkhardt and Brass, 1990). Therefore, it is conceivable that with increasing IT investments that the CIO may have an increasing e<sup>f</sup>ect on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance and potentially eclipse the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the CMO. As <sup>fi</sup>rms empower technology leaders or CIOs with representation among the top management teams, what is the impact on <sup>fi</sup>rm output, and how does that impact on output compare to other key functional leadership roles such as the CMO?

We analyze the relationship between the inclusion of a technology leader or CIO within the <sup>fi</sup>rm s highest compensated executives with <sup>fi</sup>rm output. Representation of technology specialists within the top management team can facilitate the execution of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s technology vision (Lim et al., 2012). Technical expertise can allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to better identify opportunities to transform technological change into growth opportunities. Traditionally the marketing function represented by the CMO has been seen as an engine that drives <sup>fi</sup>rm output. We challenge this assertion in this paper and demonstrate that <sup>fi</sup>rms with powerful technology leaders achieve greater <sup>fi</sup>rm output, sustained over time, than <sup>fi</sup>rms that do not. Furthermore, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that powerful technology leaders such as the CIO predict future, sustained sales output increase with greater strength than powerful marketing leaders such as the CMO.

## Theory and literature

Relevant literature for our analysis comprises of prior research from four areas: (1) The role of power in organizations and the relationships between compensation and power, (2) The role and impact of the CIO in <sup>fi</sup>rms, (3) The business value of IT investments, and (4) The role and impact of CMOs in firms. Following this literature. we argue that organizational power provides a relevant and compelling theoretical underpinning to explain our hypotheses and results.

The role of power enjoyed by a leader or manager has been discussed in trade literature as well as in academic literature, and we accept the intuitive notion that powerful individuals can a<sup>f</sup>ect outcomes in endeavors they are involved in (Bacharach and Lawler, 1980; Chandler Jr., 1962; Hage, 1980; Hambrick and Finkelstein, 1987; Hinings et al., 1974; Mintzberg and Mintzberg, 1983; Patchen, 1974; Pfe<sup>f</sup>er and Pfe<sup>f</sup>er, 1981). Within a <sup>fi</sup>rm, we would expect the Top Management Team (TMT) to be vested with power to positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence <sup>fi</sup>rm performance outcomes. While this concept is intuitive, research has shown that all executives within a TMT are not equal in terms of power, and therefore in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Zaleznik (Zaleznik, 1970) highlights the important role of power by noting that organizations operate by distributing authority and setting a stage for the exercise of power. Prior research by Finkelstein (Finkelstein, 1992) has developed constructs for measuring the power of leaders by focusing on organizational impact. This literature has shown that in circumstances where managerial discretion is high, the skills, abilities and decisions of the leader s e<sup>f</sup>ect <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, in other words, powerful leaders do make a di<sup>f</sup>erence. While the sources of power and authority in an organization can vary, research on top management teams has found that relative salary is associated with a leader’s ability to undertake strategic change within a <sup>fi</sup>rm (Carpenter et al., 2004). Hence, higher managerial compensation is considered a signal of executive power.

Devers at al. (2007) have provided a comprehensive review of the literature on compensation and its outcomes and <sup>fi</sup>nd that executive compensation does a<sup>f</sup>ect <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Others note that (Carpenter et al., 2004; Finkelstein, 1992) power is mediated through executive compensation as illustrated earlier in Fig. 1. Furthermore, prior literature has directly linked executive compensation to <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Empirical analysis is employed by some scholars (Coughlan and Schmidt, 1985; Hubbard and Palia, 1995; Murphy, 1985) to show that executive compensation is associated with <sup>fi</sup>rm performance.

Prior research has also examined the role of power within information technology (IT). In a seminal literature review of power multiple types of power are described, including “lenses” through which the power of technology professionals may be evaluated (Jasperson et al., 2002). In this study, we examine the power of technology and marketing leaders from an organizational perspective based on the types of power discussed by Jasperson et al. (2002). This power is allocated through hierarchical relationships and is marked by control of organizational resources. In contrast, much of the prior research regarding the role of technology leaders on firm performance has focused on issues such as reporting structure (Banker et al., 2011). technology leader relationships (Enns et al.. 2001), and technology leader business expertise (Bassellier and Benbasat, 2004). The strategic intentions for technology within a <sup>fi</sup>rm have been found to contribute to whether or not a CIO reports to the CEO or the CFO. and that CIO reporting structure has been found to predict abnormal returns over limited periods of time (Banker et al., 2011). Preston et al. (2008) demonstrated the varying levels of strategic authority of CIOs a<sup>f</sup>ects IT contributions in self-reported <sup>fi</sup>rm performance measures (Preston et al., 2008). Based on previous literature regarding power in organizations, it is posited that even with a comparable reporting structure, the power of a CIO may be very di<sup>f</sup>erent across organizations. This stream of literature has not analyzed the inclusion of the CIO or CTO in the dominant coalition of the <sup>fi</sup>rm by virtue of relative compensation, rather the extant literature has focused merely on reporting hierarchy. The presence of a CIO in the leadership team has been associated with higher levels of innovation (Saldanha and Krishnan, 2011), with various measures of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance (Hu et al., 2014; Yayla and Hu, 2014), and with higher levels of IT investment (Lim et al., 2013). Powerful technology leaders have helped <sup>fi</sup>rms enter new markets, as Amazon has demonstrated with the growth of its cloud computing services (Miller, 2016). Powerful technology leaders may also help <sup>fi</sup>rms adapt to changing market conditions, as exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed by General Motors e<sup>f</sup>orts to bring more integration between mobile technologies and automobiles (Shin et al., 2014). Technology adoption has been found to contribute to changes in power within the organization over time (Burkhardt and Brass, 1990) and the CIO may be an increasingly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant player in a<sup>f</sup>ecting <sup>fi</sup>rm performance.

There is extensive prior research that has demonstrated the positive impact on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance through technology investments. IT investments have been shown to be positively related to a number of di<sup>f</sup>erent <sup>fi</sup>rm performance measures, including sales output and Tobin's a (Bharadwai et al., 1999: Bryniolfsson. 1993: Bryniolfsson and Hitt. 1996). enhanced returns relative to risk (Dewan et al., 2007), and the use of human capital (Aral and Weill, 2007). Whereas a variety of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance measures are used in the literature, this paper speci<sup>fi</sup>cally analyzes <sup>fi</sup>rm output as measured by sales revenue.

Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant scholarly work has analyzed the impact of the marketing function, typically represented by the CMO, on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Scholars <sup>fi</sup>nd that marketing delivers greater <sup>fi</sup>rm performance than the functions of Research and Development (R&D) or Operations (Krasnikov and Jayachandran, 2008). Prior literature has also analyzed the impact of marketing or the CMO on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance and found positive association between the CMO and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance (Eric Boyd et al., 2010; Krasnikov and Jayachandran, 2008; Nath and Mahajan, 2011). There is also recent evidence emerging that marketing is controlling increasing IT budgets and the CMO may surpass the CIO in terms of IT spending in the near future (McLellan. 2013). Powerful marketing leaders have an established tradition of positively e<sup>f</sup>ecting <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, and are increasingly directing a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s spending on technology. As these trends continue to in<sup>fl</sup>uence technology-related decision making, we propose that developing our understanding of the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of powerful technology leaders relative to powerful marketing leaders presents a critical contribution to both scholars and practitioners. Through comparative analysis we contribute to our understanding of the role of technology leader power in a business environment in which an increasing share of technology spending is overseen by marketing (NAED, 2014).

Prior literature regarding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of powerful technology and marketing leaders on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance lead us to the arguments that support our hypotheses development. We have used time periods of one, three, <sup>fi</sup>ve and seven years as the elapsed time for observing the e<sup>f</sup>ect of a powerful CMO or CIO on <sup>fi</sup>rm output. Whereas the one-year e<sup>f</sup>ect is well understood, the choice of <sup>fi</sup>ve and seven years is based on the generally accepted useful life and depreciation timeframes for technology (IRS, 2016). Additionally, we investigate a model with elapsed time of three years as an intermediate step between one year and <sup>fi</sup>ve years. While it is not necessary that the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the CMO s IT initiatives or the CIO s IT initiatives be lock-step with depreciation schedules and useful life estimates, these time periods provide an anchor for our analysis which covers a su<sup>fi</sup>ciently large span of time for e<sup>f</sup>ects to manifest themselves in <sup>fi</sup>rm output.

## Hypotheses

Marketing scholars have demonstrated that the representation of the marketing function within the top management team can be associated with enhanced <sup>fi</sup>rm performance (Eric Boyd et al., 2010; Krasnikov and Jayachandran, 2008). Persistent attention to marketing has been found to grow sales (Dekimpe and Hanssens, 1995). As powerful leaders within the <sup>fi</sup>rm are dedicated to the marketing function it is posited that <sup>fi</sup>rms would be better able to adapt to marketplace changes, and that larger <sup>fi</sup>rm output would be observed.

Hypothesis 1a. The representation of a powerful marketing leader within the top management team will predict greater levels of <sup>fi</sup>rm output 1 year in the future.

Hypothesis 1b. The representation of a powerful marketing leader within the top management team will predict greater levels of <sup>fi</sup>rm output 3 years in the future.

While top management team attention to the sales and marketing function may be of bene<sup>fi</sup>t to <sup>fi</sup>rms over short periods, we posit that shifting market conditions over time, and a changed technology landscape will limit the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of marketers within the top management team to in<sup>fl</sup>uence higher levels of <sup>fi</sup>rm output over longer periods. We propose that the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts achievable through marketing are short term in nature and will attenuate as longer elapsed time periods are evaluated.

Hypothesis 2a. The representation of a powerful marketing leader within the top management team will not predict greater levels of <sup>fi</sup>rm output 5 years in the future.

Hypothesis 2b. The representation of a powerful marketing leader within the top management team will not predict greater levels of <sup>fi</sup>rm output 7 years in the future.

In contrast to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of powerful marketing leaders, we posit that a powerful technology leader will be able to help a <sup>fi</sup>rm more dynamically address changes in market conditions rooted in technology advancements (Ravichandran et al., 2005). In<sup>fl</sup>uential top managers with technology expertise would be better prepared to identify opportunities to leverage technology to create business value within their organizations. An in<sup>fl</sup>uential technology leader will enable a <sup>fi</sup>rm to more rapidly identify and exploit new and emerging technology opportunities (Bennis, 2013). New technology developments can create opportunities for a <sup>fi</sup>rm that are consistent with the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s overarching technology vision. However, the “sensing” and “seizing” of the new opportunities a<sup>f</sup>orded by emerging technologies may be enhanced by executives with a technology background (Nag and Gioia, 2012). We posit that <sup>fi</sup>rms with powerful technology leaders are better able to adapt to changing market conditions to increase <sup>fi</sup>rm output. Managing technology projects can be especially challenging, and understanding how to use personal relationships to facilitate strategic change has been found to contribute to implementation success. Powerful technology leaders may be better positioned to in<sup>fl</sup>uence their peers in organizations. The relationship between power and implementation success has been found to be partly mediated by in<sup>fl</sup>uence tactics (Lines, 2007). In addition to wielding in<sup>fl</sup>uence within their respective <sup>fi</sup>rms, powerful technology leaders may be better equipped to wield in<sup>fl</sup>uence outside of their organization. A broad network of personal relationships both inside and outside the <sup>fi</sup>rm has been found to enhance technology leaders power and in<sup>fl</sup>uence (Medcof, 2008).

The business capabilities of technology leaders have been found to contribute to the development of business-IT partnerships (Bassellier and Benbasat, 2004). Peer relationships, the ability to use peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence and post implementation management, have been found to contribute to technology project performance (Enns et al., 2001). By including a technology leader within the top management team, a greater concentration of power around technology projects may be achieved. Concentrating power within the top management team has been associated with strategic change (Greve and Mitsuhashi, 2007). Success at developing business savvy and strong personal relationships has been associated with technology leader’s ability to in<sup>fl</sup>uence <sup>fi</sup>rm strategy. We posit that powerfu technology leadership could assist the top management team in better adapting to marketplace changes created by technolog advancements in the business environment, leading to higher levels of <sup>fi</sup>rm output over time.

As technology continues to disrupt markets in challenging ways, a powerful technology leader may allow a <sup>fi</sup>rm to better capitalize on new opportunities.

Bill Gates once famously observed “We always overestimate the change that will occur in the next two years and underestimate the change that will occur in the next ten” (Schellong, 2007). There may be considerable hype surrounding new technology introductions and <sup>fi</sup>rms without mindful, competent leadership can fall into mindless exercises of technology experimentation (Swanson and Ramiller, 2004). However, keeping an organization on track amidst the swirl of technology inspired change may require leaders with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant credibility within their organizations. Internal status associated with relatively high compensation can function as a means to provide powerful leaders within the <sup>fi</sup>rm the clout necessary to drive strategic direction (Carpenter and Sanders, 2002). We suggest that a powerful technology leader could champion strategic investments that enhance <sup>fi</sup>rm <sup>fl</sup>exibility over time, and allow the <sup>fi</sup>rm to adapt and respond more e<sup>f</sup>ectively to market changes. Having <sup>fl</sup>exibility to adapt to change has been found to be predictive of e<sup>f</sup>ective product development (Sanchez and Mahoney, 1996). Hence, we postulate that long-term <sup>fi</sup>rm output is better predicted by the presence of a powerful technology leader than it is predicted by the presence of a powerful marketing leader.

## Data and methodology

## Data

The data included in our study comes from Compustat and Execucomp databases. The period of the analysis for this study is from January 1992 to January 2017. Data from all industries are included and captured by <sup>fi</sup>scal year. Within the analysis <sup>fi</sup>nancial results data from Compustat for each <sup>fi</sup>rm are paired with executive leadership data for that <sup>fi</sup>rm obtained from Execucomp for each year included in the analysis. If a <sup>fi</sup>rm did not have all data <sup>fi</sup>elds available for the year/<sup>fi</sup>rm combination, the <sup>fi</sup>rm was dropped from the analysis. Based on the matched pairing of this data our analysis includes at least 2000 <sup>fi</sup>rms and more than 20,000 year/<sup>fi</sup>rm combinations for testing each hypothesis.

## Independent variables

## Technology leader inclusion in the top management team

Data from 1992 to 2017 were collected from the COMPUSTAT EXECOMP database. All executives whose title included the word “information” or “technology” were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as senior technology executives. While distinctions do exist between Chief Information O<sup>fi</sup>cer (CIO) and Chief Technology O<sup>fi</sup>cer (CTO) roles, for the purposes of this study, we considered both roles as being a technology leader and the titles were consolidated. The technology leader variable was coded as “0” if there were no executives listed who included the word “information” or “technology” in their title, and coded “1” if either term was used in the executive’s title. Each record was reviewed to eliminate the potential for executives whose title may have included the terms “information” or “technology” in conjunctions with other terms that would overly divide the executives focus. For example, if an executive’s title han been “Senior Vice President of Operations, Information and Finance the executive would has not been included as a technology leader. The Security and Exchange Commission (SEC) requires <sup>fi</sup>rm to report the compensation of materially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant executives as a component of <sup>fi</sup>ling <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. While there has been some variation over time regarding the number of executives whose compensation is included in reporting guidelines, those executives included in reporting are deemed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant to the investment community. Presently, executive compensation is typically reported only for the CEO, CFO and an additional three highest compensated executives of the company (SEC, 2011).

## Marketing leader inclusion in the top management team

Data from 1992 to 2017 were collected from the COMPUSTAT EXECOMP database. All executives whose title included the word marketing or sales were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as senior marketing executives. While distinctions do exist between Chief Marketing O<sup>fi</sup>cer (CMO) and senior sales roles. for the purposes of this study, we considered both roles as marketing leaders, or leaders dedicated to growing revenue and the titles were consolidated. This variable was coded as “0” if there were no executives listed who included the word “marketing” or “sales” in their title, and coded “1” if either term was used in the executive’s title. No <sup>fi</sup>rms in the analysis had both marketing and sales executives including in the highest compensated members of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s top management team.

For the purposes of this analysis, only those technology leaders whose compensation met the SEC guidelines that are outlined in the regulatory <sup>fi</sup>ling procedures were included. This method ensures that the data contains only those technology executives who are among the highest compensated leaders within their organizations. Companies were <sup>fl</sup>agged as either having a technology leader represented in the upper echelon of management, or not having a technology leader represented in the upper echelon of management for each <sup>fi</sup>scal year included in the study. ## Dependent variables

## Firm output post X years

Sales data were collected from COMPUSTAT North America database labeled SALE within the data set. Financial results from 1992 to 2017 were collected from all <sup>fi</sup>rms included in the database. Firm output post 1 year refers to the sales output of the <sup>fi</sup>rm after a one-year lag of the inclusion of a marketing or technology leader within the top management team of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. In other words, for a one-year lag the inclusion of a technology or marketing leader within the top management team of the <sup>fi</sup>rm in 1992 is used as a predictor of <sup>fi</sup>rm output in 1993. For a three-year lag the inclusion of a technology or marketing leader within the top management team of the <sup>fi</sup>rm in 1992 is used as a predictor of <sup>fi</sup>rm output in 1995. We choose to lag output variables one, three, <sup>fi</sup>ve and seven years to re<sup>fl</sup>ect potential di<sup>f</sup>erences in technology depreciation schedules across <sup>fi</sup>rms (see Table 2).

## Control variables

## Employees

To isolate variation in <sup>fi</sup>rm-output that may be the result of a large employee base, total number of employees are used as a control variable. The COMPUSTAT value of EMP is used to represent the number of employees (see Table 3).

## Assets

To isolate variation that may be the result of <sup>fi</sup>rm size, total value of assets are used as a control variable. The COMPUSTAT value of AT is used to represent the value of <sup>fi</sup>rm assets.

## Capital expenditures

To control for corporate strategies that are focused on growing total output the value of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s capital expenditures is used as a control variable. The COMPUSTAT value of CAPX is used to represent the value of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s capital expenditures in a given year.

## Empirical specification

We analyzed the data using a variety of regression model speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate that the voice of powerful technology executives could be a means by which <sup>fi</sup>rm output can be increased.

## Limitations and future research

Implementing digital strategies requires organizations to go beyond merely aligning IT objectives to corporate strategy. Implementing digital strategies requires organizations to infuse technology strategy into all decision making within <sup>fi</sup>rms. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate that the human actor represented by a powerful technology leader is able to contribute to overarching <sup>fi</sup>rm goals, such as increasing <sup>fi</sup>rm output. As cloud computing and Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) democratize technology procurement, further research regarding the characteristics of technology leadership that are associated with <sup>fi</sup>rm success can provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant value to both scholars and practitioners.

The structure of this analysis greatly limits the number of senior technology leaders that are considered. The research model employed in this study excludes technology leaders who are not among the <sup>fi</sup>ve highest paid executives within the <sup>fi</sup>rm, even though such executives may wield substantial in<sup>fl</sup>uence in their respective organizations. Further analysis based on broader measures of technology leader’s in<sup>fl</sup>uence may provide additional insight into the contributions of technology leaders to <sup>fi</sup>rm performance.

While this model establishes that over time, a powerful technology leader predict higher levels of <sup>fi</sup>rm output than a powerful marketing leader, the research model provides little visibility to which attributes of technology leaders and technology strategy are associated with increasing <sup>fi</sup>rm outputs. Future research could identify and measure the behaviors and actions of technology leaders that contribute to increasing <sup>fi</sup>rm output.

The relationship between technology and marketing leaders is in a state of <sup>fl</sup>ux. The rise of cloud computing will potentially result in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes to the way that IT departments support their organizations. Cloud computing can dramatically increase the selfservice capabilities that business functions, such as marketing, have to meet their technology needs. Further research should examine the speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways in which technology leaders will need to evolve to continue to deliver <sup>fi</sup>rm output bene<sup>fi</sup>ts as cloud computing transforms IT organizations.

This model examines overall <sup>fi</sup>rm output, rather than growth in output. In high growth industries, an examination of growth may provide more insights into the relative bene<sup>fi</sup>ts as a consequence of having powerful technology leaders versus the relative bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of other functional specialists.

The role of technology has changed tremendously over the twenty-<sup>fi</sup>ve years included in the study. Additional research on the manner in which the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of technology leaders has changed over time will provide valuable insights into the role of technology leaders in enhancing <sup>fi</sup>rm output.

The present study uses publicly reported secondary data to examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of powerful technology leaders on performance. While substantial body of IS research has examined the impacts of IS on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, much of the existing research relies on selfreported measures of success, as opposed to publicly available data. In contrast, the use of publicly reported data can provide broadbased quantitative insights regarding how technology contributes to <sup>fi</sup>rm outcomes. The use of public <sup>fi</sup>rm performance data also facilitates the comparison of impact across business disciplines. Future research regarding the power and in<sup>fl</sup>uence of technology leaders should address two major topics: (i) the characteristics of powerful technology leaders that are associated with <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, and (ii) the technology characteristics that are associated with the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of powerful technology leaders.

While our research demonstrates that overall, powerful technology leaders are associated with greater levels of <sup>fi</sup>rm output, future research regarding technology leader characteristics is of value to both scholars and practitioners. Technology leader characteristics such as gender, training, experience, age or tenure may help provide a more robust explanation regarding the manner in which technology leaders contribute to <sup>fi</sup>rm output.

Technology characteristics have also changed dramatically over the period observed in our study. During the 25-year period covered by our research, major trends such as, the internet, cloud computing, technology standards, mobile devices, social networking and electronic commerce have individually and collectively transformed the business environment dramatically. Future research could isolate these trends in relation to technology leadership and evaluate the role of a powerful technology leader in enhancing <sup>fi</sup>rm performance during times of technology change.

## Contributions to research and practice

The role of powerful executives in enhancing <sup>fi</sup>rm performance is relatively well understood but there is little research that examines comparative power within the TMT. The rapid emergence of cloud computing enables technology spending to become more fragmented. Furthermore, IT infrastructure and services can easily move outside the <sup>fi</sup>rm ostensibly diminishing the value of the CIO or a technology leader (Vithayathil, 2017). However, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide support for the continued role of a powerful technology leader. This research demonstrates that not only do powerful technology leaders matter, their in<sup>fl</sup>uence predicts higher levels of <sup>fi</sup>rm output compared to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of powerful marketing leaders. Cloud computing may enable more decentralization in technology decision making, but our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of powerful technology leader representation in the TMT. If IS departments become more decentralized through the growth of cloud computing, expanding our knowledge of the role of comparative power in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s decision making structure may become a key component of sense-making when considering the role of technology investments on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance outcomes.

Powerful technology leaders can be a predictor of higher levels of sustained <sup>fi</sup>rm output. The implications of this <sup>fi</sup>nding are twofold. First, this research would indicate that powerful technology leaders are assisting their <sup>fi</sup>rms in making business decisions. This would imply that broad exposure to business development domains could be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for aspiring technology leaders. Second, this research indicates that <sup>fi</sup>rms with strategies focused on growth may secure bene<sup>fi</sup>ts through elevating technology leaders to powerful roles within top management teams. Wielding in<sup>fl</sup>uence is about more than having a seat at the table and may have more to do with powerful leaders also having relative compensation packages that signal the technology leader’s importance and in<sup>fl</sup>uence within the organization.

The value of computers, communications and storage is not questioned anymore in the 21st century. Technology is everywhere. Hence, we believe it is time to cease questioning the relevance of the IS discipline and discard antediluvian notions of the necessity of an ancient artifact as a bedrock of IS research because we are enveloped by information technology. Many of us are wearing information technology and carrying such technology at all times, typically in the form of ubiquitous mobile devices. With this in mind, we pro<sup>f</sup>er the notion that IS research boundaries have expanded to include all of the means by which technology is managed. As technology has become ubiquitous and pervasive, the artifact is everywhere. All other disciplines are simply unable to function in practice today without IT. In particular, as we note in this paper, the function of marketing is emerging as the department with the fastest growing and largest IT spending capability. Hence, because the artifact is now pervasive and ubiquitous as the air we breathe, there are no preconceived boundaries for IS research. We hope this paper serves to further discard irrelevant notions of IS relevance as a discipline. We support these arguments with the present study of the value generated by the CIO versus the CMO.

## Conclusion

Increasing <sup>fi</sup>rm output is a strategic objective of nearly every organization. Leaders from many functional specialties work together to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to achieve growth objectives. Marketing may be explicitly associated with increasing <sup>fi</sup>rm output, but in today’s technology-supercharged world the value of technology leaders who are well informed on business issues is becoming evident. This research supports the idea that over time, powerful technology leaders such as a CIO can be a strategic asset for increasing <sup>fi</sup>rm output.
