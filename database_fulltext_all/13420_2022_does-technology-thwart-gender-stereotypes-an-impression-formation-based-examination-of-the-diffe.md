# Does Technology Thwart Gender Stereotypes? An Impression Formation-Based Examination of the Differential Influence of Technology across Gender and Messages

Andy Luse,<sup>1</sup> Anthony Townsend,<sup>2</sup> Brian Mennecke<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Oklahoma State University, USA, andyluse@okstate.edu <sup>2</sup>Iowa State University, USA, amt@iastate.edu <sup>3</sup>Iowa State University, USA, mennecke@iastate.edu

## Abstract

This research examines the relationship between gender, message sentiment, and technology use on the way that observers form impressions of others. Building on impression formation and gender stereotype research and theory, we develop a two-study research methodology for examining how impressions of technology users are formed. The results of our two studies indicate that technology use is an important component in impression formation, significantly inhibiting the effects of gender stereotyping, such that women and men are not evaluated differently based on their apparent competency in using technology nor on the content of their messaging. Our findings indicate that the use of technology subverts both the male and female stereotypes that observers normally engage.

Keywords: Impression Formation, Sex, Gender, Stereotype, Male, Female

Virpi Kristiina Tuunainen was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on July 21, 2020 and underwent two revisions.

## 1 Introduction

Impression formation is a theoretical description of an individual processing initial information about another individual (the target) to construct a first impression of the target individual (Asch, 1946) and has long been considered an important aspect of initial interactions between individuals. <sup>1</sup> These initial impressions can have a lasting impact, as it has been shown that later judgments of the target are still influenced by these initial impressions (Higgins et al. 1977) and they further impact ongoing interactions (Brown et al. 2016). One area that has not received attention in information systems is the role of gender in impression formation. This is important because the impact of these impressions can drive low involvement of women in technology fields due to negative attitudes towards women’s aptitude for technical work (Gupta et al., 2019) and may consign women technology users to less success in their organizations, despite competency commensurate with their male colleagues (Trauth & Quesenberry, 2006; Trauth et al., 2008). The combination of gender and technology may provide a powerful observational stimulus to users, with a number of studies examining perceptions of the expected competence of women users of information technologies indicating persistent stereotypes held by both men and women about differences between male and female technology users (Craig, 2016; Heilman, 2012; Panteli, 2012). While some of these stereotypes (which are frequently negative with regard to the technological capabilities of women versus men) may be moderated in the face of demonstrated competence (Heilman et al., 2019), they remain an important subtext within organizational and personal narratives of individual’s evaluations of others (Liu et al., 2001) and have been shown to impact women in the workplace through diminished IT retention rates (Hewlett et al., 2014), lack of support (Gupta et al., 2019), and lower pay (Annabi & Lebovitz, 2018), among other impacts. In fact, evidence exists that stereotypes related to information technology come from older cultural biases that question the general technical skills of women, which implies that these stereotypes are substantially entrenched and powerful (Morley, 2004; van Zoonen, 1992). Furthermore, technology advertising has historically been dominated by depictions of male users, which creates a stereotype that women are not technology users or experts (Feeney, 2002).

While several studies have investigated the impact of gender on impression formation in “gender-relevant” fields such as technology (e.g. Heilman, 2012; Heilman et al., 2019; Johnson et al., 2008; Maurer & Taylor, 1994), these are less helpful in informing us about the impact of gender<sup>2</sup> within the context of active technology use. Research has shown that impressions of women and men differ based on competence within technology-related subjects (Heilman et al., 2019), but the role of technology in active technology usage situations is less understood with regards to its influence on impression formation. With mobile and personal technologies becoming increasingly ubiquitous (e.g., smartphones, tablets, laptops), technology use is undoubtedly influencing impression formation and as these technologies become more pervasive in the organization, this influence will presumably increase. This leads to our primary research question: How do the gender and exhibited technology-related behaviors of a target influence the impressions formed by observers? More specifically, we are examining whether women are advantaged or disadvantaged when they are actively using technology in a work setting. This is an important issue, as women are encouraged to train for IT careers and businesses are actively seeking to increase the number of women in their workforces (Ahuja, 2002; Craig, 2016).

To examine this question, we investigate the impressions formed by individual observers when an individual target is actively using or not using a technology product. Specifically, in two separate studies, we examine the impact of technology use and its interaction with the sex of the individual target using the technology. While previous research has examined impression formation and technology in a non-colocated environment (Brown et al., 2016; Choi et al., 2018; Diaz Andrade et al., 2015), we examine impression formation and technology usage in a colocated setting, indicative of organizational environments and answering the call for examining interventions using environmental factors, such as whether possessing IT skills/knowledge influences workplace culture for higher social inclusion of women (Trauth, 2017). In Study 1, we manipulate the individual targets’ message and technology use to investigate the impact of message sentiment, technology, and gender on the perceptions of the individual target developed by observers. In Study 2, we examine the role of gender and technology competence on subject impressions of male and female individual targets. Surprisingly, results run counter to previous research that suggests deeply entrenched stereotypes viewing IT skills as masculine (Trauth et al., 2016; Trauth et al., 2010) by both men and women (Trauth & Quesenberry, 2006). Instead, we find that technology provides an equalizing effect whereby the mere presence of technology usage causes men and women to be viewed based on their performance alone, a concept that has recently been found with regard to women and IT promotions (Langer et al., 2020). Our research extends that of previous scholars in the area of impression formation with regard to gender and technology competence by answering the call for a richer informational context (Heilman et al., 2019) through investigation of the active use of technology.

## 2 Background

## 2.1 Impression Formation

The research examining impression formation spans multiple disciplines and contexts, generally focusing on how the physical and behavioral information presented by a target individual is interpreted and used by an observer to develop impressions of the target (Sherman et al., 2007). In an early work, Asch (1946) demonstrated how individuals utilize specific information to form initial impressions regarding the personality of the target individual. Early research also found that observers integrate trait or behavioral information into strong dispositional impressions of the targets that they evaluate (Anderson, 1966; Asch, 1946).

Impression formation (Reynolds & Oakes, 2000; Rudman, 1998) occurs in most social contexts (Ellemers et al., 2002), but one stream of research has focused on the formation of stereotypes and impressions in the context of observers evaluating targets in situations where these parties do not directly interact (i.e., the observers are passive participants). Within this area, two perspectives have gained prominence (Reynolds & Oakes, 2000): (1) the attentional resources perspective (ARC) (Brewer, 1998; Fiske et al., 1999; Fiske & Neuberg, 1990) and (2) self-categorization theory (SCT) (Oakes et al., 1994). Research in this area has focused on identifying the factors that influence the development of impressions and whether observers interpret specific versus aggregate characteristics of the individual being observed (Neuberg & Fiske, 1987).

The attentional resources perspective, as represented by the continuum model (Fiske & Neuberg, 1990) and dual-process model (Brewer, 1998), is based on the theory that observers will attempt to minimize the cognitive effort expended to develop conclusions about a target whenever possible (Bodenhausen & Lichtenstein, 1987); thus, observers will tend to use a category-based heuristic approach (i.e., stereotypes) (Bodenhausen & Wyer, 1985) to draw conclusions about the target (Macrae et al., 1994). This categorization process is often spontaneously enacted, and hard to inhibit (Macrae et al., 1994), through the activation of a stereotype in response to the observation of a particular stimulus characteristic (e.g., gender or other social categories) (Devine, 1989; Greenwald & Banaji, 1995). Nevertheless, while stereotype-consistent information is more extensively processed (Bodenhausen, 1988), many times it will not be possible for an observer to place a target into a specific, predefined category. For example, a target may not easily fit into a category, or the observer may have motivation to seek out additional information about the target. The motivation to evaluate a target on an attribute-by-attribute basis may come from a number of factors, such as the observer’s interdependence with the other party (either cooperative or competitive interdependence) or existing goals that emphasize accuracy in the assessment of the target (Moskowitz, 1993; Reynolds & Oakes, 2000). When motivation is present, the observer engages in a process of initial categorization, recategorization, and categorization by attributes. As this process unfolds, refined impressions are formed through the consideration of individual attributes and the integration of these attributes (i.e., piecemeal integration or personalization) (Brewer, 1998; Fiske & Neuberg, 1990).

Self-categorization theory focuses on group membership and the perceived relationship between the observer and the target (Hogg & Terry, 2000; Reynolds & Oakes, 1999). Like ARC, SCT predicts that impressions will be formed using categorizations, attributes, or a combination thereof. However, SCT argues that the impetus leading to attribute-based impression formation involves perceptions of group association (Perdue et al., 1990). Three factors mediate the target categorization process (1) perceiver readiness (i.e., the perception of the perceiver is framed by goals, needs, and the context of the perceiver), (2) comparative fit (i.e., the way a target is grouped as being within or outside of a particular group), and (3) normative fit (i.e., the attributes used to identify and classify groups have certain values, direction, and meaning that influence the categorization process) (Reynolds & Oakes, 1999). Comparative fit is especially relevant in stereotype activation when targets are seen as being outside of the observer’s group based on certain attributes (Reynolds et al., 2001; Turner & Reynolds, 2008). This process is moderated by the way perceivers categorize themselves by framing the observations of others based on the personal and social identity that they assign to themselves (Reynolds & Oakes, 1999). During this process, observers use normative fit values to assign relative weights to the attributes being considered during impression formation and, as a result, different attributes may become more or less salient in different contexts (Oakes et al., 1991). For example, high value may be assigned to the target’s features (e.g., gender, etc.), actions taken by the target (e.g., skill level demonstrated during a task), or the content of the target’s message (e.g., negative or positive comments). While SCT argues that these values are relative, research has shown that certain characteristics have a relatively consistent influence in similar contexts. For example, research has shown that the nature of what the target says in relation to what the target is expected to say can more strongly affect impressions (Fiske, 1980; Jones & McGillis, 1976; Skowronski & Carlston, 1989). For example, statements that are non-normative receive greater weight in impression formation and often lead to more extreme impressions (Fiske, 1980; Jones & McGillis, 1976; Skowronski & Carlston, 1987, 1989). Furthermore, the context in which the statement is made will influence perceptions; targets giving information that is consistent with the context are perceived more favorably than targets giving information that is either noncontextual or contextual but not consistent with expectations (Jones & Goethals, 1987).

In addition to ARC and SCT, two other theories provide important insights into the process of impression formation: Expectations states theory (EST) (Berger et al., 1974) and status characteristics theory (SChT) (Berger et al., 1986) are useful as a theoretical foundation for further understanding the cognitive processes associated with the salience and valence of cues and the influence of these evaluations on impression formation. EST proposes that individual social actors will develop expectations about the capacity of other actors to behave or perform in a given task or context (Correll & Ridgeway, 2006). The two primary factors influencing impression formation posited by EST are the observer’s assessment of the target’s power and the target’s prestige. EST suggests that perceived differences from the observer’s expectations in one or both of these variables will strongly influence perceptions (Wagner & Berger, 2002). SChT extends EST and focuses on how status characteristics are defined and interpreted by observers (Berger et al., 1986). For example, exhibited characteristics include indicative cues, which involve explicit expressions or claims made by a social actor (e.g., I am the leader), and task cues, which involve information about task-specific knowledge, skills, or capabilities (e.g., fluency in completing a specific task). Inferred characteristics include expressive cues, which involve external symbols or behaviors that are used to infer status (e.g., style of dress), and categorical cues, which incorporate information about group or social affiliation (e.g., speech, dialect, or normative behavior) (Berger et al., 1986; Wagner & Berger, 1993). These status cues are particularly relevant when social actors are not familiar with each other and are not similar with respect to salient external characteristics. Research has shown that expectations are developed based on these status cues unless there is evidence garnered from other cues that demonstrates that the status cue is not relevant in the particular context (Wagner & Berger, 2002).

EST also provides a useful theoretical framework for defining the factors that influence impression formation, providing a theoretical basis for defining and categorizing cues and understanding the processes used by observers to develop impressions of targets. Often, categorical cues such as gender are immediately obvious to observers and are typically the first cues used in the formation of impressions. These cues typically interact with exhibited behaviors such as task performance or statements made by the target as the observer has time to develop and refine their impression based on these behaviors. The category/behavior linkage can be either confirmatory (i.e., the “expert” demonstrates “expertise”) or disconfirmatory (i.e., the “expert” behaves incompetently). When the linkage is confirmatory, the observer’s category-based impressions are activated; however, when the linkages are disconfirmed, the observer is forced to form an attribute-based, more nuanced evaluation of the target.

Taken together, these theories describe two overarching processes of fit and misfit of the impression formation process. This fit/misfit deals explicitly with stereotypes, specifically stereotypes held pertaining to women and their use/competence regarding technology. Based on the background research, our research assumes that these stereotypes cause women to be viewed less favorably as compared to their male counterparts pertaining to this technology use/competence. Figure 1 shows a synthesized model of impression formation for these similar theories. Overall, an observer first goes through a process of fitting the target individual by gleaning category cue information, categorizing this information, and then designating stereotypes based on those held for the group by the observer for categorymatching information. Conversely, for category cues that disconfirm the categorization process, this mismatch leads to further evaluation by the observer, whereby other information is identified and then a further evaluation of misfit is conducted where misfit is identified as consisting of the target message, skills, and/or competence that misalign with the beliefs of the observer. This fit/misfit process provides the basis for this research. First, gender stereotypes are fit to the target as identified by the observer. Next, a message is conveyed or actions are performed demonstrating competence with technology skills that are designed to stimulate interesting information to provoke further evaluation by the observer.

## 2.2 Gender and Technology

Considerable research has been conducted examining the role that gender plays in influencing the impression formation process (Carli, 1990, 1991; Granié & Papafava, 2011; Kriwy et al., 2013; Nieva & Gutek, 1980; Pugh & Wahrman, 1983; Rudman, 1998; Wagner & Berger, 2002). Much of this research has examined how gender leads to stereotype activation as well as how gender influences the way that information is processed by the observer in relation to the gender of the information source. The gender of the target is a primary influence on impression formation, as it represents a cue that activates a number of assumptions, stereotypes, and expectations on the part of the observer. Previous research has shown a strong categorization of sex-typed standards of desirable behavior for men and women (Bem, 1974), where individuals are evaluated on the basis of sex-aligned types based on a gender schema (Bem, 1981). This gender “lens” shapes perceptions by imposing malefemale attributes on various aspects of society (Bem, 2008) based on male and female roles (Bem & Lewis, 1975). This sex-based typing causes individuals to process information in terms of a gender-based schema (Bem, 1981) and causes psychological discomfort when individuals view behavior not in line with this schema (Bem & Lenney, 1976). Gender is therefore utilized by the observer to fit the target in a stereotypical gender categorization. Given the overwhelming desire to fit individuals within this gender categorization, highly interesting or relevant information must be observed of the target in order for further evaluation of misfit by the observer of the target to be conducted.

One piece of perceived relevant information is the role of IT with regard to the target, where IT provides interesting information when paired with the fit of the target to a specified gender by the observer, specifically as this relates to women. Most scholars agree that a structural stereotype exists within Western culture that discourages women from pursuing careers in IT and further situates both men’s and women’s senses of their appropriateness in the IT workplace (c.f. Burke et al., 2006; Lohan & Faulkner, 2004; Trauth, Quesenberry, & Yeo, 2008). Thus, when impressions are formed (by both women and men) of women and men interacting with technology, a set of very complex social and cultural stereotypes are activated. The simplest of these stereotypes are expressed as negative expectations about the potential technological performance of women, resulting from culturally informed beliefs about the efficacy of the different sexes. Research has repeatedly demonstrated that women believe themselves to have lower IT efficacy/capability compared to men, and that men hold the same types of beliefs (c.f. Beyer, 2008; Burke et al., 2006; Whitley, 1997). While some of these stereotype effects may be moderated in the face of demonstrated competence, they remain an important subtext within organizational contexts (Wattal et al., 2010), such as teams (Chai et al., 2011; He et al., 2007; Maruping & Magni, 2012) and personal narratives of individual’s evaluations of others (Liu et al., 2001). Morley (2004) provides evidence that negative stereotypes related to women and information technology come from older cultural stereotypes that question the general technical skills of women, which indicates that these stereotypes are substantially entrenched and powerful. But however entrenched these attitudes that are derived from the general women/technology stereotype, those that relate to women and information technology specifically are more nuanced and may be evolving. Trauth, et al. (2016) note that expectations of gender and IT capability are not a simple male/female dichotomy, but differ across gender × ethnic associations. Further, Trauth et al (2010) note that attitudes toward gender competencies have moderated within the millennial generation; thus, between these studies, we see a stereotype set that is perhaps in flux.

Overall, the role of women in IT has been shown to be understudied (Harris & Wilkinson, 2004; Wilson, 2004), and a substantial under-representation of women in both IT work and in preparatory education has been equally well documented both in the United States and overseas (Burke et al., 2006; Cooper & Weaver, 2003; Robertson et al., 2001). Benamati & Rajkumar (2013) note that less than 30% of IT graduates in the US are female, meaning that the future pipeline will not be changing the profile of women in the IT workforce. This further perpetuates the impression of a mismatch of women and IT, thereby substantiating observer views of the typical stereotype of women. Given this, further research is needed to better understand the interplay of gender stereotypes and IT.

## 3 Overview of Research

We conduct two separate studies to test our hypotheses. Conducting two studies allows us to examine the hypothesized relationships uniquely with different controls and contextual factors and refine our understanding of the relationship between technology and gender. Further, given the unique role of technology in the context of impression formation, the use of a two-study approach allows us to confirm our thinking using data from multiple samples, which results in more robust and generalizable findings. The two studies and their hypotheses are presented in detail below while the Appendix provides an extended analysis of the measurement development. Table 1 provides definitions of the terms utilized in this research.

## 3.1 Study 1

Study 1 tests an omnibus model by examining the impact of technology, message sentiment, and gender on impressions of the target formed by subject observers. To examine our research question, we manipulate three variables that are expected to influence impression formation: the gender of the target, the sentiment of the message presented by the target, and technology instantiation by the target.

## 3.1.1 Hypotheses

Our background section paints a picture of impression formation as dependent on various extenuating factors. First, the message sentiment (e.g., positive or negative information about the technology) of the target individual can affect viewer impressions of the individual, especially when this content is nonnormative in nature for the given context (Fiske, 1980; Jones & McGillis, 1976; Skowronski & Carlston, 1989). Also, this message sentiment affects impressions of a target based on the gender of that target (Abramson et al., 1977; Carli, 1991; Nieva & Gutek, 1980; Pugh & Wahrman, 1985). This research extends previous research by adding technology presence as a third factor by investigating the influence of technology, message sentiment, and target gender on the impressions individuals form of the target.

Prior research indicates that negative information provided by a male actor will cause observers to consider the target to be more powerful, forceful, and in control (Foschi, 1996; Nunamaker et al., 2011). Thus, a male target who engages in forceful comments will be viewed more favorably than a female target who makes similarly forceful comments. Conversely, women are perceived more favorably when they are less assertive; thus, when a woman makes supportive statements rather than negative statements, she will be viewed more favorably (Abramson et al., 1977; Carli, 1991; Nieva & Gutek, 1980; Pugh & Wahrman, 1985). Taken together, these expectations suggest a clear interaction between the gender of the target and the message sentiment (favorable/unfavorable information being discussed).

The literature also indicates that the utilization of technology by a man is seen as more consistent with stereotypical roles for men than the utilization of technology by a woman (Trauth et al., 2016; Trauth et al., 2010; Trauth & Quesenberry, 2006). A man interacting with technology is a consistent fit with observers’ stereotypes about men in general and results in observer impressions that are the product of simple categorizations. However, when subjects are presented with a female target that is utilizing technology, this violation of the stereotype (i.e., women are uncomfortable/less competent with technology) will inhibit the observers’ simple fit categorization and will force observers to make idiosyncratic misfit evaluations of the target (for a detailed explanation of this process of stereotype inhibition, see Bodenhausen & Macrae, 1998). Further, since the inhibition is associated with gender categorization, the presence of technology will weaken other gender categorizations as well, causing greater evaluation through impression formation misfit.

These arguments, considered together, indicate that an interaction exists between the three variables in this study. Specifically, technology nonpresence will cause men to be held to a different standard than women (i.e. simple fit stereotype categorization by observers involving gender and forceful statements), but technology presence will cause both men and women to be evaluated based on the message sentiment that they are presenting, irrespective of the gender × message sentiment expectations (i.e. technology presence forces evaluation of misfit by observers such that gender stereotypes are dismissed and message sentiment is evaluated independently).

H1: Technology moderates the effect of target gender and message sentiment on impression formation (three-way interaction of technology, gender, and message sentiment).

H1a: Absent the presence of technology, impressions of a target individual are dependent on an interaction of the target gender and message sentiment (simple two-way interaction).

H1a.i: When technology is not present and a negative message is presented, female targets will be viewed less favorably than their male counterparts (simple-simple main effect).

H1a.ii: When technology is not present and a positive message is presented, impressions of female and male targets will not differ (simple-simple main effect).

H1b: When technology is present, impressions of the target individual are dependent on the sentiment of the message (simple main effect—i.e., no simple two-way interaction with gender).

## 3.1.2 Procedure

A 2×2×2 full factorial experimental design with the independent variables of technology use, message sentiment, and sex of the target was utilized for the study. Given the dichotomous nature of the independent variables, an experimental manipulation provided a valid mechanism to evaluate differences between groups. Second, the random assignment of an experiment helps to nullify extraneous effects that could otherwise distort results (e.g., individuals with high/low numbers of female professors, advisors, friends, etc.). Finally, given our research question asks about the impact of the sex and exhibited technologyrelated behaviors of a target on impressions formed by observers, the experiment allows us to examine the causal impact, thereby strengthening our research outcomes.

The technology manipulation included two levels: the target either read information from a handheld smart device or from a paper document (i.e., the no-smart device manipulation). When using technology, the targets utilized the same handheld smart device. The sex manipulation was implemented by having either a male or a female target read the article. Both targets were young-looking students who were of the same general appearance, similar speaking ability, of about the same height and build, and approximately the same age. These similarities among the targets in both Study 1 and Study 2 were independently reviewed and confirmed by a separate panel. Furthermore, a more rigorous post hoc examination was run to help rule out alternative explanations of the differences observed between the targets under study—outside gender and the treatments of message sentiment and technology. This study consisted of subjects viewing still photographs of each of the targets with neutral facial expressions and without technology present. The subjects then answered four questions for each target regarding the target’s perceived level on each of the four impression categories under study (i.e., character, power, sociability, intelligence) on a five-point scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree (these categories are described in greater detail in the primary prose of the manuscript). For the primary two studies conducted for this research, we hypothesize that these subjects are of the same general appearance, height, build, and age; thus, when technology and message sentiment are removed from the situational setting, no significant difference should be seen between the male and female subjects in either study one or study two. We wanted to be certain that no difference would be seen between the negative and positive ends of the measurement scale for each of the five categories, which would conservatively mean there would be no significant difference between somewhat disagree and somewhat agree on the five-point scale. The distance between these two points on the scale would be at least 2, so we conservatively chose a confidence interval width for our test of 1.5 to rule out any differences between the subjects. A 2×2 within-subjects full factorial ANOVA was run where each subject saw both the man and the woman from Study 1 and Study 2. The sample size requirement needed to find a confidence interval width of 1.5 given the highest variance and lowest correlations among the scores was determined (see formula below) <sup>3</sup> (Bonett, 2022). Linear contrasts were used for a full factorial when determining the sample size as a conservative estimate, even though we were only interested in the simple main effects of male vs. female for each of the studies separately (i.e., we did not wish to consider effects between Study 1 and Study 2). Given that we were examining two simultaneous simple main-effect tests, we used a reverse Bonferroni adjustment so we could claim that the results were not significant at p > 0.1 (providing a more strenuous test of nonsignificance). Our results show that the subjects did not perceive a significant difference between the male and female targets in any of the four categories for either Study 1 or Study 2. This suggests that without message sentiment and technology present, the male and female subjects were perceived as not significantly different in the categories of interest; therefore, the differences found in Study 1 and Study 2 between men and women must be a result of the contextual treatment effects of technology and message sentiment.

The informational message sentiment manipulation was implemented by having the targets read an article that either had a positive message sentiment toward handheld smart device use or a negative message sentiment (Fiske, 1980; Skowronski & Carlston, 1989). The article described a study of Japanese handheld smart device usage in either a positive or negative light, depending on the manipulation. Japanese handheld smart device usage was used, given the widely held stereotype that the Japanese are thought to be leaders in handheld technology (Japan America Society of Southern California, 2009), which would cause cognitive dissonance in the subjects based on the disparity between the Japanese persona of high technological competence and the negative information regarding this use. Manipulation check questions showed that subjects correctly identified the technology used by the target, and the message sentiment produced a statistically significant difference in perceptions of the content of the informational article (p < 0.001).

The fictitious article used to present the message sentiment was adapted from an actual article about cell phone use in Japan—the story was recast in the context of handheld smart device usage among Japanese users. A positive version of the article included information that reported on favorable attitudes that Japanese users had regarding their use of a handheld smart device, its positive impact on their productivity, and its ease of use. Facts and figures used in the article included numeric results from surveys of users, anecdotal reports, and similar pieces of information. For the negative message sentiment, the same basic information was presented; however, the adjectives and adverbs describing Japanese user experiences were presented negatively. All of the numeric descriptors reported in the article remained the same; the negative versus positive tone was all that was changed. For example, in the positive version of the story a statement such as the following was made: “65% of the respondents under 20 years of age voiced satisfaction with connecting to a website using their handheld smart device.” In the negative version of the story a statement such as the following was made: “65% of the respondents under 20 years of age voiced dissatisfaction with connecting to a website using their handheld smart device.”

The same targets were used throughout the study by videotaping each target reading the article in each treatment manipulation and then having subjects view the recorded video of the target on a large projector at the front of the room. The reading task in the technology-present condition resembles a typical use of technology where a user finds information using their device and then relays this information to another user. The use of a recorded presentation provided consistency across research sessions by making sure the targets performed exactly the same in each treatment manipulation. The targets were instructed to read the scripted materials consistently across treatment conditions. To reduce random error from uncontrolled factors, identical rooms were used for each treatment recording and the targets wore neutralcolored shirts and blue jeans when making the presentations.

Subjects consisted of volunteers from undergraduate accounting or management information systems (MIS) courses at a large midwestern university; over 90% of the subjects were of US nationality. Subjects were recruited in classes and were offered the opportunity to participate in this exercise as an alternative to another assignment outside of class. When subjects arrived at the room, a nontarget research assistant greeted them, took attendance, and handed out a questionnaire. Students were randomly assigned to each treatment based on the session for which they signed up. The number of subjects per session varied between 10 and 15 and multiple sessions were used to collect data for each treatment cell. Subjects were told in the introduction that they would be asked to answer several questions about handheld smart devices after information about handheld smart devices was read to them. This questionnaire was presented as the main purpose of the research in order to reduce the likelihood that subjects would identify the true nature of the study (i.e., impression formation).<sup>4</sup> Following the questionnaire, subjects were asked to rate the target on 34 adjective questions related to the individual characteristics of the target. The questionnaire specifically asked subjects to rate their impressions of the person who read the article (so that they would not confuse the target with the other research assistant). Following completion of the impression formation questionnaire, subjects were asked to provide demographic information and information about their recollections related to the content of the article.

## 3.1.4 Discussion

Based on substantial gender research, hypothesis H1 predicted that there would be a three-way interaction between the gender of the target and the message sentiment presented in the article dependent on technology use. The results suggest that this is the case, and that the traditional gender/message sentiment interaction is displayed for character, power, and intelligence, but only when technology is not present (supporting H1, H1a, H1a.i, and H1a.ii). Conversely, this gender/message sentiment interaction is not displayed when technology is present, but instead a main effect of message sentiment is present for all variables, such that negative statements are perceived negatively and positive statements positively, regardless of gender (supporting H1b). Conversely, a main effect of gender is only displayed for two of the four variables. This surprising finding implies that technology may have a partial “field-leveling” effect, such that individuals are not viewed based on their gender but only on the content of their message.

## 3.2 Study 2

Study 2 holds technology use constant, but amplifies the interaction between the male/female targets and message sentiment by having them succeed or fail (i.e., competence-based sentiment) at an operational task using the technology (while observers form their impressions).

Study 2 provides a more nuanced insight into the impression formation of users of technology. Specifically, the motivation for the design of Study 2 is to better understand the relationship between technology and the gender of the target. The results from Study 1 and an inspection of the pattern of the results from Study 1 suggests that technology has a differentiating effect on the impressions of individuals. In line with previous research, message sentiment causes perceptions about female and male targets to differ when technology is not present, but when technology is present gender does not appear to interact with message sentiment, leaving a significant main effect of message sentiment, demonstrating support of our hypotheses of stereotype misfit. An important consideration in interpreting the results of Study 1 is that the message sentiment condition was presented in a relatively passive manner; that is, the condition was introduced by having the target read an article with either positive or negative information from a handheld smart device, which means that the message sentiment was secondhand in nature and not presented through firsthand demonstration of the target’s level of competency with the technology, which is more typical within an organizational setting. As a result, in Study 2, we kept technology use constant (i.e., all manipulations involve technology presence) but enhanced the message sentiment manipulation by focusing our attention on more closely associating the target and their competence with technology while keeping technology presence constant (i.e., the same technology is used, a male/female manipulation is still used, and the message sentiment manipulation is enhanced). Specifically, we wished to examine how message sentiment displayed through the success or failure of use (enacted competence) of the target when using the technology influences impressions formed about the target. In summary, given the interesting results of Study 1 when technology is present, we removed the no-technology manipulation by only looking at scenarios where technology is present, kept the gender manipulation, and enhanced the message sentiment manipulation through a competence-based message sentiment.

## 3.2.1 Hypotheses

Study 1’s hypotheses test a present/not present condition of technology vis-à-vis the male and female targets. In Study 2, we wanted to make the strongest possible test of H1b, to determine whether technology presence completely thwarts the gender stereotype; thus, we formed Study 2 hypotheses to address male and female targets directly engaging a handheld smart device in a manner that indicates their level of mastery with the technology to actively convey message sentiment.

As in H1 (and Study 1) we acknowledge that the literature overwhelmingly indicates that there is a stereotype against women using technology, in the sense that they are seen as less efficacious in IT environments. However, as in H1, we believe that stereotype disruptions cause the impression-forming subject to step beyond stereotypes and evaluate the misfit of the target, free of its effects. Therefore, we anticipate that message sentiment through demonstration of failure or success using an enacted demonstration will influence user perceptions positively or negatively toward the target under study and thereby inhibit the stereotype (Bodenhausen & Macrae, 1998).

As noted above, women and men are held to different standards with regard to how they are expected to use and master technology (Foschi, 1996). Our belief, as articulated in Hypothesis 1, is that these expectations, when confounded by observation of a specific target, set in motion a process that thwarts the gender categorization when a woman is using technology. While gender/technology stereotypes may exist for the subject in the abstract, our belief is that they can be inhibited when technology use by a woman is viewed. Study 2 is designed to test the effect predicted in H1b at a granular level. Stereotype literature would argue that in abstract evaluations (subjects being asked how men and women are likely to perform) men will be judged more harshly when they fail to demonstrate technological competence, while women demonstrating the same incompetence will be less harshly evaluated (because expectations are lower). Again, in an abstract sense, when both genders succeed with technology, men would be expected to have a more positive response from observers, due to the observers’ expectations that men should demonstrate mastery coupled with the observers’ lack of a category to recognize women’s technological mastery to their benefit. We believe that these latent categorization stereotypes are vulnerable to inhibition when the subject is faced with confounding confrontation in the case of a specific target evaluation. When message sentiment conveyed through the demonstration of each target’s mastery or nonmastery of a technology is specifically enacted for observers, we expect that the stereotype will be inhibited and that observers will be forced to make idiosyncratic evaluations of misfit regarding the target, according to the following hypothesis:

H2: When message sentiment conveyed through the demonstration of each target’s mastery or nonmastery of a technology is specifically enacted for observers, impressions of the target individual will be independent of an interaction of the target’s gender and be solely based on the message sentiment conveyed through the success/failure of the target individual’s technology use (simple main effect).

## 3.2.2 Procedure

A 2×2 full factorial experimental design was utilized for the study, with the independent variables of message sentiment (through enacted competence) and target gender. The gender manipulation was implemented by having either a male or a female target read the article. Both targets were young-looking students who were of the same general appearance, similar speaking ability, of about the same height and build, and approximately the same age. The message sentiment manipulation was implemented by having the target read a new article (not the same as Study 1) where the target either correctly operated the handheld smart device during the presentation (positive message sentiment through successful use) or made mistakes with the technology and failed in the completion of tasks while using the handheld smart device (negative message sentiment through failure use). The targets were well rehearsed for both the success and failure condition and implemented each manipulation correctly as verified by a research assistant. Observers consisted of a new sample of subjects gathered using the same population pool as in Study 1, but in a subsequent semester. Procedures for data gathering also mirrored those used in Study 1.

## 3.2.3 Results

A total of 171 subjects participated in the research and provided usable data. To examine the overall model, we analyzed the data using MANOVA, given the interrelatedness of the dependent variables. The results show that the overall model is not significant for the two-way interaction of message sentiment competence and gender $( W i l k s = 0 . 9 7 , F _ { ( 4 , 1 6 4 ) } = 1 . 3 1 , p = 0 . 2 7 ,$ ) with no main effect of gender $( W i l k s = 0 . 9 6 , F _ { ( 4 , 1 6 4 ) } = 1 . 8 3$ $p = 0 . 1 3 )$ but a significant main effect of message sentiment through enacted competence $( W i l k s = 0 . 8 0$

$F _ { ( 4 , 1 6 4 ) } = 1 0 . 1 3 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ with a high effect size (partial $\mathsf { e t a } ^ { 2 } \ = \ 0 . 2 0 )$ . Looking at the individual variables, message sentiment has a significant effect on character $( \beta = 0 . 6 6 , t = 4 . 7 1 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , power $( \beta = 0 . 2 9 , t = 1 . 8 0$ $p { = } 0 . 0 4 )$ , and intelligence $( \beta = 0 . 8 4 , t = 5 . 3 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ with target impressions lower when the target demonstrated failure with the technology as compared to success, regardless of gender, largely supporting H2. Sociability produced a nonsignificant result $( \beta = 0 . 2 2$ $t = 1 . 4 1 , p = 0 . 0 8 )$

These results again are highly at odds with previous research that says success or failure at completing a technology-based task is viewed differently when the target is female as compared to male. While this nonsignificance may be questioned, a post hoc analysis was run to determine the power of the analysis to predict the interaction. Given the number of groups and dependent variables, our analysis is capable of predicting a small $( \mathbf { f } ^ { 2 } ~ = ~ 0 . 0 5 )$ effect size for the interaction term with 0.85 power; therefore, if gender did have an effect, it would have shown up, given the substantial power to find even a small effect size if it were present. Furthermore, credibility is also shown in the findings, mimicking those in Study 1 where technology is present, as these results show that the individual is viewed more favorably when they successfully complete a task (i.e. positive message sentiment) regardless of their gender, even when displaying enacted competence of using a technology device.

An additional post hoc analysis was run to help provide further credence to the results. One of the primary suppositions of this research is that the presence of technology provides a “field-leveling” effect by thwarting inherent gender-based stereotypes of subjects with regard to men and women and their technology use. The background research points to this stereotype whereby women are viewed as less capable with regard to their use of technology as compared to men. While our experimental design is intended to drown out any other spurious effects beyond the independent variables of interest, the argument can be made that while our results support this stereotype mitigation, stereotypes may not be the specific mechanism present in the subjects. To investigate this, a further analysis was run to verify that these stereotypes did indeed exist in the subjects. Subjects were asked to evaluate the abilities of men and women by estimating the percentage of male and female undergraduate students who possessed certain technology-related skills. Skills-based questions were taken from research on technology-related selfefficacy (Murphy et al., 1989), and can be viewed in the Appendix. A within-subjects ANCOVA was run to evaluate the average answer for men as compared to women. Also included in the analysis were covariates of the two manipulations in the study (target gender, message sentiment), the interaction of these manipulations, as well as the gender of the subject performing the evaluation. The results show a significant difference in the views of subjects between the abilities of men and women regarding technology $( \beta = - 1 5 . 4 2 , t = - 1 9 . 3 4 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , with the average view of men’s abilities with technology (mean = 72) as compared to women’s abilities $( m e a n = 5 6 )$ being at least 14 percent higher (CI[-13.84,-16.99]). It should be noted that these questions were asked after the subjects had been exposed to the study. While these questions could have been asked a priori, the potential for priming subjects to the nature of the study was deemed too highly detrimental. Given this, as the subjects had already been exposed to men or women using technology per their specific manipulation, this provides a very conservative estimate of the extent of these stereotypes. Furthermore, the manipulations of the study were included as covariates in the analysis to account for these experimental manipulations. Even after this, subjects showed a highly significant stereotype between men and women regarding technology use. This provides greater credibility that these stereotypes do exist and that our findings show the ability of technology to thwart these stereotypes.

## 4 General Discussion

Issues regarding women and technology have been researched for decades, finding deeply entrenched stereotypes of IT as a field that is welcoming for men but not women (Trauth et al., 2016; Trauth et al., 2010; Trauth & Quesenberry, 2006). IT is seen as sex-typed based on desirable behavior for men and women (Bem, 1974, 1981) whereby the processing of gender-based information causes psychological discomfort when individuals view women interacting with IT, which has a masculine attribute imposed by society (Bem, 2008; Bem & Lenney, 1976). Furthermore, skills in using IT are also seen as masculine, with these stereotypes espoused by both men and women alike (Trauth et al., 2016; Trauth et al., 2010; Trauth & Quesenberry, 2006). Overall, research shows that IT is viewed as a man’s field with little room for alleviation of these stereotypes.

Pleasantly, results from this study find one solution for the problem of these entrenched stereotypes regarding women and IT; namely, the active use of technology by female users. The results of two separate studies demonstrate that the normal process of impression formation is drastically altered by the target’s use of technology. Study 1 shows that the presence of technology moderates the relationship between gender and message sentiment, creating a three-way interaction effect. When investigating the simple twoway interaction effects, we see that when technology is not present, the message content interacts with the gender of the presenter, as predicted by gender stereotype literature. Specifically, men tend to be judged more favorably when making negative comments and more negatively when making positive comments. Women, on the other hand, are evaluated within a much narrower range of favorability, regardless of whether they are speaking negatively or positively, and (in contrast to men) are perceived favorably when making positive comments and unfavorably when making negative ones. However, when technology is present, our study demonstrates that women and men are both evaluated according to the same message/favorability standards; a negative message yields a negative impression and a positive message yields a favorable one. In other words, we show that the gender-based differentiation of the message/favorability impact is subverted by the presence of technology.

Study 2 was meant to more thoroughly test the effects seen in Study 1. In Study 1, the technology is present for the targets, but it is not a focus of the activity (i.e., a passive message sentiment); in Study 2, we made the technology use of the target a highly focused part of the presentation (i.e., message sentiment whereby the targets either succeed or fail in their presentation using a handheld smart device). The results show that in this technology-present condition, the target is evaluated based solely on their success or failure with the technology, with gender having no moderating or main effect on the impressions formed of the targets. These results further demonstrate that when technology is in use, the gender of the target is no longer a factor in the subject impressions of them, and impressions are only a function of the content of the message. Table 2 provides an overview of the hypotheses and our findings.

In summary, these results indicate that the stereotype effect for gender can be inhibited. Without technology, we argue that the stereotype effect is such that it obviates a rational evaluation of the target by the observer beyond the mere fit of the target within a specific gender stereotype whereby the differential stereotype that evaluates men and women has its basis only in archaic attitudes about the sexes. The disappearance of the stereotype effect in the technology-present condition in Study 1, and the failure of the stereotype to appear in technologycentered Study 2, indicates that a target’s use of technology disrupts the simple stereotype-driven categorization process and causes the observer to further evaluate the misfit of the target’s specific performance in a given context.

We suspect that this effect is the result of one of two processes: First, it is possible that the technology distracts sufficiently from the gender cues of the target to inhibit them; second, the technology may modify the gender cues in such a way as to effectively inhibit them. In the first case, the technology presence is a simple distraction, making the environment more complex and thus prone to a nuanced misfit evaluation. The gender of the target is proportionally reduced in the cue set of the evaluator, and stereotypes associated with gender are obviated when the complexity demands a nuanced misfit evaluation. In our second possible process, the presence of the technology directly moderates the gender cue itself, subverting both male and female stereotypes as they become male and female technology users, which is a new combination that does not activate a traditional gender stereotype. Given that technology is generally held to be a masculine domain in the broader cultural narrative (Trauth et al., 2016; Trauth et al., 2010), female targets’ use of technology may effectively neutralize their gender cues, or at least create enough dissonance that the subject moves on to more dependable gender-free cues. This is compatible with dual-process models of attitude change posited by the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) (Petty & Cacioppo, 1981, 1986) and the heuristic-systematic model (HSM) (Chaiken, 1987). When confronted with a target who is speaking about a technology in the presence of no heuristic cue (i.e., physical technology), individuals are unmotivated to expend cognitive resources and therefore revert to low-effort persuasion using innate stereotypes that fit each gender (Taylor, 1981). Conversely, when technology is included as a situational factor, an observer’s confidence in their personal knowledge about the situation decreases (Chaiken et al., 1989), leading to the individual’s personal expectations being disconfirmed, which increases cognitive effort regarding the situation (Maheswaran & Chaiken, 1991). In such circumstances, the observer is presented with a scenario where they engage in a deeper, more nuanced misfit evaluation of the target resulting in perceptions that align with actions presented by the targets.

The one evaluation variable that worked differently in our study is sociability. Sociability did not reveal a three-way interaction in Study 1, and in Study 2 it was not affected by either the gender of the target nor the targets’ success or failure to correct their handheld smart device’s problem. In Study 1, sociability simply seemed to lack strong evaluative attention in the observer. In Study 2 the success or failure of the target had no effect on the sociability perceptions of the observers (similar to Study 1). In Study 1, we believe that one or both of two processes occurred. First, previous research has attributed roles to women of being more social, warm, and nurturing (Diekman & Eagly, 2000), interacting better in small groups (Rose & Rudolph, 2006), and valuing and being more focused on relationships (Konrad et al., 2000; Pinker, 2008; Tannen, 1990). These same attributes remain in the female set in the millennial study by Trauth et al. (2016). In this light, preexisting perceptual stereotypes about women’s roles (beyond technology) may have overwhelmed any perceptions developed even with exhibited use of technology.

The sociability measure is much more focused on relational factors, compared to the other measures examined in this study, and the observers’ sense of their relationship potential with the female target was thus unaffected by her technology aptitude. In essence, the stereotype of sociability itself is activated irrespective of any dissonance associated with the woman’s technology use. These same factors then affected the male target’s evaluations, as he was expected to be competent, and his technology abilities affected evaluations of his relative attractiveness for social relationships.

Overall, our findings run counter to previous research on stereotypes regarding gender and IT. We identify the active use of technology as an intervention to influence workplace culture (Trauth, 2017) to allow women to be evaluated with regard to their message as opposed to merely in terms of stereotypes related to their gender. Furthermore, we show that women gain the same benefits or suffer the same detriments based on their perceived performance using technology (Langer et al., 2020) as their male counterparts. This provides promising results for women in an increasingly technology-infused workplace and society.

## 4.1 Limitations and Future Research

The study used an undergraduate student population for its subject pool; while student subjects do represent an important user group of personal technologies, their use of stereotypes may be confined by their limited life and work experiences and their stereotypes may be different than those of different age groups. But, since these students will be in the workforce within the next three years, we believe that they are fairly representative of the coming workforce. The study also uses only handheld smart device technologies, which may not represent the impact of the use of larger-scale installed technologies (i.e., presentation systems, desktop units, servers, etc.).

While we believe the present work is an important first step in looking at the impact of technology and technology use on impression formation, we also believe that it suggests a number of interesting future projects. Primary among these is the investigation of these effects with larger technologies, particularly units where the technology itself is less physically apparent to the observer. In these contexts, mastery is more of an abstraction; thus, it would be interesting to see what kinds of signals are important for target evaluations.

Another area for future research is the impact of both target and observer competence, comfort, and interest in IT, as it interacts with gender in the impression formation process. Competence and interest have been studied as important constructs within IT (Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Luse et al., 2014), but this has only been examined in relation to focal individuals. Future research should instead look at the perceived competence of others, as well as how this effect impacts impressions of the target and whether it interacts with the gender of the target.

Impression formation with regard to sociability is also an area worthy of future research. As our research suggests, sociability behaves differently than the other variables in the impression formation process regarding men and women. Future research should aim to better understand the concept of sociability and its relation to impression formation toward men and women in the context of technology use. Finally, future impression formation research in corporate IT areas needs more development of race, ethnicity, etc., in addition to gender, as per Trauth et al (2016).

Another area for future research is the area of misfit within the proposed theoretical model. While technology use provides one avenue for categorization mismatch, other potential mismatch instigators should be investigated. Furthermore, the process following mismatch should also be investigated. Here we investigate message sentiment (both passively and through active competence) as one area affected by the instigating mismatch. Further research should investigate the deliberative and specific recalibrative thinking occurring during the mismatch process.

Finally, another important area for future research is how the current research can be used to recruit more women into technology fields. Currently, there is a large disparity of women within technology fields (Gorbacheva et al., 2019; Serenko & Turel, 2021); interventions are looking at high-level societal changes that can be implemented to affect the perceptions of individuals and external communities pertaining to gender and technology (Wynn, 2020). While these ideas are commendable, our research provides another avenue of action. Technology could be actively infused into organizations in an attempt to cause employees to bypass their innate gender stereotypes, thereby equalizing impressions of both genders due to greater mental processing. This could perhaps create a more hospitable environment to entice female workers.

## 4.2 Implications

Our findings offer important implications for users of technology as well as for anyone interested in understanding how impressions are formed. This research offers a potential organizational intervention (Quesenberry & Trauth, 2012) demonstrating that technology use can have a moderating effect on traditional gender stereotypes, meaning that the increasing proliferation of technology-present situations may well result in less gender stereotyping in the workplace. While we doubt that carrying a hightech device will completely obviate gender stereotypes everywhere, our analysis indicates that they may be moderated in presentation situations where the technology is an important and appropriate tool.

Our research is also promising in that it demonstrates that women who exhibit competence when using technology are held accountable in a manner consistent with men. As a result, women will benefit from increased successful usage, or perceived successful usage, of technology, which could help to alleviate the gender stereotypes that exist for technology use, when seen in an actual evaluative context.

In terms of changing the dynamic of women’s interest in the IT field, education and popular culture need to promote more examples of women who are successful and competent in technology use (Clayton et al., 2012). In education, this means having more female role models demonstrating competence with technology and creating successful technology experiences for all students (in particular, young women). We are starting to see some examples of tech-savvy women in popular culture as well, with a number of popular television dramas featuring women who are masters of computer technology. The combination of more women in technology education combined with increasing positive examples in popular culture will hopefully help to inspire more young women to master IT skills. In turn, technologically successful women can contribute to gradually changing the narrative and eventually the stereotype, creating an additional draw for women considering careers in technology.

## Appendix A: Instrument Development

Instrument development involved two phases: an exploratory factor analysis followed by a confirmatory factor analysis. The exploratory factor analysis was run on data from Study 1 to identify relevant underlying constructs within the data that present themselves as salient characteristics for impression formation. Next, a confirmatory factor analysis was run on data from the second study to verify the factors identified through the exploratory factor analysis. In this section, we describe the instrument development and leave the description of the experimental procedures (task, participants, manipulations, etc.) for the primary manuscript prose.

Various methods, items, and instruments have been developed to help measure impressions formed by subjects toward target individuals. One commonly used method involves subject ratings of targets based on individual descriptive adjectives. For example, subjects rate how strongly they disagree or agree with the word “relaxed” as it applies to the target. Many different word lists have been developed for use in impression formation (Anderson, 1968; Christman & Branson, 1990; McCroskey et al., 1974; Walther, 1993). These instruments vary from an ad hoc list of words (Walther, 1993), to words listed within constructs (Christman & Branson, 1990; McCroskey et al., 1974), and, ultimately, to an omnibus list of 555 different words (Anderson, 1968). While a large list provides an exhaustive mechanism for measuring impression formation, the sheer magnitude of such a list is not tenable for use in this study due to time constraints and the potential for instrument fatigue. Further, we sought to focus the evaluation on adjectives that would reflect on the nature of the treatments’ anticipated effects; that is, perceptions related to competence, intelligence, decisiveness, and similar concepts related to technology use. For this reason, a new instrument is adapted and validated in tandem with the two studies in this research.

The vast word list from the impression formation literature was first narrowed to a shorter list for use in this research. This involved the researchers analyzing the words and adjectives in each list using a number of criteria. First, duplicate words were dropped between the lists as well as antonym words, with only one of the antonym pairs retained (for example responsible and irresponsible). Second, words were examined based on their understandability by the target demographic of the study (i.e., college students). Given the exhaustiveness of the lists as well as the dated nature of some of the words, many adjectives were removed if they were thought to have the potential to be confusing, ambiguous, or misinterpreted. Third, words were analyzed for their contextual parallel to the topics at hand (namely technology and gender). Those adjectives that did not pertain to the focus of the study were dropped. Based on these criteria, 34 words remained for use in the two studies.

For Study 1, an exploratory factor analysis was performed using the selected adjectives. While some of the previous impression formation research has included adjective groupings associated with constructs (Christman & Branson, 1990; McCroskey et al., 1974), much of the prior literature associated with impression formation begins with word lists that are not associated with established factors (Anderson, 1968; Walther, 1993). Additionally, we determined that given the exploratory nature of a study of impression formation associated with technology use by a target user, it would be useful to not pre-define item groupings. Nevertheless, we did select items that we presumed would align with the important concepts associated with impression formation such as competence, intelligence, and similar evaluative concepts.

Exploratory factor analysis provides a mechanism for aggregating items into common factors, which help to better explain the constructs that are affected by impression formation. Principal components analysis was chosen as the extraction method because its primary use is for data reduction as compared to principal axis factoring or maximum likelihood estimation extraction techniques. An oblique rotation method (covaramin) was first utilized to test for high interfactor correlation. Given that no interfactor correlations were above 0.36, this allowed for the use of an orthogona rotation method (equimax), which assumes that the factors are uncorrelated and provides for a simpler interpretation of the factor solution. Equimax rotation provides a conservative compromise between varimax and quartimax rotation methods (Hair et al., 1998). Running the PCA analysis with orthogonal rotation, we find that the factorability of the data is quite good with an overall Kaiser measure of sampling adequacy (MSA) of 0.95 and each of the individual MSA values for each variable well above the 0.7 cutoff (the lowest being 0.87).

With the initial requirement met for factorability, next we determined the number of factors from the data. First, the Kaiser-Guttman rule (Kaiser, 1960) would recommend extracting six factors given that there are six eigenvalues greater than the value of one (see Table A1). The percent variance explained is also quite high for a six-factor solution at 67%. Parallel analysis (Horn, 1965) in tandem with a scree plot (Cattell, 1966) hints at a six-factor solution (see Figure A1). Parallel analysis has been found to work well in simulation studies using data sets with clear factor structure for separating items into factors (Zwick & Velicer, 1986). The eigenvalue line and the randomized parallel analysis line cross between six and seven indicating a six-factor solution as optimal, which also keeps three or more indicators per factor. For this reason, we decided to start with a six-factor solution.

Next, to assess the six-factor solution the residual correlation matrix, communalities, and uniqueness values were analyzed. The residual correlation matrix showed that very few values were close to zero, indicating an adequate solution. Next, the communality values were reviewed. Given that communality values roughly correspond to reliability values, we would like to see these high (above 0.6) and the uniqueness low as these correspond to error variance. Two communality values were found to be below 0.5 and were marked for further analysis in the next round of testing (see Table A2).

Finally, the rotated factor loadings were analyzed and categorized along each factor. Three items were dropped due to not exceeding 0.5 on any of the six factors. Research has suggested that, when conducting an exploratory factor analysis, the cutoff value for rotated factor loadings is greater than or equal to 0.5 with those above this threshold considered practically significant (Hair et al., 1998). Cross-loadings were also examined for each of the items. Previous research has suggested that cross-loadings should be 0.2 less than the highest loading and no secondary loading greater than 0.4 overall (Henson & Roberts, 2006; Matsunaga, 2015; Park et al., 2002). Any item that did not meet these criteria were dropped. Finally, the factors were also given names based on common themes among the items.

To further verify the proposed instrument, a confirmatory factor analysis was run on the data from the second study using the factors and items derived from the exploratory factor analysis run on the data in Study 1. The measurement model was evaluated using multiple fit criteria, specifically the comparative fit index (CFI), the Incremental Fit Index (IFI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Acceptable levels for each include CFI and IFI ≥ 0.90 (adequate) to 0.95 (superior), RMSEA ≤ 0.08 (good) to 0.1 (reasonable), and SRMR ≤ 0.08 (Bearden et al., 1993; Bentler, 1992; Bollen, 1989; Gefen et al., 2000; L. Hu & Bentler, 1999; MacCallum et al., 1996).

The measurement model included the six, reflectively modeled factors from the exploratory factor analysis including all 25 retained items. The first run of the measurement model indicated that the model did not fit the data well: χ<sup>2</sup> (260) = 1453.93, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.83, TLI = 0.80, RMSEA = 0.106, and SRMR = 0.062. Upon further investigation, we found that the Aggressiveness factor did not fit well with two of the three standardized loadings below 0.5. Given that two of the three items were well below the recommended cutoff for a CFA of 0.7 (Hair et al., 1998), the aggressiveness factor was dropped from the analysis. Further, inspection of the parameters also revealed that one item under Professionalism (seriously minded) and two items under character (honest and dependable) were also well below the recommended cutoff of 0.7. Considering content validity, seriously minded was deemed to be a confusing term, so this item was an obvious candidate for removal. Conversely, both honest and dependable seemed to fit well under the concept of character but given the low values and the fact that character had three other items belonging to the factor, both of these items were dropped. Finally, friendly and pleasant were found to have very highly correlated error terms $( \mathrm { M . I . } = 1 8 0 . 9 6 )$ , so only friendly was kept. Upon rerunning the measurement model, the model was found to fit the data well on most of the fit indices $\chi ^ { 2 } \ ( 1 1 \bar { 9 } ) = \bar { 4 } 1 7 . 5 6 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 , \mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 9 4 , \mathrm { T L I } = 0 . 9 2 , \mathrm { R M S E A } = 0 . 0 7 9 .$ , and SRMR $= 0 . 0 4 1$

Upon first glance, the correlation of professionalism with three of the other four constructs seemed high (0.78, 0.77, and 0.77). Previous research has recommended that correlations among dependent variables in a MANOVA fal between 0.3 to about 0.7 (Maxwell, 2001). Given this, greater investigation is warranted into this recommendation. Previous research has suggested that Type 2 errors are affected by the degree of correlation between the dependent variables in a MANOVA test, but research has varied on whether this is the case. Some simulation-based research has shown that the power of the overall test lessens as you get correlations above 0.8 (Finch & French, 2013) while other research has showed that there is no decrease in power at 0.8 except for with data that is highly skewed (Finch, 2005). To test whether the professionalism factor is significantly different from these other constructs (and thereby needed in the model), previous research (Rindskopf & Rose, 1988) has suggested running two measurement models, one with free correlations and one with the correlations between the highly correlated factors set to one. A significant difference between the models would indicate that the constructs are significantly different and are both needed. In order to provide the most conservative test, a p-value of 0.001 was used. When running the model with the correlation between Professionalism and Character set to one, the models were not found to be significantly different at $p = 0 . 0 0 1 \ ( \Delta \chi 2$ $( 1 ) = 9 . 0 8 , \mathrm { p } = 0 . 0 0 3 )$ . Given this, professionalism was dropped from the analyses, as character was statistically the same at $p = 0 . 0 0 1$ . When running models with the other correlations set to one, all the other models were found to be significantly different; therefore, all other constructs were kept. The final items were retained and their standardized loadings are given in Table A3.

The final measurement model was found to fit the data well on most of the fit indices $\chi ^ { 2 } \left( 4 5 \right) = 1 0 7 . 6 4 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1$ , CFI $= 0 . 9 5 , \mathrm { T L I } = 0 . 9 2$ , RMSEA = 0.090, and SRMR = 0.042. The means, standard deviations, Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability, average variance extracted (AVE), and correlations of the measures are shown in Table A4.

Evaluation of the quality of the measurement model included several tests of reliability and validity (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988; Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Reliability was assessed by examining construct reliability (using Cronbach’s coefficient alpha), and composite reliability. All values for Cronbach’s alpha are above the recommended cutoff of 0.7 (Nunnally, 1978), with the lowest value being 0.79. Composite reliability is an index that reflects the impact of error on the measurement scale and is widely used in reliability checks in SEM (Raykov & Grayson, 2003). All the factors have a composite reliability above the recommended cutoff of 0.7 (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988; Bearden et al., 1993; Fornell & Larcker, 1981) indicating high composite reliability for the measurement model.

Both convergent and discriminant construct validity were also tested for the measurement model. Convergent validity can be evaluated using the average variance extracted (AVE), and the factor loadings of the indicators for each factor. AVE, representing the amount of variance a construct captures from its indicators (Chin, 1998), is recommended to be higher than 0.5 to indicate good convergent validity (Chin, 1998; Hu et al., 2004). All factors in the measurement model were found to have AVE values above the 0.5 cutoff. The literature suggests that factor indicator loadings should be at least 0.6 and ideally 0.7 or above, indicating that the latent variable is accounting for 50 percent or more of the variance of the observed item (Chin, 1998; Hu et al., 2004). All factor loadings were found to be above the 0.6 cutoff, with only one item (a 0.67 indicator for Sociability) below 0.7, indicating good convergent validity (Hair et al., 2006).

Discriminant validity is assessed through examination of cross-loadings of constructs by inspection of the square root of the AVE for each factor and then comparing this to the factor’s inter-factor correlations. This square root value stands for the association of each of the factors to its respective items while correlations with the other factors indicate overlap of items among the constructs. Table A4 shows that the square root of the construct AVE values are higher than the correlations with the other constructs, indicating that each construct is more closely related to its own measure than to the measures of other constructs (Chin, 1998; Gefen & Straub, 2005; Majchrzak et al., 2005). Taken together, the results of the measurement model demonstrate high reliability as well as good convergent and discriminant validity for the factors.

The final four factors included character, power, sociability, and intelligence. Webster defines character as the mental and moral qualities that define an individual (Merriam-Webster, Character, n.d.). Intelligence describes the competence of an individual in a particular area (McCroskey et al., 1974). Sociability describes the tendency of individuals to engage in friendly relationships with others (Merriam-Webster, Sociability, n.d.). Power represents the strength and decisiveness of individuals in their daily lives (Christman & Branson, 1990).
