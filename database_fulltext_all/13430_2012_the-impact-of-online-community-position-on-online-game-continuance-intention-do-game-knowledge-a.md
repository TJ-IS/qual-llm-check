# The impact of online community position on online game continuance intention: Do game knowledge and community size matter?

Cheng-Chieh Hsiao <sup>a,</sup>\*, Jyh-Shen Chiou <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Business Administration, National Chengchi University, No. 64, Sec. 2, Zhi-nan Road, Wenshan, Taipei 11605, Taiwan <sup>b</sup> Department of International Business, National Chengchi University, No. 64, Sec. 2, Zhi-nan Road, Wenshan, Taipei 11605, Taiwan

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 1 March 2012 Received in revised form 16 June 2012 Accepted 18 September 2012 Available online 25 September 2012

Keywords: Online community Community position Continuance intention Online game Game knowledge Community size

## A B S T R A C T

This study attempts to investigate how a player’s position in an online gaming community leads to his or her continuance intention toward a Massive Multiplayer Online Game (MMOG) from the perspective of social capital theory. Using subjective and objective data collected from 340 players in a representative MMOG, our results reveal that community position significantly affects community trust and perceived social value, which in turn positively influence MMOG continuance intention. Furthermore, this study provides support for the moderating effects of game knowledge and community size on the proposed model. This study concludes with research limitations and theoretical and managerial implications.

## 1. Introduction

An online community is a social group in which users or consumers interact with each other on the Internet. It often facilitates community members in accumulating social capital [59,61], encouraging collective trust [41,50], and receiving social support [8,49,60]. With the prevalence of online communities, the issue of social capital has increasingly received attention from online community researchers. Social capital refers to any aspect of structural properties in a social system [1,20,21]. These structural properties represent the overall patterns of relations between actors [30]. An actor’s structural advantage is associated with his or her activities in an online community (e.g., [26,31,46]). From an Internet commerce perspective, the importance of social capital lies not only in fostering users’ actions in a virtual social system, but also in enhancing their continuous intention to adopt a company’s offerings. Social capital may strongly influence the business success of an Internet company.

Massive Multiplayer Online Games (MMOGs) are those online services that strongly connect to online communities of practice [18,24,25,52,64]. The global MMOG market generated 6 billion US dollars in 2010 and is expected to reach 8 billion US dollars in 2014 [66]. In MMOGs, online gaming communities are often known as ‘‘guilds’’ that represent self-organized collectives of players who gather voluntarily for instrumental or non-instrumental purposes. Since most parts of an MMOG player’s social life are formed in his or her affiliated guild [24,25,52], an online gaming community serves as stable ground for the development of online social capital [18,64]. Surprisingly, prior studies have found that these virtual guilds are relatively fragile and have high death rates. For example, approximately a quarter of guilds disappear each month in the globally leading MMOG, World of Warcraft (WOW) [24]. The fragility of guilds appears to be hazardous for a player trying to accumulate his or her online social capital. Although past studies have tested motivational and psychological determinants of consumer intention to play online games [36,39,67,69], little research has examined the effect of social capital on online game continuance in an MMOG setting. Through the accumulation of social capital, guilds often play an important role in determining a player’s stickiness toward MMOGs [68]. Ineffective management of online communities may lead to the failure of online game services.

To address the issue further, this study considers one user characteristic, game knowledge, and one community characteristic, community size, as moderating variables in the proposed model. We believe that these characteristics are of practical importance to Internet commerce regarding online gaming communities. First, the leader is often viewed as a crucial element to virtual teams [70] and online gaming communities [29,65]. In the context of MMOGs, each player is allowed to create his or her own guild. If a player wants to occupy a central position, he or she might choose to establish a new guild and become the guild leader. Although one’s structural advantage in a guild provides more social capital and resources [38], not all central players can be good leaders due to their differences in game knowledge and experience. Thus, we argue that individual differences in game expertise of the central players may moderate the impact of social capital on the consequence variables.

Second, online communities vary in their membership numbers: Some are small, and some are large. Small communities tend to have strong social trust and close relationships [62], whereas large guilds appear to be goal-oriented [65]. In MMOGs, it appears to be inevitable for a guild to expand because a large guild is much more likely to survive longer [25]. However, a persistent increase in membership can cause the collapse of a community because growth weakens interrelationships among community members [5]. Meanwhile, recruitment of new members would be a potential hazard for a current member’s structural advantage. The influence of social capital could be diluted when the current member’s community grows up. Thus, community size becomes an important moderating factor that influences the effects of social capital on the consequence variables.

As a result, we undertake an empirical study in the context of online gaming communities to answer the following questions: How does social capital (i.e., online community position) influence online game continuance intention? How is the effectiveness of social capital moderated by user (i.e., game knowledge) and community (i.e., community size) characteristics? Our argument is consistent with the development of social capital theory, which indicates that the influence of social capital is not always effective [1,17,44]. Prior social capital research has confirmed that the efficacy of social capital may depend on other variables [17,41,57,63]. As a result, we believe that our contingency view will contribute to the current theoretical advance of social capital in the context of online communities.

The objectives of this study are twofold. First, drawing upon social capital theory [1,20,21], we delineate the representation (i.e., community position) and consequences (i.e., community trust and perceived social value) of social capital, which affects a player’s continuance intention to play an online game. Second, we extend recent online social capital research by examining the moderating effects of game knowledge and community size on the effectiveness of social capital. Our model is tested with empirical data collected from online gaming communities in a globally leading MMOG. Exploring the potential boundary conditions of social capital theory will help online service providers re-think their management strategies for online games and online communities.

This paper is organized as follows: We first describe our research model from the perspective of social capital theory. Then, hypothesis development, research methodology, and empirical results are presented in sequence. Finally, we summarize conclusions, limitations, and implications of this study.

## 1.1. Theoretical framework and hypotheses

Our conceptual model is based on social capital theory, which suggests that some aspects of a social structure can create valuable consequences for an individual and facilitate his or her actions within that social system [1,20,21,30,44]. From a resource perspective of social capital, Lin [38] indicated that an individual’s structural advantage in a community can lead to either expressive or instrumental actions. Following recent development in social capital research [1,44,53], we simultaneously consider the source and consequences of social capital to provide a comprehensive explanation of the formation of online game continuance intention.

As shown in Fig. 1, this study first regards a player’s community position as his or her representation of social capital in an online gaming community. Online gaming communities (i.e., guilds) are chosen as the research context because they are fundamental social units that serve as stable ground for the development of social capital in MMOGs [18,64]. Then, community trust and perceived social value serves as two important consequences derived from a player’s position in the online gaming community. These network consequences are based on Lin’s [38] argument, and they are hypothesized to influence the player’s continuance intention toward an MMOG. Furthermore, game knowledge and community size are modeled as moderators in our framework. We expect that both player and community characteristics will moderate the effectiveness of social capital in an online gaming community. Next, we develop the model in detail.

## 1.2. Community position as social capital

Social capital is defined as any structural properties that result in valuable outcomes for an actor in a social system [1,20,21,44]. Social capital exists in one’s relationships with other actors in the social system. Since the investment of social capital is often intangible and immeasurable, Adler and Kwon [1] suggested that the common use of the term is often metaphorical. Prior research has widely recognized that an actor’s structural position (e.g., structural hole [14,16], network closure [20], and network centrality [55,59]) is a representation of his or her social capital in a social entity. The structural aspect of a social system is regarded as a fundamental form of social capital that yields numerous desirable consequences for the focal actor [1,20,21,44]. In an MMOG, a player often takes a social role and occupies his or her position in an online gaming community. The player’s community position may represent a type of structural social capital that he or she possesses in the community.

Consistent with social capital and social network literature [54,55,57], a player’s community position reflects the extent of network centrality in his or her online gaming community [31,59]. Social capital theory suggests that an actor in the central position of a social system often tends to possess higher power to manage network resources [1,15]. In a similar vein, players who occupy more central positions in a guild are likely to take leading roles in the guild (e.g., guild leaders or officers). They have more power to recruit new members and allocate community resources. Therefore, we believe that a player’s community position is quite adequate to represent the magnitude of his or her social capital in an online gaming community. This study uses an objective proxy, guild rank, to capture a player’s community position. More details will be described in the methodology section.

## 1.3. Consequences of community position: community trust and perceived social value

As mentioned, social capital is an umbrella concept that encompasses not only the source from which it is derived, but also its relationships to a set of outcomes [1,41]. According to social capital theory [20,30,38], this study suggests that community trust and perceived social value are important consequences that result from a player’s community position.

First, trust is regarded as an essential outcome of stable social exchange [10]. Schurr and Ozanne [51] defined trust as one’s ‘‘belief that a party’s promise is reliable and that a party will fulfill his/her obligation in an exchange relationship.’’ In this study, community trust involves an actor’s perception of confidence in the exchange partner who would behave in a reliable and integral manner [43]. On one hand, MMOG players often need to exchange valuable resources (e.g., in-game currency, goods, equipment) with others. Some resources may associate with real-world economic value. It is highly risky to exchange with self-interested and anonymous players. Thus, players tend to exchange with their trustworthy guildmates to avoid online deception and undesirable outcomes [64]. On the other hand, social capital theory suggests that mutual trust originates in an individual’s social interactions with others [30]. Since a central player has frequent in-group communication, social interaction, and collaborative play with community members, he or she is likely to have a stronger belief that other members will not act opportunistically [10,30]. Although social presence is limited on the Internet, individuals may find social support, companionship, and a sense of belonging in the context of online communities [62]. Online communities often begin to form a social core and offer camaraderie to their members [8]. According to social capital literature [15], an actor’s network position reflects his or her relationships with others in a social system. The actor who occupies a more central position often possesses more direct social connections to others in the social system [59]. Such structural position becomes an important source of group identity, social support, and friendship [48]. In the MMOG context, one important function of online gaming communities is to foster players’ social connections to others. A player with a central position tends to be the leader of a guild. The central player often receives more social companionship and support from the guild than peripheral members. ## 1.4. Community trust, perceived social value, and MMOG continuance intention

We further hypothesize positive impacts of community trust and perceived social value on online game continuance. On one hand, community trust represents a type of expectation that lowers the fear of uncertainty that community members may act opportunistically. Social capital research has suggested that interpersonal trust can facilitate people to engage in cooperative interaction [44]. Since an MMOG is often designed for collaborative play [45], interpersonal trust is particularly important in the setting. Virtual collaboration in an online community requires a player’s belief that his or her partners are reliable and trustworthy [32,34,70]. Players often tend to collaborate and exchange repeatedly with guildmates as a means of risk prevention [22]. As a result, we argue that community trust can improve a player’s intention to stay in an MMOG for future exchanges with other members. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H3. Community trust affects MMOG continuance intention positively.

Furthermore, social capital research has indicated that an actor who has better structural advantages is likely to have more social support and companionship [38]. These social values are similar to a type of relational switching cost that, according to Burnham et al. [13], ‘‘consumers associate with the process of switching from one provider to another.’’ The more relational costs a player has in his or her guild, the more he or she is likely to stay in an MMOG for continuous interaction with guild members. On the other hand, a player often develops intense social connections among guild members because the player’s social life in an MMOG is formed mostly in his or her guild [24,25,52]. The player is less inclined to switch to another online game when his or her social relationships have accumulated gradually in the guild. In a similar vein, researchers have suggested that personal consumption is increasingly affected by consumer-to-consumer bonds in an online community [23,37], where social supports are available. We expect that perceived social value in an online gaming community serves as an important determinant of a player’s MMOG continuance intention. Hence,

H4. Perceived social value affects MMOG continuance intention positively.

## 1.5. Moderating effects of game knowledge and community size

Although social capital can facilitate member actions in an online community, its mechanism and effectiveness in the community are complex. In this study, one player characteristic, game knowledge, and one community characteristic, community size, were considered. We expect that these factors may affect the effectiveness of social capital in a player’s guild. Understanding how the influence of social capital is affected by user and community characteristics will help online service companies reduce the fragility of online communities and implement effective customer management.

First, this study regards game knowledge as a player’s MMOGrelevant experiences that influence his or her abilities to play the MMOG and perform game tasks successfully [2]. Instead of the measure of objective knowledge, this study evaluated a player’s degree of subjective game knowledge [12,47]. When a player’s game knowledge and experiences increase, he or she will have more opportunities to initiate and collaborate with other members in numerous activities within his or her guild.

Players with a similar rank from their specific guilds may differ in game knowledge. Since most guild activities rely on a player’s virtual collaboration with anonymous members, central players with high game knowledge often become leaders who have great ability to judge the trustworthiness of community members and guide them to succeed in difficult game challenges. As compared to those central players with low knowledge, high-knowledge central players are more likely to exhibit their trust to maintain social solidarity and future cooperation. In contrast, a central player with low game knowledge may cause negative emotions among team members [54], especially when a challenge cannot be completed successfully. This will decrease guild members’ compliance and confidence toward the central player who has low game knowledge. In such a case, a self-fulfilling prophecy may occur for the central player with low knowledge: If guild members don’t trust me, why should I bother to put their needs before mine [cf. 32, p. 44]? When mutual trust is undermined, the central actor with low game knowledge is likely to have less trust toward his or her community. Therefore, it is hypothesized that:

H5a. The positive impact of community position on community trust is greater for high-knowledge players than for low-knowledge players.

Furthermore, a player’s social skills may increase as his or her game knowledge and experiences are accumulated in an MMOG. Although social capital theory suggests that central players often have strong structural connections to their community members [59], their structural advantages are not necessarily in line with online social relationships. Since low-level gamers are inclined to play with high-knowledge players for the advancement of character level [24], central players with low knowledge appear to have fewer chances to create and maintain social relationships with guild members than players with high knowledge. Mathwick et al. [41] indicated that the influence of social capital on perceived social support is greater for experienced users than novice users in an online community. It is also predicted that the effect of a player’s structural position on perceived social value will be greater for high-knowledge players than low-knowledge players. Thus, we suggest that:

H5b. The positive impact of community position on perceived social value is greater for high-knowledge players than for lowknowledge players.

Second, we regard community size as the number of members in an online gaming community. Based on the size of a community, a guild can be classified as small or large. Community trust needs to be developed in a social network where members are strongly interdependent [10,20]. In small online gaming communities (i.e., those with fewer than 200 members), each player tends to know everybody else, and interpersonal trust appears to be strong. Therefore, central players with structural advantages can effectively achieve a consensus among members in small guilds. With such consensus, community trust can be well developed [43]. On the other hand, in large guilds (i.e., those with more than 300 members), it is difficult for central players to build trust among a great number of self-interested and anonymous players with different perspectives. To maintain community solidarity, the central players in small guilds are more likely to express their feelings of trust toward members than those in large guilds. Therefore, it is hypothesized that:

H6a. The positive impact of community position on community trust is greater for players of small communities than for players of large communities.

Furthermore, Wellman et al. [62] suggested that individuals who participate in small communities tend to seek relational exchange (e.g., friendship and social support), whereas membership in large communities often serves goal-oriented purposes. Online gaming communities are important mechanisms to intensify social relationships among players [24,52]. The central players (e.g., guild leaders) have more direct social ties to other members [59] and often become the core members of community activities. They are likely to receive more perceived social value and support in small communities than those in large communities. On the other hand, a player may be less acquainted with all guildmates in large online gaming communities. We suggest that the effect of social capital on perceived social value may be attenuated in large guilds. Hence, we propose that:

H6b. The positive impact of community position on perceived social value is greater for players of small communities than for players of large communities.

## 1.6. Control variables

In our model, a player’s membership duration and average play time are included as two control variables. It can be agreed that an actor with senior membership is likely to receive significant social support in a community [41]. Meanwhile, a heavy user with more prior usage experience is more likely to remain in an online game [56]. Therefore, we control the positive impacts of membership duration on perceived social value and average play time on MMOG continuance intention in our model.

## 2. Method

## 2.1. Data collection

The research setting of this study was online gaming communities in a globally leading MMOG that has more than 10 million players around the world. As mentioned previously, online gaming communities (i.e., guilds) are regarded as important social units that facilitate the development of social capital in an MMOG [18,64]. In our target MMOG, most players belonged to a specific guild [24]. Thus, we believe that the target MMOG was quite appropriate for collecting empirical data to assess our framework.

We recruited and invited voluntary participants from three online forums related to the target MMOG because MMOG players often visit online game forums to search and exchange gamerelevant information. Players who had two months’ recent playing experience were invited to participate in our survey by clicking a hyperlink directing them to our survey website. At our website, they were first asked to identify the title of their most frequently played role and its guild and then to fill in the electronic questionnaire accordingly. The database saved their responses automatically when they completed the questionnaires.

We had more than 1000 visits to the website, and 392 respondents tried to answer the questionnaire. In total, we obtained 347 usable responses after removing 45 invalid cases. Among the 347 respondents, the average age was 23.83 (S.D. = 3.51). Some 65.13% of respondents had participated in their communities for more than six months, and the average number of members of an online gaming community was 232.61 (S.D. = 159.30).

## 2.2. Measurement

This study collected both objective and subjective data to assess the research model. The subjective data were collected from our respondents by using self-reported scales, whereas the objective data were collected directly from the target MMOG. All measures at the role level are listed in the Appendix.

## 2.2.1. Research constructs

For community position, we collected objective data (i.e., guild rank) which are recorded automatically by the target MMOG. Guild rank refers to a player’s position or status in the affiliated guild. Different from a role’s level, which is determined by the cumulative values of experience, a player’s guild rank in the target MMOG is given by the guild leader and based on the player’s contribution to or participation in the community. The guild rank ranges from rank 1 to rank 10; the smaller rank number implies a more central position in the guild. For example, rank 1 is the position of the guild leader. We reverse coded the measure in subsequent analyses.

Pertaining to other latent constructs in our conceptual model, four items for measuring perceived social value were adapted from Mathwick et al. [41]. Community trust was assessed with items modified from Tsai and Ghoshal [58] and Chiu et al. [19]. The scale of MMOG continuance intention was adapted from Fishbein and Ajzen [27] and Zeithaml et al. [71]. These scales were anchored on 7-point Likert scales from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7).

With respect to moderating variables, game knowledge was assessed by four items modified from Park et al. [47] and Algesheimer et al. [3]. A 7-point Likert scale was used (1 = strongly disagree and 7 = strongly agree). The size of the community was collected objectively from the MMOG system and re-coded into five categories (from 1 = less than 100 members to 5 = more than 400 members).

## 2.2.2. Control variables

Each respondent was asked to report his/her membership duration in the affiliated community on a 7-point scale (from 1 = less than 3 months to 7 = more than 18 months) and the average time of playing the game per day on a 9-point scale (from 1 = less than one hour to 9 = more than 8 hours).

## 2.3. Data analysis

Drawing upon Anderson and Gerbing’s [4] two-step analysis procedure, we first performed a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess the goodness of fit, internal consistency, and construct validity in the measurement model. Next, a full structural equation model was performed to test our main effect hypotheses (H1–H4).

To examine moderating effect hypotheses, we followed Baron and Kenny’s [9] suggestion to classify our respondents. For game knowledge, we classified respondents into either high- or lowknowledge groups. On the basis of the mean scores of our game knowledge scale, we identified 168 participants as the highknowledge group (mean = 6.36) and 142 as the low-knowledge group (mean = 4.73) by dropping 37 respondents with median scores (5.75). For community size, we divided respondents into small and large online gaming communities. After dropping 77 cases in the median range (between 201 and 299 members), we defined small communities as those with less than 200 members and large communities as those with more than 300 members. Small guilds had 163 respondents, and 107 respondents belonged to large guilds.

Next, we conducted a structural means analysis to test whether factor means differed significantly across different knowledge or size subsamples [3,6,35]. Then, we performed chi-square difference tests to examine whether the structural coefficients were unequal across different knowledge or size subgroups for moderating effect hypotheses (H5 and H6). All analyses were conducted by using covariance matrices with LISREL 8.0 [35].

## 4. Discussions and implications

## 4.1. Discussion of findings

To understand the social process of online game continuance, the objectives of this study are (1) to investigate how a player’s community position leads to his or her MMOG continuance intention by using social capital theory and (2) to examine moderating effects of game knowledge and community size on the effectiveness of social capital in an online gaming community. In line with social capital theory [1,20,30,38], our results showed that a player’s community position, as a representation of social capital, significantly improves his or her community trust and perceived social value in an online gaming community. In turn, these two network consequences have positive impacts on a player’s MMOG continuance intention. This indicates that a player’s community position can enhance his or her positive expectations toward other community members that may not behave opportunistically. The player can also receive more social companionship and support due to his or her structural advantage. With these desirable outcomes, a central player is more likely to stay in an MMOG.

More importantly, this study ascertains that a player’s game knowledge and community size do influence the effectiveness of social capital in an online gaming community. Our results indicated that the positive impacts of community position on community trust and perceived social value are greater (1) for high-knowledge players than for low-knowledge players and (2) for players in small communities than for players in large communities. These findings not only provide support for the contingent views of social capital [1,17], but also contribute to the effective management of online communities. We believe that user and community variables should be simultaneously considered when one attempts to understand the influence of social capital in online communities.

## 4.3. Implications for research

This study is one of the first that attempts to delineate and examine a social capital theory of MMOG continuance intention and its boundary conditions. Our contribution to the existing literature is threefold. First, this study describes how a player’s social capital improves his or her continuance intention to play an MMOG. Prior studies have examined a bundle of motivational and psychological determinants of consumer intention to play online games (e.g., [36,39,67,69]). Since the online communities of practice are critical in affecting consumer adoption of online services, this study provides a sociological perspective for understanding the formation of online game continuance intention. As social aspects of online games become more and more significant, we believe that this approach will draw scholars’ increased attention to online game research.

Second, our results confirm that a player’s community position has a positive impact on community trust, which in turn affects MMOG continuance intention. Due to users’ anonymity and moral hazard issues, trust is an important factor in the context of virtual organizations [32,34,50]. Past research has addressed the potential relationship between structural and relational aspects of social capital [20,30,58]. This study supports the positive relationship between community position and community trust. Future research can explore other structural constructs that improve community trust in the context of online communities.

Third, this study suggests that the influence of social capital does not exist universally. With the increasing use of social capital theory in online communities, there is an urge to understand the potential boundary conditions of this sociological theory. In this study, our findings confirm that the effectiveness of social capital depends on either the degree of a player’s game knowledge or the size of his or her community. This study contributes to extant research by providing empirical evidence of the contingent effects of online social capital. Future research is recommended to take different types of moderating variables into account when applying social capital theory to online communities.

## 4.4. Implications for practice

From a practical perspective, this study has several managerial implications for MMOG and online community service providers. First, this study generally supports that social capital in online gaming communities can lead to MMOG continuance intention through community trust and perceived social value. Online game service providers should design community tasks and challenges that encourage each player to collaborate with other members in his or her online gaming community. With a history of frequent interaction, guild leaders can review and renew the ranks of guild members. This helps a player to maintain community trust and receive social support, leading to his or her continuance intention toward an MMOG.

Second, our results suggest that the effectiveness of social capital is greater for high-knowledge players than low-knowledge players. A player who has sufficient game knowledge and experience appears to be more eligible to act as a central member (e.g., a guild leader or officer) in a guild. To lower the death rates of guilds, MMOG companies should consider designing an appropriate mechanism to evaluate a player’s game knowledge and experience. Such a mechanism may be used to assess a player’s eligibility to assume guild leadership and other central roles. Furthermore, MMOG companies can implement mentoring or rewarding systems that foster game experts’ collaboration with novice players. Through these mechanisms, expert players can sustain their structural advantages, and game novices can enhance their gaming experience and social capital in the gaming communities.

Third, each community needs to pay attention to its risk of growth. Since small communities can intensify a player’s interaction with other members, our findings indicate that social capital is more effective in small guilds than in large guilds. To create better efficacy of social capital, we suggest that online game service providers encourage their players to adequately control the number of guild members. To enhance the sustainability of an online gaming community, a large guild can be divided into two or three sub-communities. Based on our findings regarding game knowledge, these sub-communities should be managed by a group of expert players.

Finally, to a certain degree, our implications may be generalized to other types of online communities. Due to the collaborative nature of MMOGs [45], the attributes and functions of online gaming communities are close to those of online collaborative communities, such as Threadless and Linux user groups. In a similar vein, online collaborative communities emphasize collaborative activities that foster members to develop online social capital, which improves member loyalty toward the offerings of these communities. This fact creates an opportunity to generalize our findings and implications to the context of online collaborative communities. To manage online collaborative communities effectively, we suggest that community managers further consider our practical implications for the efficacy of online social capital and its boundary conditions regarding user knowledge and community size.

## 5. Conclusion

The purpose of this study is to delineate and examine how a player’s online community position affects his or her continuance intention to play an MMOG. Our results supported the notion that community position as a structural aspect of social capital has positive effects on community trust and perceived social value that in turn influence MMOG continuance intention. Furthermore, we confirmed that the effectiveness of online social capital depends on a player’s game knowledge and community size. With the increasing application of social capital theory in online community studies, we hope that future research will consider more boundary conditions associated with the efficacy of online social capital. Our findings have also provided online service companies with insights into the sustainability of online gaming communities and the management of online collaborative communities.
