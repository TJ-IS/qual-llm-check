# Boosting or Undermining? Unveiling the Two-Sided Effect of Updates in Medical Crowdfunding Campaigns

Chenzhang Bao,<sup>1</sup> Min Kyung Lee,<sup>2</sup> Wen Zhang<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Spears School of Business, Oklahoma State University, USA, chbao@okstate.edu <sup>2</sup>Hankamer School of Business, Baylor University, USA, MinKyung\_Lee1@baylor.edu <sup>3</sup>Hankamer School of Business, Baylor University, USA, Wen\_Zhang1@baylor.edu

## Abstract

In crowdfunding, the disclosure of campaign details and progress is widely considered beneficial to fundraising because it can resolve information asymmetry and improve operational transparency. However, does this mean that fundraisers should frequently post updates? In this study, we aim to understand the implications of update frequency and content for fundraising in the context of medical crowdfunding. We deploy various natural language processing models to analyze the type, volume, and novelty of the information disclosed in updates and how funders perceive them. Our results delineate an inverted U-shaped relationship between update frequency and donations, contingent on content type. Specifically, we found that medical updates can resolve concerns and foster positive impressions among funders, which improves fundraising. However, as the update frequency increases, further posts may contribute to cognitive overload, attenuating the effect. In contrast, nonmedical updates are associated with a lower donation amount, as this type of update tends to contain redundant information and is negatively received by funders. This study contributes to crowdfunding literature by uncovering the unintended consequences of updates on fundraising due to reduced novelty and information overload. Our results suggest that theorizing the role of updates in medical crowdfunding needs to account for the two-sided nature of information disclosure. In particular, posting novel, issue-relevant information can initially reduce uncertainty and stimulate trust, positively influencing funding outcomes. However, overly frequent updates may include excessive details or repetitive content, adversely impacting fundraising.

## 1 Introduction

The escalating cost of healthcare has emerged as a significant global concern, limiting patients’ access to necessary treatments and procedures. The concern is more prominent among middle- to low-income countries where 35% of medical spending is paid by individuals as out-of-pocket expenses (WHO, 2019). As a result, medical costs are causing nearly 100 million patients to become impoverished each year, and the number is increasing by 11% annually (Wang et al., 2021). Faced with catastrophic health expenses, patients and their families often seek financial support from relatives, colleagues, friends, and acquaintances. Due to advances in digital platforms, medical crowdfunding has become a viable option to obtain funds and reach a broader group of funders who are willing to donate. Given its potential to facilitate fundraising, medical crowdfunding is gaining momentum worldwide. For instance, in the US, over 280,000 campaigns were launched in 2018, raising more than \$3.7 billion in medical funds (Angraal et al., 2021). In China, \$2.6 billion was raised in 2017 on Tencent Gong Yi, one of the largest crowdfunding platforms, comparable to amounts raised on GoFundMe in 2016 (Jin, 2019).

Typically, medical crowdfunding campaigns are donation based, and the fundraiser uses the raised funds to cover healthcare-related expenses. In other words, the function of medical crowdfunding is fundamentally different from equity- or reward-based projects because funders do not receive monetary or in-kind returns, which may affect their decision to contribute (Behl et al., 2022). Indeed, Ba et al., (2020) report that the success rate of medical campaigns is substantially lower than that of other types. The funders’ unique incentive structure thus imposes different demands on fundraisers in terms of prudent campaign management and the development of a comprehensive understanding of operational strategies. For instance, funders of medical campaigns tend to be more skeptical, as this type of crowdfunding may entail ethical concerns, such as the veracity of claims and pressures on clinical decisionmaking, which require fundraisers to periodically update campaign details (Jin, 2019).

Prior research has investigated various factors, drivers, and risks of crowdfunding, including promotion strategies, fund misappropriation, performance opacity, communication strategies, interactive messaging, and reward limits (Belavina et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020; Tafesse, 2021; Xiang et al., 2019). However, these studies have predominantly focused on reward- or equity-based campaigns, and few have examined the operational design and implementation associated with the success of donation-based campaigns such as medical crowdfunding to help individuals with healthcare needs (Hassna et al., 2024). Our work seeks to bridge the literature gap by focusing on the information disclosure feature (hereafter referred to as updates), one of the few tools fundraisers can use and manage after a campaign is initiated. While campaign updates represent an important operational strategy, how the frequency<sup>1</sup> and content attributes of updates affect donations remains underexplored in medical crowdfunding research.

Although campaign updates share similar communicative functions as commercial advertisements, they differ fundamentally in five aspects. First, the entities behind them are distinct. While large firms have long-established brand images, funders generally do not have access to fundraisers and their past records of fundraising in a virtual environment, making it challenging to evaluate the campaign quality through updates (Hassna et al., 2024). Second, unlike consumers, funders are intrinsically motivated to supply monetary resources to charity projects. Therefore, they need to be able to perceive the medical emergencies ex ante and the value of their altruistic actions through updates (Wang et al., 2023). Third, funders often face potential uncertainty because they cannot easily foresee the appropriate use of funds at the time of donating (Strausz, 2017). This delayed gratification creates a certain level of ambiguity (Wang et al., 2023). Consequently, funder perceptions and evaluations are more susceptible to the way that fundraising is presented in updates (Coupey et al., 1998). Fourth, while consumers can generally take their time in making purchase decisions, medical crowdfunding is usually temporally constrained, creating a sense of urgency (Yang et al., 2020). For example, funders may need to make the donation decisions quickly before the campaign ends and thus may not have time to look into every detail. This requires fundraisers to diligently work on updates and carefully develop content in a short time frame. Fifth, although advertisements tend to be repetitive in the short term, firms often devote time and effort to artistically designing commercial advertisements, ensuring that they are engaging and entertaining to watch. In contrast, fundraisers often do not have the resources to effectively sculpt updates. Consequently, campaign updates often consist merely of words and terminologies that may be overwhelming, tedious, and adversely perceived by funders.

Therefore, medical crowdfunding differs substantially from advertising with respect to credibility factors, altruistic motivations, delayed gratification, urgency, and content specificity. Further, these unique features complicate funders’ donation decisions and present challenges for fundraisers to develop effective update strategies. In particular, previous studies on advertisements have predominantly examined the impact of repeated exposure to the same advertisements in experimental settings (Nordhielm, 2002; Rethans et al., 1986). This narrow approach reflects a structural limitation of advertisements, as it may neglect the distinctions between content types and how these structural variations may contribute to user experiences regarding content information. In sharp contrast, updates can be highly operatable, such that fundraisers can present different content at any time they deem appropriate. Consequently, the specific content fundraisers should include, how frequently they should update, and the mechanisms that explain the impact of various updates on donations remain largely unclear and cannot be inferred from the extant results of advertising. In this study, we aim to empirically resolve these pending issues by answering the following two research questions: (1) How does the update count (frequency) influence the fundraising process? (2) What is the impact of different types of content revealed in updates?

Situated in our study context, we integrate insights from two-factor theory and the concepts of information novelty and information overload to theorize the effect of updates. Leveraging a unique dataset that tracks the fundraising process, we implemented various natural language processing (NLP) approaches to analyze the textual content of updates, followed by econometric analyses that examine its impact on donations. Specifically, we adopted latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) to identify potential topics across updates and employed multilabel classification to predict the type of topics covered in each update. Our results suggest an inverted U-shaped relationship between update frequency and donations: While posting updates, particularly those that disclose medical progress (e.g., patient status and treatment plans), initially improves fundraising, the positive impact is attenuated as the update frequency increases. However, posting nonmedical updates (e.g., campaign status, thank-you notes, and personal feelings) is associated with lower returns.

To uncover the underlying mechanism, we quantified the novelty of information revealed in updates and evaluated the role of information overload based on the information quantity. We then linked these metrics to the sentiment of funder comments and the amount donated. Our analyses suggest that compared to nonmedical updates, medical updates tend to present contextual information, which is more positively received by funders and increases donations. However, funders may experience cognitive overload when excessive information is included, diminishing the potential benefits. This finding aligns with the inverted U-shaped relationship between medical updates and donations, indicating that information overload is the mechanism that explains the effect of medical updates. On the other hand, frequent posts of nonmedical updates likely introduce redundant information with fewer novel and informative insights, leading to negative comments and adverse impacts on fundraising. Thus, our results indicate that the detrimental effect of nonmedical updates manifests through reduced information novelty.

This study makes several important contributions to research and practice. First and foremost, our paper is one of the first studies to reveal the unintended consequence of campaign updates on donations. We reveal two distinctive mechanisms—information overload and information novelty—that explain the heterogeneous impact across different types of content. While constrained to the context of medical crowdfunding, our results create a theory-in-flux to inform future researchers that it may not be effective or accurate to measure updates as a linear effect; instead, researchers should take the bidirectional nature of information disclosure into account in their theorizing. In particular, posting fresh, relevant insights can resolve information gaps and stimulate reliability, improving funding outcomes. However, overly frequent updates may include redundant information or increase the cognitive load on funders, negatively influencing fundraising. Therefore, our results imply that decisions regarding how often updates are posted and what specific content they contain should be made cautiously. Otherwise, such a maneuver might unexpectedly hamper the fundraising process. For platforms, a feature that alerts fundraisers if updates are being posted too frequently and provides guidelines regarding the types of information to include in updates would be helpful to ensure the success of campaigns.

## 2 Literature Review and Hypotheses Development

## 2.1 Medical Crowdfunding and Campaign Updates

Crowdfunding emerged as a new approach to raising capital for projects during the financial crisis. It has since grown exponentially and become a fully fledged financing instrument. There are various types of online crowdfunding, each with different functions and procedures. This study focuses on medical crowdfunding, where funders contribute a small donation to meet the larger financial goal of a charitable project related to patient care. Unlike other types of crowdfunding, funders do not receive any monetary or in-kind rewards for their contribution, presenting a unique form of motivation for charitable giving (Behl et al., 2022). Funders in medical crowdfunding are motivated by altruistic and egoistic utilities from donating (Song et al., 2022). The altruistic utility is derived from the welfare of the beneficiary (Andreoni, 1990; Ribar & Wilhelm, 2002), which might be based on feelings of empathy, sympathy, or selflessness for others (Choy & Schlagwein, 2016). The egoistic utility is associated with a self-centered motivation derived from the inherent pleasure funders take in donating (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Together, the act of giving can create social production that is valuable to both funders and others (Timm Knudsen & Ellerup Nielsen, 2013).<sup>2</sup>

However, it is not easy to build a sense of sympathy and satisfaction from donating because there may not be a feedback mechanism that allows funders to understand how their donations were spent and how they met patients’ medical needs (Mejia et al., 2019). Indeed, it has been reported that only 12% of medical crowdfunding campaigns achieved their financial goals in 2020 (Kenworthy & Igra, 2022), significantly lower than other types of crowdfunding (Ba et al., 2020). The update feature on crowdfunding platforms provides an important tool for fundraisers to communicate with potential funders and address this concern. At any time during a campaign, the fundraiser can post additional information or the latest news about the campaign or the patient. The updates may disclose, for example, the patient’s current condition, the treatments received, endorsements from others, or how donations will be used. Fundraisers and their contacts can then share and propagate updates through their own social networks to reach a large group of potential funders.

## 2.2 Effect of Campaign Updates

Extant research has investigated various communicative channels in crowdfunding and their impact on fundraising, including campaign descriptions, updates, and comments (Majumdar & Bose, 2018; Mejia et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 2021). As summarized in Appendix A, previous studies on campaign updates have documented appeal tone, text sentiment, and update timing in influencing funding success (Bhargava et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024a; Wu et al., 2024b). However, they have mostly considered posting updates as a binary decision and focused on the positive side of it. For example, Mejia et al., (2019) examined the impact of transparency in the context of emergency donations and found that clear information provided through posting updates improves operational transparency, trust, and donation likelihood. Similarly, Kuppuswamy and Bayus (2017) demonstrated that project updates were associated with higher levels of financial support, as these updates serve as effective communication and visibility tools.

While fundraisers can potentially update any content they deem appropriate at any time, without a thoughtful strategy, such updates may not necessarily contribute to higher donations from an information disclosure standpoint. For instance, content may be perceived as repetitive if fundraisers frequently post updates, adding minimal informative value to persuade funders. Further, when updates contain lengthy narratives, funders may not be able to easily understand the beneficiary’s condition or the treatments needed, potentially leading to more concerns rather than clarity. Appendix B presents a representative sample of funders’ responses to updates. While most funders use comments to express positive wishes, we also observed negative instances express criticisms regarding, for example, the lack of health insurance, distribution of medical resources, or treatment plan. We argue that these comments reflect funders’ anticipatory concerns that have not been adequately addressed by updates.<sup>3</sup>

Building on these observations, we posit that research needs to integrate frequency and content nuances to establish a holistic view of updates. While recent work on crowdfunding has started to examine the content of updates, the focus has thus far been on sentiment and tone. For example, Wu et al. (2024a) observed that updates with both positive and negative sentiment help increase donations and those with negative sentiment exhibit a more salient effect on donation behaviors. Our work extends the literature by analyzing a spectrum of intricate attributes of update content, including the topic, type, information quantity, and novelty. We specifically investigate the nonlinear effect of update frequency and content types, aiming to understand the unintended consequences of updates and the underlying mechanisms.

## 2.3 Theoretical Framework

We deploy insights from two-factor theory to understand the effect of campaign updates on fundraising (Berlyne, 1970; Cacioppo & Petty, 1979; Schmidt & Eisend, 2015). This theory focuses on the role of arousal as the key determinant of individual preferences. It proposes that the relationship between stimuli arousal and affective response can be explained by two competing factors, namely positive habituation and tedium (Nordhielm, 2002). Specifically, initial exposure helps to form positive habituation by resolving doubts and uncertainties, leading to positive thoughts. However, beyond a critical level of arousal, users start perceiving the stimuli as tedious, with less engagement over time. That is, an aversion attitude gradually becomes activated, creating a sense of negativity (Chmiel & Schubert, 2017). This provides a behavioral lens to comprehend how continuous stimuli can generate the wear-out effect in various domains. As a result, affective response, defined as the net effect, is more favorable initially due to improved familiarity but deteriorates with increasing exposure as negative thoughts become dominant.

Additionally, we leverage information overload and information novelty as contributing factors to explain wear-out effects, recognizing their diverse psychological consequences, including boredom, annoyance, and disengagement, among others. Although information overload and boredom are both connected to psychological strain, the underlying sources and responses are distinctively different, especially in terms of how individuals process or withdraw from their environments. Information overload is a cognitive state that occurs when an individual’s ability to effectively process and utilize information is hindered by the sheer volume or complexity of available data (Edmunds & Morris, 2000). This phenomenon arises from a mismatch between the abundance of information and the limited human capacity for information processing. Individuals mentally determine the amount of information needed, cognitively assess the obtained information, and discontinue seeking more information when it ceases to provide additional insights (Gettys & Fisher, 1979). As the amount of information surpasses an optimal threshold, the efficiency of information processing declines, leading to dysfunctional consequences (Graf & Antoni, 2022). In essence, information overload results from an excess of information that overwhelms an individual’s cognitive abilities, ultimately impairing their decisionmaking and productivity.

Boredom, on the other hand, is “a state of relatively low arousal and dissatisfaction” (Mikulas & Vodanovich, 1993, p. 3), which often arises when external stimulation becomes monotonous and repetitive with no new information (Martin et al., 2006). As a result, boredom may be linked to cognitive processes, such as being indifferent or disengaged from the environment. Exposure to irrelevant or repetitive content can also make it difficult for individuals to choose relevant details (Saunders et al., 2017), whereas the discovery of new information is favored when it is likely to offer further validation for their decisions (Frey & Rosch, 1984). Empirical evidence has suggested that subjective monotony or repetition may trigger boredom; as a result, individuals may start seeking more diverse and engaging forms of stimulation (Fiske & Maddi, 1961). Similarly, Game (2007) observed that enabling individuals to envision the meaning of an event can reduce boredom and even escalate their interest.

In sum, we theorize campaign updates as an affective stimulus that influences funders’ preference to donate. While additional updates may initially increase donations, surpassing the optimal level can lead to information overload or boredom, which negatively impacts fundraising efforts. Building on this theoretical foundation, we now develop our hypotheses on update frequency and content.

## 2.4 Implications for Hypotheses Development

According to two-factor theory, initial exposure to a stimulus can greatly impact habituation and generate a positive response by reducing uncertainty toward an unfamiliar stimulus (Nelson, 1974). This first phase is called the wear-in effect, as the unfamiliar stimulus conveys relevant information to the audience directly or indirectly and tends to be effective. In crowdfunding, potential funders are often not fully informed about the campaign due to the asymmetrical information structure of the online platform (Zheng et al., 2014). From an information disclosure perspective, by regularly releasing up-to-date details or additional information about the campaign via updates, fundraisers can enhance operational transparency, which contributes to funders’ understanding and willingness to donate (Wang et al., 2021).

Further, disclosing the campaign status can indicate fundraisers’ commitment, responsibility, and effort in making the campaign successful (Kim et al., 2016; Wei et al., 2021). For instance, Mejia et al., (2019, p. 1775) defined updates as “communicating the work the campaign is doing” and observed that work-related updates, such as descriptions of how the campaign is achieving its objective, are associated with more donations due to an increased perception of effort and trust. Hence, the regular release of campaign progress can set a strong signal of credibility, which can persuade more funders to donate and improve the success of the campaign.

On the other hand, two-factor theory also suggests that the second phase (i.e., wear-out effect) occurs when additional exposures begin to have an impact on tedium, aroused by boredom and annoyance with the repeated message, resulting in decreasing effectiveness or even adverse effects of the stimulus (Anand & Sternthal, 1990; Campbell & Keller, 2003). In our context, the crowdfunding platform typically keeps those who have donated or shared the campaign in the interested group and notifies them whenever the fundraiser posts an update. As a result, multiple updates within a short time frame tend to present repetitive information that may lead to boredom and fatigue. Consequently, funders may perceive these updates as redundant, diminishing their engagement and opportunities for further donations (Schneider, 1987). Further, a surge in the volume of updates may obscure the comprehension of extensive details, which may, in turn, spur more doubts about the severity of the patient’s condition or the appropriate use of the donated money (Eppler & Mengis, 2004). Similarly, excessive updates may signal a lack of preparation, leading to significant reductions in donations. Therefore, multiple updates can be perceived by funders as redundant information with constant quality and may also result in cognitive overload and less awareness, indicating a possible wear-out effect (Niemand et al., 2019).

In theorizing the potential negative impact of frequent updates, we also used a phenomenon-based approach (Fisher et al., 2021). Specifically, by drawing on funders’ responses to multiple updates, we posit that these comments reflect their perception of updates and impact their donation decisions (see Appendix B). For example, after a fundraiser posted several updates about a lack of insurance and financial support, people started lodging criticisms that the individual should have bought insurance beforehand (NC.1). Additionally, Comment NC.8 suggests that funders may feel harassed when fundraisers keep requesting donations in updates. Similarly, NC.9 posted to inquire about how to block updates. Therefore, we argue that multiple updates may lead to annoyance and resistance among funders and that intensive posting within a short period of time can decrease their motivation to contribute. We thus hypothesize that posting updates is associated with higher campaign donations, but this effect weakens with frequent updates.

H1: In medical crowdfunding, posting updates initially increases donations, but a higher frequency of updates attenuates the effect.

While H1 establishes the overall relationship between update frequency and donations, updates are heterogeneous in content, and their impact may vary depending on the type of content shared. Xiao et al., (2021) observed that updates containing campaignrelated content enhance information transparency and positively influence funders’ donation behavior. Another study by Mejia et al., (2019) documented a positive effect of work-related updates on donations for emergency response. Indeed, medical updates are distinct from nonmedical updates because they tend to include highly specific information, such as treatment progress, diagnoses, or therapy plans. These issue-related details can directly reduce uncertainty and play a distinct role in resolving information asymmetry due to the dynamic nature of medical conditions and treatments, where new developments, test results, or changes in patient status continually emerge (Haux, 2006). Thus, medical updates can set strong signals that enhance the campaign’s perceived legitimacy and urgency, fostering trust (Majumdar & Bose, 2018) and increasing funders’ willingness to contribute (Liang et al., 2019).

On the other hand, frequent updates containing extensive details or lengthy narratives can lead to information overload due to funders’ cognitive processing limits (Edmunds & Morris, 2000). This concern is particularly relevant for medical updates, where complex medical terminology or detailed clinical information may cognitively strain funders. As a result, they may feel cognitively burdened and emotionally disengaged, negatively impacting their donation behavior. We argue that the risk of overwhelming funders increases with the frequency and complexity of medical updates. Therefore, we posit that the positive effect of medical updates is subject to the constraints of information processing. With frequent updates containing complex health-related information, the positive effect may weaken, as additional medical updates can contribute to the cumulative cognitive load on prospective funders, potentially pushing them closer to or beyond their information processing capability. Thus, we hypothesize that because of their credibility, medical updates are associated with more donations, but posit that the positive effect is attenuated with frequent updates due to information overload.

## H2: In medical crowdfunding, disclosing medical information in campaign updates increases donations, yet frequent updates including medical content attenuate this effect.

Updates may contain other content besides medical information, which may have a different level of informational value from a decision-making perspective. While valuable information can significantly enhance funders’ engagement and positively influence fundraising, simply posting irrelevant content for attention is likely to be seen as inauthentic (Granados et al., 2010). For example, updates that simply report on campaign progress or repetitively plead for more donations are unlikely to resonate with potential funders (Xiao et al., 2021) because the crowdfunding platform provides readily accessible data on real-time fundraising progress, rendering such explicit appeals for additional contributions superfluous. Moreover, Majumdar and Bose (2018) found that the excessive presence of words with negative sentiment in campaign appeals may not improve campaign success, as such emotionally charged words may be perceived as deceptive and doubtful, thereby reducing the probability of obtaining donations.

Furthermore, boredom can also arise if fundraisers frequently post nonmedical content, as this type of update contains less novel content and fails to present meaningful insights. Consequently, funders may consider nonmedical content less interesting; since they are donating in order to help patients receive needed medical treatments, such content may provide little information about what they care about. We also observed that nonmedical content is often written using clichés and is often highly repetitive, which may lead to “funder fatigue” with repeated exposure As a result, funders may perceive the campaign to be not impactful, reducing their interest in further engagement and donations. Including such redundant content may also distract funders’ attention away from and obscure valuable insights, making the updates less effective (Eppler & Mengis, 2004). Based on these arguments, we posit that nonmedical updates negatively influence donations by providing less novel information.

H3: In medical crowdfunding, nonmedical content in campaign updates decreases donations.

## 3 Data and Variable Construction

## 3.1 Research Context and Data Source

Our research context is a leading medical crowdfunding platform in China, which has launched over 2.5 million campaigns since its rollout in 2014. The platform follows a stringent policy to ensure the credibility of campaigns, including verification of patient identity, restricting fundraiser beneficiaries to immediate family members, and tracking the use of raised funds after the campaign ends. Once approved, the platform allows the campaign to run for a maximum of 31 days. The campaign does not need to reach its target amount, and the fundraiser can terminate the campaign early and still receive the funds.

After launch, fundraisers can update the campaign at any time with any content. While the platform does not have reminders or requirements for updates, fundraisers are encouraged to post updates about the patient, campaign, and other relevant information to improve visibility and fundraising. There are three main ways to access updates. First, anyone can visit the platform’s website to browse active campaigns and view updates.<sup>4</sup> Each campaign has a separate webpage, which displays recent updates along with other specific information, such as campaign introduction, funding target, and real-time fundraising progress. Second, the platform offers a one-click feature that allows fundraisers and other users to easily share updates through their social networks, which can be further disseminated to broaden their reach. Third, notifications via email or text can be sent to potentially interested people when updates are posted. This includes funders who have already contributed and prospective funders who have not yet donated but have follower status on the campaign.

Besides updates, the platform also offers other means for fundraisers to interact with potential funders. For instance, anyone who can access the campaign webpage is allowed to express their opinion about the update using the comment function. Fundraisers can reply to these comments and address any pertinent concerns. Furthermore, the platform has a private messaging feature that allows fundraisers to communicate with others privately.

We collected a unique dataset of 16,635 campaigns launched in 2017 and 2018. For each campaign, we extracted granular details about the fundraising process, including donations, updates, comments, replies, and time stamps. This longitudinal data allowed us to tease out other unobserved factors during the campaign and focus on the effect of operational strategies, such as posting updates, on fundraising. We also obtained patient characteristics, such as age, gender, medical condition, and insurance.

## 3.2 Variable Construction

The average campaign duration in our sample was 19 days, indicating that using a week as the unit of time may lead to a short panel, compromising the causal identification. However, using a short time unit (i.e., hours) may underestimate the effect because funders may not immediately view updates. Therefore, following the crowdfunding literature, we used the campaign-day as the unit of analysis for ease of interpretation and performed various robustness tests to account for temporal effects in Section 4.2 (Bhargava et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024a). Table 1 presents the definitions and summary statistics for the key variables.

## 3.2.1 Dependent Variables

Our main measure of campaign performance was donation amount, defined as the total amount of funds the campaign raised on a certain day. In our study context, fundraisers were able to retain donations even if the total funds donated did not reach the target amount. As a result, donations may not be proportionate to the funding goal. Therefore, we opted to deploy the coarse monetary amount in the main analyses to more accurately capture campaign outcomes, although using the fraction of the goal pledged led to qualitatively consistent results (Mejia et al., 2019). We also constructed future donations as the total amount of donations from day t to the end of the campaign to capture the full effect of updates on subsequent donations.

## 3.2.2 Independent Variables

Fundraisers are encouraged to post updates containing information about the medical and financial situation during the campaign, such as treatment progress, health conditions, and medical bills. Updates represent fundraisers’ efforts to persuade potential funders to make donations.<sup>5</sup> Hence, both the frequency of updates and the types of content disclosed are critical to impact donations. We constructed update count to measure the number of updates posted in a day regardless of update content. This variable takes all types of updates into account, with a higher value indicating more frequent updates. To further measure the cumulative impact of previous updates, we alternatively aggregated all updates from campaign initiation to day t-1, referred to as cumulative past update count.

Types of update content: In the absence of predefined content types, we analyzed large collections of textual content in two main steps to categorize updates. First, we considered content types as latent topics and employed LDA to extract potential topics across updates (Blei et al., 2003; Wu et al., 2024b). The LDA model characterized each update as a mix of latent topics, each of which was modeled as a probability distribution of frequent keywords extracted from all updates. We set the number of topics to 10 and configured the LDA model to iterate over training passes ranging from 30 to 230 with an increment of 20. During each iteration, we optimized document topic density and topic word density. Our final LDA model was selected based on the highest coherence score. To ensure comprehensive coverage of topics in updates, we increased the number of topics to 15 and 20 and observed a similar set of topic labels and frequent words. Further, we manually examined representative excerpts for each topic and consulted with domain experts in crowdfunding to confirm the relevance and accuracy of the LDA results.

Table C1 in Appendix C presents the top 10 most frequent words across the 10 identified topics. We observed that many topics shared common words and thus categorized them into five distinct types based on the most frequent words in each topic. Two types are closely related to medical progress: treatment plan outlines the treatments and procedures the patient will undergo; patient status provides details on the patient’s health condition, received medical treatments, and doctors’ diagnoses. Campaign progress, thank-you note, and personal feelings were conceptualized as nonmedical oriented, as these topics are respectively related to funding needs (donation requests), promotion requests, gratitude for financial support, and emotional reflections on hardships.

In the second step, we classified each update using the five type labels. Due to the rich content texts, one update could entail multiple types. Therefore, the task of assigning appropriate types to each update was essentially a multilabel classification. We employed supervised learning algorithms to handle this task. We compiled a random sample of 900 updates, annotated by two independent coders who are fluent in Chinese (Mousavi & Gu, 2024). Following prior research, we measured interrater reliability using Cohen’s kappa (Cohen, 1960). The Cohen’s kappa coefficient of the two coders’ labeling was 0.929, and the overall agreement percentage was 96.64%, validating the manual labeling. We used 80% of the labeled sample for training and 20% for testing.

Next, we implemented three prominent NLP algorithms to determine the most efficient method for the multilabel classification task, namely support vector machine (SVM), long short-term memory network (LSTM), and a bidirectional encoder representations from transformers model that was pretrained for Chinese text (BERT-Chinese). For each model, we explored different values for the key parameters and selected the best performers. Due to the nuances of multilabel classification, our primary metric for evaluation was the macro-averaged F1 score (Aguilar-Ruiz & Michalak, 2024).<sup>6</sup> Compared to SVM and LSTM, the fine-tuned BERT-Chinese model had the highest F1 score (0.776) and was used to classify update types.

Appendix C presents details of topic modeling and classification models. The result was a set of dummy variables at the update level to indicate whether the update contained any of these five types of content (patient status, treatment plan, campaign progress, thank-you note, and personal feelings). Campaign progress and thank-you note appeared most frequently in updates (60.63% and 52.66%, respectively), while medical information was included less often (10.57% and 35.05% for treatment plan and patient status, respectively). On average, an update includes two types of information (mean = 1.76). Notably, update types are not mutually exclusive, and 576 updates (< 1%) do not contain any of these types of content.

To examine the heterogenous effect of update content on donations, we aggregated the classified update types to the campaign-day level. For the main analyses, we specifically combined update types into medical and nonmedical, as discussed, to be parsimonious in counting the number of posts for each type in a day (i.e., medical update count and nonmedical update count). We also aggregated updates from each type prior to day t (cumulative past medical and nonmedical update count) to examine the cumulative impact of previous updates.

## 3.2.3 Control Variables

To alleviate confounding concerns, we included a set of control variables to capture online interactions between fundraisers and funders (Mollick, 2014). For instance, funders can send messages to the fundraisers to express their encouragement or concerns. Since these are private conversations, we did not observe the message content. Instead, we controlled for the number of message interactions between fundraisers and funders in a day (message count).<sup>7</sup> Additionally, the comment function represents a major channel for funders to communicate publicly with fundraisers and explain their perceptions regarding a particular update. Once posted, the comment appears beneath that update on the campaign’s webpage by design. While we could not access the specific time at which a comment was posted, we collected the connections between updates and comments, i.e., which update the comment addresses. To better isolate the effect of updates on donations, we controlled for comment count, which is defined as the number of comments for updates posted on a particular day. Fundraisers can also respond to these messages and comments to emotionally connect with funders and address their concerns. We thus included the number of replies to comments (reply count).

As discussed above, intrinsic philanthropy affected funders’ decision to donate. While not directly observable, we partially captured this effect by controlling for the funder’s past donation amount. The platform uses a point-based system called LovePoint to assign scores to funders based on their donation history. This feature provides an important incentive mechanism to motivate funders to donate. We thus included a variable, funder LovePoint, to measure the total score of all funders who donated on a certain day. Furthermore, we accounted for the ratio of image updates (Image Ratio), under the assumption that images may provide a more effective approach to raising funds (Wei et al., 2021). Additionally, donation count is an exposure variable that identifies the number of times funders donated to the campaign. The sum of all donations comprises the value of donation amount. It is possible that funders’ incentive to donate and the likelihood of updates may vary at different stages of the campaign. To tease out this alternative explanation, we captured day passed as the number of days since the campaign started.

In addition to the above time-varying variables, we also constructed a list of time-invariant controls at the campaign level. Platform donations measured the amount of money the crowdfunding platform donated to the campaign, which not only decreases the funding burden but also signals campaign quality. Social media posts is defined as the number of times the campaign information was shared by people (e.g., fundraisers, funders, and their connections) to their social networks. We also captured the number of prior campaigns launched for the same patient (campaign experience). At the patient level, we constructed variables for demographics and social determinants, including age, gender, type of medical condition, and insurance coverage (government and commercial).

## 3.3 Model-Free Evidence

Over 60% of the 16,635 campaigns in our sample had at least one update. On the days with updates, fundraisers mostly posted one or two updates (76% and 17%, respectively) and seldom posted more than five (<1%). To illustrate the effect of update count, Figure 1 plots the average amount of funds received in a day for each level of the daily update count. We added a locally weighted scatterplot-smoothing (LOWESS) line to visualize the trend. We observed that posting updates was generally associated with higher donations compared to the days without updates. However, the largest increase in donations occurred during the initial two updates, and the marginal improvement substantially declined as more updates were posted in a day. In effect, when fundraisers posted five updates, the average amount of donations was slightly lower than the days with four updates. Therefore, the plot shows that the relationship between donation amount and update count is not simply linear but exhibits an inverted U-curve instead.

## 4.3 Endogeneity Tests

In our econometric analyses, we included two-way fixed effects and a list of control variables that may affect both update posts and campaign donations. We also adopted a lagged independent variable approach to mitigate reverse causality. In this subsection, we implement endogeneity analyses to enhance causal identification. First, we considered the possibility that fundraisers may self-select to update due to some unobserved factors and deployed propensity score matching (PSM) to address this concern (Hassna et al., 2024; Mejia et al., 2019). Specifically, we matched campaigns with updates and those without updates based on a set of patient demographics that were excluded from the main models, including age, gender, type of medical condition, and insurance coverage (Deng et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024a). We used one-to-one matching without replacement to obtain a comparable subsample of campaigns. The covariates are balanced after PSM, as illustrated in Appendix Table D1. We examined the effect of updates (overall effect and content type) in the PSM-balanced sample with the estimates weighted by the inverse of the propensity scores. The results are presented in Table 2, Column 5, and Table 3, Column 6, and are qualitatively similar.

Second, we adopted an instrumental variable (IV) procedure to further alleviate confounding issues. In the absence of theoretically validated instruments, recent studies on crowdfunding often resort to the Lewbel IV approach to construct heteroskedasticity-based instruments (Hassna et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). This method constructs orthogonal instruments mathematically from exogenous covariates in two steps. First, each endogenous variable is regressed on all exogenous covariates to derive the residual. Second, the prespecified set of exogenous variables is meancentered and multiplied by the residual. The products are used as instruments for endogenous variables in the structural equation. Lewbel (2012) showed that the identification of this method relies on having regressors uncorrelated with the product of heteroscedastic errors, which is shown to be a common feature of many models. In our model, we designated reply count and image ratio as exogenous with respect to the outcome variable, donation amount, because these two variables are lagged and their values are not subject to funders’ decisions.<sup>12</sup> The Kleibergen-Paap rank Wald F-statistic was 2554.22, larger than the threshold for 5% relative bias, and the Hansen J overidentification test had a pvalue of 0.50, indicating the validity of our instruments. The estimation results are presented in the last column of Tables 2 and 3. Consistent with our main findings, update count had an inverted U-shaped effect on donations, primarily driven by the posted medical content. While the coefficients for nonmedical update count and the squared term were insignificant, the projected marginal effects were consistently negative for all levels of nonmedical updates and became significant if there were three or more (p-values < 0.05). Therefore, nonmedical content remained negatively associated with donations.

Furthermore, as presented in Section 6, we implemented additional endogeneity checks and robustness analyses. These analyses led to consistent results, enhancing the causal interpretation of our findings.

## 5 Mechanism of Updates

Our main results suggest that disclosing medical information has an inverted U-shaped effect on donations, whereas the impact of nonmedical content is negative. In this section, we discuss the adoption of several NLP techniques to test the mechanisms for the distinctive effects, particularly focusing on the novelty and quantity of information disclosed in updates and the sentiment of comments posted in response to updates. The results deepen our knowledge of campaign updates and enhance theory development in medical crowdfunding (Fisher et al., 2021; Tremblay et al., 2021).

## 5.1 Information Novelty

In medical crowdfunding, the information conveyed in updates plays a critical role in maintaining funder engagement and trust. While timely and relevant updates can potentially boost funders’ willingness to contribute, repeatedly posting redundant information can cause funder fatigue, diminishing the likelihood of continued support (Xiao et al., 2021). In other words, updates may be more informative for funders and more effective for fundraising if they contain novel insights. Thus, we developed a two-dimensional evaluation method that combined topic similarity and semantic similarity to quantify the novelty of information revealed in updates. Specifically, for each update, we constructed a comparison group using the campaign introduction, all preceding updates, <sup>13</sup> and the fundraiser’s own comments on previous updates. If the update was significantly different from this set of previously released content, it likely conveyed novel information to funders. In the following subsections, we discuss how we assessed the similarity (equivalently, dissimilarity) between the focal update and its comparison group.

## 5.1.1 Topic Similarity

For topic similarity, we performed topic modeling using LDA on a distinct corpus comprising all updates and their comparison groups. We followed the same procedure for preprocessing, parameter setting, and training for identifying update types, as described in Appendix C. However, we set the number of topics to 20 to more accurately capture the nuances of each update and its comparison group. In line with previous literature (Havakhor et al., 2023), we characterized each document (i.e., update or comparison group) using a vector of topic distributions. This allowed us to evaluate the cosine similarity for each focal update and its corresponding comparison group. Following Huang et al., (2018), we converted cosine similarity to cosine distance (1 minus the cosine similarity) to assess the differences in latent topic distributions. A small cosine distance indicates that the focal update is relatively similar to its comparison group, while a value closer to 1 indicates high dissimilarity and novel content types.

## 5.1.2 Semantic Similarity

To analyze semantic similarity, we utilized the BERT-Chinese model due to its advanced WordPiece tokenization and attention-driven embedding capabilities (Devlin et al., 2019). These features make it possible to capture nuanced meanings in complex semantic texts, making the model highly effective for detailed text comparison (Lin et al., 2022). We followed the same steps in the multilabeling task to preprocess each document and tokenize the content using the BERT-Chinese model. During this process, the preprocessed Chinese texts were transformed into high-dimensional embedding vectors that encapsulated the semantic essences. Similar to topic similarity analysis, we calculated the cosine similarity for each pair of the focal update and its comparison group and then converted the cosine similarity to the cosine distance. The derived value indicated the semantic dissimilarity (novelty) of the focal update content compared to what had been previously posted.

Finally, we computed the variable update novelty as the sum of the cosine distances for topic similarity and semantic similarity for each focal update.<sup>14</sup> This approach ensured a robust evaluation of how each update contributed novel information to the ongoing narrative of the crowdfunding campaign.

## 5.2 Information Overload

Information overload occurs when an individual’s ability to process information is overwhelmed by the sheer volume or complexity of data (Edmunds & Morris, 2000). This imbalance between the available information and individuals’ processing capacity leads to reduced cognitive efficiency, resulting in disengagement and impaired decision-making (Graf & Antoni, 2022). In medical crowdfunding, updates with extensive medical details, complex terminology, or lengthy narratives increase the information volume funders must process, heightening the risk of overload. In this section, we validate this mechanism by investigating the amount of information conveyed in updates.

## 5.2.1 Information Quantity

The likelihood of experiencing information overload varies across individuals depending on factors such as cognitive capacity, prior knowledge, and ability to handle complex information. Since these individual heterogeneities were not directly observable, it is difficult to measure how many funders experienced overload due to a specific update. We resorted to quantifying the information quantity as a workaround. The rationale is that higher information quantity may increase the likelihood of exceeding one’s cognitive thresholds, elevating the risk of overload. Consequently, more funders subjected to high information quantity would have experienced information overload, compared to those exposed to lower information quantity. We thus speculate that while information disclosure initially is beneficial as it can resolve information asymmetry, cognitive overload may arise when an excessive amount of information is presented, adversely affecting fundraising. That is, information quantity likely exhibits an inverted U-shaped effect on donations, where the downward part is attributed to information overload.

Following Goes et al., (2014), we employed lexical density as a proxy for information quantity . <sup>16</sup> Specifically, we preprocessed each update by removing special characters, numbers, and stop words, leaving only meaningful content related to the campaign context. We then segmented the cleaned text into individual words and designated the total number of meaningful words as the lexical density score. A higher value indicates a greater amount of contextual information in the update that could inform funders’ donation decisions and increase the probability of information overload.

## 5.2.2 Effect of Information Quantity

Similar to update novelty, we conduct update-level analyses to examine the association between update type and information quantity and the subsequent impact on donations. Table 5 represents the results. In Columns 1-3, we observe that medical updates tend to contain more contextual information, compared to nonmedical updates. However, Columns 4 and 5 illustrate that the effect of information quantity on donations is not linear but has an inverted U-shape: A great amount of information initially can enhance fundraising, yet the benefits are attenuated when excessive information is disclosed (i.e., information overload). Consistent with our conjecture, the results underscore information overload as the mechanism that explains the downward effect of updates.

To further validate this finding, we separated information quantity in medical and nonmedical updates and conducted campaign-day level analyses, presented in Appendix G. We found that information quantity consistently exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with donations. Interestingly, the pattern only emerged in medical updates, whereas information quantity and its quadratic term were insignificant for nonmedical updates. We illustrate the relationship between information quantity and donations for each update type in Figure 4. Here, we observe that medical updates exhibit a clear inverted U-pattern, whereas the curve for nonmedical updates does not significantly fluctuate. Therefore, the results support the mechanism of information overload through which the inverted U-shaped effect of medical updates is substantiated.

Comments represent a major channel for funders to communicate publicly with fundraisers. Funders often leave encouraging notes to comfort patients and their families (Kim et al., 2016). However, as demonstrated in Appendix B, they may also express concerns, question campaign credibility, and ask for clarifications (Mollick, 2014; Wei et al., 2021). In other words, funders’ perceptions of the updates and campaigns can be revealed in their comments, which also likely reflect their decision to donate and may also influence the attitudes of other potential funders (Xiao et al., 2021). Given that these comments are made after each update and are directly related to the focal update, any negative comments left beneath an update can be attributed to that update. <sup>17</sup> Therefore, we utilized these comments to examine the variations in funders’ sentiment attributed to updates. This helped further validate the role of information novelty and overload in driving the effect of updates on donations.<sup>18</sup>

## 6 Robustness Checks

Our main results reveal the inverted U-shaped relationship between update frequency and donations, contingent on the type of content disclosed. In this section, we outline the robustness checks conducted to address several empirical concerns. For brevity, we summarize these analyses in Table 7 and present the details in the Appendix.

First, we implemented additional analyses to further address endogeneity concerns (see Appendix D). We adopted alternative matching approaches, including coarsened exact matching, look-ahead PSM, and generalized propensity score approach to address the self-selection of updates. To alleviate concerns about the Lewbel IVs, we implemented a plausibly exogenous approach to examine the coefficients of key regressors when the exogeneity assumption was relaxed. We also adopted the copula-based joint estimation method to construct alternative latent IVs. Further, we quantified the magnitude of omitted variable bias by estimating the portion of the sample that needed to be replaced with cases of zero effect to invalidate our results. Moreover, we conducted a falsification test by estimating the effect of random updates. Lastly, we classified update types using the LDA-based topic weights (see Appendix E) to alleviate measurement error induced by the BERT-Chinese model. These analyses led to consistent findings, enhancing the causal link between updates and donations.

One reason that funders may not donate is simply because they are unaware of the campaign. To alleviate this concern of campaign visibility, we adopted subsample analyses where we split campaigns into more homogeneous groups using the number of times a campaign is shared on social media and the amount of donations from the crowdfunding platform (see Appendix I). Further, it is possible that fundraisers who fail in previous campaigns may launch other campaigns and actively post updates to elicit donations. To address this concern, we removed campaigns from fundraisers who had previously run campaigns to focus on fundraisers with no previous campaign experience (see Appendix J). We also combined campaigns managed by the same fundraisers and performed fundraiser-day level analyses.

To alleviate concerns about systematic differences between campaigns with different numbers of updates, we performed subsample analyses (see Appendix K) to examine campaigns where the fundraisers posted updates or multiple updates in a day. Additionally, we tested the robustness of our results with additional control variables (see Appendix L), including the readability of updates, the number of words in updates, cumulative funds pledged, province-specific time trends, and donations received by other campaigns.

We assessed the sensitivity of our findings to alternative variable definitions (see Appendices M and N). Specifically, we combined updates that were posted within a certain time window and imposed a threshold to cap (winsorize) the number of daily updates to avoid outlier effects. Regarding donations, we reconstructed future donations for the subsequent three to 10 days. We also examined the effect of updates on the total amount of donations, aggregated throughout the entire campaign, and the likelihood of meeting funding goals. Lastly, we adjusted the donation amount according to the funding goal to measure campaign performance.

We also examined the heterogenous effect of updates (see Appendices O and P). Specifically, we separated funds from new or returning funders based on funder ID and donation history and examined the effect of updates on each type of donation. It is possible that the impact of updates may depend on preceding updates or change over time. We thus accounted for campaign progress using the days passed and the number of past updates. The results of these analyses are largely consistent, demonstrating the robustness of our findings.

## 7 Discussion

## 7.1 Theoretical Contributions

Our research makes notable contributions to the extant literature on medical crowdfunding. Past studies on crowdfunding have often utilized aggregated observations at the campaign level, providing limited insights regarding the dynamics during campaigns (Mollick, 2014; Mollick & Nanda, 2016; Song et al., 2022). In this study, we collected a unique dataset that captures granular information on campaign activities, which allowed us to inspect the fundraising process and examine the role of campaign operations in driving donations. Specifically, we focused on disclosing updates about campaign details, a critical tool for fundraisers to elicit donations that is not fully understood in the literature. While existing work has examined the impact of updates, most studies have considered posting updates to be a binary decision and predominantly focused on its positive effect (Bhargava et al., 2024; Mejia et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2024a). Our study extends the literature by revealing the inverted Ushaped relationship between update frequency and funders’ willingness to contribute, as well as the heterogeneous impact of update content on donations. In particular, disclosing medical progress in updates initially improves donations; however, the impact is attenuated as the update frequency increases. In contrast, posting nonmedical updates is associated with lower donation amounts. Thus, our results shed light on how the frequency and content of campaign updates influence funders’ decisions within medical crowdfunding, illuminating the optimal strategy of posting updates to increase donations.

The results uncover two distinctive mechanisms—information overload and novelty—that explain the impact of different types of updates and offer key implications for theorizing the two-sided effect of updates from the information disclosure perspective. For ease of presentation, we summarize our findings in Figure 5, following the framework outlined by Haans et al., (2016). In particular, an inverted U-shaped relationship can be conceptualized as the result of two latent, countervailing forces that jointly affect outcomes.

In our theorization, Column A illustrates the positive impact of updates on donations. Previous studies have considered campaign updates to be an important tool to close the information gap between fundraisers and funders, and signal the quality of campaigns (Bhargava et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024a).<sup>21</sup> Therefore, through the lens of information asymmetry and the signaling effect, disclosing campaign updates can positively impact fundraising, as indicated by the upward trend. <sup>22</sup> However, it is important to note that we only present the positive effect of medical updates because this type of update is related to the purpose of the campaign and addresses what the funders care about, i.e., patient care (Lee et al., 2019). In other words, we theorize that posting nonmedical content may not significantly increase donations since such updates do not incorporate issue-relevant insights and are of less informational value for resolving uncertainty.

In contrast, Column B illustrates the negative impact of updates. Our empirical results imply that the main drivers behind this are reduced novelty and information overload, respectively, for nonmedical and medical updates. Specifically, we observe a decreasing trend of novelty for nonmedical updates in Figure 3, suggesting that fundraisers who frequently post such updates are likely including redundant content. According to twofactor theory, funders may find these repetitive updates tedious in that they arouse boredom and annoyance, leading to negative impressions and wear-out effects. Furthermore, frequent redundant updates can also create challenges in extracting useful information, spurring concerns and doubts among funders. Consequently, funders may perceive the campaign to be not impactful, lowering their interest in engaging and donating.

Regarding medical updates, Table 5 indicates that this type of content includes more contextual information and medical terms, which may be difficult for potential funders to comprehend. When frequent medical updates present extensive details or lengthy narratives, funders may experience information overload due to their cognitive processing limits. As a result, they may feel cognitively burdened and emotionally disengaged, negatively impacting their donation behavior (Figure 4).

Indeed, our analysis of comment sentiment indicates that novel updates are more likely to be positively perceived by funders, while information quantity exhibits a U-shaped association with negative comments (Table 6). Therefore, frequent updates, depending on content type, may negatively affect fundraising from the information novelty and overload perspectives. In addition, we posit that the negative impact tends to escalate rapidly with the number of updates, as the likelihood of redundancy and the burden of information processing may increase exponentially. Therefore, we theorize that the negative effect of update frequency on donations generally exhibits a decreasing concave form, as illustrated in Figure 5, Column B.

Column C demonstrates the net effect of campaign updates, reconciling the two-sided effects of information disclosure. In particular, while posting medical information can greatly reduce uncertainty and stimulate trust for funders, increasing update frequency also comes with an escalating cognitive burden. After the turning point, the negative factors start to dominate, leading to the inverted U-shaped relationship. In contrast, from the information disclosure standpoint, nonmedical updates provide less informative content to facilitate donation decisions and tend to be superfluous. As a result, the predicted net effect would follow the same downward trend as the negative impact.

Overall, ours is one of the first studies to reveal the unintended consequence of campaign updates on fundraising. The phenomenon-based theorization, coupled with our empirical analyses, indicates that the effect of posting campaign updates should not be theorized as linear. Instead, it needs to take into account the bidirectional nature of information disclosure, contingent on the nuances in the content of updates. While bound in our empirical analyses, our theorization of campaign updates may not be comprehensive, and we call for future work to further validate and extend the proposed framework (Fisher et al., 2021; Tremblay et al., 2021). Nonetheless, this work identifies an evolving research pipeline to fully understand the role of campaign updates in crowdfunding. We suggest that examining update disclosure as a binary treatment may not be accurate and that researchers should take into account how frequently the update is posted and what content of information is released, an overlooked aspect that has not been theorized in the literature.

## 7.2 Practical Implications

Increasing healthcare expenditures have become a global concern. According to a recent report from the World Health Organization (WHO, 2019), global health spending is shifting upward faster than the rest of the economy, accounting for 10% of the global gross domestic product. Consequently, many patients cannot afford the necessary treatments and may resort to medical crowdfunding to alleviate financial stress. However, despite the promise, medical campaigns tend to have a high failure rate, limiting their efficacy in addressing this societal issue. This work examines the effect of update disclosure, an important aspect of campaign operations, and thus offers important implications for practice.

Due to concerns about health conditions, fundraisers may frequently post up-to-date details in an endeavor to promptly raise sufficient funds. Our results imply that such a strategy may not lead to desirable outcomes. In particular, while updating campaign progress can be beneficial, recklessly posting multiple updates per day could backfire and reduce donations because funders may feel cognitively fatigued by the need to digest the incessant notifications. Thus, the update decisions need to be made carefully to strike a balance between the urgency of the medical condition and the strain on funders. For instance, by strategically spreading out update activities, fundraisers could potentially lower the frequency of posts without compromising the benefits of information disclosure.

Furthermore, our results suggest that fundraisers should carefully select the update topics to improve the quality of the information and mitigate the unintended effects of frequent updates. On the one hand, fundraisers should focus more on medical updates, such as the patient’s health condition, or future treatment plans recommended by medical professionals. The credible-sounding words in updates may increase the reliability of the information, leading to a higher level of donations. On the other hand, irrelevant or trivial information, such as simply posting individuals’ feelings, thank-you messages, or funding progress, can be tedious and ineffective for increasing donations. This observation thus suggests a possible way to boost donations when posting updates. By focusing on the type and content of updates, fundraisers can effectively communicate with individuals who are more likely to be interested and motivated to contribute.

In addition, useful update strategies can be derived from the results of our NLP techniques so that the updates are more likely to be meaningful. Specifically, we found that updates that present fresh insights are more favorably associated with donations than those that contain repetitive content. This result implies that the update strategy needs to consider the novelty of content that can generate informational value, as prospective funders respond better to value-added information compared to redundant or vague information. Furthermore, fundraisers should avoid lengthy narratives or complex medical terminologies, as more is not always better. For example, they should take care to include only the necessary details, providing sufficient information to persuade funders without overwhelming them. If health-related information is needed to clarify concerns, fundraisers should use plain language instead of professional jargon. Simpson and Prusak, (1995) propose five elements of value in information: truth, guidance, scarcity, accessibility, and weight. They suggest that any process that can add value to information will entail at least one of these five elements. Thus, fundraisers should strategically incorporate these considerations when drafting content for updates so that the information they deliver to funders is imbued with confidence, value, and weight, making funders less likely to neglect or overlook the messages.

Lastly, fundraisers should also consider the opinions of potential funders regarding the campaign. In this light, comments provide an important avenue not only for funders to express their wishes and concerns but also for fundraisers to observe these otherwise latent perceptions. Our findings suggest that fundraisers should diligently read and respond to these comments. Replying to a comment is analogous to customizing a solution to address a specific funder’s concern, which can counter negative emotions and elicit favorable attitudes. Moreover, regardless of the tone and sentiment, comments that are posted in response to an update indicate that this campaign has attracted more attention; therefore, the fundraiser’s replies will continue to be more visible because these conversations can be viewed publicly on the campaign website. Thus, considerate responses to commented concerns can send a strong signal to enhance transparency and credibility and foster a more positive perception among potential funders.

Our results also provide guidance for platforms interested in facilitating fundraising. For example, it would be useful to develop and implement a feature devoted to notifying or constraining fundraisers when multiple updates are posted in a day. The platform could also suggest specific language or phrases that fundraisers can use in updates as a strategy to increase donations.

## 8 Conclusion

Facing overwhelming medical bills, patients and their families often turn to crowdfunding for financial support. While ensuring campaign transparency can benefit fundraising, frequent disclosure of information through updates may not be ideal in medical crowdfunding. Our analyses suggest that posting campaign updates has an inverted U-shaped effect on both current and future donations. As the update frequency increases, donations initially rise but later decline. We further deployed several NLP models to analyze the attributes of update content, which indicates that the effect varies across content types. Specifically, disclosing medical progress, such as patient status and treatment plans, increases donations, but the effect is attenuated as more updates are posted. In contrast, including nonmedical content is associated with less funds. Furthermore, our findings suggest that the heterogeneous effect of update type can be explained by the novelty and volume of information revealed in updates, which determines funders’ perceptions of updates and subsequent donations. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first to theorize the inverted U-shaped effect of campaign updates in medical crowdfunding and demonstrate the distinctive mechanisms. Our results uncover the two-sided nature of information disclosure in fundraising, where excessive updates may induce a wearout effect, adversely influencing donations.

Our study has several limitations. First, we take a phenomenon-based approach, and our primary findings are based on medical crowdfunding, where funders do not expect monetary or in-kind returns. As such, their affective feedback to stimuli such as updates might differ from other types of campaigns. Furthermore, our results need to be interpreted with caution because the funders in this study were mostly Chinese. It is possible that people from other cultures would respond differently to repeated updates; we could not capture this in this study and thus recognize it as a limitation. Lastly, our NLP approaches are specifically designed and trained for Chinese linguistic settings, which may limit their generalizability to other languages. Nevertheless, our study opens an interesting avenue for future scholarly work on information disclosure and crowdfunding.

## Appendix C: Classifying Types of Update Content

Since our data does not include predefined content categories, we analyzed the textual content of updates in two steps to classify update types.<sup>23</sup> First, we considered the content type as latent topics and implemented topic modeling to map potential topic types across updates (Wu et al., 2024b). Second, we assigned relevant types to each update using multilabel classification. In this appendix, we present the detailed procedures.

## Identifying Topic Types

Unlike other languages with clear word boundaries, Chinese text generally lacks explicit spaces between words, which makes word segmentation intricate. To address this challenge, we first removed all stop words, punctuation, and other processing characters. Then, we followed Xu et al., (2017) to segment contents into words using the Jieba Python library.

After that, we employed LDA to extract underlying topics (Blei et al., 2003). The LDA algorithm characterizes each document by a probability distribution of latent topics and each latent topic by a distribution over all words. In our context, each update content was a document, and the LDA output for each update was a distribution over update topics. In the training process, we set the number of topics to 10 and configured the LDA model to iterate over training passes ranging from 30 to 230 with an increment of 20. During each iteration, we optimized document topic density and topic word density. Our final LDA model was selected based on the highest coherence score.

Table C1 presents the top 10 most frequent words across the 10 identified topics. We observed that many topics share common words. Therefore, we followed the practices of Atkins et al., (2012), Bao and Datta, (2014), and Quinn et al., (2010) to categorize the 10 topics into five distinct types based on the most frequent words in each topic: patient status, treatment plan, campaign progress, thank-you note, and personal feelings. The first two types are closely related to medical progress in that the treatment plan outlines the treatments and procedures the patient will undergo, while the patient status provides details on the patient’s health condition, medical treatments received, and doctors’ diagnoses. The other three types are not medically oriented: campaign progress includes details on health-related expenditures, funding needs (donation requests), and calls for promotional actions (such as sharing on social networks); thank-you note offers gratitude for financial support; and personal feelings encompasses emotional reflections on the fundraisers challenges and experiences.

We conducted three validations to ensure that these five types comprehensively covered the update topics. First, we examined representative excerpts for each topic based on the frequency of word occurrences to verify that the LDA model accurately captured the most prevalent words. Second, we increased the number of topics to 15 and 20 and retrained the LDA model using the same process. As shown in Tables C2 and C3, the most frequent words remained broadly consistent across the LDA models. Further, we consistently identify the same five labels for 15 and 20 topics according to the most frequent words in each topic. This consistency demonstrates the reliability and validity of our topic modeling results. Third, following Huang et al., (2018), we consulted with domain experts in crowdfunding who confirmed the relevance and accuracy of the five labels.

## Assigning Topic Types to Each Update

After obtaining the five type labels, we assigned relevant types to each update. Given the rich text content in updates, one update could involve multiple types. Therefore, the task of assigning appropriate types is essentially a multilabel classification. We employed supervised learning algorithms to handle this task. In the first step, we created our training and testing samples. We compiled a set of 900 randomly selected updates and hired two independent coders who were fluent in Chinese to annotate these updates using the criteria based on the topic modeling results and feedback from domain experts in crowdfunding (Mousavi & Gu, 2024; Zhou et al., 2023). Following prior research (Oh et al., 2023), we measured the interrater reliability of the two human coders using Cohen’s kappa (Cohen, 1960). The Cohen’s kappa coefficient of the two coders’ labeling was 0.929, and the overall agreement percentage was 96.64%. This indicates that the two coders had a substantially high level of agreement, supporting the validity of manual labeling (Nowak & Rüger, 2010). We used 80% of the labeled sample for training and 20% for testing.

Next, we implemented three prominent NLP algorithms to determine the most efficient method for the multilabel classification task, namely, SVM (Ahmad et al., 2018), LSTM (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997), and BERT-Chinese (Devlin et al., 2019). We explored key parameters to fine-tune each model.

For the SVM model, we focused on two key parameters. The first parameter is the regularization parameter (C), for which we chose values of 0.1, 1, and 10. This range covers three orders of magnitude, allowing us to explore different trade-offs between margin maximization and misclassification minimization. The second parameter was the class weight; we explored using no or balanced class weights to compare the model’s performance with and without adjusting for class imbalance.

For the LSTM model, we tested three parameters. First, we tested the number of LSTM layers, for which we included the values of 1 and 2. This allowed us to compare the performance of a single LSTM layer with a stacked LSTM architecture. The second parameter was the number of LSTM units, where we tested 64, 128, and 256 units. This explored different capacities for the LSTM layers, from smaller to larger representations. Additionally, we varied the number of dense layers, testing both one and two layers, to evaluate the effect of adding an additional fully connected layer after the LSTM. For the dense units, we included values of 64, 128, and 256, varying the size of the dense layers similarly to the LSTM units. To address regularization, we explored dropout rates of 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7, examining different levels of regularization from moderate to aggressive dropout. The learning rate was another crucial parameter, and we tested values of 0.01, 0.001, and 0.0001 to find the optimal learning dynamics for the Adam optimizer.

In addition to these parameters, we fixed several other settings, including a 64-dimensional embedding layer for a vocabulary of 1000 words, an input sequence length padded to 100 tokens, and an output layer with a dense layer using sigmoid activation for multilabel classification. The model was trained for 10 epochs with a batch size of 32 and a 10% validation split. This comprehensive grid search explores 324 different model configurations (based on parameter setups), aiming to find the optimal combination of network depth, width, regularization, and learning rate for the multilabel text classification task. This thorough exploration allowed us to balance model complexity and potential for overfitting, ensuring robust and accurate performance.

For the BERT-Chinese model, we explored two key parameters. The first was the learning rate, and we included 1e-5, 2e-5, 3e-5, and 4e-5, following Devlin et al., (2019). This range enabled the exploration of different learning dynamics, from the most conservative (1e-5) to aggressive (4e-5) updates. The second parameter was epsilon in Adam, a small constant added to the denominator to prevent division by zero and improve numerical stability. We include values of 1e-6, 1e-7, and 1e-8. Additionally, as Devlin et al., (2019) suggested, we used three epochs to train our model. Finally, we fit the model using the Adam optimizer and employed a grid search approach to find the optimal combination of parameters that minimize loss.

Due to the nuances of multilabel classification, our primary metric to evaluate model performance was the macroaveraged F1 score (Aguilar-Ruiz & Michalak, 2024). The SVM model’s best F1 score was 0.747 with a C of 5 and the hinge loss function. The LSTM model’s highest F1 score was 0.734 with one LSTM layer, 128 LSTM units, one dense layer, 128 dense units, a 0.5 dropout rate, and a 0.01 learning rate. The fine-tuned BERT-Chinese model’s highest F1 score was 0.776 with a learning rate of 4e-5 and an epsilon in Adam 1e-6. Therefore, the fine-tuned BERT-Chinese model had the best performance compared with the other two models and was used for the multilabel classification task of update types.

## Appendix D: Additional Endogeneity Checks for Main Analyses

This appendix presents additional analyses for endogeneity checks. First, we checked whether PSM adequately balanced the patient demographics between campaigns with updates and those without updates. The results are presented in Table D1. We observed substantial mean differences between these two campaign groups in the unmatched sample (i.e., before PSM), as indicated by the standardized percentage bias and significant t-tests. After matching, the bias became minimal, and the t-tests of mean equality were statistically insignificant, suggesting that the PSM reduced bias associated with these observed patient demographics.

Second, we also used CEM, which has been argued to generate matched datasets with lower imbalance (Iacus et al., 2012). We matched campaigns on the same set of patient demographic variables. We coarsened the continuous variable (patient age) into 10 equally spaced bins and used Sturge’s rule to coarsen other variables. The CEM substantially reduced the multivariate L1 distance from 0.172 to 0.121 and improved covariate balance based on univariate distances. We reran the main analyses using the matched sample. The CEM generated weights for each campaign, which were used to weigh the estimates. We present the results in Table D2, Columns 1 and 2. Notably, while the coefficients for nonmedical update count and its squared term are insignificant, the projected marginal effects are consistently negative (p-values < 0.02). Therefore, our main findings remain unchanged.

Third, traditional matching methods (PSM and CEM) may not account for unobserved factors, as they could only match campaigns on observables. To address this issue, we implemented the look-ahead PSM (LA-PSM) procedure, following Bapna et al., (2018). Since the median time to post the first update was on Day 2, we used this as the cutoff to classify early or late updates (Nian et al., 2021), although our results remained consistent using Day 1 to Day 5 or the median date of campaign duration. Then, we excluded campaigns without updates and only matched campaigns with early updates to those with late updates. By doing so, the matched sample ensured that the two groups of campaigns were similar in unobserved characteristics that drive the decision to update since they eventually posted updates. Consequently, the two groups of campaigns only differed in the timing of updates, which was used to identify the effect on donations. We estimated the main models using the matched sample and present the results in Table D2, Columns 3 and 4; the results are qualitatively consistent.

Fourth, while PSM, LA-PSM, and CEM could address the binary decision to post updates, the number of updates may also be endogenous. We adopted a GPS matching method to mitigate this issue. GPS is an extension of the propensity score for binary treatment, developed to address continuous variables (Hirano & Imbens, 2004; Imai & van Dyk, 2004). This approach first estimates the conditional density of update count, conditional on all observed control variables and patient sociodemographics. We then derived the propensity for each level of update count, which was used as the weight to adjust the estimates (Hammersley, 2013; Robins et al., 2000). The results are largely similar in Table D2, Columns 5 and 6. Through alternative matching approaches, we further ensured that our results were robust to the self-selection of updates.

Regarding omitted variable concerns, Ravichandran et al., (2017) contended that endogeneity due to confounding factors is less of a concern when models contain interaction terms and quadratics. This is because for the quadratic term to be endogenous, the omitted variable would not only need to correlate with both the key independent variables and the dependent variable but it would also have to influence the outcome in a distinctly nonlinear fashion (Liu et al., 2024). Thus, we argue that the specific nature of the inverted U-shape makes omitted variable bias far less likely. Nonetheless, we deployed the Lewbel IV procedure in the main manuscript to alleviate this concern. We now present additional analyses to supplement it.

First, we quantified the magnitude of bias associated with confounders that can invalidate the estimated effect, following the framework in Frank et al., (2013). This approach employs Rubin’s causal model and calculates the proportion of the sample to be replaced with cases where the effect is zero to invalidate an inference. We present the ratio, termed inference bias, for each key coefficient in Table D3. The results suggest that we would need to replace at least 57.41%, 45.80%, and 22.63% of the sample to invalidate the estimated effect of update count, medical updates, and nonmedical updates, respectively. Given these large percentages, we are confident that the results are unlikely to have been driven by omitted confounders.

Second, while the Hansen J tests failed to reject the null hypothesis, one may still question the exogeneity of the Lewbel IVs. We sought to alleviate this concern in several ways. Conley et al. (2012, p. 261) state: “The sensitivity of the 2SLS estimator of β to violations of the exclusion restriction depends on the strength of the instruments. For example, relatively minor deviations from the exclusion restriction may greatly decrease precision … when instruments are weak, whereas large deviations may have only small influences on precision when the instruments are strong … In many applications, instruments can yield informative results even under appreciable deviations from an exact exclusion restriction.”

Following this rationale, our Lewbel IVs are highly correlated with the endogenous variables (based on the Kleibergen-Paap rank Wald F-statistics), enhancing the confidence on the validity of the Lewbel IV results.

Further, we implemented the plausibly exogenous approach to directly address the robustness of our results due to the potential violation of the exclusion restriction in IV estimation (Conley et al., 2012; Oh et al., 2023). This approach relaxed the exclusion restriction that IVs have no correlation with the unobserved factors by allowing for the construction of IV bounds under weaker-than-traditional assumptions. For ease of interpretation, we illustrate the results in Figure D1, which suggest that the estimated coefficients of update count and types (except for nonmedical update count) remain far from zero for various levels of correlation (i.e., violation of exogeneity assumption), thus supporting the validity of our main findings. In addition, we adopted the copula-based joint estimation method to further account for potential endogeneity (Park & Gupta, 2012). This approach used Gaussian copulas to model the correlation between updates and the idiosyncratic error terms as additional regressors to disentangle confounding factors. It relies on the nonnormality of the endogenous variables for model identification and does not require external IVs. We implemented the copula-based IV model using the REndo package in R. As shown in Table D4, the results remain consistent.

Lastly, we conducted a falsification test using random implementation models to examine whether the effect of updates was due to random shocks on the platform (Burtch et al., 2018; Greenwood & Agarwal, 2016; Wang et al., 2023). Specifically, we first randomly selected campaigns that posted updates as the placebo group and then generated a random number of updates (overall, medical, and nonmedical) for each day following the true distribution of the update count. We reran the main models using the randomized sample and repeated this procedure 1,000 times. The results are summarized in Table D5. For the falsified updates, the means of coefficients β are small and not significantly different from zero. Furthermore, the distribution of coefficients is significantly different from the coefficients in our main analysis, as indicated by the z-scores and p-values. The only exception is nonmedical update because its effect is also indistinguishable from zero in the main results. The results of the random implementation model suggest that it is unlikely that a similar effect of updates could have been obtained by chance. That is, our main results are not spurious or driven by random confounders.

Collectively, these additional tests suggest that our main findings are robust after taking endogeneity into account, thus validating the causal effect of update count and types on the donation amount

## Appendix E: Effect of LDA-Based Update Type

Recent studies suggest that the results of subsequent econometric analyses could be biased due to the bias induced by the measurement error in classification tasks (Qiao & Huang, 2021; Yang et al., 2018). To alleviate this issue, we used the topic weights derived from the LDA model, as detailed in Appendix C, to classify update types. Specifically, we categorized the 10 topics from the LDA results into five distinct types based on the most frequent words in each topic (see Appendix C). The LDA model assigned each update a distribution over topics, which represents the probability of the update belonging to each of the 10 topics. We calculated the probability for each label by summing the probabilities of the topics associated with that label. Finally, we assigned all appropriate labels to the update if the aggregated probabilities for these labels exceeded 0.5.<sup>24</sup>

The rationale is that the two machine learning algorithms (i.e., LDA and BERT-Chinese) will have different errors and that obtaining similar results in both models would therefore signal the validity of our findings. Therefore, we reestimated the main model for the effect of update types using the LDA-based update types to reduce the impact of measurement error induced by the BERT-Chinese model. This remedy has been used in several recent IS studies (Gao et al., 2022; Mousavi & Gu, 2024). Table E1 presents qualitatively consistent results, suggesting that our main findings are robust to the measurement error induced by the BERT-Chinese model.

## Appendix F: Additional Tests for Update Novelty Based on Repeated Information in Updates

In our main analyses, we demonstrated the heterogeneous effect of medical and nonmedical updates on donations. Specifically, disclosing medical information in updates initially can elicit more funds, but the effect is attenuated as more updates are posted. In contrast, including nonmedical content in updates is associated with lower donation amounts. According to our theoretical framework, we contend that the heterogeneous effect of update type is driven by two different mechanisms: (1) The inverted U-shaped effect of medical updates is due to the amount of contextual information conveyed, which could initially resolve information asymmetry and help gain funders’ trust but may also lead to cognitive overload if too much is included. (2) The negative effect of nonmedical updates is attributed to the decline of information novelty. Our update-level analysis in Figure 3 provides empirical evidence showing that the extent of information novelty in nonmedical updates drops substantially as more updates are posted, whereas the trend for medical updates is quite stable.

In this appendix, we further support this argument by investigating the role of repeated information in updates that affect donations. Our aim is to delineate information novelty as the mechanism that drives the impact of nonmedical updates (vs. medical updates) on donations. While almost no updates contain exactly the same text, the content could be highly similar. Thus, we classify an update as repeated if the topic similarity and semantic similarity are above the respective medians (i.e., a novel update).<sup>25</sup> A repeated update means its textual content is highly similar to previous updates.

Aligning with Table 4, we first conducted update-level analyses to illustrate the effect of repeated updates on donations,<sup>26</sup> contingent on update type. Table F1 presents the results. As shown in Column 1, repeated updates are associated with a 5.37% lower donation amount, compared with nonrepeated (i.e., novel) updates. This association remained consistent after we introduced the basic effect of update type, as represented by medical update and nonmedical update (Column 2). To examine the effect of repeated updates for each update type, we interacted repeated update with medical update and nonmedical update as additional independent variables in Column 3.<sup>27</sup> Although the coefficients for the basic effect of update type and the interaction terms appear to be insignificant, we project the marginal effect of repeated updates for each update type. Interestingly, we observed that the effect of repeated medical updates equals 0.0101 (-0.0344 + 0.0445) and is not significant (p-value = 0.62). In contrast, the effect of repeated updates when the update type is nonmedical equals -0.0621 (-0.0344 – 0.0277) and is significant (p-value < 0.01). The results suggest that repeated information in nonmedical updates is associated with lower donation amounts while repetitive medical updates do not exhibit a significant impact on donations. In other words, nonmedical updates tend to reduce donations through repetitive content (i.e., lower information novelty) while the effect of medical updates does not materialize through information novelty.

Furthermore, we performed additional analyses at the campaign-day level (consistent with the main specification in Tables 2 and 3) to bolster the findings. Specifically, we aggregated the binary indicator repeated update to the campaign-day level to calculate the number (count) of updates that contain repeated information on day t for each type of update. We also constructed the number of updates that contained novel content. These variables were used as the key independent variables to examine the impact on donations. We report the results in a hierarchical manner in Table F2 and F3 for update count and update type, respectively.

In Table F2, Column 1, we observe that repeated update count is negatively associated with donations, consistent with the update-level results in Table F1. Further, the effect of repeated updates is exacerbated when more repetitive content is posted, as indicated by the negative coefficient of repeated update count<sup>2</sup> in Column 2. In sharp contrast, the coefficient of novel update count is positive and significant in Columns 3 and 4 after controlling for repeated updates, suggesting that funders are more likely to donate to campaigns when fundraisers post novel content in updates. These results underscore information novelty as a mechanism that drives the impact of updates on donations.

Is this mechanism uniform for both medical and nonmedical updates? We answer this question in Table F3. Consistent with the update-level results in Table F1, Column 1 presents a negative impact that repeated nonmedical updates have on donations, while the association between repeated medical updates and donations is insignificant. Column 2 includes quadratic terms, showing that more repeated nonmedical updates may further reduce donations, as suggested by the negative and significant repeated nonmedical update count.<sup>2</sup> Interestingly, repeated medical updates have an inverted U-shaped effect on donations. However, note that results in Columns 1 and 2 are not sufficient to draw conclusions about the role of information novelty for update types, as we also need to compare repeated and novel updates, which are presented in Columns 3 and 4. In particular, we see that the coefficients for repeated and novel medical update count and their squared terms are close in size in both columns, indicating that repeated information and novel information have comparable effects for medical updates. In other words, information novelty is unlikely to be the mechanism through which medical updates affect donations—whether repeated or not, medical updates continue to exhibit an inverted U-shaped effect, likely through other channels, i.e., information overload as we observe in Table 5.

Regarding the nonmedical updates, consistent with Column 2, the coefficient for repeated nonmedical update count<sup>2</sup> is negative and significant, indicating that more repeated nonmedical updates can exaggerate the negative impact. Unlike repeated updates, novel nonmedical updates can improve donations, as the positive coefficient suggests. This substantial difference sheds light on the novelty of information conveyed in nonmedical updates, which determines the impact on donations.

Selection issues may exist that compromise our identification. For instance, one reason that funders may not be donating is simply that they are not aware of the campaign (Burtch & Chan, 2019). On the other hand, fundraisers can increase campaign visibility to potential funders by tweeting the campaign information on social network platforms. In general, the campaign and updates are visible to funders through two channels wherein selection issues may arise: (1) information that is passively pushed to funders through social media posts,<sup>28</sup> and (2) funders actively browsing campaigns on the platform. ## Appendix J: Campaign Experience

One may question whether fundraisers might potentially initiate multiple campaigns for the same patient, leading to endogeneity concerns. For example, fundraisers who failed in previous campaigns may choose to launch another campaign and actively post updates to elicit donations. Campaign experience could also affect the effectiveness of online activities and interactions in fundraising due to learning effects (Greenberg & Gerber, 2014). However, we observed that out of 16,477 distinct fundraisers, only 110 had operated multiple campaigns, accounting for a mere 1.57% of our sample (262 campaigns). This suggests that campaign experience may not impose significant threats to our results.

To further alleviate this concern, we performed two sets of analyses. First, we removed these 262 campaigns and conducted a subsample analysis where fundraisers had not run previous campaigns prior to the current campaign (i.e., no campaign experience). As such, the fundraisers were more homogenous in terms of their update decisions. Table J1, Columns 1 and 2 present the results, which underpin the inverted U-shaped effect of update count on donations, primarily driven by medical updates. While the effect of nonmedical updates is positive, the effect is relatively small and marginally significant (p = 0.093).

Second, we combined campaigns managed by the same fundraisers to compare updates in new campaigns with those posted during previous campaigns. We conducted the same two-way fixed effects model but controlled for fundraiser and time fixed effects. The estimation results of the fundraiser-day level analysis are presented in Columns 3 and 4, leading to the same conclusions. Hence, the results suggest that our main findings are robust to concerns about campaign experience.

## Appendix L: Additional Control Variables

Although we applied multiple methods to address endogeneity, omitted variable bias may remain an issue. In this appendix, we tested the robustness of our results with additional control variables to alleviate this concern. First, our key independent variables only captured the frequency and type of updates, while the quality of content may also affect donations. For instance, depending on readability, updates may increase funders’ trust or lead to more doubts. We thus constructed a readability score for each update, similar to the Gunning Fog Index in the Chinese linguistic context (Hassna et al., 2024). Specifically, we calculated the average number of words in a clause and the percentage of adverbs and conjunctions in each sentence, which were averaged to derive the readability index. A higher value suggests that the text has a complex structure and is thus difficult to read. We included readability as an additional control and obtained consistent results, as shown in Column 1 of Tables L1 and L2.

Second, the length of updates (in Chinese characters) may also play a role, but its effect is ambiguous. Past work has considered word count to be a quality indicator of online text (Khurana et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023). However, lengthier updates are more likely to deliver similar information, obscuring the effect of update count. In Column 2, we present the number of words in updates as a control and observe that the negative effect dominates, reducing donations. Nonetheless, our main conclusions for update count and types hold.

Third, the incentive to donate may vary at different stages of the campaign. For instance, the tendency to donate to a campaign that has almost reached its funding target may differ from the tendency to donate to a new campaign. Therefore, we controlled for funding target pledged, which is defined as the ratio of total funds raised to the funding target prior to the current day. The estimates in Column 3 are similar to our main results.

Fourth, idiosyncratic time trends may exist across economic areas that our main analyses do not account for. To resolve this concern we extracted the geographic location of patients and included a province-specific linear time trend, which allowed for varying tendencies in donations across province regions, e.g., due to regional economics (Burtch et al., 2018).<sup>32</sup> The results are presented in Column 4, where we observe large consistency with our main models.

Fifth, since campaign information can be observed on the crowdfunding platform’s website, donations to a campaign may affect other campaigns, leading to a spillover effect. Thus, we included the total amount of donations that other campaigns from the same province received on the same day as a control variable, leading to similar results (Column 5).<sup>33</sup>

## Appendix M: Alternative Definitions for Updates

In this appendix, we examine alternative constructs for updates. First, we accounted for temporal heterogeneity in posting behaviors among fundraisers. For example, fundraisers may post multiple updates related to one central theme within a short period due to unique posting habits or misuse of the feature. This may result in an artificially high number of updates that should be combined into one update. To address this concern, we imposed a time window of 10, 30, or 60 minutes as the constraint to combine updates and subsequently calculate the update count and classify update types (Aaltonen & Seiler, 2016).<sup>34</sup> We used the count of overall updates and used the updates with medical content or nonmedical content as alternative independent variables to examine their association with the donation amount. Table M1, Columns 1-3 present the results for the effect of overall update count, while Table M2 Columns 1-3 present the results for the medical and nonmedical updates. We observe qualitatively consistent results, where updates, especially those that contain medical information, have an inverted U-shaped effect on donations. In contrast, nonmedical content is negatively associated with the donation amount.

Second, even though fundraisers can post multiple updates in a day, funders may not read all of them, leading to an outlier effect. To alleviate this concern, we added a threshold to cap (winsorize) the number of updates in a day and reestimated our main models (Greenwood & Agarwal, 2016). We present the results in Columns 4-6 in Tables M1 and M2, indicating that the effect of updates is amplified when a more restrictive constraint is imposed. Overall, the results suggest that our main findings are consistent with alternative definitions for updates.

## Appendix N: Alternative Definitions for Donations

We examine several alternative constructs to ensure that our findings were not driven by the definitions of donations. First, although we tested the cumulative effect of updates on subsequent donations in the main analysis, the model implicitly assumes that the past updates are equally weighted in affecting funders’ decision-making, which may not hold true. Specifically, fundraisers typically do not post updates every day, and funders may read updates that have been released for a few days. Consequently, updates in a given day may decay over time and thus only affect donations over the following few days. Considering this possibility, we aggregated donations for the subsequent three to 10 days (i.e., from t to t+3 or t+10) and examined their association with the update count on day t-1. Table N1, Columns 1-3 present the results for the effect of overall update count, while Table N2 Columns 1-3 present the results for the medical and nonmedical updates, which are largely similar to our main findings. Notably, the effect of updates was stronger with a shorter time window, consistent with our speculation regarding the dynamics of updates over time.

Second, we studied the fraction of funds raised to the fundraising target by adjusting the donation amount according to the funding goal as an alternative dependent variable (Hassna et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024a). As shown in Table N1, Column 4, the coefficient of update count is positive but insignificant, while update count<sup>2</sup> remains negative and significant, suggesting that the overall effect of updates is negative and significant. Table N2, Column 4 further indicates that medical updates consistently exhibit an inverted U-shaped effect on the adjusted donation amount. In contrast, posting nonmedical update content is projected to have a significantly negative effect (p-values < 0.09), driving the overall effect.

Third, one may argue that fundraisers care more about the overall donation amount than daily donations, as it is the ultimate measure of campaign performance. While we examined the effect of updates on the total subsequent donations, we further alleviated this concern by exploring two alternative dependent variables—total donations is the aggregated measure of the donation amount throughout the entire campaign, while campaign success is a binary variable indicating whether the total donations exceeded the funding target. It is important to note that these two variables were aggregated at the campaign level; consequently, we performed cross-sectional analyses, i.e., OLS for total donations and a logistic model for campaign success. We took the summations of independent variables and covariates, except for image ratio and day passed, for which we used the average and maximum values, respectively. In addition, we also controlled for other patient demographics, including age, gender, type of medical condition, and insurance coverage.

Column 5 illustrates the estimation results for total donations. We observe a highly consistent effect of overall updates, while the results for update types are slightly different. Specifically, the count of medical updates is positively associated with total donations throughout campaigns, while the quadratic term becomes weaker in both magnitude and significance (p-value = 0.10). Further, the effect of nonmedical content has an inverted U-shape. Together, the results suggest that updates tend to have a more positive (equivalently, less negative) impact on total donations across the entire duration of the campaign. As shown in separate analyses in Appendix P, this is because funders are more compassionate and tolerant of frequent updates toward the later stage of the campaign. When aggregating data to the campaign level, this pattern changes the overall effect of updates, mitigating the negative impact while amplifying the positive impact. Column 6 presents the results for campaign success, where we find that the overall effect of update count is insignificant. Nonetheless, the effect of medical and nonmedical updates holds steady. In sum, the results suggest that our main conclusions remain robust to alternative measures of donations.

## Appendix O: New and Returning Funders

One may question the heterogeneous effect of updates among different groups of funders. For instance, it is possible that prospective funders who have not donated (hereafter, new funders) are more likely to read and rely on updates to make donation decisions compared with those who have already donated (hereafter, returning funders). Furthermore, funders who have already donated may not contribute again, even if they receive update notifications. Consequently, the effect of updates might be more salient among new funders.

To alleviate this concern, we identified whether a donation was from a new or returning funder based on the funder ID and the associated donation history. We found that, on average, each campaign attracted 1,346 unique funders, among whom only 41 (3.05%) were returning funders. However, these returning funders made more than 95 donations and donated nearly 57 CNY each time, substantially higher than the 33 CNY donated by new funders. As a result, returning funders, although small in number, contribute a sizable portion of the funding pool, amounting to 11.37% of total funds. Therefore, we separated donations from new and returning funders and examined the effect of updates on each type of donation.<sup>35</sup>

Table O1 presents the results, where the dependent variable is the donation amount from new funders in Columns 1 and 2 and from returning funders in Columns 3 and 4. We observe that the update count continues to exhibit an inverted U-shaped effect across models, primarily due to the released medical content. Interestingly, the adverse effect of nonmedical updates only emerged among new funders. For returning funders, the effect of nonmedical updates was mostly positive, as the nonmedical update count was positive and large in magnitude compared with the negative nonmedical update count<sup>2</sup>. This nuance suggests that using nonmedical language, such as personal feelings and appreciation letters, is also important to motivate returning funders to donate. Overall, the results underscore the inverted U-shaped effect of updates on both groups of donations and thus largely support our main findings.

## Appendix P: Effect of Updates by Campaign Progress

It is possible that donations may decrease as the campaign progresses, while the number of updates may simultaneously increase, leading to a concern about alternative explanations to our findings. In our main models, we explicitly controlled the number of days that have passed since the campaign started (day passed), which largely accounts for the natural progression of crowdfunding campaigns. Additionally, the temporal trend only explains the negative association between donations and updates; it may not explain the inverted U-shape curve that we focus on in this study. Therefore, we contend that our main findings are not qualitatively altered by this concern to any great extent.

In this appendix, we further alleviate this concern by examining the heterogeneous effect of updates as the campaign progresses. We used both the number of days that have passed (day passed) and the cumulative number of past updates (cumulative past update count) to measure campaign progress and included additional interaction terms between each indicator and update variables in the models. Table P1, Columns 1 and 2 present the results, where the progress indicator is day passed, while Columns 3 and 4 examine how the effect of updates is influenced by previous updates. We observe that the interaction terms are generally positive although insignificant. This indicates that funders may tend to be more compassionate and tolerant toward the later stage of the campaign, attenuating the negative effect of frequent updates. More importantly, the coefficients for update count, medical update count, nonmedical count, and their squared terms remain consistent, as in Tables 2 and 3. Therefore, our main findings remain robust even after accounting for the campaign progress trend.

## Appendix R: Alternative Constructs of Information Quantity

To ensure that our results of information quantity were not driven by the construct of lexical density, we deployed two alternative measures. First, we deployed semantic incoherence as a proxy for information quantity. Semantic incoherence measures the degree of divergence within a document (i.e., an update) based on the concept of lexical relatedness across textual units (Halliday & Hasan, 2014). Unrelated textual units (e.g., sentences) indicate that they convey different semantic meanings and are likely about distinctive things. In light of this, we posit that when an update has a high semantic incoherence score, its content may contain various different types of information and thus have a high information quantity.

Following Qiao and Rui (2023), we constructed the semantic incoherence for each update in four steps. First, we split an update into sentences using punctuation marks such as periods, exclamation points, and question marks. If the update could not be split, we used commas or spaces as fallback delimiters. Second, we leveraged the BERT-Chinese model to tokenize each sentence and generated high-dimensional embeddings that encapsulated the semantic essence of each sentence. Third, we calculated the cosine distance between consecutive sentence embeddings to quantify the semantic distance between adjacent sentences.<sup>36</sup> Lastly, the semantic incoherence score for the update was derived by averaging these cosine distance values across all sentence pairs. The resultant semantic incoherence ranged between 0 and 1, with a higher value indicating that the update contained more unrelated content and thus a greater quantity of information. We used semantic incoherence to replace lexical density as the proxy for information quantity and examined its correlation with update type and donations. Table R1 presents the results. While the coefficients of medical and nonmedical updates are both negative in Columns 1-3, we were interested in comparing the size of the effect, which consistently suggests that medical updates had a higher score for semantic incoherence compared with nonmedical updates and thus contained more information. Further, Columns 4 and 5 demonstrate that higher incoherence (more information) initially enhances donations, while the positive impact is attenuated as the score continues to increase. Consequently, semantic incoherence, as an alternative proxy for information quantity, exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with donations, consistent with the results in Table 5.

Second, we followed the methods in Chen et al., (2019) and Huang et al., (2019) to identify medically related words in campaign updates and mapped them to professional medical terminologies. It is important to note that medical terms could appear in both medical and nonmedical updates, and our classification of update type was holistically based on the semantic meaning of the update instead of simply on the words used. While it is intuitive that medical updates should contain more medical terms, how these terminologies impact donations remains unclear. Compared with regular phrases, medical terms provide specificity and credibility as they can accurately describe the beneficiary’s condition, treatments needed, and potential outcomes (Ren et al., 2020). This precision can help funders better understand the medical situation and the urgency of the need, making the updates more informative. However, the use of medica terminologies may present cognitive challenges for funders because they may not understand complex medical terms. Therefore, we deem medical terms a suitable proxy to measure information quantity.

Specifically, we implemented a two-stage process leveraging the THUOCL (Tsinghua University Open Chinese Lexicon) medical dictionary (Han et al., 2016) to assess the concentration of medical terminologies in update content. In the first stage, we preprocessed the raw text by removing special characters and stop words, followed by segmenting sentences using the Jieba Python library, enhanced with the THUOCL medical lexicon. This enhancement improved the Jieba library’s ability to accurately identify medical terms during segmentation. In the second stage, we applied a flexible matching algorithm to identify medical terms through both exact and partial matches, accounting for cases where medical terms appear as components of larger compound phrases or in slightly modified forms. For each preprocessed word, we checked whether it either contains or is contained within any term in the medical dictionary. Lastly, we counted the number of identified medical terms in each update and used this as the alternative measure for information quantity to replicate the models in Table 5. Table R2 presents the results. As expected, Columns 1-3 demonstrate that medical updates contain more medically related terms than nonmedical updates. Interestingly, the count of medical terms has an inverted U-shaped effect on donations, as indicated by Columns 4 and 5. Consistent with our main findings, the results suggest that disclosing medical information can improve fundraising by resolving information gaps, but excessive medical details can cause cognitive stress on funders, attenuating the positive impact.
