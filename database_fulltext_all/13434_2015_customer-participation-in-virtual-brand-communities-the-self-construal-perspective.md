# Customer Participation in Virtual Brand Communities: The Self-construal Perspective

## Abstract

By introducing the self-construal theory to virtual brand community literature, this study examines how self-construal affects customer participation and how self-construal interacts with community rewards and public self-consciousness to influence customer participation. Based on data collected from two communities in China, we found that independent construal had a stronger positive direct effect on the intention to participate than did interdependent construal. The effect of independent construal on intention was attenuated by community reward and by public self-consciousness, respectively. In comparison, the effect of interdependent construal on intention was enhanced by public self-consciousness, and this strengthening effect did not exist for community rewards. A significant association between intention and actual participation was also found.

Keywords: Self-Construal, Independence, Interdependence, Community Rewards, Public Self-consciousness, Participation, Virtual Brand Communities

## 1. Introduction

Many firms have added a virtual brand community (VBC) to their marketing activities. A VBC, defined as “a specialized, non-geographically bound community based on a structured set of social relationships among admirers of a brand” ([42], p. 412), is an Internet platform for firms to disseminate product information, conduct service support, and educate customers [22, 45, 46]. A study found that 90% of VBC members are passive readers, and most of them are just “one-shot” participants who do not maintain their memberships [51]. Therefore, how to improve VBC participation and enhance community sustainability is a major challenge for firms [3, 46].

In response to the challenges, firms have developed various strategies to satisfy their customers in VBCs. For instance, firms build group platforms, such as discussion forums to meet customers’ needs for interaction and belongingness. Firms also allow members to build individualized personal spaces, such as homepages and blogs to maintain customers’ independent identities. The implementation of these strategies reflects a balanced view of homogeneity and heterogeneity of VBCs. The homogeneity view emphasizes shared norms, shared interests, a collective identity, and collective belonging in a VBC and is supported by a majority of prior VBC literature [22, 38, 42, 43]. The heterogeneity view posits that VBC participants have diverse backgrounds, different commitment, a wide range of expertise, and complex identities [59].

The heterogeneity view has recently emerged in the literature suggesting that practitioners build VBCs with the commitment to create distinctive values and provide maximum freedom for individuals to express their diverse opinions, rather than disciplining these individuals in order to maintain shared bonds or respectful interactions [15, 18, 34, 59]. On the other hand, supporters of the homogeneous view note that VBC individuals look for mechanisms to connect with others to satisfy their need for belonging [14, 42]. Homogeneity and heterogeneity may not be conflicting, but exist simultaneously [20]. While heterogeneity may incur conflict that destabilizes a community [18], it may also strengthen community sustainability if effective practices can be implemented to align customers with shared norms in VBCs [59].

How do members with different interpersonal orientations toward one another affect VBC participation? How can VBC practitioners manage these heterogeneous orientations collectively? Prior literature does not provide clear answers. In order to fill these gaps and reconcile the homogeneity view and heterogeneity view, we developed

#

a research model based on three theories, which are self-construal theory [57], cognitive evaluation theory [19], and impression management theory [32]. We first applied self-construal theory [57] to describe how different orientations toward the self may affect an individual’s participation in VBC. Focusing on the relationship between self and others, self-construal means the extent to which individuals see themselves as individualized entities (independent construal) or in relation with others (interdependent construal) [57, 58]. Self-construal, therefore, represents a spectrum of heterogeneous motivations toward interpersonal relationships, where interdependent individuals seek commonality to socialize with others, and independent individuals pursue heterogeneity to be distinctive from others [36]. Our first research question is, how does self-construal impact VBC participation?

Based on cognitive evaluation theory [19] and impression management theory [32], we then examine what effective mechanisms can be implemented in VBC to align participants’ heterogeneous motivations with the homogeneous commons of VBC. Alignment practices are believed necessary to reconcile different views and identities so that VBC sustainability can be facilitated [59]. In particular, the two theories suggest we examine two mechanisms (i.e., community rewards and public self-consciousness). Community rewards are mechanisms to discipline participants to comply with shared commons of VBC, which firms can implement as an external incentive. On the other hand, public self-consciousness is the awareness of an individual’s unique role from the public’s perspective. It is an internal psychological process that governs an individual’s behavior to be consistent with the community. An examination of both external and internal practices enables us to evaluate these alignment strategies and provide guidance to VBC practitioners. Our second research question is, how do community rewards and public self-consciousness interact with self-construal in order to align heterogeneous actors with shared norms?

The structure of the paper is as follows. We first provide a literature review of VBC and the relevant background theories. We then develop a research model and research hypotheses. Next, we report an empirical study, conclude with a discussion of the findings, and draw some implications for research and practice.

## 2. Literature Review

Community sustainability means the longevity over an extended period of time in a community [50]. It can be measured in terms of the number of participants in the community, the number and the quality of posted messages, participant loyalty and trustworthiness [33,51]. Some VBCs demonstrate longevity while others don’t. A better understanding of customer participation can help achieve community sustainability.

## 2.1. Antecedents of Customer Participation in VBCs

The prior literature has identified various antecedent factors of customer participation in VBCs from different theoretical perspectives, for example, the use and gratification theory [21, 45, 46, 62], the utilitarian and hedonic perspective [63], the view of chronic personality (i.e. extroversion and openness), and customer individual difference (i.e. community identification and brand knowledge) [24], as well as customer interaction or customer engagement [9, 46]. In particular, Dholakia, Bagozzi, and Pearo [21] revealed several internal motivations for customers to participate in VBCs, such as self-discovery, maintaining interpersonal interactivity, social enhancement, and entertainment value. Wang and Fesenmaier [62] identified several intrinsic motivations such as psychological, status gaining and hedonic needs. Wang et al. [63] complemented the list by adding motivations for social and entertainment needs. Nambisan and Baron [45] investigated social, personal, integrative and hedonic needs. Nambisan and Baron [44] examined self-image and expertise enhancement.

These motivations can be categorized as motivations for interdependence and motivations for independence. Interdependence-related motivations emphasize building and developing the relationship and affiliation with others. This is consistent with the intrinsic motivation literature, which suggests that individuals seek belongingness to others, such as maintaining interpersonal interactivity, social enhancement, and social and psychological needs. Independence-related motivations are more concerned about internal traits. This is in line with another stream of intrinsic motivations that emphasize independent identity, such as self-discovery, personal integrative needs, self-image, and expertise enhancement.

These two categories of motivations for interpersonal relationships share similar notion with self-construal theory [36]. The theory identifies independent construal and interdependent construal as two distinctive interpersonal orientations. Previous research has found that self-construal is able to explain a wide range of self-motives, such as self-regulation and self-enhancement, as well as a VBC customer’s interpersonal and social behavior [17]. We posit that self-construal theory may be a useful overarching theory to integrate different theoretical perspectives that explain the motivations to participate in VBCs. Also, it may be an underlying mechanism to classify the different types of motivations for individuals to participate in a VBC that have been reported in the prior literature. Self-construal theory may provide a more parsimonious explanation for VBC participation. We, therefore, introduce the theory to the VBC literature.

## 2.2. Self-Construal Theory

Self-construal is the extent to which an individual interacts with others and views self as an independent identity or in relation with others [1, 57]. The theory of self-construal originates from cross-culture research on individualism and collectivism. Individuals are characterized as individualistic in western culture, while people are considered collectivists in eastern culture [48].

Self-construal is composed of an independent self and an interdependent self. Within a given culture, the other part of the self also exists within an individual [36]. Individuals with predominantly independent self-construal organize their thoughts, feelings, and actions concerning separateness and uniqueness from within rather than referencing to others. Individuals with predominantly interdependent self-construal interpret their thoughts, feelings, and actions meaningfully when associating with connectedness and integration with the external social environment [36].

Self-construal theory has been applied in different areas. Escalas and Bettman [23] investigated the relationship between brand connection and self-construal. They posited that consumers purchased brands in order to build their self-concepts in terms of independence and independence, and independence-oriented individuals had stronger self-differentiation goals in their purchase of brands. Kim et al. [29] introduced self-construal to conflict resolution literature. Independence-oriented people were more likely to be dominant in conflict resolution, while interdependence-oriented people adopted avoidance and integration approaches.

It is widely accepted that independence and interdependence are two distinct dimensions of self-construal and co-exist within an individual [23, 29]. Singelis [57] found that independence and interdependence were orthogonal. An individual has both independent and interdependent self simultaneously, and these two dimensions differ in their relative strengths in determining an individual’s overall self-construal [23, 57]. As interdependence increases and independence decreases (interdependence dominates), people tend to conform to social norms and value group harmony; whereas people with predominantly independent construal are oriented for distinctive and autonomous identities [48].

This view is also evidenced in VBC literature. Thomas et al. [59] noted that “to achieve authenticity and distinctiveness, members depend upon each other to confer status and legitimate experience” (p. 1021). Independent members mainly behave customers in VBCs own the independent construal or interdependent construal but rather a “both/and” condition that customers have both of the two dimensions, while the relative strengths are different [58].

## 2.3. Cognitive Evaluation Theory

According to cognitive evaluation theory (CET) [19], the effects of an external incentive or event on internal motivation are different depending on whether the event can influence an individual’s perceived autonomy and competence. For example, if an external reward promotes a greater sense of perceived autonomy and competence by the individual, the individual’s intrinsic motivation will be enhanced. Otherwise, intrinsic motivation will be diminished. Therefore, a reward has both an enabling or informational function and a controlling or complying function [53]

Community rewards are often given to a member to recognize the member’s efforts, consistent behaviors, and contributions to the VBC [28, 41]. When a reward is given to an individual, it can be seen as an external alignment mechanism to frame or discipline members with heterogeneous interests and backgrounds to comply with the shared norms and collective beliefs of the VBC [59]. There are various types of rewards given to VBC participants, such as a member-ranking list, a virtual medal to symbolize virtual status, and granting access to some special information that otherwise cannot be accessed.

The role of rewards and incentives in VBCs is questioned and the corresponding impact is unanswered. On one side, rewards may stimulate a member to participate, while on the other, it may lower a member’s intrinsic motivation and result in low engagement. How to use rewards to motivate customers to participate in VBCs has been investigated in terms of the direct relationship between community rewards and customer loyalty [28]. This may be a simplistic view of the impact of a reward because rewards may have different roles among heterogeneous community members. It is imperative to explore how to design different types of rewards to motivate different individuals to participate. This study is to extend prior research to investigate how community rewards interact with heterogeneous community member characteristics.

Similarly, community rewards in VBC settings, as an external mechanism to align a member’s behavior, may also have two types of impacts, a bright side as an informational reward and a dark side as a controlling or complying reward. The information reward is focused on meaningful feedback about a member’s activities. It tells a member what he/she can do in order to obtain what he/she needs. The controlling reward works as a pressure or force for a member to comply with community norms in order to achieve some specific goals. It acts as an external pressure on unfavorable goals or behaviors and decreases a member’s intrinsic motivation [53]. Furthermore, a community reward may be monetary or a psychological reward [28]. Such a reward enables members to access more informational resources in VBCs and helps them gain higher status. If members do not comply with the rules, certain informational resources or VBC design features, such as individualized avatar, may be unavailable to these members.

## 2.4. Impression Management Theory

According to impression management theory [32], people attempt to create desirable public images or identities in their relationships with others. They are always motivated to create impressions in others’ eyes, rather than how they “should” behave [32]. Public self-consciousness is the awareness of the self as a social object or the tendency to think about the self from the public’s perspective [35].

We include public self-consciousness as an alignment practice for two reasons. First, public self-consciousness is not only important in face to face interactions [56] but also plays a vital role in VBCs to influence customer interactions and motivations [64]. Second, public self-consciousness may be helpful for the design of VBCs to facilitate effective communications among members. The VBC is the collective memory of members’ interactions and is the public platform seen by participants of similar interests [22]. Public self-consciousness can help members enhance their public images and govern their behaviors to be consistent with the collective community. It may also embarrass some members to strive for the private self [31].

People with a high level of public self-consciousness tend to interpret an object from the public’s view [30, 56]. This is consistent with the impression management perspective, which not only “plays a protective role for people who fear rejection” ([56], p. 821) but also “equates with pretension and deceit..., for the individuals to engage in self-presentation to others” ([56], p. 820). People with a high public image or a high public presentation usually place great importance in adhering to societal norms and are less aggressive [40]. However, they seem to possess less autonomy to purse their authentic selves [30]. Public self-consciousness can be a valuable alignment mechanism for an individual to comply with social norms, even though the individual may not be aware of the force of this psychological process.

VBCs provide the chance for people with similar interests to get together and present themselves to gain approval from others. When engaging in VBCs, people with a high public self-consciousness are likely to be actively involved, to communicate to others, and to act in a way that may enhance their public image [11, 30]. High reputation and status motivate community members to be highly involved and to make contributions to the community [44]. On the contrary, they are more concerned about other members’ perception of their behaviors, which leads to low psychological safety [64]. Therefore, properly cultivating an individual’s public self-consciousness can be an effective practice for an individual to align his/her perceptions, interests, and behaviors to be consistent with the community.

## 3. Conceptual Framework and Hypotheses

We develop a research model in Figure 1. We apply self-construal theory as a useful perspective to capture the spectrum of VBC heterogeneity, from interdependence to independence. We then propose that such heterogeneity can be managed by two alignment mechanisms, which are community rewards and public self-consciousness, in order to enhance community continuity. To be specific, we first examine the direct effects of self-construal on a customer’s continuance intention to participate in VBCs. We then reveal the interactive effects of self-construal with community rewards and public self-consciousness. Finally, we investigate the relationship between continuance intention and actual participation behavior.

## 3.1. Self-construal and Continuance Intention

As stated in the last section, prior studies have examined some antecedent factors of VBC participation. These factors are similar concepts to interdependence and independence. However, the two dimensions of self-construal have never been tested in an explicit manner. As we introduce self-construal theory to the VBC literature, it is necessary to explicitly test the direct effects of interdependence and independence.

Self-construal is an important factor to use in explaining an individual’s behavior. Interdependent self pays attention to the embedded relationships in social networks, seeking and building relationships and affiliations with the collective [36]. It tends to take the community as a platform in which to socialize and share information as well as to acquire recognition [7, 46]. A VBC works as a platform for people with similar interests and experiences to get together and socialize with one another [42]. Shared consciousness preserves the continuity of VBCs [38, 42]. Therefore, VBCs benefit people with the interdependent construal who have the needs of belongingness, social benefits, and trust, all of which may help them shape favorable attitudes toward VBCs and enhance their intention to be continuously involved. Therefore, we hypothesize H1a: Interdependent construal is positively associated with continuance intention.

Individuals who are independently oriented usually seek uniqueness and emphasize the freedom to express their own internal thoughts and attributes [36]. They may take a VBC as a platform to escape from reality and to discover themselves [21, 49]. Furthermore, heterogeneous resources in VBCs also provide various opportunities to gain distinctness and excellence [59]. For instance, customers may be uniquely identified and distinguished from others through various ranking lists. Along with a member’s title and the status in VBCs, as well as the customization of an avatar, are additional means to signify a member’s status and image [44]. All of these opportunities and functions provided in VBCs may position the members to enhance their reputational gains and self-image, which satisfies the need of the individual with an independent orientation [44, 49]. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1b: Independent construal is positively associated with continuance intention.

Weak ties among members who may not know each other in their offline relationships characterize VBCs. This is different from other types of communities that are characterized by strong ties, where friendship starts from offline relationships [5]. Members may present different information and identity images in different VBCs [61]. In a VBC characterized by weak ties, members do not need to mask their true identities, and may take the VBC as a place to escape from reality. They may be inclined to show their true identities rather than seeking acceptance by others [37]. In addition, self-interest and independence are highly valued by young people in VBCs. For example, young people strive for “true self” and “self-enhancement” to extend the self [8]. ## 3.2. Community Rewards and Self-construal

According to CET [19], a reward or external incentive may have two different functional impacts on an individual’s intrinsic motivation. For an interdependent individual who is inclined to support the commonalities of the community, the self is always connected with others, and the force of recognition and identification by others may drive his/her behaviors to be consistent with those of the community [36, 55]. Community rewards that recognize an interdependent individual in a VBC may act as informational cues that value the individual’s competency to maintain consistent and supportive behavior in the VBC. If the members do not contribute to the community, various resources in the VBC are not available to them. Thus, external incentives tend to be interpreted as information cues to motivate members to support their groups.

By means of community rewards, interdependent individuals’ efforts, competency, and contributions in the VBC can be recognized. For example, a VBC acknowledges participants with titles or symbols of status (e.g. the title of “golden member”), which is helpful to enhance the participants’ social identities [44]. This type of an external reward facilitates participants to enact their role identity and communicate meanings of the role to others [59]. Therefore, the interaction of interdependent construal and community rewards may positively influence a member’s continuance intention.

H2a: The effect of interdependent construal on continuance intention will be greater when a community reward is higher rather than lower.

On the other hand, external rewards may have a controlling role for independent individuals. Some VBCs allow members to reply and post threads freely (e.g. with no time or content limits) only if they have some corresponding level of expertise. In order to achieve some level of expertise, members have to participate and contribute to the community. Community rewards can be seen as instrumental tools for a member to move up to a higher level of expertise. An individual with independent construal may consider such an external reward as a means to comply with the rules because the individual seeks autonomous identity, “self-actualization,” or “self-development” [29]. These individuals are more likely to exhibit their private selves, enjoy the moment of freedom to express themselves, and show their expertise or knowledge as a means of distinctness from others [1, 21].

While community rewards require members to comply with the rules, such external incentives defer the uniqueness-seeking process by undermining the intrinsic motivation for independence-oriented people [10]. If the independent individuals do not comply with the rules, they may not fully realize their goals. For example, an individualized avatar representing distinctiveness or virtual medal signifying their status, may not be attained by members who ignore the VBC rules. For independence-related individuals, community rewards are interpreted as a means to extrinsic ends, which results in psychological reactance [3]. Thus, we hypothesize: H2b: The effect of independent construal on continuance intention will be greater when a community reward is lower rather than higher.

## 3.3. Public Self-consciousness and Self-construal

People with high public self-consciousness stress public image, and to avoid social anxiety, they will even mold their actions to live up to others’ expectations [56]. These individuals pay attention to managing impressions and gain recognition from others [26, 35]. This may regulate them to seek commonality with other members to enhance their social identity and secure psychological safety [59].

High public self-consciousness encourages interdependent individuals to behave in ways that strengthen their public image and provide recognition by others, thereby enhancing their identity in the community. Applied to the VBC context, interdependent individuals with high public self-consciousness are more aware of the threads they post. They may provide responses to these threads to enhance the feeling of “we-ness” or the feeling of being part of the community [44]. Conversely, when members exhibit low public self-consciousness, they may care less about their public expression in an accurate way, which attenuates identity alignment for interdependent individuals [40]. Therefore, the interaction of public self-consciousness and interdependent construal positively associates with continuance intention.

H3a: The effect of interdependent construal on continuance intention will be greater when public self-consciousness is higher rather than lower.

Public self-consciousness is associated with social anxiety and pressure, which reminds an individual to conform to subjective norms [40]. Therefore, public self-consciousness may steer individuals away from pursing heterogeneity and distinctiveness. Schlenker and Weigold [56] noted that individuals with high public self-consciousness might engage in self-deceptive practices to secure and enhance their public impression. In contrast, independent construal is concerned with genuine self-presentation to themselves, instead of self-deception to others [30, 35]. For the community member who pursues independent identity, self-consciousness acts as an obstacle to his/her actual and inner self-expression, which may result in an imbalance of inner self and public perception of themselves. Therefore, public self-consciousness may attenuate an independent individual’s willingness to participate in a VBC [64].

When public self-consciousness becomes weak, an individual’s sensitivity of social rejection is attenuated [11]. Malär et al. [35] stated that individuals with low public self-consciousness were less likely to be concerned about others’ impressions and more likely to seek their own ideal self-identity. Applied to the current VBC context, they may care more about the internal self and distinctiveness in the VBC.

H3b: The effect of independent construal on continuance intention will be greater when public self-consciousness is lower rather than higher.

## 3.4. Continuance Intention and Customer Participation

According to the Theory of Planned Behavior, one’s actual behavior can be predicted by the intention toward the behavior. Intention is the sign of how hard an individual is willing to perform the behavior and how much effort the individual is going to exert [2]. The stronger an individual’s intention, the more likely the individual will take subsequent actions [6]. Similarly, a member’s high level of VBC continuance intention constitutes his willingness and motivation toward contributing to the community, which in turn leads to a high level of participation [7, 6, 21]. H4: Continuance intention is positively associated with actual participation.

## 4. Methods

## 4.1. Data Collection

Data were collected through a web-based survey of people who participated in two Chinese VBCs, i.e. Meizu (bbs.meizu.com) and Apple (iphonebbs.cnmo.com). Both Meizu and Apple are major providers in the smart phone market in China. Meizu community was founded by Meizu Corporation and managed by the company’s employees. The company provided constant updates and news regarding current and future product in the virtual community. The community was also a platform for the company’s smart phone users to interact with each other. The Apple virtual community also created for a similar reason. The two VBCs were similar in terms of purposes, functions, and user interface design. The reasons for selecting these two VBCs are as follows. First, smart phones are very popular in China and draw many virtual members into VBCs. According to the websites of the two communities, the number of community members grew by about 20% and 70% for Meizu and Apple respectively from Jan. 2012 to Sep. 2012. The total number of members on Meizu and Apple amounted to 3 million and 4 million respectively in 2012. There were about 10 thousand threads and 18 thousand threads in Meizu and Apple community respectively on a daily basis. Second, both communities had more than 20 established discussion boards, each of which was further tuned to some specific categories and interests. This not only provided special places for members to seek autonomous identities but also included group discussion forums for members to make friends and interact with one another. Third, the two communities provided rewards to members, such as a virtual medal to enhance a member’s status or access to more resources.

#

We accessed various open discussion boards in the two communities and randomly selected the email contacts of the participating members. Duplicated email addresses were removed. Finally, 1,000 members were chosen from each community. We then sent a total of 2,000 invitations to the members via email. In the email, we stated the purpose of the study and invited the member to respond to the survey. We also included the URL of the website where the questionnaire was hosted. We offered a pre-paid mobile card as an incentive for each successful response to the questionnaire. The data collection process continued for about one month.

Finally, 199 questionnaires were received. We excluded 20 responses that did not include respondents’ ID numbers in the VBCs and 12 careless responses. A total of 167 responses were considered valid. This resulted in a response rate of 8.7%. Although this response rate was lower than the conventional response rate, it was comparable to other VBC studies [46]. The profile of respondents is presented in Table 1. It is noteworthy that 85.6% of the respondents were males, consistent with previous studies that males were the majority in product-related and technology-related VBCs [18, 46].

## 4.2. Measures

Existing scales were adopted to measure the research constructs in the study. Face validity was assessed through an interview with twenty customers in a VBC. A pilot study was also conducted by collecting questionnaires from 60 informants to refine the items to fit the specific VBC contexts. The measures and their sources for all constructs are shown in Table 2. A seven-point Likert scale was used in this study.

The items for self-construal were adopted from Kim et al. [29], who developed the scale based on Singelis [57]. Interdependent construal and independent construal were measured using different items. We measured community rewards based on Morhart et al. [41]. The scale for public self-consciousness was adopted from Malär et al. [35]. The scale for continuance intention was adopted from Algesheimer et al. [3].

Customer participation was measured using objective data collected in the two VBCs, following a similar approach in the prior literature [46]. After a VBC member answered the survey (Time 1), we retrieved the total amount of time that the individual member spent in the community and the amount of virtual money received by the individual, using the individual’s ID. Six months later (Time 2), we collected the same data. We used the changes in online time and virtual money between Time 1 and Time 2 to measure participation. We log-transformed the data to reduce skewness.

We also included several control variables through the survey, such as age (dummy variable, the age above 30 was coded as 1), gender (dummy variable, male was coded as 1), industry (dummy variable, computer related industry was coded as 1), community type (dummy variable, apple community was coded as 1). Other variables were also included by tracing an individual’s ID, such as title, which signaled status in the community, the number of friends in the community, the number of the discussion boards on which members participated, and tenure in the community. All of these variables were considered as control variables to reduce nuisance effects, as they might possibly affect continuous intention [46, 60].

## 6. Discussions, Implications and Future Directions

## 6.1. Discussions

The findings provided support for most of our hypotheses. Specifically,

#

independent construal, rather than interdependent construal was found to be positively associated with continuance intention. The brand community is not only a place for the need of belongingness, but also a place for an individual to discover and exhibit the real self [14, 16, 21]. The two specific VBCs investigated in this study might be different from other VBCs such as a travel community where entertainment and connection with others are valued [62]. In VBCs such as Apple and Meizu, the individual may be more inclined to seek a unique identity of being a use of one smart phone (such as iPhone), which processes distinguished features that are unavailable to users of other types of smart phones. Independent construal, therefore, had a dominating impact and, thus, attenuated the relationship between interdependent construal and continuance intention.

The difference of the coefficients of both independent construal and interdependent construal was significant. Independent construal explained more variance of future participation intention than did interdependent construal. This is consistent with prior studies where the values of young Chinese people were more individualistic [52], and where members connected through weak ties were more likely to show off themselves [37].

The interaction of independent construal and community rewards negatively affected continuance intention. This is consistent with the existing literature, which suggests that independent construal seeks more freedom and is not constrained by the rules [3]. On the other hand, the interaction of interdependent construal with community rewards had no significant effect on continuance intention. The possible reason is that for interdependent construal, community rewards in the two VBCs did not signal as sufficient incentives to enhance a member’s status and reputation. In fact, the reward of an individual’s contribution efforts in the two VBCs includes ranking list and virtual medal. It takes a longer time to accumulate these rewards to achieve a higher community status. Interdependent individuals need to constantly contribute to the communities in order to be recognized. They may be less tolerant for delaying gratification (reward) [54]. Therefore, the effect became non-significant. Another possible reason is that interdependent individuals valued other motivation (e.g. such as socializing with others) to engage with the community rather than superficial rewards.

The interaction of independent construal and public self-consciousness was negatively associated with continuance intention. This suggests that public self -consciousness prohibited the process for seeking private selves for the independent identity [30, 35]. The interaction of interdependent construal and public self-consciousness positively influenced continuance intention, which was consistent with prior literature stating that public self-consciousness helps people with the collectivist mindset to manage their impressions [30]. Finally, continuance intention enhanced actual participation behavior, which was consistent with prior studies [21].

## 6.2. Theoretical Implications

The current study contributes to both self-construal and VBC literature as follows. First, to our best knowledge, this study is one of the first to introduce self-construal theory to VBC literature. We provide empirical evidence of the generalizability of self-construal in emerging VBC literature. Unlike prior literature of self-construal that investigates traditional contexts, such as face-to-face communications and interactions, we demonstrate that the concept is also applicable in virtual and computer-mediated environments. Extending the concept of self-construal to VBC literature may encourage future studies to examine the concept in other virtual and online environments and contexts.

Second, we are one of the first to reveal the nature of VBC heterogeneity [59], based on a specific theoretical perspective—self-construal theory. Prior studies found that virtual members had diverse motivations, e.g., seeking affiliation with others, filling social needs, and maintaining interpersonal interactivity [21, 62, 63], as well as self-discovery, escaping from reality, image enhancement, and reputation seeking [21, 49]. However, no literature has explicitly examined self-construal in a systematic manner as we do in this study. We demonstrate that self-construal theory is a useful framework to cover the wide range of motivations and is able to provide a parsimonious picture of VBC participation.

Third, we further explore how to govern VBC heterogeneity through two mechanisms (i.e., community rewards and public self-consciousness) to align participants’ individual and diverse identities with the commonalities of VBCs. VBC homogeneity is suggested to dilute the sense of belongingness and act as a destabilizing force to community continuity [42]. Other studies suggest that heterogeneity may facilitate distinctiveness and excellence for the members [14, 59]. We demonstrate that effective alignment practices can balance such a conflicting view.

## 6.3. Practical Implications

Our study has implications for community designers and managers. It is difficult for a community to grow and sustain without understanding the mechanisms that explain the rationale for customer participation [24, 46]. This study suggests the importance of how an individual’s self-construal and community alignment mechanisms affect community participation.

The findings may be helpful for managers to design technological or motivational tools to induce members to participate in VBCs. First, managers are encouraged to collect information about individual differences in terms of self-construal and public self-consciousness when an individual registers for a VBC and builds his/her community profile. Managers should utilize high-quality profiling algorithms and mechanisms to identify and target specific individuals with different self-construal orientations. Community designers can further refine discussion forums on specific topics for independent people to be recognized as experts in their relevant knowledge domains. Managers can encourage independent individuals to initiate some discussion forums. For interdependent individuals, designers can develop some social network maps to describe how these individuals are connected to different groups.

Second, community managers need to be cautious in the design of different rewards to satisfy different customers. As we have found out, community rewards are a strong approach for individuals to align their identities to the community. However, the independent self sees community rewards as constraints rather than benefit. Managers should support the freedom of speech, rather than controlling discussion or dialogue [39]. To encourage independent individuals to participate actively, constraints should be relaxed. For individuals with interdependent construal, reward should be immediately recognized, instead of waiting for a longer duration.

Regarding the role of public-consciousness, community managers can design different tools for interdependent members to publicize private files. Using these tools, interdependent members can decide how much they can publicize. Managers can also apply technological designs for independent members to privatize their public selves. For example, community members can choose what information can be set to private, so other members can only see part of the postings, such as the title or key words.

## 6.4. Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations exist in the study that may affect the generalizability of the findings to other contexts. First, our data were collected from China, which is still considered a collective society that emphasizes interdependence among people, even though significant transformations are undergoing in the country. The findings based on these data may not apply to other countries like United States. Second, a majority of the respondents to our survey were young, whose culture value might have changed from the traditional collectivism to the individualism. This change may have impacted how these individuals behave in VBCs, which may be expected differently from the older generation. Further, most respondents were male, who might have different perceptions and behave differently from females. Future studies can test the research model using data collected from other populations. The roles of age and gender difference can be investigated. Third, the two VBCs we chose were technology-oriented and may not represent other types of VBCs. They were also sponsored by firms, which may be different from VBCs managed by members of common interests [28]. Future studies can test the model in other VBCs and explore the differences between self-managed VBCs and firm-sponsored VBCs. Fourth, how participants used VBCs, such as regularly contributing or just viewing and trying to build rewards, was not examined. The content of the actual participative behavior can be investigated in future research. Fifth, following our sampling process, we selected those members who were more rather than less active in the discussion boards. Future studies can randomly collect email contact information based on the company’s database. Finally, future studies can investigate other community-related mechanisms, e.g., outcome control (i.e. control of the content of the threads) or process control (i.e. control of the time interval to post the treads), in order to explore how to manage heterogeneity. An open and sharing culture may also enhance a member’s participation.
