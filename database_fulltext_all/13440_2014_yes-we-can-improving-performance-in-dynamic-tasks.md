## a b s t r a c t

Facilitation, a special kind of decisional aid, is all forms of information, strategies, and heuristics delivered by a facilitator to aid decision makers in dynamic decision environments. It is assumed that facilitation has profound effects on decision making, but these effects are understudied and empirically unproven. By incorporating the three levels of facilitation, pre-task, combined pre-task and in-task, and combined pre-task, in-task, and post-task, in the design of a simulation-based interactive learning environment (ILE), this study provides an empirical, laboratory-experiment-based evaluation of the effectiveness of facilitation on performance in dynamic tasks. We develop and use a comprehensive model consisting of four evaluation criteria: task performance, structural knowledge, heuristics knowledge, and transfer learning. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the subjects provided with combined pre-task, in-task, and post-task facilitation performed the best, followed by those provided with combined pretask and in-task facilitation. Contrary to the hypothesis, subjects provided with pre-task facilitation performed poorly.

## 1. Introduction

Managers and policymakers face problems that are increasingly complex and dynamic in nature. In a dynamic task, a number of decisions are required rather than a single decision, decisions are interdependent, and the environment in which a decision is set changes either autonomously or because of the decision made or both [7,15,49]. For instance, inventory management, <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghting, software development, and sustainable use of renewable resources are all dynamic tasks. Improved decision making in these tasks is an absolute necessity for the success of decision makers and the performance of their organizations.

The recognition that these real world dynamic tasks (e.g., medical emergency preparedness, project management, and long term planning and strategic decisions in organizations) do not lend themselves well to real world experimentation has long motivated the use of computer simulation based interactive learning environments (ILEs) as decision support systems. Moreover, in dynamic tasks, managers need ways to test their decision strategies before a costly and often irreversible implementation follows. Computer simulation-based interactive learning environments provide a potential solution here. For instance, ILEs are often used to improve decision making in dynamic tasks [12,13,16,25,50]. We use “ILE”, a special kind of decision support system, as a term suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently general to include microworlds, management <sup>fl</sup>ight simulators, simulation-based learning laboratories and any other computer simulation-based environment — the domain of these terms is all forms of action whose general goal is the facilitation of learning [25]; ILEs allow the compression of time and space and provide an opportunity for managerial decision making in a non-threatening way [25].

Learning is a process whereby knowledge is constructed by the transformation of experience [32]. Simulations in general and ILEs in particular are one form of experiential learning. In an ILE session subjects make a series of decisions and have access to the instantaneous feedback. Subjects also have the opportunity to evaluate and re<sup>fl</sup>ect on their performance in the post-simulation debrie<sup>fi</sup>ng session. Despite promising an increasing interest in ILEs, their bene<sup>fi</sup>t to the decision making and learning in complex, dynamic tasks is far less promising [5,6,16,33,48].

The increasing urge to improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of ILEs has led researchers to suggest improvements. One such way to improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>- cacy of an ILE is to incorporate structured and systematic facilitation [10,23,50,56]. Facilitation, a special kind of decisional aid, is all forms of information, strategies, and heuristics delivered by a facilitator to aid subjects' decision making and learning in a task. In dynamic decision environments, facilitation can provide decisional aid at three levels — pre-task, in-task, and post-task levels [50]. For instance, in the aviation industry, learning “how to <sup>fl</sup>y a plane” requires the acquisition of expertise on a complex, dynamic task. A trainee pilot begins training with classroom instructions and intensive simulator-based learning, a pretask level facilitation. Next, hands-on-practice in the cockpit follows but always with the help of an experienced pilot, an in-task level facilitation. Finally, after the <sup>fl</sup>ight is over, performance is analyzed and feedback is provided, a post-task level facilitation.

Although research on dynamic decision making and learning in ILEs has embraced the concept of facilitation, it has generally been de<sup>fi</sup>cient in several ways. First, while prior research has evaluated pre-task and post-task level facilitation in the context of dynamic tasks (e.g., [11,20,49]), ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of in-task level support has rarely been tested. Second, no prior study has explored ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of facilitation as a combined pre-task, in-task, and post-task facilitation hypothesis. Third, prior research has often relied on a limited assessment of performance outcomes, focusing frequently on game performance to index ILEs' effectiveness.

The present study contributes by addressing some of the de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies noted above. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we investigate the relative ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of three decision support alternatives – (i) pre-task, (ii) combined pre-task and in-task, and (iii) combined pre-task, in-task, and post-task level facilitation – in the context of a dynamic task. In addition, this research distinguishes the independent effects of facilitation alternatives on the multidimensional decision outcomes: task performance, structural knowledge, heuristics knowledge, and transfer learning. This paper thereby <sup>fi</sup>lls an extant gap in research on decision making in dynamic decision environments.

## 2. Theoretical premise and hypotheses development

## 2.1. Background concepts and definitions

We aim to investigate the relative ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of three decision support alternatives in promoting decision making and learning in dynamic tasks. Consistent with prior studies on dynamic tasks, subjects' decision making is conceptualized as their ability to do the task [1,14,50,59]. Maximizing the pro<sup>fi</sup>t of a <sup>fi</sup>rm or doing the task with minimum cost or in a minimum time is a commonly used example of task performance measures.

On the other hand, learning bears on multiple perspectives [30,44,56]. We may consider learning either as a progression towards expertise [29,60] or as becoming part of a community of practitioners [55]. Drawing upon the modern objective-oriented ‘constructivist’ approach to learning [57], we follow Sternberg's view of learning. In Sternberg's view, people are not really experts or non-experts, but rather are experts in varying degree — prototypes [60]. Prototypic attributes may vary across domains, times, and space and thus, are dynamic in nature. A prototype view of expertise implies a broader view of learning and can well accommodate the diversity of skills and knowledge acquired by decision makers through participation in ILE sessions.

We consider transfer learning as our main learning outcome measure. Transfer learning measures how well decision maker learn from a previous task by making them attempt another task, either in the same domain or a different domain [25,66]. Most education and training programs aspire to transfer learning [56,57,67]. Graduates are expected to do well in their jobs by applying the skills they learned in college. Pilots, after intensive simulator-based training, are assumed to transfer the acquired skills to actual plane <sup>fl</sup>ying task. In fact, if the skills developed by education and training programs do not transfer beyond the training context, much of the investment may be considered wasted, as noted in a National Research Council report on enhancing human performance [65]. Training with ILEs is no exception, so transfer learning is our major outcome measure in this research study.

We also include structural knowledge and heuristics knowledge as measures to better capture a range of expertise development. Structural knowledge pertains to knowledge about principles, concepts, and facts about the underlying model of the task system (e.g., in our dynamic task, the concept of carrying capacity refers to the maximum population of “<sup>fi</sup>sheries” that the “<sup>fi</sup>shing area” can sustain inde<sup>fi</sup>nitely). Heuristics knowledge concerns how decision makers actually control a task (e.g., strategy that a subject used to order new ships is an example of heuristics knowledge). Therefore, improved performance and learning in dynamic tasks could be evident from an improvement in any of these dimensions of expertise [56,63,64].

## 2.2. Pre-task level facilitation in dynamic decision environments

Pre-task level facilitation is conceptualized as information provided by the facilitator to a decision-maker about the model of the task prior to performing the task [11,20]. It is typically conveyed through a set of heuristics for effectively performing the task. Pre-task level support may help the subjects perform better in dynamic decision environments at least in two ways. First, providing the subjects information about the central variables of the task system as well as the relationships between these variables, the task may become salient. Subjects perform better in decision-making environments where the relationships among the key variables of the task system are made more salient than those with nonsalient task systems [20,21]. Second, pre-task facilitation may reduce cognitive load of a subject because a substantial amount of the information the subject has to infer through the complex interactions with the ILE is already transmitted through prior instructions. Studies conducted in a variety of tasks have found that the performance of the subjects provided with task heuristics is better than what would be achieved through task experience [4,46].

In dynamic situations, therefore, the effectiveness of pre-task facilitation lies in its ability to convey a model of the task system to the decisionmaker. This enables the decision-maker to understand the key relationships prior to the interactions with the task system. Pre-task facilitation can thus serve as an effective method of planning overall strategy. This conception of pre-task facilitation embodies the combined characteristics of task information or feed forward [54] and cognitive information including heuristics [4,26,31,53]. Information about the causal relationship between the variables of the task system adds to the task transparency. Increased task transparency leads to improved performance in dynamic tasks [20,21,50]. Moreover, an appreciation and understanding of key structural elements of the task system (e.g., delays give rise to oscillations in the task system behavior (output)) are not only useful in developing better structural knowledge but also enhance the subjects' transfer learning skills [21,42]. Thus,

H1a. Subjects receiving pre-task facilitation will outperform those without pre-task facilitation on task performance.

H1b. Subjects receiving pre-task facilitation will outperform those without pre-task facilitation on structural knowledge.

H1c. Subjects receiving pre-task facilitation will outperform those without pre-task facilitation on heuristics knowledge.

H1d. Subjects receiving pre-task facilitation will outperform those without pre-task facilitation on transfer learning.

## 2.3. In-task level facilitation in dynamic decision environments

In-task level facilitation attempts to improve the individual's decision-making performance by: (i) making the task goals explicit at early stages of learning, (ii) helping keep track of goals during the task, and heightening the importance of relevant features of the task so that the learner can assess what action is appropriate [10,49,50]. Such facilitation can also help decision-makers avoid long episodes of counter-productive <sup>fl</sup>oundering with the task [56].

In the absence of in-task facilitation, self-directed learners face at least three problems: (i) the “motivational paradox” (the goal of accomplishing the task con<sup>fl</sup>icts with the goal of learning the system), (ii) the “assimilation paradox” (new knowledge is distorted to <sup>fi</sup>t with existing mental models), and (iii) learners are often unaware of what they do not know [8]. Previous research has reported the positive effects of facilitation in overcoming the abovementioned dif<sup>fi</sup>culties but only in static environments [16,23]. No prior study has evaluated, for example, the effects of in-task level facilitation in the context of a dynamic task.

In dynamic environments, the effectiveness of in-task level facilitation lies in its ability to provide appropriate goal-directed and diagnostic information that can aid decision makers in (a) focusing on the task objectives, (b) enhancing their ability to track the changes in the underlying system, and (c) forming an adequate model of the task system [9]. Such facilitation can be provided through information that enhances the salience among the key relationships in the decision environment, e.g., the tradeoffs among important variables and the delays intrinsic to the system [20,54]. Thus, key to the effectiveness of in-task level facilitation in dynamic decisions is the delivery of information that enables decision makers to learn more about the decision variables and the decision environment [40] and thereby form an adequate task system model. This model can then guide decision-makers towards designing appropriate decision strategies and aligning them with system changes. Improved understanding of how changes in input variables (decisions) affect the outcomes (e.g., pro<sup>fi</sup>tability) leads to the development of transfer learning [3,42,50]. Therefore,

H2a. Subjects receiving combined pre-task and in-task facilitation will outperform those who receive pre-task facilitation alone on task performance.

H2b. Subjects receiving combined pre-task and in-task facilitation will outperform those who receive pre-task facilitation alone on structural knowledge.

H2c. Subjects receiving combined pre-task and in-task facilitation will outperform those who receive pre-task facilitation alone on heuristics knowledge.

H2d. Subjects receiving combined pre-task and in-task facilitation will outperform those who receive pre-task facilitation alone on transfer learning.

## 2.4. Post-task level facilitation in dynamic decision environments

Post-task facilitation or a de-brie<sup>fi</sup>ng review aims at improving decision making and learning of the subjects by providing an opportunity to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on their experiences with the dynamic task [11,22,37,38,44]. Such discussions, necessarily held after accomplishing the task, purport to foster learning by making the decision makers aware of their entrenched beliefs and provide them with information which facilitates their eventual re-interpretation [23,25,34]. Moreover, there are many experiences that the decision-makers can have with ILEs. They initially have no way of knowing which are important and useful in the real world [36]. Post-task facilitation may provide this knowledge and hence may aid in the development of expertise.

The behavior of dynamic systems is most often counter-intuitive [18]. Outcome feedback alone is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to improve performance in dynamic tasks [54,59]. Decision-makers have their own views about what types of decision strategies are needed to achieve desirable behavior in the system as a whole. But, when they play out their assumptions and strategies, often the outcome is not what they perceive, even though they thought they understood the interactions in the system [58,59]. Post-task facilitation in the form of debrie<sup>fi</sup>ng discussions may effectively <sup>fi</sup>x the inadequacies of their mental models. Better understanding of the task system is a pre-requisite to overcome the misperceptions of feedback [43,57]. The decision makers can discover gaps between their expectations of system behavior based on their understanding of system interactions and the behavior that actually arises [25,27,49]. This discovery may help the decision-makers to have an “adequate model” of the system and hence improve learning, especially the transfer learning — how well the decision makers learn from the previous task by making them play another task either in the same domain [24] or in a different domain [21]. However, this increased understanding of the task system comes at the expense of increased cognitive effort expended (e.g., in systematic exploration and testing of hypotheses regarding the relationship between systems variables). On the other hand, post-task facilitation where “an optimal solution”

is presented, subjects might bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the presented solution in developing their decision rules to do the dynamic task. Hence the third hypothesis,

H3a. Subjects receiving combined pre-task, in-task, and post-task facilitation will outperform those who receive only pre-task facilitation or those who receive a combined pre-task and in-task facilitation, on task performance.

H3b. Subjects receiving combined pre-task, in-task, and post-task facilitation will outperform those who receive only pre-task facilitation or those who receive a combined pre-task and in-task facilitation, on structural knowledge.

H3c. Subjects receiving combined pre-task, in-task, and post-task facilitation will outperform those who receive only pre-task facilitation or those who receive a combined pre-task and in-task facilitation, on heuristics knowledge.

H3d. Subjects receiving combined pre-task, in-task, and post-task facilitation will outperform those who receive only pre-task facilitation or those who receive a combined pre-task and in-task facilitation, on transfer learning.

Overall, crucial to the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of ILEs in promoting decision making in dynamic tasks is the role of facilitation. Three levels of facilitation – pre-task, combined pre-task and in-task, and combined pre-task, intask, and post-task levels – have the potential to effectively aid the decision makers in ILEs to perform better in dynamic decision environments. With the incorporation of facilitation, ILEs may well support the subjects in improving their task performance, structural and heuristics knowledge, and transfer learning in dynamic tasks. ## 2.5. Post-task level facilitation and its lagged effects

In the context of any educational and training program, a time lag refers to the time interval between the last day of the training program and the measure for assessing transfer learning [68]. Posttask facilitation training session, where several structure–behavior graphs (e.g., Fig. 2 presents an example of such graphs) are elaborated on, constitutes a form of higher skills experience, a mechanism to support far transfer [66,67]. Therefore, its lagged effects on task performance are expected. At the same time, our objective is to assess the effectiveness of facilitation-based ILEs on subjects' decision making and learning in dynamic task. Therefore, on a transfer task, LichenBankILE, subjects have to recall the learned skills and its applicability, as well as ability to execute the task, another dimension of far transfer [68]. In dynamic tasks, improved casual understating between the relationships of the task system is a good predictor of improved performance in the task [47,47,57]. Transfer learning skills, therefore, are assessed through a causal retention test and elapsed time between post-task facilitation and this retention test is twelve weeks. Consistent with Barnett and Ceci's taxonomy for far transfer [68], therefore, we selected this twelve week elapsed time to ascertain the assessment of far transfer.

## 3. Methodology

To test our hypotheses, we applied four criteria to select our methodology. First, the experimental setting was computer simulation-based laboratory experimentation. This experimental approach is advantageous in the study of decision making in dynamic environments. In such environments, much of the complexity in the tasks is the result of the interaction among the <sup>fl</sup>ow of information, actions, and consequences during the performance of the task [18,59]. For instance, there are time delays between actions and their consequences which may make learning very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult if not impossible. On the other hand, the use of ILEs allows for compressing these dynamics, thereby facilitating successful learning. Moreover, laboratory experimentation is an established method of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c inquiry within psychology and organizational behavior literature. A review of this literature has found remarkable similarities between research <sup>fi</sup>ndings obtained in laboratory and <sup>fi</sup>eld settings [38]. A priori, there is no reason to believe that use of an experimental approach will be less successful in studying dynamic decision making than studying judgment and choice in the traditional discipline of cognitive psychology [22,43,49]. Second, we wanted our hypotheses to be testable across decisional aids' (facilitation types) designs. Therefore, we included three decisional aids. Third, our subjects should be real decision makers or have had suf<sup>fi</sup>cient training in managerial decision making. Finally, our subjects should be available for two sessions separated by a minimum of 12 weeks thereby allowing us to assess transfer learning.

## 3.1. Dynamic task environment

The research question was explored in the context of an ILE, FishBankILE [51]. The simulation was based on the system dynamics model of Denis Meadow's board game built around the “tragedy of the commons” phenomenon [19,42]. Subjects played the role of a <sup>fi</sup>shing <sup>fl</sup>eet manager. They made capacity acquisition and utilization decisions over the 30-year life of a <sup>fi</sup>shing area contract.

The task of managing a <sup>fi</sup>shing <sup>fl</sup>eet was selected for a number of reasons. The task embodies the essential characteristics of a dynamic task — a series of multiple, interrelated decisions made over a period of time, and in a dynamic environment [15,50]. The task is interesting because standard normative theories for decision making are not applicable to such tasks [7] and it is not possible to compute the correct course of action through analytical means [57]. Finally, understanding the dynamics of such tasks (e.g., consequences of overexploitation) in itself is critical to the successful management of increasingly mismanaged renewable resources [43].

As <sup>fi</sup>shing <sup>fl</sup>eet managers, subjects had to manage a ‘simulator’ portraying a <sup>fi</sup>shing <sup>fi</sup>rm over a 30-year period. The <sup>fi</sup>sh resource was a cod population in an isolated fjord to which the subjects had the exclusive property rights, and where nobody else was <sup>fi</sup>shing [43]. Each year the subjects were required to make two decisions: (i) order new ships, and (ii) decide the percentage utilization of the <sup>fl</sup>eet. New ships are assumed to have a one-year delivery delay and an average lifetime of 25 years. The weight of each <sup>fi</sup>sh was held constant. Also, the price of <sup>fi</sup>sh was assumed to be <sup>fi</sup>xed. The player was a small seller in a large market. Each year subjects received information about the economic status of their <sup>fi</sup>rm as well as about catch and resource estimates. Information about the <sup>fi</sup>rm's performance variables was provided both in current time as well as across time. Task performance is measured by cumulative pro<sup>fi</sup>ts made by each <sup>fi</sup>rm over a period of 30 years plus the remaining resource (<sup>fi</sup>sh) value in the <sup>fi</sup>nal year. The dynamic behavior in the model arises due to two fundamental accumulation processes: accumulation of ships and stock of the common resource, <sup>fi</sup>sh. ## 3.2. Experimental design

The experiment had one independent variable: the level of facilitation provided to the subjects. The subjects were randomly divided into four experimental groups: ILE with no-facilitation, NOF; ILE with pre-task facilitation (PRF); ILE with combined pre-task and in-task facilitation (PRF + INF); and ILE with combined pre-task, in-task, and posttask facilitation (PRF + INF + POF).

## 3.3. Participants and experimental setting

The experiment was conducted with 104 MBA and Executive-MBA students, recruited from a large Canadian university, equally balanced across all four conditions. The average age of the student was 27.3 years and 51% were male. Participation in the experiment was entirely voluntary. A sum of US\$ 50 was paid to each participant who completed the experiment. These working students were highly motivated and eager to improve their decision making skills to enhance their prospects for better jobs, promotions, and salary packages through formal schooling. They had at least four years of work experience in business organizations where they had made supervisory decisions up to a middle management level. Besides other courses, all the subjects had taken “Spreadsheet Modeling for Management” or an equivalent course which would have made them aware of basic simulation methodology. There were no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences across treatments with respect to gender $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 2 . 9 8 9 , \mathsf { p } = . 8 1 0 ) , \mathsf { a g e } \left( \mathsf { F } \left( 7 , 9 6 \right) = . 2 7 , \mathsf { p } = . 9 5 0 \right)$ work experience $\left( \mathrm { F } \left( 7 , 9 6 \right) = . 6 2 , \mathrm { p } = . 7 1 1 \right)$ , prior structural knowledge $( \mathrm { F } \left( 7 , 9 6 \right) = 1 . 5 5 7 , \mathrm { p } = . 1 7 0 )$ and heuristics knowledge $( \mathrm { F } \left( 7 , 9 6 \right) =$ 1.728, p = .123) about the task system.

A pilot study was conducted with 15 subjects to ensure that FishBankILE worked as intended and the subjects understood the instructions. None of the pilot study students participated in the main study. The main experiment was conducted in two phases. The <sup>fi</sup>rst phase was accomplished in the second week of the semester during which we assessed task performance, structural knowledge, and heuristics knowledge. The second phase was completed in week 14, the last week of the semester, which allowed us to assess subjects' transfer learning through a retention test. A total of 26 subjects were randomly assigned to each of the four experimental conditions on the same day, and were not told that different experimental conditions (i.e., facilitation levels) were being tested. They were not allowed to interact. All subjects received a 6-page set of training materials three days prior to their sessions and also again on the day of the experiment, in which they were introduced to key features of the game including a description about the task model and the relevant concepts of the domain.

In order to become familiar with the simulation interface, all subjects performed a practice trial before starting on the two formal trials. Task parameters were changed for the practice trial. This practice trial also helped to mitigate the potential “task novelty” effects. Subjects were told that scores on the practice trial would not count. In addition, subjects in each of the facilitation conditions were given unique information through separate sessions. The experiment was conducted on desktop computers in three computer labs each with a seating capacity of 40. At most 26 participants took part in each session. Sessions were staggered. The researcher delivered the three facilitations to the respective experimental groups. Three trained assistants helped in the execution of the experiment. On their arrival at the site, each of the participants was seated in front of a randomly assigned desktop, signed a consent form, received the general instructions on the tasks, completed a pre-task knowledge test and demographic questionnaire, completed the practice and two main trials interceded by the respective level of facilitation, and completed the knowledge and facilitation manipulationcheck questionnaires.

For each year in the 30-year period of the simulation, the subjects made two decisions: (1) ship capacity acquisition (i.e., number of new ships to order), and (2) capacity utilization (i.e., percentage of ships to send in for <sup>fi</sup>shing). The new ship order ranged from 0 to 4 (re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the ship production capacity constraint of the only manufacturer of the special ships required) and the ship utilization could be from 0 to 100%. The software ran the simulation in one year intervals at the end of each of which a status report was provided. Afterwards, time was advanced and the subject made new decisions on both ship ordering and utilization. The cycle continued until the simulation was completed, after 30 decision intervals. All subjects completed two main trials. Subjects took between 70 and 100 min in total to complete the experiment.

## 3.4. Design of decisional aids

## 3.4.1. Pre-task facilitation

Pre-task facilitation (ILE + PRF) consisted of a presentation by the facilitator about the FishBankILE task system. The subjects were told that the content of the presentation was crucial for successful task performance. In these instructions, subjects got information about more central variables, i.e., <sup>fi</sup>sh density, net growth rate of <sup>fi</sup>sh, catch per ship, <sup>fl</sup>eet capacity, and resource estimate. The relations between these variables were stated in qualitative terms. All the information provided to the subjects was taken from the game description material. This information was not made available to subjects in NOF group. Subjects were also instructed and encouraged to ask the researcher for detailed information in all situations and for all aspects of the system. By instructing the subjects in this way, the relations between several variables of the system were made salient.

## 3.4.2. In-task facilitation

Subjects in this condition were instructed to consult the facilitator The pilot study revealed that by this period, systems' variables were exhibiting the dynamics (e.g., lag effect of ship ordering) to allow an effective intervention [61]. Therefore, once the subjects accomplished their 10th period decision making selection, the facilitator intervened. In this facilitation $( \mathrm { I L E } + \mathrm { P R F } + \mathrm { I N F } )$ , subjects were told to notice the development of key system variables over time without telling them which are those variables. They were encouraged to combine an overall perspective, i.e., a decision about what to achieve in the long term, with detailed measures. They should implement their decisions and check their effects carefully. By doing this, they were told that they would enhance their chances of <sup>fi</sup>nding a state of balance in the system so they could feel that they had the situation under control. They were also told that by examining changes from one situation to another, they should be able to understand what things are more important than others and what is needed to check continuously. The great importance of adapting one's thinking and behavior to the demands and circumstances of the situations was stressed. The subjects were told that this sometimes meant demands for careful examinations and new decisions, while at other times it may be better to wait and see.

## 3.4.3. Post-task facilitation

In post-task facilitation $( \mathrm { I L E + P R F + I N F + P O F } )$ , debrie<sup>fi</sup>ng was the main activity. Not only ‘what happened’ but also ‘what did not happen’ was discussed. Subjects' performance charts were shown and discussed to relate the structure of the system with its behavior. Fig. 2 depicts such a graph. With the help of such graphs and associated text, the built-in explanation facility in the Help System of FishBankILE elaborated on each structure–behavior graph of the task system. ## 3.5. Dependent measures

The dependent variables examined were task performance, structural knowledge, heuristics knowledge, and transfer learning. The task performance metric for each subject was chosen so as to assess how well each subject did relative to a benchmark rule [49]. The task performance measure for subject s, TP has the following formulation:

$$
T P _ {s} = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n _ {y}} \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {n _ {T}} \left| y _ {i t} - b _ {i t} \right|}{n _ {y} * n _ {T}}
$$

where $n _ { y }$ is the number of task performance variables, n is the number of trials the task has to be managed, $b _ { i t }$ is the benchmark value of task performance variable i at time t, and $y _ { i t }$ is the empirical value of task performance variable i (i = 1 (number of ships ordered), i = 2 (percentage of ships utilized)) at time t [49]. Every decision period, the benchmark's performance (pro<sup>fi</sup>t)' values are subtracted from subject's. The subject's <sup>fi</sup>nal performance, TP, is the accumulation over 30 periods of this difference, averaged over the number of performance variables and number of trials.

Structural knowledge and heuristics knowledge constituted the task knowledge. Task knowledge was extracted through a post-task questionnaire. There were two main parts of the questionnaire. The <sup>fi</sup>rst part consisted of two open-ended questions asking subjects about their general strategy for ordering new ships and ship utilization. Three experts on domain, other than the investigator, independently graded the responses to these open-ended questions. The sum of the scores for these two questions measured heuristics knowledge (operator's knowledge). The second part of the questionnaire measured structural knowledge (designer's logic) through a set of twenty closedended questions on the relationships between pairs of the key variables of the task (e.g., which one of the following statements re<sup>fl</sup>ects your understandings about the FishBankILE task (check one)? Other things being equal, a decrease in Net Growth Rate of Fish will lead to (a decrease, an increase, neither — no relation, a decrease followed by an increase, an increase followed by a decrease) in Fish Stock).

Transfer learning, was also assessed through questions. This retention test had sixteen closed-ended questions on a different task, LichenBankILE but an isomorphic to FishBankILE and was administered twelve weeks later (Appendix $\mathsf { A } ) .$ . In addition to these performance measures, two self-reported scales (Usefulness of Facilitation in Arousing Interest in the Task (FIT) and Usefulness of Facilitation in Understanding the Task (FUT)) were used to assess the subject's reaction to the facilitation.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

## 5.1. Key findings

The primary goal for this research was to investigate the effectiveness of three decisional aids: pre-task, pre-task + in-task, and pretask + in-task + post-task facilitation on subject's performance in dynamic tasks. We proposed positive effects of facilitation on subjects' performance (i.e., task performance, structural knowledge, heuristics knowledge, and transfer learning) in dynamic tasks. Using several ANOVAs including one on performance improvement across the trials (to further ascertain the effects of facilitation) and a MANOVA (to assess the subjects' reaction to the facilitation), the overall results of this study suggest that facilitation is an effective decisional aid for dealing with complex, dynamic tasks. In ILEs, facilitation is effective because it improves participants' task performance, helps users acquire knowledge about the decision domain and develop heuristics, and contributes to their transfer learning—all important elements of an ‘expertise’ development endeavor.

All the groups with facilitation, with any level, showed better task performance on the dynamic task except the group with pre-task facilitation. Although the average value on task performance for pre-task group was better than the group with no facilitation, it was not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. It is worth noting that despite the better performance by the groups with facilitation, none of these groups performed at the optimal level. In fact, they could not do better than the benchmark rule. These results are in line with several studies' <sup>fi</sup>ndings in dynamic decision making area [44,49,60].

As was hypothesized, all levels of facilitation helped the users improve their structural knowledge about the task. However, the provision of combined pre-task, in-task, and post-task level facilitation appeared as the most successful decisional aid. Participants with this aid developed better knowledge about the relationships between the variables of the task system because of their access to: (i) information on the key task variables (ii) goal-directed information, and (iii) what [2,54] called “re<sup>fl</sup>ective conversation with the situation” through respective levels of facilitation.

All levels of facilitation helped the participants develop heuristics. Again, the combined pre-task, in-task, and post-task level facilitation had the most pronounced effect on subject's heuristics knowledge. Contrary to our hypothesis, pre-task level facilitation did not do signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly better (p = .670) than the group with no facilitation. One possible explanation could be that subjects in pre-task facilitation, in the absence of simulation-based experiential learning, did not make use of this facilitation. The results, given in Section 4.5, do support this possibility: subjects in pre-task facilitation group scored lower both on “utility of facilitation in arousing interest in the task” and “utility of facilitation in understanding the task” in the self-reported scale.

All levels of facilitation helped users to develop transfer learning skills. Subjects with combined pre-task, in-task, and post-task level facilitation performed the best on the retention test. Consequently, it seems plausible to acknowledge the important role of “post-task” facilitation in transfer learning skills. Post-task facilitation gave the decision makers greater adaptability in recognizing task system changes and updating their mental models [59]. Overall, when it comes to which level of facilitation is the most effective in developing expertise in dynamic tasks, the group with combined pre-task, in-task, and post-task level facilitation, performed the best on all the measures.

## 5.2. Limitations and future research

The usefulness and applicability of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this study must be viewed in light of its limitations. In this study, we used computer simulation-based laboratory experimental approach. Despite the inherent concern of external validity, the laboratory experimentation is advantageous in the study of decision making in dynamic tasks. In these tasks, much of the complexity is the result of interactions among the <sup>fl</sup>ow of information, actions, and consequences [59]. In ILEs, the underlying simulations can capture some of the dynamic inter-temporal aspects of the task that are essential characteristics of natural settings but are absent from preprogrammed stimuli in a controlled laboratory experiment [1]. Moreover, laboratory experimentation is an established method of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c inquiry within the psychology and behavioral science literature. According to Locke [39], there are remarkable similarities between research <sup>fi</sup>ndings obtained in laboratory experimental method and <sup>fi</sup>eld settings. Therefore, there is no reason to believe that the use of experimental approach should be less effective to study dynamic decision making than to study judgment and choice in traditional psychology [43].

Our results on the efficacy of facilitation should be interpreted with caution. Although our results suggest that the combined pre-task, intask, and post-task facilitation is the best facilitation type in improving subjects' decision making and learning in dynamic tasks, we only consider it better than those with no facilitation, or with pre-task or with combined pre-task and in-task facilitation. Only a future study with three additional facilitation groups: in-task alone, post-task alone, and combined in-task and post-task facilitation, can effectively evaluate the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the whole inter-connected set of three levels of facilitations.

We also recognize that larger sample size (at least with 120 subjects) could have improved the explanatory power of our model. To address this limitation and to provide further con<sup>fi</sup>rmation to our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we intend to do a future research where we will recruit a larger sample size and also non-MBA participants.

The use of graduate students as participants raises the question of the generalizability of the results of this study. However, student participants have been used in many decision-making studies, including software project environments [1], production scheduling tasks, market strategy tasks [47], and <sup>fi</sup>sheries management tasks, on the basis of the general conclusion that the formal properties of tasks are much more important determinants of decision making than participants pro<sup>fi</sup>le [43]. Although the task of managing a <sup>fi</sup>shing <sup>fl</sup>eet is somewhat different from all of these tasks, it is similar enough to assume that student participants are acceptable surrogates in this experimental investigation. The use of business graduate students as surrogates for practicing managers is considered adequate because the FishBankILE task is an information-processing oriented task and the information processing behavior of students and managers rarely differs in dynamic tasks [17,52,64].

We recognize that learning in a laboratory and or a class room setting is not the same as ‘actual real world decision making’. Nevertheless, learning with ILEs possesses the three fundamental ingredients of such a transfer: (i) active feedback seeking, and (ii) monitoring one's learning experiences [3,16,41], and (iii) opportunity to re<sup>fl</sup>ect and overcome the misconceptions [41,59]. Furthermore, prior research has shown successful perceived transfer learning from simulation-based teaching methods [16,35]. These perceptions, as suggested by attribution theory, may result in the actual behaviors [28].

## 5.3. Facilitation in service of dynamic decision making

This study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst comprehensive study of facilitation as a decisional aid in dynamic tasks and its different levels. In contrast to prior studies on dynamic decision making and effectiveness of ILEs, we were able to show signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvements in performance outcomes when decision makers in ILEs were supported with pre-task, in-task, and post-task facilitation. First, providing facilitation is useful because it improves task performance, structural knowledge, heuristics knowledge, and contributes to transfer learning. In concert with prior literature [4,39], our research shows the need to combine these decisional aids—motivational (primarily delivered through pre-task level facilitation), goal-oriented (primarily delivered through in-task level facilitation), and re<sup>fl</sup>ection-based information (primarily delivered through post-task level facilitation) about the task—in order to enhance decision makers' expertise in dynamic tasks.

Contrary to our hypothesis, subjects with pre-task facilitation did not improve task performance. There are at least three plausible explanations. First, this result appears to be consistent with wisdom of general consulting services — inspiring clients how to <sup>fi</sup>sh (encouraging the decision makers to actively explore the relationships between task variables) rather than giving them <sup>fi</sup>sh (providing the detailed information about the central variables of the task system). In other words, it would better facilitate decision-makers by providing them with the minimum task information that stimulates the exploration for decision heuristics and structural knowledge rather than by feeding them with the example's decisions. Only through a controlled experiment, with one group with “example's decisions” and other group without such information, could effective design of pre-task facilitation be evaluated. Second, in dynamic decision making, people's mental models are grossly simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed compared to reality. For example, subjects don't appreciate the effect of time delays between action (e.g., ordering of ships) and the consequence (e.g., availability of ships). Poor system understanding can lead to poor task performance [3,43]. Finally, high-performing decision strategies signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly differ from those most people bring to bear — often leading to rejection of the expert's advice [33,49]. Therefore, pre-task strategy recommendations by the facilitator that con<sup>fl</sup>ict with subjects' existing mental models might be ignored altogether rendering pre-task facilitation ineffective. In fact, when we look at data on subject's reaction to facilitation, pre-task group appeared to perceive facilitation as of not much help in arousing interest in and understanding of the task system. Only future studies, by focusing on subject's decision strategies both espoused (prior to the task) and actual (in the task), could explore this important issue.

The effect of “practice” for the group without any facilitation support on task performance, structural knowledge, and heuristics development was insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. This is consistent with prior research <sup>fi</sup>ndings [57,35] that subjects in simulations, in the absence of facilitation, often succumb to ‘video arcade syndrome’ — they might win the game but without developing much understanding of the underlying task. Instead, our results support the notion that additional resources in terms of systematic and structured facilitation – embodying motivational, goal-oriented, and diagnostic information about the task – should be used to develop better understanding of dynamic tasks [49].

One of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study that facilitation helped the participants acquire more transfer learning should be interpreted with some caution. Here our subjects did not perform on a simulation task. Instead, we assessed their transfer learning through a retention test with structure and heuristics related questions on a different task. It is believed that task knowledge in general and heuristics knowledge in particular support transfer learning either in the same domain [24] or in a different domain [21].

Developing expertise in dynamic tasks is a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult undertaking at best. With the positive effects of facilitation on decision making in a dynamic task, FishBankILE, does this mean subjects have become experts? With limited assessment of transfer learning and only two rounds of decision making, it would be naïve to think that subjects have become experts. Instead, these performance improvements should be interpreted as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution to subjects' progression towards prototypic expertise: subjects are experts in varying degrees on the whole spectrum of no expert to expert [60]. Only future studies, with task with different levels of task complexity and with sophisticated measurement tools for expertise development, could more fully explore the impact of facilitation on the range of expertise development.

This study proposed and used a comprehensive model to evaluate the effectiveness of facilitation based ILEs. The model comprised of four evaluative criteria — task performance, structural knowledge, heuristics knowledge, and transfer learning. Most of the prior studies on dynamic decision making [14,43,52,57] measured ILE's effectiveness on one or two criteria. Consequently, their conclusions are incomplete, if not biased. For instance, an ILE may improve structural knowledge, but it may fail to improve task performance (e.g., as was the case with our PRF treatment group). This study has addressed the need to comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of ILEs. Furthermore, this research model is an all-inclusive instrument that can be used by other researchers in future studies to evaluate various decisional aids in the service of dynamic decision making. For instance, researchers can use this research as a base to examine other important issues, such as the effect of facilitation on group decision making, and the value proposition of facilitation.

## 5.4. Implications for practice

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are important for the computer simulation-based education and training industry which is a multi-billion dollar industry [45]. In particular, the relevance and application of facilitation in computer simulation-based learning environments are well established, for instance, in aviation, military, medical, and education domains [41]. To accrue transfer learning skills – a raison d'être for education and training industry – practice with the simulation task alone won't be enough, our results suggest. Instead, a deliberate and structured facilitation especially post-task facilitation is essential. Subjects develop kinds of experiences with the simulation task. With facilitation at hand, they can better understand the task system and develop appropriate heuristics. It is the facilitation that helps the subject better assimilate the new knowledge with their existing mental models and overcome the misperceptions about the task system they might have [10]. Therefore, overwhelming con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of our hypothesis, “the group with combined pre-task, in-task, and post-task facilitation did the best on transfer learning,” should be valuable to the designers, developers, and users of simulation-based systems. Understanding the relative effectiveness of simulation-based training systems represents a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant value to managers, who must decide which training method to implement to realize the gains desired from decision technologies.

The outcomes of this study will be particularly bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to professionals in the education sector of the economy. In education, prior research has indicated that students were best able to develop a deeper understanding of the observed phenomena when they made connections between the micro and macro levels of the phenomena, while most school curricula deal with macro and micro phenomena in separate classes [62]. System dynamics based ILEs, such as FishBankILE, have the potential to bridge this gap. Educators, both at undergraduate and graduate levels, can encourage the development and use of ILEbased courses at appropriate levels in their program curricula.

Will it increase immediately or after a delay? Will it remain unchanged? Will it decrease immediately or after a delay?

An immediate increase implies that an increase or decrease in the left variable is followed by an increase in the right variable in the same year. No change means that if the left variable stays constant after an initial increase or decrease, so will be the right variable. In a delayed relationship, however, the right variable continues to increase or decrease for some time even after the left variable is constant (of course initially it was either increased or decreased). As an example, if the left variable is “Hiring New Employees” and right variable is “Retirements”. Other things being equal, an increase in “Hiring New Employees” leads to a delayed increase in “Retirements”, because “Retirements” continue to increase even after “Hiring New Employees” have stopped. New Employees remain in the pipeline for some time and “Retirements” thus are a delayed function of “Hiring New Employees”.
