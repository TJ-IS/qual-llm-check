# Investigating illusions of agreement in group requirements determination

Glenn J. Browne<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Radha Appan<sup>b</sup>, Roozmehr Sa<sup>fic</sup>, Vidhya Mellarkod<sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Rawls College of Business, Texas Tech University, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Information requirements determination Illusion of agreement Abilene Paradox Joint application development Group conformity User participation Con<sup>fl</sup>ict

## A B S T R A C T

The success of information systems development e<sup>f</sup>orts hinges largely on eliciting accurate requirements from users and other stakeholders. Requirements determination is difficult due to the complexity of the systems to be built, analysts’ and users’ cognitive and motivational challenges, and the highly politicized nature of many development e<sup>f</sup>orts. The present research addresses a problem that arises from users’ motivations during the requirements determination process. Most past studies have focused on explicit con<sup>fl</sup>icts that arise between systems analysts, users, and other organizational stakeholders. The present research is concerned instead with the opposite problem—illusions of agreement between participants in the systems development environment. Our study investigates a type of illusion of agreement known as the Abilene Paradox in requirements de termination. The Abilene Paradox refers to situations in which each member of a group believes (incorrectly) that the other group members want to pursue a particular course of action, which leads everyone to avoid con<sup>fl</sup>ict by agreeing to the action publicly while disagreeing privately. We <sup>fi</sup>rst provide theory underlying the paradox and review past literature. We then build theory to motivate our investigation and generate hypotheses. We then report results of a laboratory experiment utilizing group requirements determination e<sup>f</sup>orts. Results indicated behavior consistent with the presence of the paradox and an illusion of agreement. These results have important implications for research and practice in requirements determination speci<sup>fi</sup>cally and systems de velopment in general. Our results contribute to both the requirements determination literature and the literature on illusions of agreement and social conformity.

## 1. Introduction

Systems development e<sup>f</sup>orts and success are dependent on eliciting accurate requirements from users and other stakeholders. However, requirements determination is often di<sup>fi</sup>cult due to system complexity, cognitive and motivational challenges for analysts and users, and the politically charged environment surrounding most large-scale devel opment e<sup>f</sup>orts [1,2]. In the present research, we explore ways in which motivational factors may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the accuracy of requirements elicited in group requirements determination processes. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we investigate how these factors may lead to an illusion of agreement known as the Abilene Paradox .

The Abilene Paradox refers to situations in which individual members of a group believe (incorrectly) that other group members want to pursue a particular course of action. To avoid con<sup>fl</sup>ict, everyone agrees to undertake that action in contradiction to what all group members personally want and believe [3–5]. In the original formulation of the paradox, Harvey [6] reported a story in which four reasonable and sensible people agreed to take a 106-mile trip during a dust storm on a scorching 104° West Texas day to eat unpalatable food at a hole-in-the wall cafeteria in Abilene when none of them had really wanted to go. Following this misadventure, the participants discovered that each person had incorrectly believed that everyone else wanted to go and had thus agreed to make the trip to avoid con<sup>fl</sup>ict. Although the Abilene Paradox has been discussed in the organizational behavior literature, and anecdotal reports of the paradox have surfaced in a variety of in terpersonal situations, little research has investigated the phenomenon. In the present research, we provide theoretical support for antecedents of the Abilene Paradox to support the anecdotal evidence o<sup>f</sup>ered by previous researchers (e.g., [5,7]), test for evidence of the paradox in group requirements determination, and add to the scant prior research on the phenomenon. We make two distinct contributions. First, we add to the sparse literature on illusions of agreement in requirements determination and systems development contexts, demonstrating the dangers posed by such problems to complete and accurate requirements. Second, we <sup>fi</sup>ll a gap in the social conformity literature by linking general conformity <sup>fi</sup>ndings with Abilene Paradox-type situations by testing hypothesized antecedents of the paradox in a group decision-making context.

Major systems development projects typically occur in complex, politicized environments involving numerous interested parties [1,8–10]. Because of the occurrence of con<sup>fl</sup>ict in such environments, research on information requirements determination (IRD) in this context has focused primarily on con<sup>fl</sup>ict management in general and more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally on the role of facilitators in achieving goal congruence and fostering agreement among the con<sup>fl</sup>icting parties (e.g., [11]). However, research to this point has underemphasized a di<sup>f</sup>erent pro blem—the role of motivational factors and the pressures of organizational politics that can lead to an illusion of agreement among the participants. Illusions of agreement include several dysfunctional behaviors that lead to conformity (and often poor decisions) among members of a group. Two instances of these illusions are groupthink and the Abilene Paradox. The illusion of agreement problem has been addressed in the information requirements determination literature only by examining groupthink. However, the Abilene Paradox is hy pothesized to arise when agreement or consensus in the requirements determination process masks individual di<sup>f</sup>erences and disagreements among participants regarding the requirements of the proposed system, ultimately resulting in inaccurate and incomplete requirements. For this reason, the present research investigates Abilene Paradox-type situations in requirements determination sessions that involve groups of users.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section we discuss previous research, describe our theoretical argument as to why IRD participants are susceptible to the Abilene Paradox, and provide theoretical support for antecedents to the paradox. We then provide a conceptual framework for the research and develop hypotheses. Next, the methodology for an experiment is described, followed by the results of the data collection. We conclude with implications for researchers and practitioners and prescriptions for identifying and mitigating the Abilene Paradox.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Information requirements determination

Information requirements determination refers to the set of activities performed by a systems analyst when assessing the functional, nonfunctional, and technical requirements for a proposed system [2,12–14];. It also involves collecting relevant information about the current business processes, data needs, goals of the system, and the behaviors of users [15].<sup>1</sup> Errors made at the IRD stage have rippling e<sup>f</sup>ects on the subsequent stages of the systems development process and are di<sup>fi</sup>cult and expensive to rectify later [16,17]. Therefore, the requirements determination process is critical to the success of the entire systems development e<sup>f</sup>ort [18,19].

Traditionally, requirements elicitation has been conducted by systems analysts through a series of one-on-one interviews with users [20,21]. However, problems such as time constraints, con<sup>fl</sup>icting views, and requirements integration issues led to a shift in preferences for many analysts to group requirements elicitation techniques such as Joint Application Development (JAD) [22].<sup>2,3</sup> JAD is a facilitated group technique that is used in IRD to increase team member rapport, reduce communication barriers, and take advantage of participants’ combined knowledge [23][24]. As noted by Dean [24, pp. 184-185] “The JAD session also promotes commitment, group cohesion, resolution of con-<sup>fl</sup>icting views, and productive group meetings.”

Research on JAD and other group requirements elicitation techniques has examined a variety of issues relating to group communication (e.g., [25]) and dysfunctional group dynamics (e.g., [22,26–30]). This research has recognized that the management of group con<sup>fl</sup>icts during the systems development process in general and during IRD in parti cular is crucial to the ultimate success of the system [1,31,32]. Given the need to manage such con<sup>fl</sup>icts and disagreements early in the systems development process [33,34], requirements determination has traditionally focused on “managing the convergence of stakeholder interests toward agreement” [35, p. 41]. This focus on con<sup>fl</sup>ict management to achieve agreement has proven important and useful [36,37]. However, it has also diverted IS researchers’ attention from a related but fundamentally di<sup>f</sup>erent problem: illusions of agreement. Illusions of agreement provide compelling motivation for the current study.

The potential for illusions of agreement in IRD is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. For example, as a symbol of agreement concerning the requirements of the proposed system, it is common for analysts and users to “sign-o<sup>f</sup>” on a requirements document. However, user sign-o<sup>f</sup> may simply create an illusion of agreement. Users may provide requirements contradictory to their beliefs, for example, to avoid con<sup>fl</sup>icts. Such agreements therefore may mask signi<sup>fi</sup>cant underlying problems. Illusions of agreement may be a major source of the failure of requirements determination e<sup>f</sup>orts, and the phenomenon has not been given due attention in the information systems literature. The present research thus <sup>fi</sup>lls a gap in the requirements determination literature by investigating the extent of false agreement in group requirements settings.

## 2.2. Illusions of agreement

The theoretical foundations of illusions of agreement rely in large part on the pioneering work of Asch [38–40] concerning group conformity. We discuss speci<sup>fi</sup>c aspects of these theories in our theoretical argument Section 2.4 below. Following Asch’s work, scholars have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed groupthink and the Abilene Paradox as two distinct mani festations of group conformity [3,4,6,41–44]. As noted by Sims [43, p. 54], groupthink and the Abilene Paradox have “super<sup>fi</sup>cial similarities,” but they can be clearly distinguished. They have di<sup>f</sup>erent implications for group cohesion, commitment, and satisfaction, and they require di<sup>f</sup>erent diagnostic and remediation strategies [43,44]. Groupthink occurs when long-term, insular, cohesive groups make con<sup>fi</sup>dent deci sions without full discussion or consideration of alternative courses of action, believe in the inherent morality and correctness of their deci sions, and are enthusiastic about the decisions [41,43]. Group members are usually not conscious of the dysfunctional decision-making process that leads them irrationally to avoid examination of alternative courses of action [41] and thus feel no dilemma concerning the decision. They feel satisfaction and commitment toward their decisions and have high group cohesiveness, loyalty, and esprit de corps both before and after making their decisions [42,43].

On the other hand, each member of a group under the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the Abilene Paradox wants privately to take one action but fails to state that preference to other group members and willingly and consciously agrees with a group decision to do the opposite [3,4,7,44,45].<sup>4</sup> Members “agree” to the outcome no one wants because no one publicly disagrees [5,43]; [43]. Group members feel frustration and irritation about the group’s decision, are dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed and not committed to the decision, may subsequently experience considerable group con<sup>fl</sup>ict, and have low group cohesiveness both before and (especially) after making the decision [42,44]. Therefore, groupthink “makes people feel good about bad public decisions, while Abilene makes people feel bad about good private decisions withheld from the group” [44, p. 404]. In the words of Jatkevicius [46, p. 77], the Abilene Paradox is the unfortunate “union of misunderstanding and silence that leads to surprise and dis appointment and, just possibly, disaster.”

Kelman’s [40] work on social in<sup>fl</sup>uence also provides theory underlying the present work. Kelman conceptualized three potential rea sons for conformity or attitude change: compliance, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and internalization. Compliance is linked to rewards and punishment, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is the result of bonding with a likeable source, and in ternalization is due to acceptance of information from an expert source [47]; see also [48]. Kelman [40, p. 51] provides a key motivating phrase for the study of illusions of agreement: “Did the communication produce public conformity without private acceptance, or did it produce public conformity coupled with private acceptance?”

As noted, prior work concerning the Abilene Paradox has been sparse. Although not investigating the Abilene Paradox directly, relevant research has been provided by Zhu and Westphal [49], who investigated a psychological phenomenon known as “pluralistic ignorance.” Pluralistic ignorance has been researched by behavioral scholars for many years and, like the Abilene Paradox, relies on social conformity literature for its foundation (see, e.g., [50–52]. Zhu and West phal [49, p. 870] state that pluralistic ignorance “is a systematic tendency for members of a group or community to underestimate the extent to which others share their concerns about practices, policies, or events under the group’s purview.” They go on to note that “a prerequisite for pluralistic ignorance is the hesitancy to voice minority opinions or to raise questions about policies or practices that are favored by the majority of group members (p. 872). Such behavior is also characteristic of the Abilene Paradox. Zhu and Westphal discuss the relationship between pluralistic ignorance and the Abilene Paradox, suggesting that the former may be one factor contributing to the latter (see also [52]). Although conducted in the context of stock analyst reactions to stock repurchasing plans, the work provides useful theore tical background for the present research. Conceptual (but not empirical) work on the Abilene Paradox also appears occasionally in the Management (e.g., [53]; [7]), Library Science (e.g., [46]), and Educa tion (e.g., [54]) literatures. The overarching message from all these domains is that not voicing one’s opinion when it con<sup>fl</sup>icts (or appears to con<sup>fl</sup>ict) with the opinion of the majority is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant psychological bias in a variety of group decision-making contexts.<sup>5</sup>

In the systems development literature, the only work on the Abilene

Paradox of which we are aware is found in several papers by McAvoy and Butler [55]. Two papers by these authors McAvoy and Butler [56] used the Abilene Paradox as a possible explanation of resistance to change behavior observed in an agile software development case study. However, this explanation was suggested post hoc by the authors, no controls on the interpretation were reported, and the work provides no empirical evidence of the paradox. Another paper by the same authors McAvoy and Butler [57] found the Abilene Paradox as a possible explanation of the “mindless adoption” of technology in a qualitative study. The present paper can be distinguished from the papers by McAvoy and Butler in terms of theory (social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and conformity), domain (requirements determination), and method (experiment).

## 2.3. Illusions of agreement in information requirements determination

The abundance of research on groupthink has brought increased awareness to practitioners, who consciously take measures to address this issue when dealing with highly cohesive groups. Systems analysts may be warned to avoid groupthink during the requirements gathering process [58]. However, there is no indication in either the research or practitioner literature that analysts ever receive guidance regarding the possible in<sup>fl</sup>uences of the Abilene Paradox (although for practitioner concerns about a related issue in general management, see [59]. This is unfortunate, because we contend that in the context of systems development in general and requirements determination in particular, the Abilene Paradox is more likely to be a problem than groupthink. Our contention is based on the following reasons:

1. Information requirements determination often occurs in a highly politicized environment [8]. Organizational politics, incentive systems, power issues, etc. can result in group members feeling coerced into conforming to the favored views of the group. This leads group members to feelings of frustration and dissatisfaction characteristic of Abilene Paradox-type situations.

2. The requirements determination process often brings together sta keholders from di<sup>f</sup>erent functional departments who do not know each other [37]. Further, members of such groups may have divergent opinions, interests, and goals [32,60,61]. The transient nature of such groups [1] with diverging interests prevents group cohesion from occurring. Group cohesion is essential for groupthink to occur, but low cohesiveness is characteristic of Abilene Paradox situations. Thus, the absence of group cohesion in IRD contexts makes the environment more conducive to the Abilene Paradox than to groupthink. Additionally, the typical diversity of group members in systems development may have paradoxical e<sup>f</sup>ects concerning illusions of agreement. Diversity reduces the likelihood of group think, due to di<sup>f</sup>erent backgrounds of its members, but because those members may speak “di<sup>f</sup>erent languages” [62], the diversity can lead to the Abilene Paradox. Although individual group members may privately disagree with what appears to be a majority opinion, they may believe—incorrectly—that others in the group have access to more information or expert knowledge and therefore choose to remain silent.

3. The IS literature has documented that the aftermath of systems failure often includes con<sup>fl</sup>icts among di<sup>f</sup>erent parties, shirking of responsibilities, and acts of <sup>fi</sup>nger pointing [1,31,37]. This is a characteristic of groups that are subject to the Abilene Paradox rather than groupthink. In the latter case, members of the group remain cohesive even after the repercussions of the faulty decisions come to light [42,44].

In the context of requirements determination, analysts rely heavily on users for information, guidance, and support. E<sup>f</sup>ective communication is essential [63]. Though soliciting user input is critical during systems development, it becomes problematic when the analyst seeks a “consensus opinion” from a user group [53]. Such practices make the requirements determination process vulnerable to the Abilene Paradox. Thus, this study contributes to the IS literature by investigating Abilene Paradox-type situations as a potential cause for problems in IRD.

## 2.4. Theory underlying characteristics of the Abilene PParadox in IRD

In describing the Abilene Paradox, Harvey [6,64] suggested various reasons that group members may be susceptible to the paradox, including action anxiety and negative fantasies. However, no theoretical support for these antecedent factors has been provided; rather, in the words of Taras [44, p. 405], the arguments by Harvey are “largely anecdotal and atheoretical.” Taras discusses numerous variables from the group dynamics literature that are relevant to groupthink and the Abilene Paradox. Although her paper does not explicitly tie the variables to the causes of the Abilene Paradox described by Harvey, she provides some of the grounding for our theoretical arguments. We extend Taras’ reasoning to lay a strong foundation for the stimuli we use in the present research to test for the Abilene Paradox by <sup>fi</sup>rst providing theoretical justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for action anxiety and negative fantasies. We now describe these two factors.

## 2.4.1. Action anxiety

Action anxiety refers to the strong apprehensions that are sometimes created when a person thinks about taking actions that he believes are necessary in a decision-making environment but that he be lieves are not approved by his peers, superiors, or other stakeholders [5]. Action anxiety can be explained using theories of group conformity and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence [40,65,66]. People observe the behaviors of others in groups and can choose to act consistently or inconsistently with those behaviors. When group members conform to group norms or standards of behavior, they are rewarded (directly or indirectly), and when they deviate, they are typically met with rejection and derision [66,67]. Thus, when one believes that he needs to argue in favor of an action that contravenes the otherwise (perceived) unanimous or strong majority opinion in a group, he can experience deep anxiety. He may therefore report an opinion (or take an action) that coincides with that of the group even though he privately disagrees and does not change his underlying attitude [40,68]. This helps him avoid con<sup>fl</sup>ict associated with deviating from the majority opinion and the anxiety accompanying it. Such behavior is characteristic of the Abilene Paradox.

Action anxiety is hypothesized to occur in the process of require ments gathering due to various organizational and group pressures. In requirements determination, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant responsibility is placed on users who possess valuable information essential to the development of the proposed system. When providing requirements, users may ex perience action anxiety because they believe the needs they should state may not coincide with the beliefs of other users, their superiors, or the systems analysts. In such cases, users may not report their true beliefs to circumvent confrontation or other discomfort (e.g., appearing as someone who “rocks the boat”) with their peers or superiors in the organization who may not share the same beliefs.

## 2.4.2. Negative fantasies

Negative fantasies refer to pessimistic perceptions that individuals have about what will happen as a consequence of acting in accordance with their beliefs [64]. That is, while action anxiety refers to anxiety arising out of the need to take an action, negative fantasies refer to the expectation of negative consequences that might result from any such action. Anticipating perceived negative consequences from actions and attempting to reduce or eliminate them is a well-known psychological phenomenon with several theoretical origins. First, the theoretical tradition in approach-avoidance motivation provides important un derlying rationale. Approach-avoidance motivation theory posits that people are motivated to approach situations with positive expected outcomes and avoid situations with negative expected outcomes [69,70]. When people anticipate negative expected outcomes from an action, they will avoid that action if possible. A second source of theoretical support for negative fantasies is anticipated regret theory [71–73]. People seek to avoid regret from foreseeable negative consequences. Thus, fear of regret plays a strong role in people’s choices across a wide variety of contexts, and people will make choices to help themselves avoid such regret and perceived negative consequences [73–75]. Third, although clearly distinguishable, action anxiety and negative fantasies are in some sense two sides of the same coin.<sup>6</sup> Thus, the theory discussed above concerning group conformity and antici pated evaluation by others as a basis for action anxiety also applies for negative fantasies. People may fail to speak or vote against a perceived unanimous or strong majority group opinion because of their fear of negative evaluations by that group (Cialdini and Goldstein, 1994), which can be viewed as a form of punishment [40]. As stated by Taras [44, p. 413], “Abilene su<sup>f</sup>erers [have]. . catastrophic fantasies about humiliation at the hands of an otherwise-united group.” More generally, as noted by Bond and Smith [76, p. 133], “Individuals will publicly accept the majority view while privately retaining their initial view, motivated by a desire not to appear deviant or to risk possible negative sanctions from the majority, such as ostracism or ridicule.” Thus, anticipation of potential negative consequences is hypothesized to induce the Abilene Paradox.

During requirements determination, users may negatively fantasize that their opinions will be criticized or ridiculed by their peers or the analyst. Such fantasies are likely because users are typically at lower levels of the organizational hierarchy [44] and may also have low computer self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy. Further, a user may fear that his suggestions could result in a major failure in the implemented system or changed work process and consequently lead everyone to blame him for the problems that ensue. In such cases, users may not voice their true beliefs about requirements for the proposed system. There is some evidence in the literature that team members do not voice disagreements during IT development projects because of negative fantasies about repercussions from project managers or company executives (e.g., [59]).

Thus, action anxiety (AA) and negative fantasies (NF) can cause people in groups to suppress their true beliefs and conform to what super<sup>fi</sup>cially appears to be the majority opinion. Using Kelman’s [40] framework, this suppression should be due to compliance, i.e., fear of punishment or negative consequences, rather than internalization due to a change in true beliefs. The false consensus can lead to the Abilene Paradox and an illusion of agreement. We hypothesize that the presence of AA or NF will make users less likely to reveal their true beliefs when compared to users with no such AA or NF.

## 2.4.3. Support from group member

We introduce one additional factor to the context of the Abilene Paradox in the present study. Research on group decision-making has shown that conformity is typically greatest when a person believes that he or she is the only person deviating from the normative group posi tion [77]. Asch [39] found that conformity with the majority group opinion is substantially reduced when participants perceive that at least one other group member is also willing to deviate from the group norm (see also [78]. Much subsequent research demonstrating the willingness to voice one’s true belief in a group setting when there is support from another group member was performed by Allen and others (summarized in [79]. Based on this reasoning, we contend that the occurrence of the Abilene Paradox should be reduced when a group member’s private opinion is supported publicly by at least one other group member. However, we hypothesize that the e<sup>f</sup>ects of group member support on participants’ responses will be di<sup>f</sup>erent for the two antecedents (AA and NF) of the paradox. Action anxiety refers to apprehensions caused as people think about acting in accordance with what they believe needs to be done. In the presence of support from even one of the group members such apprehensions are expected to be greatly reduced because the individual will not have to justify his or her stance alone. The individual will foresee that any subsequent action that might have to be taken as a result of acting in accordance with his true beliefs will now be jointly performed. However, in the case of negative fantasies, individuals foresee negative consequences to their own personal interests. Therefore, even if someone has support from another member of the group, it is unlikely that he will be willing to deviate from the group’s response. Perceived negative consequences for someone in addition to the group member himself are not su<sup>fi</sup>cient to mitigate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the Abilene Paradox. In our experiment, described below, we included AA and NF treatment groups plus a control group. In the presence of sup port from one of the group members, we expect that decision makers subject to AA will be more likely to reveal their true beliefs when compared to decision makers subject to NF. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences are also hypothesized to occur between decision makers subject to NF and those in a condition in which neither AA nor NF is present (the control condition). but not between decision makers subiect to AA and those ir the control condition.

It is also important to investigate the impact of group member support within treatment conditions. In the AA condition with group member support, it is expected that more participants will express their true beliefs because support from at least one group member can be expected to reduce a participant’s anxieties (in contrast to participants behavior without group member support). However, in the case of NF, no such shift in responses is expected even with group member support because the participant will still strive for the majority’s approval (based on the reasoning above). In the case of the control group, because participants in this condition are not subject to the Abilene Paradox, we do not expect support from a group member to cause any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences in the requirements provided (similar to the results of [39]).

## 3. Hypotheses

Given the theoretical background discussed above, we generated hypotheses, stated in the alternative form. In the cases of H1 and H2, we were interested only in the sub-hypotheses; therefore, we hypothesized only main e<sup>f</sup>ects for the overall hypotheses. Prior to each hypothesis, we provide a summary of the rationale from the theory section above.

The Abilene Paradox is most likely to occur when someone believes that he or she is the only dissenter in a group (Bourne and Ekstrand 1982). Both action anxiety (AA) and negative fantasies (NF) are expected to be operative under such conditions. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1. In the absence of support from other group members, there will be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant main e<sup>f</sup>ect for participants in the control, AA, and NF conditions.

H1a. In the absence of support from other group members, participants in the AA condition will be less likely to reveal their true beliefs than participants in the control condition.

H1b. In the absence of support from other group members, participants in the NF condition will be less likely to reveal their true beliefs than participants in the control condition.

H1c. In the absence of support from other group members, the responses of participants in the AA condition and responses of participants in the NF condition will not be di<sup>f</sup>erent.

Allen [39], Morris and Miller [78], and others found that people are more likely to speak out when they know they have the support of at least one other group member. Action anxiety should be reduced. However, we hypothesize that negative fantasies will not be reduced because of the personal impact on the individual who is withholding his or her beliefs. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2. In the presence of support from one other group member, there wil be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant main e<sup>f</sup>ect for participants in the control, AA, and NF conditions.

H2a. In the presence of support from one other group member, the responses of participants in the AA condition and the responses of participants in the control condition will not be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent.

H2b. In the presence of support from one other group member, participants in the NF condition will be less likely to reveal their true beliefs than participants in the control condition.

H2c. In the presence of support from one other group member, participants in the NF condition will be less likely to reveal their true beliefs than participants in the AA condition.

Following the logic from H1 and H2 directly, we hypothesize the following:

H3. Participants in the AA condition with no support from another group member will be less likely to reveal their true beliefs than the same participants in the AA condition with support from another group member.

Because people in the negative fantasies condition will be subject to the paradox regardless of support from another team member, we hypothesize no di<sup>f</sup>erence in the two situations.

H4. Responses of participants in the NF condition with no support from other group members and the responses of the same participants in the NF condition with support from another group member will not be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent.

Because the control group was not subject to the experimental manipulations, there should be no di<sup>f</sup>erence in participants’ behavior regardless of whether they have support or no support from other group members.

H5: Responses of participants in the control condition with no support from another group member and the responses of the same participants in the control condition with support from another group member will not be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent.

## 4. Methodology

To study the potential existence of the paradox in the context of requirements determination, an experiment was performed. Participants were 56 students (37 male and 19 female) in an undergraduate course at the college of business at a research university (20 in the control condition 19 in the AA condition, and 17 in the NF condi tion). Participation was voluntary and subjects received extra course credit in exchange for their participation. Subjects were informed that they were participating in a requirements determination task for building a retail website. College students make up a disproportionately large share of online users [80], and thus, this population was appropriate for the present experiment.<sup>7</sup> The experiment was conducted in

three phases.

## 4.1. Phase I

During Phase I, subjects’ individual preferences regarding certain generic website features were gathered using a survey containing a list of 20 features, such as a dynamic home page, order tracking, threedimensional views of products, brand comparison features, researching product features, online help, store locator, weekly advertisements, etc. Features for websites were chosen as the type of requirement in the stimulus materials because of the ubiquity of the web and the increased importance of customization of websites. Subjects indicated their preferences for features being available on the website using a seven-point Likert scale (with 1 indicating Strongly Disagree and 7 indicating Strongly Agree). At the end of Phase I, the experimenters determined website features for which subjects had expressed strong opinions. That is, subjects had rated these features as “1” or “7” (strongly disagree or strongly agree) on the Likert scale. These features were used for discussion during Phase II of the experiment.

## 4.2. Phase II

One week after gathering subjects’ preferences, subjects were invited for a group requirements session for further discussion of the requirements for the retail website. Every requirements session involved an analyst and <sup>fi</sup>ve participants, of which four participants were confederates employed for the purposes of this experiment. The “analysts” in all the group requirements sessions and subsequent follow-up interviews (discussed below) were Ph.D. students knowledgeable in both requirements determination and meeting facilitation. Subjects were told that the analysts were from a consumer buyer behavior institute. The analysts served solely as group facilitators during the group requirements sessions and o<sup>f</sup>ered no opinions or other information related to the manipulations. No demand e<sup>f</sup>ects were expected or observed due to any type of perceived power asymmetry between analysts and subjects. The confederates were undergraduate students trained prior to the experiment and were paid for their participation. A pool of 12 confederates was used for the experiment. They were grouped differently for each session (that is, the same groupings of confederates were not used each time to prevent them from becoming too familiar with each other and thus potentially making the participant feel like an outsider). Although each confederate knew his or her role and understood the procedures of the experiment, all confederates were blind to the hypotheses of the experiment. The responses of the confederates were scripted before each session because the confederates’ responses in each session were based on the particular participant’s preferences. Each group of confederates had a rehearsal before each session. During every session four debatable website features from Phase I were discussed (the discussion during the requirements session concerned the same type of website features as the survey in Phase I). These debatable features were customized for each participant; that is, the features were

## (footnote continued)

ones for which this participant had taken a strong position (1 or 7 on the rating scale) and which had solicited ratings of predominantly 1 s or 7 s from the study participants as a whole.<sup>8</sup> For the <sup>fi</sup>rst two features discussed in the requirements session, all the confederates expressed opinions opposite to the original preferences of the participant (from the week-earlier survey). For the last two features, one of the confederates expressed an opinion similar to that of the participant and the other confederates expressed opinions opposite to those of the participant. The participant was seated at the end of the table and thus heard all the opinions before providing his or her own opinion. Once all the members of the group expressed their opinions on any given feature, the group was asked to vote as to whether the feature should be included on the website. The subject’s vote concerning the feature was later analyzed to examine any shifts in preferences from his or her opinion on the prior survey.

At the beginning of Phase II, each subject was randomly assigned to one of the three conditions – control, action anxiety, or negative fantasies. The only di<sup>f</sup>erence in the experimental procedure administered to the three conditions was in the initial instructions given to the participants. Participants in the action anxiety group were told that in some cases it is possible that one or two group members might hold an opinion di<sup>f</sup>erent from that of the others in the group. Because such unique opinions are essential for gathering an accurate understanding of user requirements and critical for the systems development process, the group member with the di<sup>f</sup>ering opinion would be asked to write a page describing why he held such an opinion and why he thought that others’ reasoning might be <sup>fl</sup>awed. The participant’s statement would be read and evaluated privately by the analyst. It was expected that requiring participants to write a one-page explanation that would be evaluated by the analyst if they disagreed with all other group members would induce action anxiety. This is based on the theory, discussed earlier, that anxiety is caused when people anticipate having to disagree with group opinions. Writing an advocacy essay to re<sup>fl</sup>ect a stated position is the typical methodology utilized in the induced compliance paradigm” in cognitive dissonance studies [81], which are studies with characteristics similar to the current research, and is appropriate in the current research as well.

Participants in the negative fantasies condition were told that they would automatically receive <sup>fi</sup>ve extra credit points for their partici pation. They were also told that they could receive up to <sup>fi</sup>ve more points based on peer evaluations (aimed at evaluating each other’s contribution during the session) provided by other members of the group at the end of the session (although such peer evaluations were not in fact performed and every subject received full extra credit for his or her participation). Such peer evaluations were introduced to induce negative fantasies in the minds of the participants while participating in the requirements determination session. We expected that participants in this treatment condition would perceive that expressing views against the majority opinion could result in the loss of some or all of the <sup>fi</sup>ve points to be assigned by other group members. This is based on the theory, also discussed earlier, that people may have negative fantasies about being criticized by members of an otherwise unanimous or near unanimous group [44]. Participants in the control group received no special instructions.

## 4.3. Phase III

At the end of Phase II, the participants in the requirements determination session were each asked to go to another room for a follow up interview with a di<sup>f</sup>erent analyst. In reality only the subject was interviewed. The interview was aimed at eliciting detailed information regarding the subject’s perceptions and thought processes while participating in the requirements session and at ensuring that changes in the subject’s responses were due to the hypothesized causes and not because of genuine learning e<sup>f</sup>ects during the group discussion. None of the manipulations from Phase II were maintained during Phase III. Phase III consisted of a relaxed conversation between the analyst and user. Phase III functioned in part as a manipulation check and is discussed in greater detail in the results section below.

Each phase lasted approximately 15–20 min. All the sessions were tape-recorded and later transcribed and coded for data analysis. Every subject’s response was coded by comparing his initial response con cerning a feature gathered on the survey during Phase I with his response (whether he voted to include the feature) during the requirements session in Phase II. If the subject’s initial response (e.g., a high rating for the feature in Phase I) matched his response during the requirements session (e.g., voting in favor of including the feature in Phase II), the subject was given a score of “0”; otherwise, the subject was given a score of “1.” Thus, for example, if a subject rated a feature as highly undesirable on the survey in Phase I and then voted to include the feature in the website during Phase II, this pair of responses received a score of “1” (because his or her responses were inconsistent between the two phases). Subjects’ scores on the <sup>fi</sup>rst two discussion items were summed to calculate a “no confederate support” score, and the scores on the last two discussion items were summed to calculate a “confederate support” score for purposes of data analysis.

Further, between-group comparisons were made to understand the e<sup>f</sup>ect of providing support from one confederate on the subjects’ responses in the three conditions. Finally, within-group comparisons were made using paired t-tests to understand the e<sup>f</sup>ect of providing support from one confederate on the subjects’ responses. ## 5.2. Manipulation check

Phase III of the study acted as a type of manipulation check. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the phase was used to gauge whether subjects were basing their opinions on negative fantasies or action anxiety, or whether they had genuinely changed their minds because they had learned important information from other group members during the discussion. During the interviews, no subject mentioned that he had learned anything from other group members that a<sup>f</sup>ected his opinion or caused him to change his mind. Numerous subjects stated one or both of the following: (1) that it was not fair that other group members were permitted to give the extra credit points, and (2) that it was unfair that they should have to write a page of explanation if they disagreed with the group’s opinion. Both of these comments are consistent with the explanation of the Abilene Paradox as the cause of subjects’ behavior. Subjects’ responses are also consistent with the compliance explanation in Kelman’s (1958) conceptualization of conformity. Subjects complied with other group members’ responses because of the threat of “punishment,” not due to identi<sup>fi</sup>cation or internalization. Overall, the manipulation check showed that the results of the experiment were not due to learning by the subjects during the requirements sessions.

## 6. Discussion

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal a strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence of Abilene Paradox-type be havior on the requirements elicitation process in an information systems development setting. The illusion of agreement we found is clearly not due to groupthink, since the necessary conditions for groupthink were not present (the groups were transient rather than long-term, they did not have strong cohesiveness, and many subjects complained about other group members in the post-session interviews and indicated that they were not satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with or enthusiastic about the decision or the process). We showed that people in whom action anxiety or negative fantasies have been introduced are more likely to make statements in a requirements session that contradict their self-reported prior beliefs. Finally, we showed that support from one peer in a group requirements session can reduce the incidence of the conformity behavior. Together, the results suggest that Abilene Paradox-type conformity is a powerful phenomenon that must be addressed to ensure that candid and appropriate requirements are gathered from users. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings have important implications for theory, research, and practice.

## 6.1. Implications for theory and research

Prior research has linked conformity and illusions of agreement in the context of groupthink. However, no existing research has provided theoretical support for illusions of agreement due to Abilene Paradoxtype contexts in which groups are short term, transient, and non-cohesive. Thus, the present research <sup>fi</sup>lls a gap in the social conformity literature by documenting illusions of agreement in these types of groups. Such groups are prominent in IRD and IS development. Many user groups consist of people from various functional areas and often from di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of an organization’s hierarchy who are brought together for the single purpose of providing and discussing requirements, and who may not have ever met before and are unlikely to meet again (see, e.g., [28,100,101]). As noted by Carmel and Sawyer [101, p. 14] “IS project teams are actually more like transient groups of people.” We have also added to the social conformity literature by testing hypothesized antecedents of the paradox (action anxiety and negative fantasies) in a group decision-making context and <sup>fi</sup>nding support for the impact of these antecedents. We have also made a theoretical contribution to the systems development literature. As we discuss in more detail below, user participation is generally considered critical for successful requirements determination e<sup>f</sup>orts [8,30,102]. However, when the Abilene Paradox is present, users may not reveal their true beliefs and thereby undermine or reduce the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of user participation. Because our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest a di<sup>fi</sup>culty in the theoretical rationale for user participation in systems development, we provide an important contribution to theory for the area of requirements determination for systems development.

The requirements determination process is crucial because of its impact on downstream activities in systems development. As noted by Valusek and Fryback [103, p. 104], “Requirements are the representation of the desired condition. The quality of that representation sets an upper bound on the quality of the resulting system.” Clear and accurate requirements are the overriding objective of the IRD process, and threats to accuracy must be understood and mitigated. The present research demonstrates Abilene Paradox-type conformity as a previously undocumented threat to accurate requirements. Several areas of IS research should be reevaluated in light of the current <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

For example, researchers and practitioners in the area of IRD have often considered group requirements determination sessions (e.g., JAD) as a panacea for gathering complete and accurate requirements from users [22]. Though some research has examined group processes and their associated problems (especially groupthink) in the context of group requirements determination processes [11,19,104,105];, the Abilene Paradox has received no attention to this point. The present paper has demonstrated empirically that individuals will agree with other group members even when such agreement con<sup>fl</sup>icts with their own prior beliefs, consistent with predictions based on the Abilene Paradox. The results of the study, though found in a laboratory setting, are of considerable signi<sup>fi</sup>cance because the magnitude of the manipulated variables (action anxiety and negative fantasies) is often found to be more intense and complex in organizational settings [45,53]. Further research can examine other variables as causal in<sup>fl</sup>u ences on the Abilene Paradox and investigate all the variables under di<sup>f</sup>erent settings and conditions.

Research in both academia and industry shows that information systems projects continue to fail at a relatively high rate (e.g., [106–108]). Low user involvement and high con<sup>fl</sup>icts between user groups and development groups have been cited as reasons for IS project failures [31,61,109]. The results of the present study provide a new lens through which to examine the problems of user involvement and con<sup>fl</sup>icts. Con<sup>fl</sup>icts during systems development have been investigated in a substantial body of literature over the last three decades (e.g., [31,32,62]). Resource and time constraints have been commonly cited as causes of con<sup>fl</sup>icts during IRD. However, the current study suggests that these factors may result in Abilene Paradox-type conformity. For instance, time constraints could lead group members to simply agree with each other even though they do not personally believe in the validity of the group’s decision. It is also important to note that both con<sup>fl</sup>ict and the Abilene Paradox could arise within the same requirements gathering session. While group members might argue about some issues, they may agree on other issues (about which they truly disagree) to overcome resource constraints. Also, it is possible that when con<sup>fl</sup>icts escalate, group members might start agreeing with others to avoid further con<sup>fl</sup>ict and to end the session.

As noted above, user participation during information systems development is considered bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for a variety of reasons, including acquiring more complete and accurate requirements, building a better picture of the organization and the unit for which the system is being built, eliminating unnecessary system functionalities, facilitating user understanding and generating realistic user expectations about the system, decreasing user resistance to the changes imposed by the system, and increasing user commitment and system success [8,30,102]. Nevertheless, participation cannot be considered a com plete solution for the many problems encountered during IRD because the results of numerous studies on user participation are mixed and thus inconclusive [110–112]. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the present study may help explain the mixed results of past research and thus have important implications for the user participation literature. The results observed in earlier studies on user participation may have been due to the presence of Abilene Paradox-type conformity in some studies but not in others. In the presence of the paradox, although users participate, they may not provide requirements that re<sup>fl</sup>ect their true beliefs. Therefore, further research into this area can provide useful insights for improving our understanding of user participation and the success of the IRD process.

The results of this study also have important implications for the literature on requirements elicitation. Although group requirements elicitation methods are important for the creation and surfacing of knowledge, there are limits to what can be learned from users in group settings. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the present research indicate that one-on-one interviews with users remain an important elicitation method for the systems analyst. Although the possibility of demand e<sup>f</sup>ects (that is, users agreeing with the analyst’s opinion to please the analyst) remains in such situations, di<sup>fi</sup>culties due to illusions of agreement with other group members should be largely eliminated. Further, the results reinforce the importance of multi-technique approaches to requirements elicitation, as no single approach is likely to yield the best possible list of requirements.

The present study also has a number of important implications for systems development generally. First, the type of conformity seen here can lead to reduced discussion concerning critical issues in the development of a system, leading to potential alternative solutions to problems being overlooked. Systems development is an expensive endeavor that requires substantial investment in terms of both time and money. There are often multiple potential system designs and con<sup>fi</sup>gurations that may solve the organizational problem, and there may also be numerous potential projects competing for scarce company resources. Groups su<sup>f</sup>ering from the paradox are unlikely to question and probe the various alternatives thoroughly due to erroneous private beliefs about group consensus. Such a tendency can have high costs in terms of missed opportunities or unwarranted projects. Second, commitments made to projects that have been started may be escalated irrationally because developers choose to continue to perform work they have been asked to perform to avoid perceived negative consequences, despite their own private reservations. For example, a system designer who believes that a software development project on which he is working will not ultimately satisfy users’ requirements may not report that belief because his project manager and teammates appear to believe in the usefulness of the project (when in fact everyone on the team may instead share the same belief about the poor match to requirements). This can lead to escalation of a system development e<sup>f</sup>ort that is doomed, resulting in wasted time and resources [59,113].

Additionally, the present results have implications for the adoption of technology. It is common for technology managers to adopt technologies quickly without a full assessment of the organization’s needs [90,114]. Rapid adoption of technologies is often attributed to band wagon e<sup>f</sup>ects, but such decisions also may not be questioned by members of the adoption group because of their own action anxiety and negative fantasies and because of misperceptions about other group members’ beliefs. Therefore, the Abilene Paradox could lead organizations to venture into projects or to adopt technologies without fully addressing the fundamental value proposition for the organization.

Finally, the present research contributes to the general phenomenon of group conformity in organizational contexts by providing an empirical demonstration of conformity in a requirements determination context. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with pluralistic ignorance and show that both action anxiety and negative fantasies can cause conformity in group decision-making situations. To our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical test of these factors, and the results connect the general conformity literature to Abilene Paradox-type contexts. This <sup>fi</sup>lls an important gap in the literature since, as noted, there have been no empirical tests of Abilene Paradox-type conformity and illusions of agreement.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

Requirements gathering in group settings has become an increasingly common practice, and it is therefore important for systems analysts to be aware of the limitations of group requirements elicitation processes. Analysts need to monitor such sessions for indications of dysfunctional group dynamics. In so doing, they need to recognize the distinctive role of the Abilene Paradox in hindering quality requirements elicitation. As we have demonstrated, people under certain circumstances will not reveal their true beliefs about IS features, which may lead to undesirable features included in or omitted from systems. As these same people may ultimately have to use the systems, such situations are fraught with threats to system success.

Users’ unwillingness to state their true beliefs can have numerous negative impacts on the accuracy and completeness of requirements. As noted above, groups may agree on requirements for which no one truly agrees. Additionally, analysts face considerable potential “white-space risk” for requirements that are omitted (e.g., [115]). If users do not raise issues or suggest requirements because they believe everyone else in the group would disagree, potentially important requirements will not be discussed or documented. This leaves “white space” in the analyst’s list of requirements, reducing completeness of requirement elicited.

Given the weaknesses of consensus-based methods of decision making that a<sup>f</sup>ect the IRD process, a number of prescriptions can be o<sup>f</sup>ered to analysts. We recommend that analysts consider structural designs such as rotating people on and o<sup>f</sup> of JAD teams and role assignments such as devil’s advocates to build constructive con<sup>fl</sup>ict into requirements determination [7,44,58]. Methods such as these have been shown to produce better group decisions in organizational contexts (e.g., [116]) and thus can be employed by analysts as a strategy to break illusions of agreement.

Additionally, existing literature in organizational development has shown that when forces discouraging dissent can be reduced, individuals are willing to voice alternative viewpoints [5,44,117]. Because anonymity reduces pressures such as conformity [66,118], analysts could potentially address the problem of the Abilene Paradox by employing techniques such as the nominal group technique and online versions of group requirements determination, which allow aspects of group processes to be conducted anonymously. These techniques could alleviate threats perceived by group members while still reaping many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the group requirements elicitation process.

## 6.3. Limitations

The present study has several limitations. It is important to point out that we tested for the presence of an Abilene Paradox-type illusion of agreement by manipulating one individual only (with the other four group members acting as confederates). In a true Abilene Paradox situation, all group members would be subject to the same condition as the single subject in our experiment. However, individual-level behavior is the primary building block for group-level behavior. Moreover, given the evidence that most or all people present in a group meeting may not express their true beliefs (e.g., [59]), there is no reason to believe that the results would be di<sup>f</sup>erent had we manipulated everyone. It may be theoretically possible to manipulate an entire group of people in a similar manner, but such a design would result in considerably less experimental control, extraordinary complexity, and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher di<sup>fi</sup>culty in the interpretation of results. Although testing the presence of the paradox in organizational teams should be the long-term goal of this type of research, we wonder aloud whether such a design is experimentally realistic in a behavioral setting and whether the results, if obtained, would be reliable (as discussed in Footnote 7 above). Absent these conditions, inferences from single subjects, as performed here, may be the only reasonable possibility.

Second, it is worth noting that for purposes of experimental control, subjects did not engage in a complete group requirements determination session. That is, each participant expressed his or her opinion and had the opportunity to observe the reasoning and opinions of the others in the group, but participants did not thoroughly debate the website features as would occur in an ideal group requirements session. Thus, this experimental control factor must be considered a limitation of the results on the implications for group requirements sessions. However, given the e<sup>f</sup>ects of initially stated opinions on group conformity, as well as potential anchoring e<sup>f</sup>ects [119], it seems unlikely that further discussion would eliminate illusions of agreement. Illusions of agreement due to participants not speaking against a majority opinion are simply too widespread, even with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant discussion (e.g., [6,59]).

Third, it is important to note that the motivation level of the par ticipants in the current experiment is likely lower than in most requirements determination settings in organizations. In organizational contexts, users may have a strong interest in the outcome of the system. In such situations the expected response may be that people are more likely to voice an opinion even if it di<sup>f</sup>ers from the opinions of everyone else in the group. However, past research indicates that groups facing highly salient decisions, especially when they involve external threats, show, in the words of Taras [44, p. 408], “a dramatic increase in group solidarity.” Taras (p. 408) continues the previous quote with Janis’ [120, p. 227] observation: “[T]hey manifest increased motivations to retain a<sup>fi</sup>liation with a face-to-face group and to avoid actions that deviate from its norms.” On the other hand, research in social psychology has shown that people who are strongly motivated to report accurate information and who are held accountable for their responses are more likely to report their true beliefs rather than conform to a decision by others that they know is factually <sup>fl</sup>awed [68]. This situa tion is not a perfect parallel, however, because in the current experiment we were dealing with beliefs rather than facts. Further, the motivation and accountability of individual users in organizational settings are likely to vary widely. Although equivocal in their implications for the Abilene Paradox, these mixed results do serve as a potential limitation of the present study.

Fourth, we performed our experiment in a traditional JAD-type setting rather than an agile development setting. Although agile methods are common in systems development e<sup>f</sup>orts, we assumed a staged methodology because of our interest in traditional requirement elicitation and because of the potential di<sup>fi</sup>culties with many confounds in an agile setting. As our study is foundational research for the Abilene Paradox in IRD, we wanted to exert as many experimental controls as possible. Nonetheless, testing the presence of paradox-type behaviors in agile settings is a productive area for future inquiry.

All the issues discussed, although necessary for experimental control, are potential limitations in group requirements determination sessions in organizations. We believe, however, that because conformity and political pressures are likely stronger in organizational settings, the direction and magnitude of our results are likely to be the same or higher in organizational contexts.

## 7. Conclusion

Abilene Paradox situations have been discussed in the organizational development literature, but there have been no prior empirical demonstrations of this illusion of agreement. As we have shown, the requirements determination process is subject to the e<sup>f</sup>ects of false agreement consistent with the paradox in group settings. For an information system to be successful and meet the needs of its users, it is critical that accurate requirements be gathered. Given the importance of information systems in today’s competitive business environment, organizations cannot a<sup>f</sup>ord systems failures due to inaccurate requirements resulting from illusory agreement among members of groups. Therefore, it is important to recognize and mitigate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the Abilene Paradox.
