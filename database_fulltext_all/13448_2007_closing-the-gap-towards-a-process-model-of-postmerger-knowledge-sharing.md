# Closing the gap: towards a process model of post-merger knowledge sharing

Youngjin Yoo,\* Kalle Lyytinen<sup>†</sup> & Dongcheol Heo<sup>‡</sup>

\*Fox School of Business and Management, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA, USA, email: youngjin.yoo@temple.edu, <sup>†</sup>Weatherhead School of Management, Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, OH, USA, email: kalle@case.edu, and <sup>‡</sup>Department of Decision and Information Sciences, Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University, Rotterdam, the Netherlands, email: dongcheolh@yahoo.com

Abstract. We develop a process model of post-merger knowledge sharing based on distributed cognition, a systems perspective and path dependence. The framework conceptualizes knowledge sharing by layers of management choice and employee appropriation of knowledge resources seen as knowledge as content and knowledge as connection. We use the framework to study a merger of two polymer companies. The study reveals that mergers represent a discontinuity in knowledge sharing. Yet, chosen strategies often mirror the learned knowledgesharing practices of one of the merged companies and match poorly with the postmerger knowledge-sharing needs. Five factors emerged contribute to this knowledge gap: (a) the nature of the merger; (b) a lack of shared context; (c) the incompatibility of existing knowledge systems; (d) the tacit dimension of knowledge; and (e) time pressures of the merger. Our study shows that, employees enacted knowledge new sharing practices that differed significantly from the official strategy to close to the post-merger knowledge gap.

Keywords: knowledge sharing, distributed knowledge, post-merger integration, path dependence, knowledge management strategy

## INTRODUCTION

Recently, mergers and acquisitions (M&A) have been recognized as an important mechanism by which firms acquire knowledge. Unlike prior waves of mergers, complementary M&A acquire expertise, technologies and capabilities that are difficult to obtain via other mechanisms (Jensen, 1993; Farjoun, 1994; Brush, 1996; Rantf & Lord, 2002). They can release synergies among the knowledge resources within the merging firms (Shleifer & Vishny, 1991; Farjoun, 1994; Brush, 1996; Rantf & Lord, 2002). Consistent with this, Larsson & Finkelstein (1999) found that merging partners need not only have fit knowledge resources – similar and complementary – but they also have to create appropriate interactions. Similarly, Brush (1996)

argue that the improved performance of merged companies emanates from their heightened focus on knowledge resources. Finally, Vermeulen & Barkema (2001) point out that knowledge sharing during M&A can revitalize firms through leveraging new capabilities.

Mergers are plagued, however, by difficulties to integrate knowledge resources. The challenges during post-merger integration (PMI) have been widely documented through cases like Time-Warner vs. AOL. This challenge is borne of the same cause that originally motivated the merger – the diversity of knowledge resources. To date, research on knowledge management has focused primarily on intra-organizational knowledge sharing (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Schultze & Leidner, 2002). What surprises us is the scant attention paid to rare and atypical situations such as knowledge sharing across organizational boundaries during PMI, when knowledge must cut across borders quickly. The knowledge-based view of the firm (Kogut & Zander, 1992; Grant, 1996) posits that organizations exist to minimize the efforts to share knowledge and establish barriers. Mergers, however, disrupt this routine and present a discontinuity in the knowledge-sharing activities. Therefore, companies need a strategy to deal with the postmerger disruptions in knowledge sharing. The current literature provides us with few guidelines on how to effectively manage post-merger knowledge sharing (Farjoun, 1994; Brush, 1996; Rantf & Lord, 2002). Given the strategic importance of IT-based post-merger knowledge sharing (Larsson & Finkelstein, 1999), we need more research on post-merger knowledge sharing. To address current paucity of knowledge, we engage in theory development and validation cycle by conducting a case study of a post-merger knowledge sharing. Specifically, we address the following questions:

1 How does merger activity disrupt knowledge sharing?

2 How does management choose an appropriate post-merger knowledge-sharing strategy? What factors explain this choice?

3 How do individuals appropriate the chosen strategy given their post-merger knowledgesharing needs?

In answering these questions, we articulate a dual process model of post-merger knowledge sharing.

In what follows, we summarize pertinent literature and outline the knowledge-sharing framework. We then present main findings of case study of knowledge sharing during a merger of two polymer companies. We generalize our findings into a model that explains post-merger knowledge-sharing strategy choices and their appropriation. We conclude the paper by discussing implications of our findings for knowledge management research and practice.

## THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## Knowledge sharing

Knowledge sharing is process of creating a mutual stock of knowledge among individuals or groups – the knowledge that someone in the organization already knows – through direct or indirect interaction. The difficulties of knowledge sharing are well documented (von Hippel, 1994; Szulanski, 1996). In the literature, two different challenges pertinent to knowledge sharing have been identified. The first relates to the tacit nature of an individual’s knowledge (Kogut & Zander, 1992; Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Teece, 1998). Scholars have therefore argued that the key challenge in knowledge sharing is how to effectively translate tacit into explicit knowledge (Szulanski, 1996; Zack, 1999). This line of research emphasizes that knowledge is ‘out there’ to be created, collected, stored, retrieved and reused as a material object. It also underscores that knowledge needs to be codified in order to be managed effectively. Thereby, this line of research promotes explicit knowledge-sharing practices and investments that help ‘package and transfer’ knowledge through knowledge repositories.<sup>1</sup> In the literature, this approach is called the codification approach (Hansen et al., 1999).

The second approach focuses on the contextual nature of knowledge. Knowledge is hard to share as it is embedded in local relationships and material practices. Therefore, knowledge is socially constructed and collectively maintained in the relationships (Orr, 1990; Brown & Duguid, 1991; Boland & Tenkasi, 1995; Wenger, 1998). This line of research promotes knowledge sharing that builds ‘communities of practice’ that will nurture collective knowledge. Individuals’ identification with communities becomes a prerequisite for knowledge sharing. This approach is called the personalization approach (Hansen et al., 1999).

Earlier studies have viewed these approaches as two exclusive alternatives for knowledge management (Hansen et al., 1999). Yet, it is increasingly recognized that both approaches coexist and are necessary. For example, Birkinshaw & Sheehan (2002) argue that companies must mix these approaches while knowledge matures. A similar idea is expressed by Nissen (2006) who studied dynamic knowledge flows.

Much of the past research has been devoted to knowledge sharing under normal operating conditions. Yet, drastic changes in environments like a merger create a discontinuity to the taken-for-granted assumptions about knowledge sharing. Even mature knowledge (Birkinshaw et al., 2000) may become difficult to share while a newly formed context is created through a merger. Furthermore, knowledge sharing takes place through a diverse set of systems, structures, people and processes (Garud & Kumaraswamy, 2005; Nissen, 2006). Such dynamic uncertainty and heterogeneity will be amplified during PMI.

In the following, we draw on the distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995), systems perspective (Churchman, 1968; Garud & Kumaraswamy, 2005) and path dependence (David, 1985; Garud & Karnoe, 2001) to develop a process framework to analyse how conditions prior to the merger influence the management choice of post-merger knowledge-sharing strategies, and how faithfully employees enact these strategies during post-merger knowledge sharing.

## Knowledge in organizations as socio-technical networks

Knowledge is conceptualized here as an enacted socio-technical network of knowledge resources and capabilities (Yoo & Ifvarsson, 2001), a reservoir of individual knowledge (Argote & Ingram, 2000), and series of organizational routines (Winter & Szulanski, 2001). It is sociotechnical in that it recognizes dynamically interlaced technical and social subsystems (Pasmore et al., 1982). It takes into account the multiplicity and the heterogeneity of artefacts and actors involved in knowledge sharing (Garud & Kumaraswamy, 2005; Nissen, 2006). The approach conceptualizes further knowledge as having two dimensions, knowledge-as-content (nodes) and knowledge-as-connection (links) (Pasmore et al., 1982; Griffith & Dougherty, 2001).

Knowledge-as-content covers explicit knowledge held by individuals (Polanyi, 1966) and knowledge embodied in documents and digital information systems (Walsh & Ungson, 1991; Hutchins, 1995; Zack, 1999). It includes both explicit knowledge (e.g. scientific theories, formulae, facts and their relationships) and tacit knowledge (e.g. stories, know-how, metaphors) (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995). Knowledge-as-connection includes meta-knowledge of know-who and know-where (Moreland et al., 1996) shared mental models (Mohammed & Dumville, 2001) and an understanding of how to interrelate (Weick & Roberts, 1993; Faraj & Sproull, 2000). Knowledge-as-connection resides ‘primarily in specialized relationships among individuals and groups and in the particular norms, attitudes, information flows and ways of making decisions that shape their dealing with each other’ (Badaracco, 1991, p. 79). By using the language of Clark (1996), knowledge-as-connection rests on common ground – a set of shared mutual beliefs – between individuals. In organizations, knowledge-as-connection is supported by information technologies (Finholt & Sproull, 1990), as well as by the physical arrangements and behavioural codes, which either promote or hinder face-to-face interaction (Blau, 1977). Knowledge exists only virtually as a socio-technical network that is enacted through the actions within material practices (Weick & Roberts, 1993; Orlikowski, 2002).

Past research on knowledge management also suggests that knowledge sharing takes place across different levels (Nonaka, 1994). A systems perspective heeds us to pay attention to interactions across levels (Garud & Kumaraswamy, 2005). The outcome of knowledge sharing, then, can be understood as an outcome of interactions between the actions taken at the management level and the actions taken at the employee level. On one layer, management has the capability and power to mobilize and allocate resources and promulgate rules and norms as knowledge resources. Through these actions, management configures the knowledge resources. Thus, management actions shape knowledge-sharing strategies of the firm in a form of a desirable configuration of knowledge-as-content and knowledge-as-connection.

On the second layer, employees appropriate the configurations of knowledge resources. They can accept, reject, or modify rules and norms in their daily practices. They can actively follow or not follow the standards to meet their daily knowledge needs. This leads to the reproduction of knowledge resources in varying and unpredictable contents and connections (Schön, 1983). The outcomes of these actions become the basis of actions of the management in further refining the official knowledge-sharing strategy. The subsequent management actions will then transform the knowledge resources of the firm in response to appropriations by the employees. ## Post-merger knowledge sharing: path dependency and appropriations

Organization constantly seek to minimize efforts to integrate knowledge among groups (Grant, 1996) by relying on directions (Grant, 1996), routines (Nelson & Winter, 1982), norms and cultures (Walsh & Ungson, 1991) and information technology (Alavi & Leidner, 2001) to channel and integrate knowledge. Therefore, special configurations of knowledge-as-content and knowledge-as-connection are established and taken for granted that are deemed effective for the organizational action.

Mergers, however, disrupt these configurations. First, mergers change available knowledge resources. New partners bring new knowledge-as-content in the form of experts (Meyer & Lieb-Doczy, 2003), processes (Birkinshaw et al., 2000), products, tools (Main & Short, 1989; Sumi & Tsuruoka, 2002) and structures (Olie, 1994). Such knowledge-as-content invalidates some of the existing knowledge-as-connection, and demand firms to establish new sets of connections. Second, mergers change ways in which actors appropriate resources. Merger partners have different norms and cultures (Chatterjee et al., 1992; Weber & Camerer, 2003), which affect expectations and the appropriation of resources (Bock et al., 2005; Kankanhalli et al., 2005). Third, PMI processes are conducted under tremendous time pressure (Yoshikawa, 2003) in the midst of great uncertainty. All these factors suggest that knowledge sharing that was deemed satisfactory before a merger is likely to be inadequate after the merger.

In analysing post-merger knowledge-sharing challenge, the concept of path dependence is useful in understanding the dynamics of configurations. The idea of path dependence grew out of the works of David (1985) and Arthur (1989) in economics, and it brings a dynamic systems view into analysing organizational change. A path dependent process is one in which any action or event, seemingly insignificant at the time of its occurrence, becomes a major determinant of the future actions and events. The model suggests that history and temporality are pivotal in understanding how routines are adopted and enforced in organizations. According to Arthur (1989), path dependency is reinforced by large fixed costs, learning effects, coordination effects and adaptive expectations. Likewise, organizational learning theorists have posited that organizations rely on their past experience in their responses and therefore have a tendency to refocus on activities in which they have been successful (Levitt & March, 1988). Path dependence explains how past events become reinforced by subsequent events in a way that a self-perpetuating cycle is established (Arthur, 1989; March, 1991). Often, it influences the effectiveness of management actions (Garud & Karnoe, 2001), and in the end state organizations experience suboptimal technological and/or behavioural lock-in.

Path dependence can cause a vicious cycle in knowledge sharing in organizations as it reinforces the same behaviours acquired through learning-by-doing (Garud & Kumaraswamy, 2005). Organizations with established knowledge-sharing routines may experience therefore increased challenges during PMI. Yet, studies on post-merger knowledge sharing do not make assumptions about the historical context and thus fail to consider the inertia associated with knowledge sharing and how it influences the choice of post-merger knowledge sharing.

Path dependency in management action is more pronounced if the companies do not frequently engage in similar situations, or their learning from few samples is ineffective (Levinthal & March, 1993). Furthermore, as a result of the time pressure, management must spread its cognitive effort thinly in response to diverse and urgent challenges during the merger. In the context of the post-merger knowledge sharing, the management team is likely to develop a post-merger knowledge-sharing strategy based on existing knowledge-sharing practices. This happens, in particular, if either of the merging companies has effective knowledge-sharing practices. Thus, chosen post-merger knowledge-sharing strategies are likely to be path dependent, rather than reflective of the new demands. Thus, managerial choices may not fit with post-merger knowledge-sharing needs. We refer to this mismatch as a post-merger knowledge gap. We examine next a single PMI process in light of the dual layer process of post-merger knowledge sharing outlined above.

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

## Research design

Executing a research project to study PMI knowledge sharing presents a challenge for several reasons. First, pre-merger discussions are conducted secretly among high-level executives. Therefore, information about the merger rarely becomes public before the final announcement. At the same time, gaining access to an organization requires a long lead time. Moreover, postmerger processes should be studied shortly after the merger in order to tap into fresh experiences that individuals face. Mergers create a high degree of uncertainty about job security, role and task definitions. All these factors limit the choice of effective data collection methods. Given all this, cross-section surveys are in most cases inconceivable. Given the time pressures and the high-degree of uncertainty that accompanies PMI, it is equally difficult to conduct ethnographic studies. Given these unique challenges, a case study methodology was deemed appropriate, as our goal was to not to generalize to a population, but from a case to a theory.

According to Yin (1994, p. 40), a single case study can be useful when the purpose of the study is revelatory in nature. Corporate mergers are relatively rare for individual companies, but at the same time they offer a unique opportunity to examine knowledge sharing across organizational boundaries. In our case, we opted to carry out a single case study in order to theorize about the nature and the process of knowledge sharing during PMI. The analytical framework formulated in Figure 1 was deemed too high-level to provide detailed research propositions about post-merger knowledge sharing. Therefore, we sought to enrich the framework through empirically grounded observations (Glasser & Strauss, 1967; Miles & Huberman, 1984). We hoped to generate rich theoretical explanations of changes in knowledge-sharing practices during and after the merger and to what extent they were caused by contingencies that characterized the organizational context by examining: (a) the organizational and historical context of the merger, (b) a close follow-up of post-merger knowledge-sharing processes, and (c) tracking changes associated with knowledge sharing after the merger.

## Site selection

The study took place in one of the world’s largest polymer compound manufacturing and service companies hereafter referred to as Omni. Omni was chosen for the study for two primary reasons: first, it has been recognized as having a strong culture of managing knowledge, and second, it has recently experienced a merger between two almost equal but complimentary firms in an industry that is becoming increasingly knowledge intensive. The case provides an excellent opportunity for studying the post-merger knowledge-sharing strategy developed by the management, their post-merger knowledge-sharing needs, and the actual knowledgesharing practices.

Omni was formed through a merger of two firms that we refer to hereafter as Alpha and Beta. The merger was regarded as collaborative by combining the capabilities of two equal but complementary firms. Prior to the merger, Alpha was known as one of the world’s largest producers of vinyl compounds and vinyl specialty resins/formulators. It had grown its business by acquiring companies with complimentary products and through joint ventures. Alpha had generally been successful in integrating the acquired companies into its business. Beta produced a wide range of colour additives and custom plastic/rubber compounds and offered resin distribution services. Like Alpha, it grew through a series of acquisitions and in the late 1980s began to exclusively acquire firms in the polymer industry. Throughout the 1990s, Beta acquired a number of polymer companies and participated in joint ventures, all of which helped position Beta as a global Fortune 500 company.

In 2000, Omni produced 55 000 products, generated \$3 billion in sales, employed 10 000 individuals and served 35 000 customers in over 30 countries. Omni was organized into three business segments: (1) performance plastics; (2) elastomers and performance additives; and (3) distribution. Five separate business units worked independently in these three units.

## Data collection and analysis

In total, 22 in-depth interviews were conducted. Table 1 describes the job area, positions and original membership of the interviewees. We also conducted direct observations of the systems and analysed archival documents that included the company’s annual reports, press releases, news media coverage and interviews given by senior executives. In designing our interview strategy, we tried to ensure that we could obtain data about the knowledge-sharing practices both prior to and after the merger. Thus, interviews included key executives, managers of information systems and human resource, and the manager and staff members of Knowledge Center in order to learn about the intended knowledge-sharing strategy. We also interviewed employees working in the sales and marketing and research and development areas to understand the actual knowledge-sharing needs and practices before and after the merger.

Data were collected through three steps. First, we collected archival data and documents on the history, sales, organization and policies of Omni (including Alpha and Beta) and made direct observations of the firm’s official knowledge management system. Two pilot interviews were performed to explore the potential research issues. Finally, based on the insights obtained from the pilot interviews, semi-structured interviews were conducted to examine the current and chosen knowledge-sharing strategy, new knowledge-sharing needs and actua practices. When asking about actual knowledge practices, we followed a modified critical incident method, asking for detailed accounts of the interviewee’s recent knowledge-sharing experiences with individuals from Alpha, if the person used to work for Beta, and vice versa. This allows us identify key factors that inhibit and facilitate knowledge sharing at Omni after the merger. In addition, we asked about employees’ merger experiences and discussed the new role of information technology in enabling knowledge sharing before, during and after the merger. Interviews lasted between 1–2 h. Most interviews were tape-recorded and transcribed verbatim (except when the interviewee did not allow tape-recording and we had to rely on our personal notes from interviews).

## FINDINGS

## Knowledge sharing at alpha and beta prior to the merger

Before the merger, Alpha and Beta had different knowledge management practices. Alpha’s managers encouraged knowledge sharing among people by providing managerial support knowledge sharing among employees. The firm established a Knowledge Center in 1997. The Knowledge Center evolved from the corporate library, which was established at Alpha in 1963. Established in 1980a digital knowledge repository called the Central Technical File (CTF) holds polymer and plastic R&D information. Before the merger, CTF contained about 70 000 documents. The Knowledge Center also managed the corporate intranet, which hosted CTF, human resource modules and other research-related databases. The intranet features a search engine. Alpha’s top management actively supported all these activities by allocating financial and human resources. Most new employees at Alpha participated in a training session hosted by the Knowledge Center. During these training sessions, they were taught the importance of knowledge sharing, how to search for knowledge and how to post to CTF the new information they had obtained. Researchers and scientists voluntarily followed the policy by generating, storing and leveraging reports from projects. One researcher made the following comment: ‘We had a motto, “In God, we trust and bring knowledge.”’ Alpha’s corporate culture had been recognized as open, trusting and collaborative. A former Alpha employee recalled the following:

Back then, we used to know one another very well. Promotions, marriages, births and retirements were company-wide news and all employees took the time to celebrate the occasions. . . . Also, we trusted each other.

Beta’s culture differed from Alpha’s. Since Beta had grown primarily through a series of acquisitions, it had always cultivated an entrepreneurial spirit. Consequently, competition among divisions was encouraged and expected. Most top executives made decisions independent of one another and there was minimal, if any, communication outside each division. A former Beta manager stated:

There was no so-called Beta culture. There were only cultures representing units or divisions of Beta, not for all of Beta . . . Beta didn’t much care about culture.

The lack of organizational culture also led to a lack of knowledge sharing. As one Beta manager said:

Some Beta departments used to have rooms filled with file cabinets. In each cabinet, there were files and documents never shared with others outside the department. Sometimes, even members of the department had no idea what’s in there.

Complicating the situation, Beta had 18 information systems that operated independently as a result of its uncontrolled growth strategy. Information was rarely shared especially across divisions. A staff member of the Knowledge Center described the situation at the time of the merger:

We tried to have Beta people [who were specialized in knowledge management] in our Knowledge Center at the time of the merger. But we couldn’t, because we couldn’t find any. There was no one doing knowledge management or even a library job at Beta. Since they were bigger than we were, we expected some facilities and systems [for knowledge management]. But, there was nothing. One time we heard that they had a library at one place. But, it was a bunch of books located at the corner of small office. And there was no one managing those books.

In summary, Alpha had installed knowledge-sharing practices throughout the firm, particularly among scientists and the senior executives provided necessary support. CTF and the Knowledge Center played a vital role in establishing a knowledge-sharing culture at Alpha. In contrast, Beta had no systematic efforts to support knowledge sharing.

## Post-merger knowledge-sharing strategy at Omni

Knowledge sharing at Omni was developed following the Alpha model. Beta’s employees were integrated into Alpha’s systems and educated into its sharing culture under the initiatives launched by the new CEO. Beta’s databases, enterprise resource planning systems and other systems were all absorbed into Alpha’s systems. As Omni’s CIO mentioned, ‘We did an inventory of all different Beta systems and tried to get our arms around it and then developed one Omni system.’ Alpha’s intranet, with its knowledge management system, received some cosmetic changes and became the official company site, called Knowledge Web. This was not a difficult decision, as Beta had neither an active knowledge management practices nor an intranet. Former Beta employees were introduced to the knowledge management systems and culture by offering training sessions through the Knowledge Center.

The codification approach was dominant regarding the preferred knowledge management strategy among Omni’s upper management and staff members at the Knowledge Center. Their beliefs were evident with regard to what should be considered ‘desirable’ knowledge sharing as well as what was the best way to implement it. Most Omni senior managers and Knowledge Center staff members thought that Omni’s corporate knowledge should be documented and fed into a system following a set of standardized rules. As one upper manager described:

. . . Our knowledge management process follows three steps. First, we gather ideas and opinions from people. Then, we put these together. And finally, I think that this is the most important step, we generate reports for the decision-making or future use. . . . We have a standardized format to store knowledge in our Intranet. So, all the reports follow this standardized format.

In most cases, coding and storing were considered to be the best methods of knowledge sharing. Among the managers and staff members of the Knowledge Center, documentation and knowledge management are synonymous. For example, an Omni manager commented:

I always emphasize the importance of knowledge sharing to my staff members. So, I keep telling my people that we should document and feed the Intranet with what we have done. And, I am sure that all my team members are pretty good at how to generate, download and upload the documents. At least, in my team, we actively create and share most important work-related knowledge.

Here, the phrases ‘knowledge sharing’ and ‘document and feed the intranet’ are interchangeable.

From the vantage point of the planned knowledge-sharing strategy, Omni valued the reliable content that emerged from codified knowledge. Codified knowledge was distributed as ‘official’ knowledge to relevant individuals and teams. Unofficial forms of communication such as phone conversations, voice mail and email messages were discouraged and used only to convey informal feedback. One manager of a research group mentioned the following:

We normally send written document to the other staffs and production people and [we] hardly use e-mail or videoconference, because these methods cannot store and codify the knowledge at all.

These content-oriented attitudes and practices stem from the widely held belief that sharing a standardized format of knowledge builds a similar understanding among people. As one manager explained:

It is so important for us to share the same understanding of each issue that we handle. We also need to keep all the records for our future use . . . So every time we [he and his staff] discuss a project, we try to document our idea about what the goal of the project is, how to approach the issue, what the possible solution is, and what the expected problems are. . . . And, next time we discuss or work on the same issue, we always go back to what we had documented first and then build on it. . . .

Finally, a knowledge repository was the most important tool in knowledge-sharing strategy. As noted, the firm’s library had served as Alpha’s central knowledge repository and become the epicentre of knowledge management activities. Major outputs from R&D projects were documented and fed into central repositories, including as CTF and other databases. Staff members were encouraged to document other knowledge and to upload the material onto the intranet. Similarly, Knowledge Web had become a new knowledge repository that extended Alpha’s library after the merger. As the manager of the Knowledge Center described:

The greatest part of Knowledge Web is pulling things together in one place. In other words, all the contents and the applications can be integrated in Knowledge Web.

Taken together, the codification approach executed through the centralized knowledge repository (CTF and Knowledge Web) and supported by the Knowledge Center was the chosen knowledge-sharing strategy. Most of these beliefs were inherited from Alpha, which had followed a successfully codification approach prior to the merger.

## Post-merger knowledge-sharing practices at Omni

While the codification approach was the official knowledge-sharing strategy at Omni, its effectiveness was rather limited when compared with what had been envisioned. At Omni, employees complemented the codification approach with a personalization approach. The personalization approach was particularly dominant when the employees sought knowledge across the boundaries. Many employees noted that they could ‘access the intranet and CTF less than before’, ‘frequently resort to face-to-face interaction’ or ‘double check the meaning of the document through face-to-face interaction or phone calls’ when they sought knowledge within the company. A former Beta employee described it simply: ‘employees don’t use the intranet much.’ Similarly, a former Alpha employee reported the situation as follows:

I think I am pretty much confident that I can find Beta knowledge from the [knowledge management] systems. However, I hardly tried it. . . . Rather, I call the [Beta] people or go to them and get what I want to know directly.

The personalization approach included email, phone calls and frequent face-to-face meetings. Some employees also used other technologies to support the personalization. Furthermore, some employees used the centralized knowledge repository to enact the personalization approach. For example, one member of Omni’s quality group noted the following:

We have Quality Web in our group and there we post questions and exchange what we know through a discussion board. To me, this site and these people have been the most important knowledge source because I can get almost any knowledge, which I can’t get from the manual, from here. It’s like a small community. I make a phone call or sometimes I visit their offices for my tough questions. In this way, we help one another.

The Quality Web site was originally developed by the Knowledge Center as a part of the Knowledge Web. The manager of the Knowledge Center approached the quality group to find the ‘business critical contents.’ He recalls the following:

What they suggested to me, on the other hand, was to create a discussion board with which people can share quality-related information with one another. It can be thought of as a knowledge repository residing in the intranet in a sense. However, the more important role of this board soon became making connections among these people, which later enabled further knowledge sharing through various communication channels. And now I think it is a great way of sharing knowledge.

In this example, an intranet was designed to be a repository but it later became a vehicle for creating connections to support new knowledge-sharing patterns. Although the discussion board was ‘technically’ set up by the Knowledge Center, the members of the quality group performed the maintenance of the board and yet the board was not recognized as part of Omni’s official knowledge resources.

Another example of a post-merger knowledge-sharing practice was the weekly Friday conference call among 220 scientists and engineers. The call was instituted shortly after the merger by the CTO who came from Beta in an effort to complement the official knowledge management in the engineering side. He sought to connect researchers and engineers scattered at different parts of the world. A member of the Friday conference calls commented:

Before the merger, most of Alpha’s engineers were located in [one geographical] area. However, after the merger, Omni realized the need for communication and coordination on a more regular basis, because Beta engineers were distributed all over the world.

Taken together, while Omni’s post-merger knowledge-sharing resources were officially configured around codification, its actual knowledge-sharing practices produced elements of both codification and personalization. In particular, the personalization approach was used when individuals sought knowledge across the boundary. Knowledge-sharing practices at Omni were thus different from what was originally intended by management. While the locus of the officia knowledge-sharing strategy was to create, manage and distribute codified knowledge, the actual practices focused on the informal sharing of knowledge using primarily personalization approaches. Although the central knowledge repository was considered to be a critical important asset to Omni’s employees, after the merger it was the network of peers that became the most important knowledge resources. In addition, Omni’s employees appropriated technologies that were originally designed to support codification to enact both personalization and codification.

An important question to ask, then, is why did Omni’s employees enact hybrid knowledge practices despite management’s deliberate effort to promote codification? It is important to ask this question because the very same approach was successful at Alpha prior to the merger. The analysis of the critical incident interviews focusing on post-merger knowledge sharing between former Alpha and Beta employees suggests that after the merger Omni experienced a post-merger knowledge gap. The enacted hybrid knowledge-sharing practices can be seen as the employees’ attempt to close the gap.

Five factors emerged from the interviews as key factors contributing to gap: (a) the nature of the merger; (b) the lack of shared context; (c) the incompatibility of knowledge systems; (d) the tacit dimension of knowledge; and (e) time pressure.

## Nature of the merger

The two companies merged to become a service solution provider in the polymer area, serving their clients with a comprehensive set of solutions. The CEO commented on the purpose of the merger in an interview:

We’ve created something unique, particularly at this scale, because, in forming Omni, we’ve set out to create a worldwide polymer service company. The concept of the company is to have a breadth of product and service technology combined with a market infrastructure, so that we can bring a wide array of technology and service solutions to a wide array of markets, and do that on a global scale. With Omni’s breadth of product line, we do businesses in virtually every market that consumes plastics or rubber.

Reflecting on this notion of a polymer service company, the management characterized Omni as becoming ‘a single source for customers to look to for solutions and growing needs’ as one of the top three strategic initiatives. The urgency to effectively share and integrate knowledge is made evident by the repeated announcements by the top management that Alpha and Beta needed to act as ‘a single company.’ To successfully implement the concept of a polymer service company, Omni’s employees had to integrate knowledge resources from both Alpha and Beta. Sales people were expected to offer complementary products or offer a complete solution that required a combination of Omni’s products. Employees in the research and development area were also expected to be aware of developments in other areas. Given that Alpha and Beta initially had little overlap in their product lines, this created a huge challenge for most employees. The situation was further complicated by the radically changed relationships between knowledge-as-content and knowledgeas-connect after the merger.

## Lack of shared context

One factor that changed the relationship between knowledge-as-content and knowledge-asconnection at Omni was a lack of shared context. For example, a former Beta marketing employee stated the following:

Every time you try to get information from the systems or this new group of people [Alpha people] whom you never had any contact with, you can’t accept that information at face value because you should consider what the context behind the knowledge is and what the assumptions are. Then you need to be clear about what your situation is. And finally, you should compare these two sets of contexts to figure out whether it really fits you or not.

This was aggravated by the increased variance in markets, technology and products. A former Beta employee explained these widening differences in the products as an example of the shared context problem. His remarks follow:

There is some confusion in understanding each other. Most of them are technical in nature. They [Alpha employees] come from different technical assumptions. They [Alpha employees] are mostly working with vinyl and we [Beta employees] handle resin. And this makes huge differences in terms of the market approaches, product methodologies and so on. For example, their color match is suitable to vinyl but it’s not appropriate with ours because ours are for resin . . . For Beta, when we talk about knowledge and about how to save raw materials, we are talking about resin. However, for Alpha, in the same situation, they are talking about vinyl and additives because they were specialized in them.

The shared context problem also arose as a result of differences in organizational norms. For example, a former Alpha human resource manager illustrated different history backgrounds and traditions as follows:

I would say that it [a primary issue in communicating with Beta people] is more about confusion than about difficulty in understanding. . . . No, it’s more than just a confusion. In many times, when I talk to Beta people, I realize that we frame the issues and questions differently . . . For example, at Alpha, we have a policy that if you are sick, you are still paid for it. It is for everybody. I mean it’s for a salary paid person and an hourly paid person. After the merger, I accidentally discovered that Beta had a different sick time policy. They only paid for salaried employees, not for hourly paid employees. Historically, there has been a high rate of turnover at Beta’s hourly paid worker, and that’s why they didn’t pay for it. So, that came from different historical background and cultural differences as a holding company. It was shocking to me because for almost one year after the merger, I didn’t know it. For a year, we have integrated HR policies and data into the system. And all of a sudden, we discovered that Omni employees are getting paid by two different sick time policies.

The lack of shared context created a sense of uncertainty and incompleteness. These were not, however, inherent attributes of the codified documents, but rather resulted from the lack of shared context. If the same codified knowledge were to be shared between the individuals who already shared the context, it would have been viewed complete and less uncertain. The following excerpt from an Alpha employee illustrates why employees from Alpha felt it was necessary to complement codification with personalization:

‘Getting the documents from the system is just the beginning of the project. Of course, it is a very important process and that’s why we retrieved the documents and drawings of past projects when we start new projects. However, to me, having a thorough understanding of what really happened and how did it happen is much more important than obtaining a nice description. And, I got this information through face-to-face interactions, phone calls, and email communications with other people.

This comment explains why the codification approach was effective. Such shared contexts established effectively pragmatic knowledge among employees so that they could yield similar interpretations. After the merger, however, employees could no longer establish pragmatic knowledge through documents alone (Carlile, 2002). In fact, the chosen knowledge-sharing strategy that focused on a codification approach widened the gap.

## Incompatibility of knowledge systems

Alpha had spent many years developing and refining its centralized knowledge repository and had successfully implemented Enterprise Resource Planning Systems. In contrast, Beta never had a centralized knowledge resource and its critical knowledge was stored in locally in file cabinets and bookshelves on various platforms. A former Alpha employee noted the differences:

Alpha has taken a disciplined and structured approach to knowledge management. It developed knowledge sharing policies written on documents, put together technologies and organized KM concepts in a systematic way aligned with its marketing plan. Also, it constantly educated its employees. On the other hand, Beta was a bunch of companies that had no communication . . .

Such incompatibility significantly reduced the speed and effectiveness with which the centralized combined knowledge repository was built. One staff member noted the following:

System integration is also important in the post merger period. I saw that sometimes people spend their precious time in trying to contact one another to get information directly from the source because they cannot access to it through the systems. Especially, Beta side systems and information should be immediately integrated into the corporate systems and intranet. Of course, I know it will never be easy because Beta’s data and documents had been poorly managed.

Similarly, the CIO of Omni commented on the challenges of integrating two systems:

Tasks such as establishing a common platform, collecting knowledge from both Alpha and Beta, coding, formatting and storing them are so important for successful knowledge management. So, we should go through all these events, although it takes time.

Such incompatibility between systems in Alpha and Beta made it difficult for Omni’s employees to rely on the knowledge repository alone and decreased the effectiveness of codified knowledge. This is vividly illustrated by a former Alpha employee:

Because the way the system organizes, knowledge may be hidden in a totally different place under a totally unexpected category and so on. However, if someone in Beta says, ‘you can find what you want from here,’ it would be probably fine and safe.

The quote suggests that the lack of integrated knowledge systems led to the decreased level of shared semantics. The same thing was stored under different categories (synonym) and different things were stored under the same category (homonym). Such differences over time led to increased post-merger knowledge gaps.

## The tacit dimension of knowledge

Omni employees pointed out that hard-to-formalize and tacit dimension of knowledge formed increased challenge in post-merger knowledge sharing. Employees felt no policy was in place to help them capture and manage the tacit aspects of knowledge. They often resorted to face-to-face meetings to share knowledge. For example, a former Beta employee commented:

Oh, [the importance of having face-to-face meetings] is for having the knowledge from the Alpha side. . . . Actually, in many cases, using the knowledge management system [may be] faster. I can access it any time, and can get what I want in a standardized form. I like it . . . Well, when I seek the Alpha side knowledge, I need more information. And, usually, it’s not on paper.

As a result of Omni’s multiple product lines, which required deep hands-on knowledge in diverse manufacturing processes, employees emphasized the tacit dimension within scientific, engineering and manufacturing knowledge that could not be easily captured in documents. A former Alpha employee commented as follows on reasons why he could not rely on the Knowledge Web:

I think knowledge from written sources is cold and factual in nature and does not let one know the depth of understanding of the knowledge in the organization nor does it show how deeply in the organization a practice is deployed. For example, I may read that it is a practice to verify all incoming raw materials against established specification. However, it is only when I get fresh knowledge directly from the site that I can find out whether people know how to find specifications, whether they understand what they are looking at, and how often an issue arises. In this regard, face-to-face contact or hands-on experiences are more credible sources of knowledge.

The tacit dimension of knowledge was also important in administrative domains such as human resources and finance. A human resources manager commented:

If I want basic information, then reading manuals and documents are O.K. However, if I should assess performances, then I need face-to-face interactions and direct observation.

A lack of appreciation of the tacit dimension of knowledge made employees unsure of the relevance of the codified knowledge, while at the same time the importance of knowing about the ‘faces behind the documents’ became more critical. Employees therefore resorted to a personalization approach in order to better judge the relevance of the available content. The following quote illustrates this:

In a sense, well-specified knowledge can be dangerous because documentation simplifies the whole picture of things, and almost always omits some possibly important components you should know. You know, things are not that simple and clear in reality. Besides, things keep changing. . . . So, to me, knowing what really happened and how it happened is much more important than getting some nice descriptions.

Past research has noted that all knowledge has both explicit and tacit dimensions (Polanyi, 1966; Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Cook & Brown, 1999), and that tacit knowledge can be shared through the socialization process (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). Prior to the merger, Alpha was able to share tacit knowledge by deploying a widely shared context that resulted from effective socialization. After the merger, however, it was no longer possible to share tacit knowledge as employees lacked a shared context. As a result, the effectiveness of knowledge sharing through codification became limited, which in turn contributed to widening post-merger gap. Knowledge sharing through codification can thus only be effective when there is a shared context created through socialization.

## Time pressure

The merger created an unprecedented sense of time pressure. One Alpha employee noted:

[Merger process] just tried to do an awful lot in such a short period of time. We put a lot of issues on the table. They decided to go to market differently. So, they structured a new organization very differently than what it was structured before. Everybody thought it was an almost new job. It made things fairly confusing at first. It was hard to understand.

During this busy period, searching for the right document on the Knowledge Web took too long:

But, I don’t think that the knowledge repository and database have been helpful in the integration process. We are extremely busy in every project so we don’t have time to search the system to see what else is available out there. So until we are sure where that useful knowledge exactly exists in the system, we will not use the system.

The employees therefore responded by bypassing the Knowledge Web and going directly to experts. The following reflects these changes:

When I need knowledge and I don’t know where to locate it, I always rush to a bunch of people who I think are experts. To me, it is much easier and saves time in most cases. Since the knowledge I need is more general and context dependent, searching the systems and documents does not give me a direct answer to my question. So, even though I expect I can find some related knowledge from the files and documents, I first go and ask other people about it.

The sense of increased time pressure also created a sense of information overload, and most individuals associated this with the perception of a rapidly growing knowledge repository, which was created. A former Alpha employee said the following:

Information overload is another current issue: just too much information for people to absorb. So they simply ignore it instead. Things have been getting worse since the merger because a huge volume of information was brought in from the Beta side.

One Alpha employee noted that some employees responded to the information overload by relying less on the codified knowledge. He commented:

I think that sometimes people even access the Intranet and database less than before and make conscious efforts to communicate less so that they will absorb information and knowledge effectively.

Omni employees found after the merger that the new knowledge-sharing environment was overwhelming both in terms of speed and volume. This was aggravated by the increased lack of shared context and the incompatibility of knowledge systems. This finding aligns with Rantf & Lord’s (2002) claim that rapid integration after the acquisition can be detrimental to knowledge retention and integration.

## DISCUSSION

Building on the framework of the knowledge sharing as a dual layer process, we have sought to analyse the post-merger knowledge-sharing challenge in light of three key questions. Below, we will scrutinize answers to these three questions.

## Discontinuity of knowledge sharing after a merger

The first research question is about factors that affect the discontinuity of knowledge sharing after a merger. The Omni case revealed five such factors: the nature of mergers, the lack of shared contexts, the incompatibility of knowledge systems, tacit knowledge and time pressure.

The complementary nature of the merger between Alpha and Beta and the new strategic orientation of the firm required rapid and effective sharing of diverse knowledge. The knowledgesharing requirements would have been very different had the merger taken place between two similar companies that shared overlapping capabilities as well as the goal of improving capital efficiency. In such a case, post-merger knowledge management would have focused on identifying and purging redundant knowledge. In the case of HP and Compaq’s merger, one of the critical post-merger knowledge management activities was indeed the identification and realignment of redundant and similar resources (Tam, 2003). In the case of Omni, because of the economic downturn after the merger, the company had to go through a series of consolidations. Such consolidations, however, were not part of the original merger objectives and did not affect post-merger knowledge sharing during our data collection.

Other factors also influenced the balance between mechanisms that emphasize knowledgeas-content and those that emphasize knowledge-as-connection in that the merger radically changed their mutual dynamics. The gaps in the knowledge-as-connection mechanism that were caused by the merger reduced the effectiveness of the sharing of knowledge-as-content. Overall, the effectiveness of sharing of knowledge-as-content depends highly on the effectiveness of sharing knowledge-as-connection. Contrary to what prior works suggests (Hansen et al., 1999; Zack, 1999), tacitness of knowledge is not an inherent characteristics of knowledge-as-content, but influenced by the degree to which knowledge-as-connection is shared.

The notion of a common ground (Clark, 1996) clarifies the role of a shared context in knowledge sharing. A common ground refers to the beliefs that each individual engaging in conversation believes the other conversant to possess (i.e. ‘I know that you know that I know’). Clark (1996) demonstrates the importance of common ground in understanding language by showing that the two individuals engaging in conversation can each help their partner better understand them by seeking to build a common ground. Accordingly, Clark rejects the notion that the meaning of a sentence is inherently encoded in its words and that the meaning can be discovered through decoding (see also Wittgenstein, 1953; Rommetweit, 1974). The meaning of a sentence emerges in the context of a common ground established in a dialogue. Similarly, the meaning of knowledge-as-content emerges from the meaning of knowledge-as-content around it.

Past research on knowledge management emphasizes the importance of task characteristics, organizational contingency planning, organizational strategy and the type of knowledge that needs to be shared in choosing a knowledge-sharing strategy (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Hansen et al., 1999; Becerra-Fernandez & Sabherwal, 2001). Organizational culture has been recognized as a critical element in effective knowledge sharing (Lam, 1997; Bock et al., 2005; Wasko & Faraj, 2005). Yet, differences in these key factors were precisely why firms were attracted to enter into a merger. Therefore, we need different theoretical models into postmerger knowledge sharing where partners have drastically different cultures, tasks, processes and practices.

## Choosing post-merger knowledge sharing

Our second question ask about what explains the choice of post-merger knowledge sharing. We observed clear path-dependency in Omni’s choice of a post-merger knowledge-sharing strategy. Such path dependency occurred as a result of the following factors: (a) specific technological artefacts had already been implemented in the organizations; (b) a ‘knowledge base’ of the firm consisted of a shared framework of ‘fundamental design concepts’; and (c) organizational routines (Coombs & Hull, 1998). Our study also shows that organizations are particularly susceptible to path dependencies when reaction times are short (Levinthal & March, 1993). Alpha had an effective knowledge repository (hardware), a strong knowledge-sharing culture, a centralized supporting staff (knowledge base), a clearly defined codification process (routines), and a management team under time pressure. Omni’s management was early on ‘locked-in’ to its codification strategy even though it might not have been the optimal choice given the dramatically different knowledge-sharing needs. It is not clear, however, what would have happened had Beta had a different (i.e. connection-oriented), yet successful, knowledgesharing practice. It is equally unclear what would have happened if neither of the merging firms had established institutional knowledge-sharing patterns. What is clear, however, is that the chosen knowledge-sharing strategy around the central knowledge repository did not reflect new knowledge-sharing needs.

The results point out the important roles of history in considering the choice of a knowledgesharing strategy. Future research needs to consider the role of path dependency, management’s interpretation of the knowledge needs and environments, and the incremental revision of organizational routines in choosing knowledge-sharing strategies.

## Enacted post-merger knowledge-sharing practices

The answer to our third question shows that when there is a post-merger knowledge gap, employees will appropriate the knowledge-sharing strategy in ways that seek to fill the knowledge gaps. Such a gap can grow if the managerial response is path-dependent, as in the case of Omni. The gap will also grow when the merger results in different knowledge-sharing needs, as was the case with Omni. In contrast, if the company has developed well-established PMI routines through repeated complementary mergers, the gap is likely to be smaller (Finkelstein & Haleblian, 2002).

Omni’s employees enacted a hybrid approach that combined flexibly content- and connection-oriented approaches. The enacted practices are not consistent with the chosen knowledge-sharing strategy, consistent with empirical studies which show that knowledge-sharing plans often do not mirror what occurs in daily knowledge management practices (Wenger, 1998; Nidumolu et al., 2001; Orlikowski, 2002). Our study adds a dual layer framework to these studies that integrates the formal knowledge-sharing plans and the ongoing daily knowledge practices. The framework suggests that knowledge sharing cannot be understood by solely looking at the management’s actions, or by examining situated knowledge-sharing practices. Instead, we need to carefully examine the relationship between the two over time. Moreover, both are path dependent and mutually shaping. Thus, the effectiveness of an organization’s knowledge sharing is a product of a complex web of actions taken by different actors at different levels. This is in line with Garud & Kumaraswamy’s (2005) claim of the unpredictable and unintended knowledge-sharing practices that are caused by interactions among different organizational levels.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, being a single case study, it exhibits mainly analytical generalizability (Yin, 1994). To increase statistical generalizability, our findings need to be validated against a variety of merger situations. Second, the data were collected shortly after the merger, mostly through face-to-face interviews. Data collected during the post-merger period – especially interview data derived through self-reporting – may be biased as a result of recent negative experiences. Third, it should be noted that the merger between Alpha and Beta exemplifies only one type of merger. Some mergers are initiated to generate capital efficiencies through the consolidation of resources, while others are orchestrated to add new products or enter new markets. In other words, some mergers are product-related while others are resource-related. Ultimately, some mergers are unrelated and relate to financial balancing and portfolios. Despite these limitations, the study represents a step towards understanding knowledge-sharing dynamics during PMI.

## Implications for future research

By analysing field data, we were able to develop an integrative model of the post-merger knowledge strategy choice and appropriation. It offers a thick description of knowledge configurations and other contextual factors that affected post-merger knowledge sharing.

We propose post-merger knowledge-sharing activities to consist of two layers of processes. The first layer focuses on the managerial choice for a post-merger knowledge-sharing strategy.

Figure 2. An integrated process model of post-merger knowledge strategy and appropriation.

The Omni case suggests that management’s choice of the post-merger knowledge-sharing strategy is influenced by the type of the merger, differences in the organizational contexts, the existing knowledge systems of the merging companies, the nature of the knowledge and the time pressure. Management formulates their response to knowledge sharing based on their ‘reading’ of the post-merger strategic environment. The outcome of these actions produces a configuration of knowledge-as-content and knowledge-as-connections as their knowledgesharing strategy.

The second layer focuses on post-merger interactions between knowledge configurations as socio-technical networks and ongoing knowledge-sharing practices. The Omni case suggests that employees attempt to overcome the post-merger knowledge gaps by reflexively enacting the chosen post-merger knowledge-sharing strategy. This results in post-merger knowledgesharing practices that are different from those intended by management. Future research needs to further validate the dual layer process. Carrying out the studies in several contexts offers the opportunity to validate, refine and refute the factors that contributed to knowledge gaps.

Post-merger knowledge sharing can be in part attributed to path dependence. Future research, particularly on post-merger knowledge sharing, needs to explicitly consider the path dependent nature of post-merger behaviours during empirical analyses.

We also observed that the nature of the merger influences knowledge-sharing needs. In the case of Omni, the complementary nature of the merger required effective and flexible knowledge sharing. Other types of mergers might will require different knowledge management activities. All of these contexts should be carefully considered while trying to develop a generalized understanding of post-merger knowledge management. In particular, we need to understand how knowledge sharing relates to knowledge purging, retention and creation during the post-merger. In particular, simultaneous purging and creation are critical for the success of mergers. Future research also needs to examine the relationship between types of mergers and the role of specific knowledge management activities.

Finally, our study contributes evidence to the growing body of literature on emergent knowledge sharing (Nidumolu et al., 2001; Orlikowski, 2002). Future knowledge management research needs to explore knowledge sharing as an outcome of dynamic interactions between the managerial choices and employee enactments.

The past literature in PMI indicates that mergers suffer a high failure rate because of the poor integration of knowledge (Bleeke et al., 1993) and synergy-seeking mergers have not been found to add more shareholder value than unrelated mergers (Seth, 1990). Consistent with these arguments, recent studies have shown that changes sharing resources significantly improve the performance of synergy-seeking mergers (Brush, 1996; Larsson & Finkelstein, 1999; Rantf & Lord, 2002). Yet, there has been little research into managerial choices and how they affect knowledge management, and what makes these choices difficult and ineffective. By analysing in more detail these patterns our study highlights that an incorrect knowledge management strategy can significantly influence the success of PMI. In particular, the results demonstrate the importance of providing ample opportunities for direct and informal connections among key employees that will reduce the uncertainty surrounding the value, meaning and credibility of codified knowledge.

## Biographies

Youngjin Yoo is Associate Professor in Management Information Systems and Irwin L. Gross Research Fellow at the Fox School of Business and Management School of Management at Temple University. Prior to joining Temple University, he was Lewis-Progressive Chair of Management at the Weatherhead School of Management at Case Western Reserve University. He holds a PhD in information systems from the University of Maryland. His work covers a wide range of topics broadly related to IT-enabled innovation and organization transformation. In particular, he is interested in integrating design approaches in managing innovations and information technology, knowledge management and ubiquitous computing. His research was funded by NSF, NASA, Korea Research Foundation and Lotus Institute. His work was published at leading academic journals such as Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, Organization Science, the Communications of the ACM, the Academy of Management Journal, the Journal of Strategic Information Systems, the Journal of Management Education, and Information Systems Management. He also edited two books on ubiquitous computing and wrote several books chapters. He is serving on the editorial boards of several IS journals including Journal of Strategic Information Systems (senior editor) Information Systems Research (associate editor), Organization Science, Journal of AIS, and Information and Organization.

Kalle Lyytinen is Iris S. Wolstein professor Case Western Reserve University, USA and adjunct professor at University of Jyvaskyla, Finland. He serves currently on the editorial boards of several leading IS journals including Journal of AIS (Editor-in-Chief), Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Information & Organization, Requirements Engineering Journal, Information Systems Journal, Scandinavian Journal of Information Systems and Information Technology and People, among others. He is AIS fellow (2004), and the former chairperson of IFIP 8.2 and founding member of SIGSAND. He has published over 150 scientific articles and conference papers and edited or written 10 books on topics related to nature of IS discipline, system design, method engineering, organizational implementation, risk assessment, computer supported cooperative work, standardization and ubiquitous computing among others. He is currently involved in research projects that looks at the IT-induced radical innovation in software development, IT innovation in architecture, engineering and construction industry, design and use of ubiquitous applications, and the adoption of broadband wireless services in the UK, South Korea and the USA.

Dongcheol Heo is a Doctoral Candidate in the Rotterdam School of Management at the Erasmus University of the Netherlands. He have two master degrees: an MS in Information Systems from Case Western Reserve University and an MA in Sociology from Seoul National University. His areas of research are knowledge management, organizational impact of IT, IS strategy and qualitative management research methodology.
