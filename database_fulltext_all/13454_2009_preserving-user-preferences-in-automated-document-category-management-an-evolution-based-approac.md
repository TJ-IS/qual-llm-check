Abstrac t: Analysis of prevalent document management practices shows the popular use of categories (e.g., folders) to organize documents for subsequent searches and retrievals. The coherence and distinction of an existing document category can diminish considerably as influxes of new documents arrive over time. The complexity of and effort requirements for document-category management favor an automated approach that can be supported by appropriate document-clustering techniques. A review of the extant literature shows a predominant focus on document content analysis in automated document-category management, which cannot preserve the user’s document-grouping preferences. This research develops two advanced evolution-based techniques for preserving user preferences in their management of document categories. The first technique (CE2), which supports the automated evolution of a set of flat (i.e., nonhierarchical) document categories, extends a promising evolution-based technique (category evolution, CE) by addressing its fundamental limitations inherent to the use of holistic measures. The second technique, category hierarchy evolution (CHE), is developed on the basis of CE2 to support scenarios where document categories are organized with a hierarchical structure. Empirical evaluations of the effectiveness of each technique in various category evolution scenarios created using two different document corpora (i.e., news documents from Reuters and research articles from the ACM digital library), as compared with those of associated salient techniques for benchmark purposes, show that CE2 and CHE outperform their respective benchmark techniques. Their performance is reasonably robust and appears more effective when the quality (coherence) of the previously created categories does not deteriorate excessively. According to our results, the evolution-based approach is viable, appealing, and capable of preserving user preferences in automatic reorganizations of document categories.

Key w ords and phrases: category evolution, category hierarchy evolution, documentcategory management, document clustering, hierarchical agglomerative clustering, text mining.

The advanc ement and proliferation of innovative information technology have fostered the rapid creation and dissemination of information on a massive scale. Voluminous information has become available at an explosive and seemingly accelerating pace. Despite the growing popularity of multimedia, text remains the dominant form of information, particularly with respect to the contents or resources available on the Internet. These developments demand effective document management to support document search, access, and utilization by individuals and organizations.

Analysis of existing practices shows a predominant use of categories (i.e., folders) to organize documents. In a nutshell, such practices reveal a generalization strategy:

users group similar documents to create a manageable span of control in the presence of bounded rationality constraints [33]. People have limited cognitive processing capacities and intuitively organize voluminous documents by creating higher-order categories that follow a flat or hierarchical structure. Over time, new documents become available and are assigned to existing categories, thereby necessitating document category reorganization that may involve splitting and merging existing categories and creating new categories. When new documents arrive in great quantity and high frequency, conventional manual approaches to document-category management become prohibitively tedious and costly. If not properly managed, the previously created document categories will evolve in an ad hoc manner, diminishing the coherence or distinctness of each document category and adversely affecting users document searches and access.

The complexity of and effort requirements for document-category management favor an automated approach that can be supported by appropriate text-mining techniques. Of particular importance is document clustering, which partitions a collection of documents into distinct groups, such that the documents in each group share great similarity and collectively reveal a specific theme in the document corpus [3, 11, 22, 29, 38, 41]. Previous research examining the use of document clustering for automated documentcategory management predominantly takes a discovery approach (specifically, total rediscovery approach) that clusters all documents to recreate (document) categories without considering those previously created by the user. This approach, however, cannot preserve the user preferences in document grouping that are manifested in the previously established document categories.

The importance of preserving user preferences emerges in the context theory of classification [2, 6, 19, 21, 30], which suggests that an individual’s document grouping behavior not only requires a thorough analysis of each document’s contents but also involves the context of his or her classification perspective (e.g., user role, task). Document grouping is an intentional act that reflects an individual’s view of semantic coherence or document categorization [32]. In this light, existing categories, though deteriorated over time as influxes of new documents arrive, still reflect the user’s document-grouping preferences to some extent. Preserving these preferences is essential because they influence a user’s search efficiency, cognitive processing, and satisfaction. People are habitual in their document search behaviors and can obtain considerable efficiency gains through repetition (e.g., the power law of practice [15]). This habitual characteristic in turn demands continuity in document-category evolutions over time; its absence can greatly hinder a user’s document search efficiency and lead to dissatisfaction. All else being equal, people can locate relevant or target documents more easily and faster when they search a familiar set of document categories rather than categories completely new to them. In addition, a user usually coadapts with document categories as they evolve over time, thereby adapting his or her perspective to existing categories while learning the semantics and distinctions of these categories simultaneously [24, 25]. As a result, people are likely to incur more stringent cognitive processing loads in this coadaptation process when they search documents from a set of unfamiliar categories than otherwise. The benefits of preserving user preferences also appear in expectation-disconfirmation theory [28], which suggests that people form expectations about their use of document categories on the basis of their knowledge, experiences, or perspectives. If their expectations are negatively disconfirmed because of drastic changes of document categories that fail to preserve their perspective or preferences, they will become dissatisfied.

Wei et al. [40] propose an evolution-based technique, category evolution (CE), that automatically reclusters documents while taking into account the document categories previously created by the user. CE can preserve a user’s document-grouping preferences and consists of category decomposition and category amalgamation phases. During category decomposition, an existing category may be split into multiple subcategories, each containing documents pertaining to a finer-grained topic of a more cohesive (defined) scope. In category amalgamation, CE merges similar categories or subcategories to form more general categories, each of which contains documents pertaining to a broader scope. Although the evaluation results are encouraging [40], they point to several fundamental limitations of CE that are inherent to its dependence on holistic measures (defined at the document-category level) to make category decomposition and amalgamation decisions. Furthermore, CE only supports flat, nonhierarchical document categories and therefore cannot meet the challenge of preserving user preferences in scenarios involving document categories hierarchically organized by users.

The current research proposes and empirically examines two techniques that extend from CE by addressing its fundamental limitations. The first proposed technique, CE2, replaces CE’s holistic measures for decomposition and amalgamation assessments with appropriate measures defined at a document level. Similar to its predecessor, CE2 is designed for flat document categories; we extend it further to develop the second proposed technique, category hierarchy evolution (CHE), which is capable of supporting the evolution of document categories organized in accordance with a hierarchical structure. Our motive for proposing two separate techniques is to better support a wide range of real-world scenarios that involve nonhierarchical or hierarchical document categories. We empirically evaluate CE2 using two real-world document corpora and two benchmark techniques, namely, CE and hierarchical agglomerative clustering (HA C)—an associated salient document-clustering technique that follows the discovery-based approach. We also use two hierarchically categorized document corpora to evaluate CHE and include an extended HA C technique (i.e., HA C+P) for benchmark purposes. Our evaluative focus centers on whether the proposed techniques (i.e., CE2 and CHE) are more effective than their respective benchmark techniques.

The proposed techniques can support document management at both individual and organizational levels. For instance, both CE2 and CHE can be applied to manage document categories or category hierarchies for e-learning (e.g., organizing learning or training material) and environmental scanning (i.e., organizing news wires or press releases about events and emerging trends in the business environment [37]). Furthermore, they are essential to knowledge management support. In particular, the creation of organization knowledge repositories prevails in current knowledge management practices [9, 13, 39], and many knowledge repository–building endeavors focus on managing explicit knowledge, which often includes formal knowledge documents (e.g., lessons learned, best practices). For example, Ernst & Young has created a knowledge repository consisting of more than 5,000 leading practices that pertain to executive processes, finance, new business development, order management, and supply-chain management [27]. Over time, the categories in a knowledge repository may become less appropriate because of the changes in the business or repository expansions. Thus, automated category evolution facilitated by the proposed CE2 and CHE techniques becomes pertinent and appealing for effective support of knowledge searches, retrievals, and browsing from such organization knowledge repositories.

The proposed techniques can also support the evolution of categories or category hierarchies in Web 2.0 applications, in which users produce or coproduce contents while also using these contents. For example, Yahoo! Answers (http://answers.yahoo. com), Sina iAsk (http://iask.sina.com.cn), and Baidu Zhidao (http://zhidao.baidu.com) are community-driven knowledge market Web sites that enable individual users to ask questions of other users or provide answers to questions posed by those other users.<sup>1</sup> Typically, a knowledge market site organizes all user-posted questions and answers according to a category hierarchy. Because of the openness of such knowledge market sites and the unpredictability of the posed questions, creating a comprehensive category hierarchy in advance is extremely difficult, if feasible at all. Assuming a knowledge market site adopts a category hierarchy, when a user posts a question that does not fit any existing category, he or she is then forced to choose the “closest” category instead. Consequently, the appropriateness of the existing categories can diminish over time, which necessitates the evolution (i.e., reorganization) of the category hierarchy of the knowledge market site.

## Previous Document-Clustering Research

Document-clustering groups similar documents into distinct clusters on the basis of a document content analysis. The documents in each resulting cluster exhibit maximal similarity to those in the same cluster and share little similarity with the documents in other clusters. Existing document-clustering techniques predominantly depend on document content analysis and usually consist of three phases—feature extraction and selection, document representation, and clustering [14, 40, 41]. Feature extraction begins by parsing each source document to produce a set of features (typically nouns and noun phrases), excluding a list of prespecified “stop words” that are nonsemantic-bearing words. Representative features are then selected from the set of features previously extracted. Feature selection is crucial to document-clustering efficiency and effectiveness because it condenses the size of the extracted feature set and mitigates plausible biases that may exist in the untrimmed feature set [10, 31]. Common feature selection metrics include term frequency (TF), term frequency and inverse document frequency (TF×IDF), and their hybrids [3, 22].

According to the top-k selection method, the k features that have the highest selection metric scores are chosen to represent each document in the document representation phase. That is, each document is represented by a feature vector jointly defined by the k features selected. A review of relevant previous research suggests several salient representation schemes that include binary (i.e., presence versus absence of a feature in a document), within-document TF, and TF×IDF [22, 31, 41].

In the final clustering phase, documents are grouped into distinct clusters on the basis of the selected features and their respective values in each document. Existing clustering techniques can be classified as partitioning based [3, 8, 22, 34] (including the k-means algorithm [1] and the partitioning around medoids (PAM) algorithm [16, 26]), hierarchical [11, 31, 35], or Kohonen neural network [12, 17, 18, 20, 23, 31]. The hierarchical approach is notably more effective for generating a clustering hierarchy than are other approaches and can be further classified as agglomerative and divisive, depending on how the hierarchical decomposition proceeds (i.e., top-down or bottom-up). The HA C algorithm, which exemplifies a bottom-up strategy, starts with as many clusters as there are documents—that is, each cluster contains only one document [35]. Judged by an intercluster similarity measure of choice (e.g., single link, complete link, group-average link, Ward’s method), the two clusters that exhibit the greatest similarity merge to form a new cluster. This process continues until the predefined termination condition is satisfied (e.g., when the intercluster similarity is less than a prespecified threshold). The hierarchical divisive clustering algorithm [16], in contrast, follows a top-down strategy that first places all documents in one cluster, which is then subdivided into two clusters most distinct of each other. This division process continues until the predefined termination condition is reached.

However, the hierarchical clustering approach (e.g., HA C) organizes a document corpus into a binary-tree structure and therefore creates a deep but narrow hierarchy that is difficult to comprehend or traverse by users. To address this limitation, Chuang and Chien [7] propose the HA C+P technique, which extends HA C with an additional min-max partitioning for hierarchy compression and thereby generates a flatter, multiway clustering hierarchy. Procedurally, HA C+P first uses HA C to organize all source documents into a binary clustering hierarchy and then applies min-max partitioning that follows a top-down strategy to search for an optimal cut level to compress the resulting binary hierarchy iteratively.

At each candidate cut level, HA C+P assesses the quality of the clusters at that level. Let CS be a set of clusters at a given cut level. For each cluster $C _ { i }$ in CS, HA C+P measures the intercluster similarity between $C _ { i }$ and all other clusters in CS and divides it by the intracluster similarity of $C _ { i ^ { \ast } }$ Accordingly, HA C+P averages these estimates across all the clusters in CS, denoted as Q(CS), which then assesses the quality of the cluster set at the cut level under evaluation. Evidently, the smaller the Q(CS) value, the better the quality of CS.

Given the number of clusters in CS (i.e., |CS|), HA C+P measures the appropriateness of |CS | with respect to a preferred number of clusters $( N _ { c l u s } )$ specified by the user. Specifically, HA C+P uses a simplified gamma distribution function to model the degree of preference, favoring a |CS | value closer to $N _ { c l u s }$ . HA C+P then estimates the cluster size preference metric for CS—namely, N(CS). Finally, the optimal cut level selected from all the candidate levels is the one that exhibits the minimum Q(CS)/N(CS). After determining the optimal cut level, HA C+P starts partitioning the binary clustering hierarchy (created by HA C) into multiple subhierarchies, such that the number of subhierarchies equals the number of clusters at the selected cut level. This process proceeds for each subhierarchy. To avoid partitioning a cluster with substantially fewer documents or generating an extraordinarily deep hierarchy, HA C+P uses a constant ε to restrict the size of a cluster and another constant ρ to confine the depth of the resulting hierarchy. That is, HA C+P stops partitioning a cluster when the number of documents in the cluster is less than ε or its depth is greater than ρ.

A review of extant document-clustering literature suggests the predominant use of the discovery or total rediscovery approach, which re-creates document categories without considering those previously established by the user. This approach apparently cannot preserve user preferences in document grouping. Wei et al. [38] propose a personalized document-clustering approach using partial clustering that denotes a user’s categorization of a subset of documents in the document collection to be clustered. This approach extends the feature selection phase common to most conventional document-clustering techniques by identifying and considering a set of features that best differentiates each cluster in the input partial clustering while excluding those features that do not discriminate with respect to the partial clustering. Although this approach can support personalized document clustering by taking advantage of the user’s partial clustering, its effectiveness is constrained significantly when only a small partial clustering is available. To address this limitation, a collaborative filtering-based document-clustering (CFC) technique is developed [41], which considers the partial clustering by both the target user and relevant others to approximate the categorization preference of the target user.

Both the partial-clustering-based personalized document-clustering and CFC techniques take into account user preferences, in the form of partial clustering, in document grouping; however, they are primarily developed for personalized document clustering and thus cannot effectively address the evolving nature of document categories over time. In response, Wei et al. [40] propose the CE technique to address the document category evolution problem by taking into account the user’s perspective and preferences in document grouping, as manifested in the document categories he or she has established previously.

## Overview of CE and Analysis of Its Limitations

In essence, CE takes as inputs all source documents and existing document categories (e.g., those previously created by a user) to reorganize document categories, so each resulting category contains documents of increasing similarity or coherence.

CE supports automated document-category management and preserves the user’s document-grouping preferences through appropriate reorganizations of the existing categories that may lead to the creation of new categories.

Broadly, CE consists of category decomposition and category amalgamation phases. In the category decomposition phase, CE splits an existing document category into multiple new subcategories, each of which contains documents that are increasingly cohesive or germane to a finer-grained topic. For each existing category, CE first selects a set of representative features (using the TF×IDF selection metric) and uses them to represent each document in the category. Next, CE tentatively splits an existing category into two subsets, each containing documents that exhibit maximal similarity to those in the same subset and minimal similarity with documents in the other subset. For each category, CE evaluates the disjointedness between the resultant subsets using the following intracategory disjointedness measure:

$$
\text { disjointedness } \left(C, \sigma_ {d}\right) = 1 - \frac {2 \times \left| F _ {S _ {1}} \cap F _ {S _ {2}} \right|}{\left| F _ {S _ {1}} \right| + \left| F _ {S _ {2}} \right|},
$$

where $C$ is the target category whose documents are split into $S _ { \nu }$ and $S _ { 2 }$ subsets, $\sigma _ { d }$ (feature inclusion threshold) eliminates features with low frequency (i.e., less than $\sigma _ { d }$ of documents) in $C ,$ and $F _ { s _ { i } }$ is the set of features that appear in at least $\sigma _ { d }$ of the documents in $C$ and are present in some documents in $S _ { i ^ { \star } }$ Accordingly, CE decomposes an existing category when its intracategory disjointedness exceeds a prespecified threshold. CE adopts the PAM algorithm [16, 26] to split an existing category into multiple subcategories and uses the silhouette coefficient measure [16] to determine an optimal number of subcategories to be created.

In the category amalgamation phase, similar document categories or subcategories that result from the category decomposition phase merge to form more general categories, each of which contains documents that pertain to a topic of broader scope. Specifically, CE reselects features for each category or subcategory and uses the resulting features to represent individual documents in those respective categories or subcategories. When completing the feature reselection and document re-representation tasks, CE considers each category or subcategory an initial cluster and estimates the similarity of two clusters using the following intercategory overlapping measure:

$$
\text { overlapping } \left(C _ {i}, C _ {j}, \sigma_ {o}\right) = \frac {2 \times \left| F _ {C _ {i}} \cap F _ {C _ {j}} \right|}{\left| F _ {C _ {i}} \right| + \left| F _ {C _ {j}} \right|},
$$

where $C _ { _ i }$ and $C _ { _ i }$ are the categories or subcategories under evaluation, $\sigma _ { o }$ denotes the feature inclusion threshold for removing features of low frequency in each category, and $F _ { _ { C _ { i } } }$ is the set of features that appear in at least $\sigma _ { o }$ of the documents in $C _ { i ^ { \prime } }$

The two most similar clusters then merge to form a new cluster. This merging process continues until no intercluster similarity is greater than the prespecified merging threshold. At the completion of category amalgamation, CE generates a set of new categories that in effect have evolved from those previously created by the user, then reassigns the documents to the appropriate (resulting) categories accordingly.

Although capable of preserving user preferences in document grouping, CE has several fundamental limitations. First, inherent constraints result from its use of holistic measures. Specifically, CE uses an intracategory disjointedness measure to assess whether to decompose an existing category in the decomposition phase and then depends on an intercategory overlapping measure to evaluate whether to merge categories in the amalgamation phase. That is, CE depends on aggregate feature sets (obtained from the two subsets of a document category) to assess the disjointedness in the category (in the decomposition phase) and also uses aggregate feature sets to evaluate the similarity between two categories or subcategories (in the amalgamation phase). These holistic measures, defined at a document category or subset level, can mask significant differences at the document level. For example, assume that CE splits an existing category into two subsets $S _ { 1 }$ and $S _ { 2 }$ As we illustrate in Table 1, each document in $S _ { 1 }$ is dissimilar to those in $S _ { 2 } ,$ , which have distinctly different features. For example, document $d _ { \scriptscriptstyle 1 }$ (consisting of five features) in $S _ { \nu }$ and $d _ { \tau }$ (three features) in $S _ { \gamma }$ share only one common feature $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , f _ { 5 } )$ , whereas $d _ { \scriptscriptstyle 2 }$ (five features) does not share any common features with $d _ { \tau }$ in $S _ { 2 } .$ . Noticeably, the category under analysis contains two distinct subsets of documents and should be decomposed. However, these two subsets have highly similar collective feature sets. As we show in Table 1, the collective feature set of $S _ { 1 } \left\{ f _ { 1 } , f _ { 2 } , . . . , f _ { 9 } \right\}$ is comparable to that of $S _ { _ 2 } \left\{ f _ { _ 3 } , f _ { 4 } , . . . , f _ { _ { 1 0 } } \right\}$ . As a result, CE, constrained by its use of the intracategory disjointedness measure, will not decompose this category.

Second, CE is designed for the automated evolution of a flat $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , nonhierarchical) set of document categories and therefore cannot support hierarchical document categories. Conceivably, users may prefer organizing documents on the basis of some hierarchical structure. This constraint can greatly mitigate the value and applicability of CE for supporting automated document-category management in real-world scenarios. We therefore extend CE by addressing these inherent limitations and propose two techniques: CE2, designed to support the automated evolution of a flat set of document categories, replaces CE’s holistic measures for category decomposition and amalgamation assessments with adequate measures defined at the document level; and CHE further extends CE2 to support the evolution of document categories organized in a hierarchical structure.

## Design and Implementation of CE2

To address the inherent problems of CE, we develop CE2 by replacing the intracategory disjointedness measure of CE with a document-level category coherence measure on the basis of the average similarity of all document pairs in a category. Specifically, CE2 distinguishes the most dissimilar documents from other documents within each category and then decomposes that category accordingly. The proposed CE2 technique continues this process for all document categories and subcategories until the coherence of each resulting category or subcategory exceeds a specified threshold. In addition, CE2 uses a document-based intercategory similarity measure to replace the intercategory overlapping measure in CE.

As shown in Figure 1, the overall process of CE2 consists of category decomposition and category amalgamation phases. During category decomposition, CE2 splits each existing category, if appropriate, into multiple subcategories, each containing documents that pertain to a finer-grained topic of a more defined scope. In category amalgamation, CE2 merges similar categories or subcategories into a more general category that contains documents associated with a broader topic.

## Category Decomposition

As we show in Figure 1, the main tasks that CE2 performs in the category decomposition phase are feature extraction and selection, document representation, and category split. Unlike CE, CE2 does not assess intracategory disjointedness; rather, it incorporates such evaluations into the category splits directly. When performing the feature extraction and selection task, CE2 extracts from the categorized documents a set of representative features (i.e., nouns and noun phrases) for each existing category. We employ a rule-based part-of-speech tagger to tag each word in a document syntactically [4, 5]. We then follow the approach proposed by Voutilainen [36] to implement a noun-phrase parser and extract noun phrases from each syntactically tagged document. Subsequently, a set of k features is selected for each category, using the TF×IDF feature selection metric. We adopt a binary scheme to represent documents, which is capable of producing clustering quality comparable with, if not better than, that attainable by other representation schemes [31]. It is worth noting that CE2 can easily be extended to adopt other representation schemes that may include TF and TF×IDF. At the completion of document representation, the documents in each existing category are represented by the feature set specific to that category.

For the category split, CE2 depends on the hierarchical divisive clustering (HDC) algorithm [16] to decompose an existing category into a set of subcategories. The choice of this hierarchical algorithm over other algorithms (e.g., partitioning-based or Kohonen neural network) is made mainly because it does not require explicit specifications of the number of clusters to be generated. This enables CE2 to increase or decrease the number of subcategories generated by moving up or down the resultant clustering hierarchy. For each existing category, the HDC algorithm begins by placing all documents in one cluster and then subdivides the category into two smaller clusters until the average similarity of all document pairs (i.e $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , category coherence) in each cluster exceeds a predefined similarity threshold $( \alpha _ { _ s } )$ . Conceivably, a lower $\alpha _ { _ s }$ will result in fewer subcategories decomposed from an existing category. In this study, we estimate the similarity between two documents $d _ { i }$ and $d _ { j }$ using the cosine similarity measure, defined as follows:

$$
\operatorname{sim} \left(d _ {i}, d _ {j}\right) = \frac {\vec {d} _ {i} \cdot \vec {d} _ {j}}{\left| \vec {d} _ {i} \right| \times \left| \vec {d} _ {j} \right|},
$$

where $\vec { d } _ { i }$ is the feature vector of document $d _ { \phantom { i } _ { i } }$ and $| \vec { d } _ { i } |$ is the length of $\vec { d } _ { \scriptscriptstyle i }$

## Category Amalgamation

The categories or subcategories created in the category decomposition phase become inputs to the subsequent category amalgamation phase, in which CE2 merges similar categories or subcategories to form more general categories. The major tasks CE2 performs in category amalgamation are feature reselection, document re-representation, and category coalescence. First, CE2 reperforms feature selection across all the categories or subcategories generated in category decomposition, thereby creating a global dictionary that consists of a universal feature set for all document categories or subcategories. We revise TF×IDF for feature selection by replacing the traditional IDF value of a feature $f ,$ IDF( f ), with log $( n c / n _ { \mathrm { \Gamma } } ) + 1$ , where nc is the total number of categories or subcategories that serve as inputs to category amalgamation, and $n _ { f }$ is the number of categories or subcategories whose constituent documents contain the feature $f .$ The revised TF×IDF measure can evaluate the power of a feature $f$ for characterizing a document category by distinguishing that category from other categories. The $k _ { { _ m } }$ features with the highest TF×IDF scores are included in the global dictionary and used to represent each document accordingly. For document re-representation, CE2 adopts a binary scheme, identical to that used in category decomposition.

CE2 then performs category coalescence to merge similar categories or subcategories on the basis of their intercategory similarity. CE2 prohibits the direct merging of two subcategories (e.g., $C _ { i }$ and $C _ { j }$ that have been decomposed from the same category in the category decomposition phase, because this merging would revert the subcategories to an initial state by nullifying the previous decomposition effects, which is logically undesirable. However, two subcategories $( C _ { i }$ and $C _ { j }$ split from the same category in the decomposition phase can be merged only if there exists another category $C _ { h } ,$ such that (1) $C _ { h }$ does not originate from the same category as $C _ { i }$ and $C _ { j }$ and (2) $C _ { h }$ has been merged with $C _ { i }$ or $C _ { j }$ previously. This restriction makes the use of the HA C algorithm [35] and its extension more viable and appealing than other clustering algorithms. Specifically, our extended HA C algorithm starts with as many clusters as there are categories or subcategories generated in the decomposition phase, each forming an initial cluster. The two clusters exhibiting the highest similarity that exceeds a prespecified merging threshold $( \alpha _ { _ m } )$ are merged if they do not violate the merging restriction described earlier. Furthermore, CE2 uses the group-average link method (i.e., average similarity between all intercluster document pairs) to measure the similarity between two clusters. This merging process continues until the similarity of the permissible merges is lower than the prespecified similarity threshold $\alpha _ { { } _ { m } } .$ . After the completion of category coalescence, CE2 generates a set of categories that, in effect, have evolved from those previously established by the user.

## Evaluation Scenarios and Procedure

We consider the original categories in each document corpus “true” categories. We randomly select some documents from each true category and purposefully reassign them to other categories, thus creating inappropriate document categories that mimic real-world scenarios requiring category reorganization. Following a particular Gaussian probability distribution, we first segment each true category into a dominant subset and multiple minor subsets. Table 3 summarizes the particular category evolution scenarios we study. The number of minor subsets we examine ranges from two to five, following the Gaussian-3 through Gaussian-6 distributions. To illustrate, take the Gaussian-3 distribution as an example. A true category is decomposed into a dominant subset and two minor subsets, which respectively contain 86.6 percent, 13.1 percent, and 0.3 percent of the documents in the original true category. The Gaussian-6 distribution seems to be a reasonable upper bound for simulating real-world category reorganization scenarios, because new documents are included in existing categories over time and therefore their content coherence is not likely to deteriorate in a rapid or drastic fashion.

In each evaluation scenario, the dominant subset remains in its true category, whereas each minor subset gets randomly assigned to and combined with the dominant subset of another true category. Take the Reuters corpus as an example. We create, in each scenario, a synthetic data set with a total of nine inappropriate document categories that need to be reorganized by CE2 and the two benchmark techniques (i.e., CE and HA C). To minimize plausible biases that may result from the randomization process we used to generate the synthetic data set, we randomly select 80 percent of the documents from the 9 true categories to create a synthetic data set for a particular evaluation scenario and repeat this process 30 times. We report the effectiveness of each investigated technique, obtained from its average performance across these 30 random trials.

## Evaluation Criteria

We evaluate the effectiveness of CE2 and each benchmark technique in terms of cluster recall and cluster precision. Both measures are fundamental evaluation criteria for document clustering [31, 38, 41] and anchor at the association of a document pair from the same cluster. Specifically, cluster recall (CR) is calculated as $C R = | C A | / | T A | .$ where TA represents the set of associations in the respective true categories, and CA denotes the set of correct associations contained in both the clusters generated by an investigated technique and the true categories. Cluster precision (CP) is defined as $C P = { | C A | } / { | G A | }$ , where GA denotes the set of associations in the clusters created by an investigated technique.

## Parameter-Tuning Experiments and Results

We perform a series of experiments to tune the important parameters of each investigated technique. We adopt the Gaussian-4 distribution and follow the procedure (used to create our evaluation scenarios) to generate 30 synthetic data sets for our parameter-tuning experiments. Because the benchmark HA C technique follows the discovery-based approach, it does not consider the document categories already existing in each synthetic data set and only uses the documents of each synthetic data set when organizing these documents. In contrast, CE and CE2 take as inputs all documents and the existing categories in each synthetic data set in the parameter-tuning experiments.

The number of features (k) is a key parameter of HA C. For each document corpus, we examine the effect of k on cluster recall and cluster precision over the range from 100 to 500 (in increments of 100), as well as from 500 to 3,000 (in increments of 500). For the Reuters corpus, we note that the overall performance of HA C improves noticeably when k increases from 100 to 400, beyond which the performance improvement levels off and then diminishes gradually. We observe a similar pattern in the ACM corpus: noticeable improvements over the range of 100–2,000 features but decreasing thereafter. Our parameter-tuning results thus suggest setting k to 400 for the Reuters corpus and 2,000 for the ACM corpus.

The use of CE requires proper tuning of several parameters that include the number of features considered in category decomposition $( k _ { s } )$ , the intracategory disjointedness threshold value $( \alpha _ { _ s } )$ , and the number of features for category amalgamation $( k _ { _ m } )$ CE involves two additional parameters: $\sigma _ { d }$ (i.e., feature inclusion threshold in the intracategory disjointedness measure) and $\sigma _ { o }$ (i.e., feature inclusion threshold in the intercategory overlapping measure). We investigate the effects of $\sigma _ { d }$ (ranging from 5 percent to 20 percent in increments of $^ { 5 }$ percent) and $\sigma _ { o }$ (ranging from $^ { 5 }$ percent to 20 percent in increments of $^ { 5 }$ percent) on the effectiveness of CE for each corpus. Our tuning results suggest that an increase of $\sigma _ { d }$ decreases CE’s effectiveness, but the impact of $\sigma _ { o }$ on the effectiveness of CE is marginal at best for both corpora. Thus, we set both $\sigma _ { d }$ and $\sigma _ { o }$ to 5 percent in our evaluations. Subsequently, we tune $k _ { s }$ (ranging from 50 to 200 in increments of 50) and $\mathbf { \alpha } _ { s } \left( 0 . 1 5 \ – 0 . 5 \right.$ in increments of 0.05) while keeping $k _ { { _ m } }$ constant at 100. We experimentally determine optimal values for $k _ { s }$ and $\mathbf { \alpha } \propto _ { _ s }$ and then use the selected values to evaluate $k _ { { _ m } }$ over the range $5 0 \mathrm { - } 2 0 0 ,$ in increments of 50. For the Reuters corpus, the overall effectiveness of CE, as measured by cluster recall and cluster precision, is generally comparable across all investigated $k _ { _ s } / \alpha _ { _ s }$ combinations. We observe better performance when setting $k _ { s }$ to $5 0$ and $\alpha _ { _ s }$ to $0 . 3 ;$ accordingly, we select these values for CE’s reorganization of the Reuters corpus. In addition, the impact of $k _ { { _ m } }$ on the effectiveness of CE’s reorganization of the Reuters documents is marginal over the range of $k _ { { _ m } }$ investigated. Thus, we set $k _ { { _ m } }$ to 100 in the subsequent evaluation. For the ACM corpus, the effectiveness of CE appears comparable among the different $k _ { s } / \alpha _ { s }$ combinations. Our analysis further suggests that CE is more effective when $k _ { s }$ equals 50 and $\alpha _ { _ s }$ is 0.5. We therefore adopt these values for $k _ { s }$ and $\alpha _ { _ s }$ and use them to determine the value of $k _ { { _ m } }$ experimentally. Similar to that observed with the Reuters corpus, the impact of $k _ { _ m }$ on the effectiveness of CE’s reorganization of the ACM documents is mostly marginal. Over the range of $k _ { _ m }$ we study, a value of 150 for $k _ { _ m }$ appears optimal and capable of generating the best effectiveness. We therefore set $k _ { { _ m } }$ to 150 in our evaluations.

The use of CE2 also involves the proper tuning of $k _ { s } , \alpha _ { s }$ , and $k _ { _ m } .$ We follow experimental procedures similar to those for CE to determine appropriate values for these parameters. For each corpus, we first determine adequate values for $k _ { s }$ (50–200 in increments of 50) and $\alpha _ { _ s }$ (0.15–0.5 in increments of 0.05) while keeping $k _ { { _ m } }$ constant at 200. We choose a larger $k _ { { _ m } }$ value for CE2 than that for CE because, whereas CE uses multiple local dictionaries (one for each document category), CE2 uses a global dictionary for all document categories during category amalgamation. We therefore can reasonably anticipate that CE2 will require a larger $k _ { _ m }$ default value than does CE. We experimentally determine the values of $k _ { s }$ and $\alpha _ { _ s }$ and then examine the effect of the number of features $( k _ { _ m } )$ on CE2’s effectiveness in the category amalgamation phase. We assess $k _ { { _ m } }$ over the range 50–200, in increments of 50. According to our analysis, $k _ { s }$ seems to covary adversely with $\alpha _ { _ s } { , }$ that is, given a lower value of $\alpha _ { _ s }$ (e.g., 0.2 or 0.25), the use of a larger $k _ { s }$ (e.g., 200 or 150) yields greater effectiveness. In contrast, a higher value of $\alpha _ { _ s }$ (e.g., 0.35 or 0.4) requires fewer features $( k _ { s } )$ in the category decomposition phase (e.g., 50). When clustering the Reuters documents, CE2 is more effective when $k _ { s } = 1 5 0$ and $\mathbf { \alpha } \propto _ { s } = 0 . 2 5$ . Accordingly, we select these parameter values and use them to search for the most appropriate value for $k _ { _ m } .$ . Over the range of $k _ { _ m }$ values we study, the overall performance of CE2 improves slightly as $k _ { _ m }$ increases from 50 to 200; hence, we set $k _ { { _ m } }$ to 200 when using CE2 to reorganize the Reuters corpus. We perform similar experiments for the ACM corpus. The respective effects of $k _ { s } , \alpha _ { s } ,$ and $k _ { { _ m } }$ are similar to those observed with the Reuters corpus, so we set $k _ { s }$ to 50, $\alpha _ { _ s }$ to 0.15, and $k _ { { _ m } }$ to 200 when using CE2 to reorganize the ACM documents. ## Comparative Evaluation Results

We compare the effectiveness of CE2, CE, and HA C in different reorganization scenarios (see Table 3). As Figures 2a and 2b show, CE2 and CE are obviously more effective for reorganizing the Reuters corpus than HA C in both the Gaussian-3 and Gaussian-4 scenarios. Our results reveal that CE2 noticeably outperforms CE in these scenarios as well. As we show in Figure 2c, the effectiveness of CE2 and CE diminishes when the coherence of the input categories deteriorates $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Gaussian-5 distribution). Nevertheless, both CE2 and CE remain more effective than HA C. The effectiveness of CE declines in comparison with HA C when each input category contains more topics and its coherence deteriorates (e.g., Guassian-6 scenario in Figure 2d); meanwhile, CE2 remains more effective than HA C. Overall, the empirical results derived from the Reuters corpus show that CE2 is more effective than CE and HA C and that the effectiveness of CE2 appears more robust than that of CE over the range of evaluation scenarios examined.

The evaluation results obtained from the ACM corpus are similar to those observed with the Reuters corpus. In the evaluation scenarios that follow Gaussian-3 and Gaussian-4 distributions, both CE2 and CE apparently outperform $\mathrm { H A C } ;$ furthermore, CE2 is noticeably more effective than CE (Figures 3a and 3b). However, as the coherence of the input categories degenerates further (simulated by Gaussian-5 and Gaussian-6 distributions), the effectiveness of CE2 and CE decreases but still remains more effective than HA C (Figures 3c and 3d). Similar to the results obtained from the Reuters corpus, our evaluation results involving the ACM corpus show that, across all evaluation scenarios, CE2 outperforms CE and HA C, and the effectiveness of CE2 seems more robust than that of CE when the quality of the input categories deteriorates considerably.

We also examine the computation time requirements of CE2. The document-level similarity measures of CE2 are more computationally complex than the holistic measures, defined at the document-category level, used in CE. However, according to the results of our experiments conducted using a typical hardware configuration (i.e., a CPU clock rate of 3GH z, 4GB of main memory, running Linux operating system), CE2 needs 184 seconds to reorganize 2,116 Reuters documents (originally categorized in nine incoherent categories) in the Gaussian-3 scenario and 187 seconds in the Gaussian-6 scenario. CE2 needs similar amounts of time in the scenarios that involve the ACM corpus (containing 1,742 documents organized in 14 incoherent categories), ranging between 135 and 140 seconds. Overall, the computation time requirement of CE2 is not particularly demanding and can be reasonably justified by the magnitude of improvement in document-category reorganization effectiveness. Together, our results show that CE2 is appealing and viable for category evolution; it outperforms CE across different scenarios and document corpuses without demanding excessive computation time or requirements.

## Design and Implementation of CHE

To sup ort the evolution of hierarc hic al doc ument c ategories, we develop CHE on the basis of CE2. The inputs to CHE are the category hierarchy under study and its categorized documents. For the purpose of our intended viability illustration and assessment, we assume documents residing only in the leaf nodes (categories) of a hierarchy—that is, no intermediate nodes contain any documents. The overall process of CHE closely resembles that of CE2 but includes a category hierarchy refinement phase that follows category decomposition and category amalgamation.

During category decomposition, CHE decomposes each leaf category (in the input hierarchy) containing documents that lack coherence into multiple subcategories in which coherence exceeds a prespecified threshold. The tasks (i.e., feature extraction and selection, document representation, and category split) performed in this phase and their designs are identical to those in the category decomposition phase of CE2. Accordingly, for each leaf category in the input hierarchy, CHE performs category split to decompose the target category into multiple subcategories if its coherence is lower than the predefined similarity threshold (i.e., α ). The resulting subcategories then become the child nodes of the original category.

In category amalgamation, CHE merges subcategories for which the intercategory similarity exceeds a prespecified threshold. As in CE2, category amalgamation consists of feature reselection, document re-representation, and category coalescence. Among these tasks, the first two tasks are identical to those in CE2. For category coalescence, CHE considers combining two leaf categories if their intercategory similarity exceeds the specified merging threshold $\alpha _ { { } _ { m } }$ . The procedure for merging two leaf categories $( \mathrm { e . g . , } C _ { \mathrm { i } }$ and $C _ { j }$ depends on their structural relationship in the hierarchy, which can be classified as one of the following: $( 1 ) C _ { i }$ and $C _ { j }$ are siblings that result from the same category in the category decomposition phase, $( 2 ) C _ { i }$ and $C _ { j }$ are siblings but are not generated from the same category in the category decomposition phase (i.e., $C _ { i }$ and $C _ { j }$ are leaf categories in the original hierarchy), or (3) $C _ { i }$ and $C _ { j }$ are not siblings. In the first case, the coalescence of $C _ { i }$ and $C _ { j }$ occurs only when one has merged with another leaf category or other categories. In the second case, the merge of $C _ { i }$ and $C _ { j }$ can proceed without any constraints. ## Empirical Evaluations of CHE

In this sec tion, w e report important evaluation results pertaining to CHE, using the results obtained from HA C+P [7], a salient discovery-based technique, as the performance benchmark. The Reuters corpus (used in our CE2 evaluation) has a flat structure. To evaluate CHE and the benchmark HA C+P, we create a hierarchy by specifying appropriate structural relationships between or among the categories it contains. Two experienced business faculty members (at different research universities) thoroughly reviewed the nine categories in our Reuters corpus; each of them created a category hierarchy individually. The consistency of the hierarchies generated by the two faculty members exceeds 95 percent. Through face-to-face discussions, they ultimately established a category hierarchy on the basis of their consensus, which serves as the “true” hierarchy in the subsequent evaluations. As shown in Figure 4, the root node contains three intermediate categories at level 1 (i.e., international business, general commerce, and monetary market). Most leaf categories reside at level 2, with the exception of coffee, sugar, and crude (level 3), which jointly form the commodity intermediate category at level 2 in the hierarchy.

Our CHE evaluation also includes the ACM corpus, which contains 1,742 singlecategory documents spanning 14 genuine categories. We follow the ACM Computing Classification System and adopt its hierarchical structure for these 14 categories as the “true” hierarchy in our evaluation. As Figure 5 illustrates, the hierarchy contains two intermediate categories at level 1 and five categories at level 2. All leaf categories reside at level 3.

## Parameter-Tuning Experiments and Results

Prior to our comparative evaluations, we conduct a series of experiments to tune the important parameters of each investigated technique. We again apply the Gaussian-4 distribution and follow the same parameter-tuning procedure to generate 30 synthetic data sets (i.e., hierarchies). Because HA C+P follows the discovery-based approach, it does not consider the existing category hierarchy in each synthetic data set and uses only the documents of each synthetic set to generate its clustering hierarchy. In contrast, CHE considers both the documents and the existing category hierarchy for each synthetic set in the subsequent parameter-tuning experiments.

The use of HA C+P requires the proper tuning of several parameters: the minimum number of documents in a cluster (ε), the maximum depth of the resulting hierarchy (ρ), the number of features (k), and the preferred number of clusters at each layer of the resulting hierarchy $( N _ { _ { c l u s } } )$ . At each value of $N _ { _ { c l u s } }$ examined, we search for the k/ρ combination that yields the best performance of HA C+P for each document corpus. For the Reuters corpus, the effectiveness of HA C+P appears mostly comparable for all the $k / \rho$ combinations investigated. ## Conclusion

In this study , w e attempt to preserve user preferences in automated document-category management by addressing the fundamental constraints of a promising evolution-based technique—CE. Specifically, CE2 extends from CE by addressing essential limitations inherent to its use of holistic measures, whereas CHE manages the evolution of a set of document categories that have a defined hierarchical structure. We empirically evaluate the effectiveness of CE2 in different category evolution scenarios created using two different document corpora (i.e., Reuters and ACM) and include CE and HA C as performance benchmarks. In addition, we examine the effectiveness of CHE for reorganizing the Reuters and the ACM category hierarchies, using a prevalent discovery-based technique (i.e., HA C+P) as a benchmark. Our empirical results suggest that CE2 and CHE outperform their respective benchmark techniques by showing considerably higher cluster recall, cluster precision, and structure similarity (in the case of CHE).

This study thus makes several contributions to document management research and practice. First, we address the need to preserve a user’s document-grouping preferences in automated document-category management by designing and implementing advanced techniques that extend from CE, a promising evolution-based technique. Second, we perform systematic evaluations on the basis of real-world document corpora and generate empirical evidence suggesting the viability of the proposed techniques and their relative effectiveness compared with prevalent existing techniques. Third, we contribute to general document management research by examining the evolving nature of document categories following a flat or hierarchical structure, a fundamental challenge in automated document management that has yet to receive due attention. Fourth, we propose a novel similarity metric for assessing the structural similarity between an evolved hierarchy and the true hierarchy. Overall, our analyses and comparative evaluation results shed light on the viability, utilities, and applicability of an evolution-based approach that supports automated document-category management and highlights its relative effectiveness compared with some salient discovery-based techniques commonly used in previous research. Although our empirical evaluations involve research articles and news documents, the proposed techniques can be applied to support an array of applications, including document management, knowledge management, and knowledge markets in Web 2.0.

Our study has several limitations that warrant additional research attention. First, our evaluations use simulated category reorganization scenarios, though they are created from real-world document corpora. To mitigate this limitation, further empirical investigations should involve specific subjects and take place in routine contexts. Second, the documents in our evaluations are drawn from two application domains and therefore are limited in number and diversity. Additional research should include larger-scale evaluations that involve more documents or a diversified set of domains. Third, our analysis focuses on single-category classification, a constraint that should be relaxed in further research. Understandably, a document may pertain simultaneously to multiple categories, equally or to different degrees, which demands appropriate category evolution techniques that can cope with multi-category documents. The visualization of (document) category evolution represents another interesting future research direction, which would allow individuals and organizations to understand better how the main topics of their documents change or the focus evolves in a target domain.
