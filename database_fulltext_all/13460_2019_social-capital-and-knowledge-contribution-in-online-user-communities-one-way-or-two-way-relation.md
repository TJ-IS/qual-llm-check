# Social capital and knowledge contribution in online user communities: Oneway or two-way relationship?

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Social capital Knowledge contribution Online user communities Two-way relationship Granger causality

## A B S T R A C T

Firms have been increasingly relying on online user communities to access external, distant knowledge and expertise. Previous research on online user communities has largely investigated the influence of users' social capital on their knowledge sharing behavior. In this study, we propose a two-way relationship between social capital and knowledge contribution of users in online user communities. To test our proposition, we collected and analyzed participation data of 1766 users from the online user community of BMC, a global leader in innovative software solutions. Overall. our study shows evidence of significant bi-directional relationships between social capital (structural, cognitive and relational) and knowledge contribution, while the bi-directional relationships mainly exist among peripheral participants. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of our study for the online user community literature as well as the broader context of online community.

## 1. Introduction

Firms have been increasingly relying on online user communities<sup>1</sup> (hereinafter OUC) to access external, distant knowledge and expertise [5,6]. Participants or members in OUC are mainly product users of the host firm. They voluntarily engage in the community and contribute their knowledge and expertise about existing product support and new product development. Much research has focused on understanding why users are willing to cooperate with host firms and contribute their knowledge to the community (e.g., [5,7,8]). An emergent line of in quiry has emphasized the social capital aspect of community engagement. This research stream overall suggests that users are embedded in the social structure of their community and that social capital building goals play a major role in their knowledge contribution behavior (e.g., [2,9,10]).

While existing research has revealed significant efects of social capital on knowledge contribution in OUC, it has primarily emphasized and theorized a one-way relationship between social capital and knowledge contribution (as we will discuss in detail further). Such a relationship falls short of revealing the dynamic co-evolution between social capital and knowledge contribution that might explain why the social capital emerged in the first place or how it can be sustained over time. Thus, the goal of this study is to contribute to a better understanding of the interaction between social capital and knowledge contribution in OUC. We investigate the interactions between users' knowledge contribution, including both the quantity and the quality dimensions, and their social capital, including the structural, cognitive, and relational dimensions. Through a multidimensional, dynamic panel analysis of knowledge contribution and social capital, we seek to provide a robust and complete picture of the interplay between social capital and knowledge contribution. More specifically, we ask the following research questions: 1) is there a one-way or two-way relationship between social capital and knowledge contribution, 2) is the relationship, if causal, transitory or persistent, and 3) does the relationship apply to users with diferent participation levels?

Investigating the above questions has several theoretical implications. First, existing theories on social networks have proposed a twoway, dynamic relationship between structural social capital and knowledge contribution in both online and ofline settings (see [11,12]). However, to the best of our knowledge, no published studies have empirically investigated such a relationship. Our study therefore takes the first step in this direction by empirically examining a two-way relationship between social capital and knowledge contribution. Further, we extend current research by investigating the three dimensions of social capital (structural, cognitive and relational) simultaneously. This is important in order to distinguish the impact each dimension has in the presence of the other two. From a broader online community perspective, the findings of our study should apply not only to the OUC context but also to other forms of online communities including Q&A and various open innovation communities (e.g., open-source software communities) where the dynamics of knowledge creation and exchange determine the long-term success of communities (see [6] for a detailed comparison and discussion on these diferent forms of online commu nities).

Second, our study, while examining the interplay between social capital and knowledge contribution, diferentiates users based on their participation levels. This is imperative given that communities of practice usually involve members with diferent levels of participation – e.g., core and peripheral [13]. Diferent groups of users often possess diferent characteristics and motivations with respect to community interactions and contributions [14]. Nevertheless, a large body of extant OUC literature ignores such diferences when investigating the efect of social capital on knowledge contribution (e.g., [2,15,16]). Our study hence holds the potential to extend existing literature by demonstrating the diferential efects of the interplay between social capital and knowledge contribution on diferent categories of users.

Third, our study holds implications for other research streams in the online community literature. For instance, online community leader ship as an emerging research stream focuses on the formation of leadership and the role of leaders in online communities [17,18]. It has been found that knowledge contribution and social capital of in dividuals are two key antecedents of leadership in online communities [17]. Members with a central network position who contribute frequently, for example, are more likely to be identified as leaders [17]. Our study suggests that the path to leadership in OUC may entail ongoing interactions between individuals' knowledge contribution and social capital. As one changes, so does the other. In other words, while both knowledge contribution and social capital afect leadership independently, it is essential to account for the reciprocity between knowledge contribution and social capital and examine how such dy namics determine the emergence of leadership in online communities. Our findings may also help explain how social capital initially emerges from knowledge contribution in some online marketplaces (e.g., Amazon reviews) and how social capital translates into other capital forms (e.g., game credits) as a result of participation in online games. Answers to these questions are still unclear to date [1,19].

Finally, our study may explain some inconsistent findings in prior studies. For example, Chiu et al.'s [15] finding that users' norm of reciprocity (relational capital) in OUC is positively associated with their knowledge contribution is not supported in Wiertz and De Ruyter's [16] study. Our findings suggest that diferentiating users based on their participation levels may resolve this inconsistency. Overall, the present paper holds the potential to not merely consolidate extant OUC literature but also to provide extensions by examining a two-way relation ship between social capital and knowledge contribution. Our findings would also add a layer of explanation to the participation and knowl edge contribution dynamics in many other types of online communities.

## 2. Social capital theory and knowledge sharing

Social capital refers to the collective resources emerging from and embedded inside a social network as a result of the interconnected relationships of its members [20]. The central proposition of social capital theory is that social relationships among individuals can be productive and valuable resources [21]. One distinct theme in examining the consequences of social capital for action is that social capital can facilitate cooperation and coordination for mutual benefits [22]. This is largely because social capital helps diminish barriers to information sharing and learning, which is essential for the exchange and combination of resources to take place. Organizational studies have found social capital to be central to the understanding of institutional knowledge creation, sharing and innovation [20]. In particular, several empirical studies demonstrate how social capital facilitates information and resource exchange, and the efects of social capital on knowledge acquisition and contribution within organizations (e.g., [23,59]).

In online settings where practices and communications between individuals occur primarily through electronic networks (e.g., email listservs, electronic bulletin boards and websites), some scholars suggest that social capital is dificult to develop. This is because social capital is more likely to develop in collectives characterized by high interdependence, frequent interaction, and closed structures [20,24]. Also, electronic networks may create barriers to knowledge sharing and integration at multiple levels (see [25,26]). As a result, significant le vels of knowledge sharing may not develop. However, other researchers argue that electronic networks help share information quickly, globally, and with large numbers of individuals, facilitating social capital and knowledge exchange between geographically dispersed participants (e.g., [27,28]). A seminal work in this research stream is from Wasko and Faraj [29] who examine how social capital relates to knowledge contribution in electronic networks of practice.

Online user communities difer from organizational settings in that the former not only rely on online communication but also consist of group members engaged in a shared practice. Compared to participants in general electronic networks like websites, OUC members are either external product users or internal employees of the host firm and brought together by shared goals, interests and needs [6,15]. Many product users and host firm employees even know each other and work together, communicate and coordinate with each other directly (e.g., meet ofline and face-to-face). In such communities of practice, problem solving and joint sense-making are likely to create norms of direct reciprocity and enhance the formation of strong interpersonal ties between members [30]. A large body of extant IS research has hence investigated the relationship between social capital and knowledge contribution in OUC, discussed next.

## 3. Literature review and proposition

In this section we review studies on OUC and based on the review, propose a two-way relationship between social capital and knowledge contribution in OUC.

## 3.1. Literature review

As noted earlier, studies on OUC have largely focused on examining the efects of users' social capital on their knowledge contribution. Drawing upon the seminal work of Nahapiet and Ghoshal [20], this research stream typically conceptualizes social capital as consisting of three distinct dimensions: structural, cognitive, and relational. Structural capital refers to the connections or the structural links created through social interactions among individuals in a network [20]. OUC research on the structural aspect of social capital has tended to focus on the structural position of users and how it impacts knowledge exchange in the community [7,9]. Whelan [31], for example, proposes that individuals' core/periphery structure and connectivity in a community network will influence their knowledge contribution. And Dahlander and Frederiksen's [2] empirical study finds that users' position in the core/periphery structure of an OUC is consequential for knowledge contribution.

In contrast, relational capital refers to assets that are rooted in the relationships such as trust, mutual respect and generalized reciprocity [20]. Trust, for example, is often developed through repeated interactions between individuals. It can induce joint eforts and get other individuals' support for achieving objectives that would not be possible in a situation where trust did not exist [23]. Several studies on OUC have found that trust in other community members (or the norm of collaboration) afects users' knowledge-sharing intentions with both peers and the host firm (e.g., [8,32]). And building a norm of reciprocity has significant efects on the quality and quantity of knowledge contribution (e.g., [16,33]).

Organizational studies, for example, have measured cognitive capital by assessing shared language (see [34]) and shared vision (see [23]) at the individual level. In online settings such as electronic networks, cognitive capital has been referred to as members' common understanding of community tasks and the capability to understand and apply knowledge when connecting with each other [29]. In the OUC context, however, cognitive capital is often related to (or proxied by) the cognitive benefits (e.g., expertise enhancement and community learning) users anticipate from engaging in the community [35]. ## 3.2. Proposition

The discussion above reveals significant efects of social capital on knowledge contribution in OUC. However, the relationship between knowledge contribution and social capital might be bi-directional. It is plausible that an individual's knowledge contribution should influence his/her social capital in the community. For example, users who extensively participate in product support and contribute their knowledge might be expected to receive more connections or ties. This then might alter their structural capital in the community [40]. While studies relying on social network and collective action theories have shown that network outcomes are afected by network structures [20,41], the latter issue – how network outcomes (e.g., knowledge exchange) afect network structures – has been largely ignored.

Further, users should also develop their cognitive capital by contributing knowledge in the community. As a user contributes his/her own knowledge, the user is involved in the knowledge exchange process, either actively or passively. The user may, for example, discuss his/her answers with others, read and comment on answers of others or simply receive a notification of the marking of a best answer contributed by others. Such community interactions and learning processes (i.e., cognitive benefits) are likely to enhance shared vision among users and impact their subsequent attitude towards knowledge sharing [12,36]. Overall, we suggest that in OUC, knowledge contribution is oftentimes accompanied and/or followed by community interactions where the contributor can learn the knowledge, skills and norms of practice from others, either actively or passively. This would change a user's cognitive capital which then drives the user to continue to share his/her knowledge in the community. Unlike previous studies largely assuming knowledge contribution depends on pre-determined cognitive capital levels (e.g., [26,29]), our study seeks to examine the change of cognitive capital as a key outcome of knowledge contribution.

Finally, the norm of collaboration and trust should be cultivated between users who reactively and proactively contribute their knowl edge in the community. Research has demonstrated that as users receive incoming ties and derive expertise and skills resulting from their knowledge contribution, they are willing to develop trust and a sense of reciprocity with others [42]. This is consistent with social exchange theory which indicates that individuals' trust and afective attachment is largely governed by the entity with which they interact and exchange support [43]. A strong norm of reciprocity and trustworthiness in the collective will, in turn, reward individual eforts and ensure continual contributions [44]. These combined should impact relational capital between users in the community. Taken together, we propose and expect that there is a two-way relationship between knowledge contribution and social capital of users in OUC, as shown in Fig. 1. Testing and understanding this proposition, as discussed earlier, is of great importance for scholars to advance theories and for practitioners to build a thriving community.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Data

To address our research questions, we collected data from the OUC of BMC, a global leader in innovative software solutions headquartered in Houston, Texas. BMC built its OUC as early as 2002 to interact with external product users. The community has nurtured BMC's long-term relationships with its product users for existing product support and new product development of the company. As of January 2016, the community has 26,597 registered members. To create a sample for the study, we employed a stratified random sampling strategy. The use of stratified random sampling allows us to ensure that the dataset represents various user populations by dividing the entire population into homogeneous groups – i.e., strata – and select random samples from each stratum.<sup>2</sup> We created the homogeneous groups based on the cumulative community points members have earned. According to the reputational system of the community, a member can earn points through various community activities. For example, a user will earn 1 point if the user replies to a discussion question and 5 points and 10 points if the answer is marked as helpful and correct, respectively. We specified 13 groups using diferent cumulative community points as threshold values, as detailed in Table 2, and selected a stratified random sample of 3000 users in total.

We then checked the record of recent community activities of each of the 3000 users and removed those users who did not have any community activities during the previous 3 months. This is because a member who did not have any login record during the previous 3 months was assigned an “inactive” status by the community system. We suggest that these individuals were “zombie” users who may not participate in the community any more. This results in a total of 1766 users included in our dataset. Table 2 further summarizes the demo graphic characteristics of the sample.

We collected the users' participation data from their online community profiles. Community profiles include each user's demographic data (e.g., name, company and title), network data (following/followers), contribution data (e.g., answers and ideas), reputation data (e.g., points), etc. Through the profile menus such as “Content”, “Connections” and “Reputation”, users can access more detailed information such as the discussions and threads they have participated in and the profiles of people following them. Fig. 2 provides an example of an online community profile.

## 4.2. Variables and measures

To examine the dynamics between social capital and knowledge contribution, we focus on three groups of variables. They are: 1) users' initial social capital including structural, cognitive, and relational, 2) users' knowledge contribution including both the quantity and quality dimension, and 3) the changes of users' structural, cognitive and rela tional capital resulting from their knowledge contribution. We seek to investigate whether the initial social capital predicts users' knowledge contribution, which then, in turn, influences users' social capital. We measured all the variables via each user's online community profile on a weekly basis from January 4th 2016 to July 31th 2016, a total of 30 weeks.

The variables of knowledge contribution (i.e., quantity and quality) were measured at the end of each week (i.e., the beginning of the next week). We measured knowledge contribution using each user's answer contribution data during the current week. We coded the answers as follows: an answer would receive 3 points if it was marked as “correct” (by the asker), 2 points if marked as “helpful” (by the asker), and 1 point if not marked. We then summed up all the points to measure the quantity dimension of the user's knowledge contribution. The quality dimension was measured by calculating the percentage of correct and helpful answers – i.e., points of correct and helpful answers divided by total points – to minimize the bias towards users who contributed a fewer number of answers.

To measure the initial structural capital each user possessed at the beginning of our research period, we utilized the total number of followers a user has in the community before January 4th 2016. The more followers a user has, the more closely the user moves towards the core structure of an online community [45,46]. For the initial relational capital, theories on social networks and research on social media [15,21,30,47] indicate that when individuals choose to follow each other, they tend to trust each other to an extent and are willing to interact for reciprocity. We hence measured the users' relational capital at the beginning of our research period by counting the number of ties where two users followed each other. For example, if a user had 5 connections where the user and the other user followed each other, the user would receive a relational score of 5.

For the measure of cognitive capital, studies on virtual teams and electronic networks have assessed shared common understanding of taskwork among teammates (e.g., [26]) and self-rated expertise and experience in the field (e.g., [29]) to measure individuals' cognitive capital. Considering the OUC context, we focused on the cognitive benefit a user has obtained from community learning, as suggested by our literature review (Table 1), to measure each user's initial cognitive capital at the beginning of our research period. Specifically, for all the discussion threads a user had replied before January 4th 2016, we iden tified whether the correct and/or the helpful answers marked in the discussion threads were contributed by the user or others. We suggest that the user should, to some extent, learn from peers if there are peers' correct and/or helpful answers marked in the discussion threads.<sup>3</sup> Such a learning process should change the user's cognitive capital, impacting the user's capability to understand and apply knowledge when interacting with others in the community. We then used the cumulative score of correct answers (2 points/each) and helpful answers (1 points/ each) contributed by others (of all the discussion threads a user replied before January 4th 2016) to quantify the initial cognitive capital for each user.

The variables of the changes of structural, cognitive and relational capital were measured at the end of each week using each user's participation data. For the change of cognitive capital, we again used the cumulative score of correct answers and helpful answers contributed by others, of all the discussion threads to which a user replied in the current week, to reflect the user's change of cognitive capital. The change of structural capital was measured by recording the number of new followers of each user at the end of current week. However, whether the new followers resulted from a user's knowledge contribution is unobservable. To overcome this issue, we checked each new follower's online profile to determine whether the follower and the focal user had replied in the same discussion threads. We suggest that the decision to follow should, to some extent, result from the knowledge contribution if the two users had answered to the same discussions. Based on this criterion, we were able to remove those new follower whose motives were “unidentifiable”. We then recorded the resulting number of new ties as the change of structural capital and the number of those new structural ties where the focal user and the new follower followed each other as the change of relational capital. ## 4.3. Modeling and estimation approach

In this study we are interested in 1) whether there is a one-way or two-way relationship between social capital and knowledge contribution, 2) whether the causal relationship is transitory or persistent, and 3) whether the relationship is applicable to users with diferent levels of participation. To examine the first question, we employed the Granger causality test [48]. The Granger test for causality considers the following questions: is it social capital that leads to knowledge contribution or is it knowledge contribution that causes changes of social ca pital, or does both causal relationships exist?

The Granger causality test of two variables is commonly conducted by regressing one variable on its own lagged values and on lagged values of the other variable [49,50]. The Granger test concludes that one variable causes the other variable if the past values of both vari ables are useful in predicting the dependent variable [49]. Given this, we used the following models to examine the Granger causal relationship between social capital (y), social capital change (Δy) and knowledge contribution (x):

$$
\Delta \mathrm{yit} = \alpha 0 \mathrm{t} + \sum_ {\mathrm{L} = 1} ^ {\mathrm{n}} \alpha \mathrm{Lt} \Delta \mathrm{yi}, \mathrm{t} - \mathrm{L} + \sum_ {\mathrm{L} = 1} ^ {\mathrm{n}} \beta \mathrm{Ltxi}, \mathrm{t} - \mathrm{L} + \varepsilon \mathrm{it}\tag{1}
$$

$$
x \mathrm{it} = \gamma 0 \mathrm{t} + \sum_ {\mathrm{L} = 1} ^ {\mathrm{n}} \gamma \mathrm{Ltxi}, \mathrm{t} - \mathrm{L} + \sum_ {\mathrm{L} = 1} ^ {\mathrm{n}} \delta \mathrm{Ltyi}, \mathrm{t} - \mathrm{L} + \varepsilon \mathrm{it}\tag{2}
$$

where y represents structural, relational, or cognitive capital, Δy represents the change of structural, relational, or cognitive capital, and x represents the quality or quantity of knowledge contribution. In addition, t is the time period (i.e., 30 weeks from January 4th to July 31th), i is the number of observations (e.g., 1766 users) in each week, and n is the number of lags selected. The error term (ε) follows a two-way error component structure [51]. It consists of an unobservable user specific (λ), a time specific (μ) and a random error term (τ), i.e., εit λit μit τit

In terms of the choice of number of lags, overall, using large lags helps rule out autocorrelation while small lags increase degrees of freedom [49,50]. Our selection of number of lags is empirical rather than theoretical. Specifically, for each causality test, because our sample size is large [52], we added one lag at a time and compared the statistics based on the Schwarz information criterion (SIC). We then used the results from this procedure to determine the appropriate lag length for the causality tests. We suggest that the causal efect is transitory if it holds only for a few lags and is persistent if the efect holds for multiple, consecutive lags [49]. In addition, to examine the long-term efect of the causal relationships, we complemented our analysis using impulse response functions (IRFs). IRFs are often applied to visually interpret the coeficient estimates generated for dynamic panel models [49]. The results of IRFs describe the impact of one unit increase in one variable on the future values of all variables in the model and thereby can be utilized to show whether an exogenous shock to one variable will have a transitory or persistent efect on other variables (e.g., [53,54]).

Our last question concerns whether the relationships, if causal, hold true for users with diferent levels of participation. To this end, we categorized users into diferent participation levels based on the group a user is in (see Table 2). As discussed earlier, diferent groups represent diferent levels of points earned by a user in the community. They hence reflect how long and active users have participated in the community. Overall, we assigned all the 1766 users into two participation levels: core and peripheral. We chose Group 9 as the cut-of group considering that Groups 9–13 represent highly experienced users with much higher community points, compared to the users in the remaining groups. As a result, there are 1688 users (Groups 1–8) at the peripheral participation level and 78 users (Groups 9–13) at the core participation level. As additional analyses, we also moved Groups 7–8 to the core group, resulting in a total of 199 users at the core participation level and 1567 users at the peripheral participation level. The corresponding results are reported in the Appendix (Tables A.3\~A.4).

Given the above, our empirical study employs two strongly balanced panel models spanning 30 weeks. One panel includes 1688 users (Panel 1) and the other panel has 78 users (Panel 2). We constructed both panels based on user-week pairs, resulting in 50,640 observations in Panel 1 and 2340 observations in Panel 2. For each user-week pair, variables examined include social capital (i.e., the initial social capital level at the beginning of week t), knowledge contribution during week t, and the change of social capital during week t (which, in turn, determines the initial social capital level at the beginning of week (t + 1)). Next, each Granger causality test between two variables was conducted using vector auto regressive (VAR) with the within estimation method. The VAR estimation helps mitigate endogeneity issues when exogenous instruments are not available; and the use of fixedefects method allows us to account for unobservable user and time specific efects in the error term [49].

## 4.4.2. Impulse response functions

As discussed earlier, we use IRFs to further examine the transitory/ persistent nature of the causal relationships. Given that we chose the 2- lag structure for all the causality tests, employing IRFs allows us to complement the analyses and results for those pairs of variables that may fit well with other lag structures as suggested in Tables A.1 and A.2 in the Appendix. Figs. 3(a)\~3(f) demonstrate selected IRFs. For users at the peripheral participation level, Figs. 3(a)\~3(d) combined indicate that the two-way relationship between social capital and knowledge contribution is transitory. Fig. 3(a), for example, shows that while the coeficients of Lag 1 and Lag 2 are positive and significant (β = 0.0120, $p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 ; \ \beta = 0 . 0 1 1 9 , \ p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1$ in Table 6), the response of knowledge contribution in quality to a shock in cognitive capital is initially positive and high but decreases and approaches zero over time (reaching zero after about 6 weeks). In other words, the positive efect of cognitive capital on users' knowledge contribution in terms of quality is not long-term. Likewise, Figs. 3(e) and 3(f) show that for users at the core participation level, the impact of knowledge contribution on the change of structural capital, while significant, is transitory as well. We will discuss these findings in detail to follow.

## 5. Discussion

In this study, we are interested in examining the interactions between users' social capital and their knowledge contribution in the OUC context. Further, we investigate the transitory/persistent nature of the causal relationships based on users with diferent levels of participa tion. For users at the peripheral participation level, our results (Table 6) indicate that a two-way relationship exists between their knowledge contribution and social capital. However, the causal relationships, either between social capital and knowledge contribution or between knowledge contribution and the change of social capital, are not persistent but are transitory. Our explanation of this finding is twofold. One, we suggest that the efect of social capital on knowledge contribution is nonlinear. This is because when users' social capital in creases, they may not be as motivated to contribute more compared to the time when they only possessed a small amount of social capital. For example, an additional 5 incoming ties (i.e., structural capital) should matter more to a user who has no ties than to a user who already has 100 ties. In other words, the marginal efect of more social capital on knowledge contribution should be decreasing in the long term. The results (Table 7) of users at the core participation level (i.e., those who possess extensive social capital) also echo with this explanation showing that the efects of structural, cognitive and relational capital on knowledge contribution are largely insignificant.

Two, we suggest that community growth and the resulting community size may have an impact on the transitory/persistent nature of the efect of knowledge contribution on social capital change. A large community provides more interaction opportunities, but individual interactions are more likely to remain unnoticed by other members [12]. These may impact the emergence, form and loss of social capital within a community [29,40]. On the other hand, it may be dificult for members to continue to grow their social ties and expertise in a community that grows slowly, regardless of their knowledge contribution [56,57]. Unfortunately, in the present study we were not able to examine the influence of community growth and size on the causal relationships. Future studies applying comparative analysis based on multi-community data of community growth and size would be valuable to deepen and extend our understanding of the interactions be tween social capital and knowledge contribution.

Regarding users at the core participation level, our results indicate that overall, there are no bi-directional causal relationships between social capital and knowledge contribution. Nevertheless, several findings are worth discussing. First, the results (upper right in Table 7) show that for this group of users, social capital overall does not influence knowledge contribution. This finding may be explained by the concept of lead users in the OUC research. We suggest that users at the core participation level are largely lead users who possess great expertise and community experience and often exhibit diferent personality traits (e.g., locus of control and innovativeness) than non‑lead users (e.g., those in Panel 1). Studies have shown that users with a high degree of lead user characteristics tend to enjoy revealing their knowledge to other users, and their motivation for participation and contribution to the community are largely related to a wish to be recognized by the host firm and peers [5,35,58]. Combined, we suggest that social capital (structural, cognitive and relational) should cause knowledge contribution only for non‑lead users.

Second, there are mixed results (bottom left in Table 7) on the effects of knowledge contribution on social capital change. On one hand, the IRFs results (Figs. 3(e) and 3(f)) show that the response of structural capital to a one unit increase in knowledge contribution, both quality and quantity, is initially high and positive but decreases and approaches zero over time. This indicates a transitory efect of knowledge contribution on the change of structural capital. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that when measuring the change of structural capital, we excluded those new ties (i.e., followers) whose motives were “unidentifiable”. It is likely that some users, especially novices and newcomers, made the decision to follow the experienced users after viewing the answers contributed by them. Including these new ties resulting from passive participation (e.g., viewing) may change the transitory/ persistent nature of the causal relationships. Future research employing subjective measurement (e.g., user self-report or survey) could be used to confirm and complement our findings.

On the other hand, the efects of knowledge contribution on the change of cognitive and relational capital are insignificant (bottom left in Table 7). We measured the change of cognitive capital of a user by calculating the number of correct and/or helpful answers contributed by others that the focal user may learn from. We suggest that compared to peers, experts and experienced users may be overall more likely to contribute helpful and correct answers when they participate in a discussion. This could lead to few cognitive capital changes even though they have contributed in multiple discussions, based on the measure. Furthermore, our measure ignored other community participation forms that would impact users' cognitive capital but were unobservable for us. For example, a user may learn something by only observing the discussion threads or interacting with others via community messages. Likewise, we measured the change of relational capital by only calculating the connections where the focal user and other users followed each other (i.e., trust) without considering other relational capital forms such as identification and norms [20]. This limitation may also explain why the relationship between the change of relational capital and knowledge contribution became significant (see Table A.4) after we moved more users, who tend to follow back when they receive new followers, to the core group. Given these limitations in variable measurement, therefore, future research using subjective measures to take into account these ignored changes of social capital may lead to different results. Also, given that our study is built on a single OUC in the software industry, additional research is needed to confirm and complement our results in the context of other industries.

## 6. Conclusion

The objective of this study was to investigate the interaction between social capital and knowledge contribution in OUC. While previous research has overall shown that users' social capital is a good predictor of their knowledge contribution, this study shows that the relationship between social capital and knowledge contribution is far more complex than just a one-way relationship. We find that a two-way relationship exists between social capital and knowledge contribution among users at the peripheral participation level. For users at the core participation level, our study shows that their knowledge contribution is not caused primarily by the social capital they possess in the com munity. In sum, our study demonstrates evidence of significant bi-directional relationships between social capital and knowledge contribution and examines the conditions (i.e., diferent participation levels) under which the relationships exist.

With these findings, our study also provides practitioners valuable guidelines for the design of OUC. Specifically, we suggest that host firms should integrate reputation systems with social networking features in the community. Implementing social network/media features in the reputation systems would further motivate contribution by visualizing users' social capital in the community. The following/fol lowers feature used by BMC, for instance, helps visualize users' social ties and connections and thereby their positions in the network. It also allows users to know their mutual friends and who knows whom. Additionally, the bookmark and notification features allow users to derive information and knowledge without logging into the community. Such instrumental and informational support in community design

can play an important role in supporting diferent types of decision making of members. Using the following/followers feature in BMC's OUC, for example, a newcomer can readily explore all the individuals/ profiles connected with the lead/experienced users and decide which ones to follow in his/her own interests. Users would also be more motivated to participate in the community as they recognize the changes in their structural and relational capital. Managers and community designers should therefore strategically adopt and implement new features and tools that will enhance social exchange and con nectedness within the communities.

Jie Yan (Kevin) is an assistant professor in the department of Supply Chain, Information Systems and Analytics at Dalton State College. Kevin received his Ph.D. in Information Systems from Baylor University. Before beginning his Ph.D., Kevin possessed over 6 years of work experience in the Telecom and Datacom industries. He worked for companies including Ericsson AB, Cisco Systems and General Electric (GE). His research focuses on cloud computing, IT consumerization and online user innovation communities.

Dorothy E. Leidner is the Ferguson Professor of Information Systems at Baylor University and a visiting professor at the University of Lund. Since 2005, she has been a summer visiting professor at the University of Mannheim. Dorothy received her PhD in Information Systems in 1992 from the University of Texas at Austin. As a scholar, Dorothy is widely published in such journals as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Organization Science, Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Sciences Journal, and Journal of Strategic Information Systems, among others, with other 30,000 citations (scholar.google). Dorothy has held various editorial roles: co-Editor-in-Chief of Data Base for Advances in IS, associate editor and senior editor for MIS Quarterly, associate editor for Decision Sciences Journal, and senior editor for the Journal of Strategic Information Systems. She currently serves as Editor-in-Chief of MIS Quarterly Executive and Senior Editor for Information Systems Research and for the Journal of the Associatior of Information Systems.

Hind Benbya is Professor of Information Technology (IT) and Innovation Management at Montpellier Business School. Her research and consulting consist in developing frameworks and tools for implementing and evaluating complex IT to support knowledge and innovative work (i.e., Co-Creation Communities, Innovation Platforms & Knowledge Management Systems). Her work appears or is forthcoming in top academic journals such as MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems and Journal of Information Technology; as well as top practitioner journals such as MIT Sloan Management Review, and MISQ Executive. Hind is author and co-editor of four books including: (1) Knowledge Management Systems Implementation: Lessons from the Silicon Valley, (2) Exploring the Design and Efects of Internal Knowledge Markets and (3) Successful OSS Project Design and Implementation. She has been a visiting academic at the UCLA-Anderson Business School and at the London School of Economics and is a recipient of several best paper awards.
