# Positive and negative aspects of online community cultivation: Implications for online stores’ relationship management

Hsien-Tung Tsai <sup>a</sup>, Peiyu Pai <sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> Department of Business Administration, National Taipei University, 151 University Rd., Sen-Shia, Taipei 237, Taiwan <sup>b</sup> Department of Business Administration, National Chengchi University, 64 Sec.2, Zhi-Nan Rd., Taipei 116, Taiwan

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 8 July 2009 Received in revised form 14 September 2011 Accepted 4 November 2011 Available online 3 December 201

Keywords: Consumer power Community identification Community participation Loyalty Relationship investments

## A B S T R A C T

We investigated how online community building might affect the development of relationships with customers of online stores. Adopting social identification and relationship marketing perspectives, we examined the positive and negative aspects of online community cultivation and tested the meditating mechanisms involved in member participation processes. A survey-based study, conducted with 913 online consumers, revealed that community interaction can lead to consumer power through community identification and relationship investments. Furthermore, we found that perceived e-tailer support negatively affected the relationship between identification and consumer power. We concluded with a discussion of the key managerial and research implications of our findings.

## 1. Introduction

Online communities provide a new way to encourage deep, enduring affective bonds between consumers and firms [23]. Thus, research in the past decade has focused on the positive results of online community cultivation, including consumer loyalty [14], product innovation [11], and reduced customer service costs due to peer-to-peer problem solving [13]. We took a different approach: we investigated both negative and positive aspects of online community activities and also determined whether meditating mechanisms might be involved.

It is important to understand the negative consequences of online communities, such as the hidden costs of e-tailers’ online communities [16]. In trying to encourage consumers to participate in their communities, e-tailers unwittingly might boost consumers’ power, which reduces their own autonomy [22]. As social identification theory predicts, encouraging participation in online communities may motivate consumers to integrate themselves into the community and thereby induce identification-based commitment, which in turn strengthens their perceptions that their claims on brand providers are legitimate and urgent [1,7]. With these beliefs, they are more likely to press their claims assertively and consistently and with greater power over the e-tailer. However, little research has been devoted to the role of online communities in managing customer relationships, and researchers have largely neglected modeling the process by which community participation leads to the formation of consumer power or other relational outcomes. The importance of an in-depth understanding of online communities for managing customer relationships is underscored further by its inclusion at the intellectual core of the IS discipline [18].

Against this backdrop, we developed a theoretical framework of the effects of cultivating an online community. Prior research has shown that consumer participation in a brand community is a function of perceived relationships with the brand [2,19]; we extended this community behavior research by developing and testing a model in which internal and external antecedents influence members’ participation in the online community. Then, we investigated potential mediating mechanisms that underlie the relationships between community participation and its dual outcomes of consumer power and loyalty. By doing so, we accounted for the effect of online community cultivation on consumer behavior. Finally, we examined the moderating effect of perceived e-tailer support on the relationship between identification and consumer power, which is theoretically important because it may specify a boundary condition for the prediction of consumer behavior based on community identification. If a firm’s management fails to address this negative relationship, it could suffer idiosyncratic stock returns, whereas knowledge of such moderating effects could provide practical guidelines for e-tailers to manage their customer relationships more effectively.

## 2. Conceptual framework and hypothesis development

Our conceptual framework (see Fig. 1) included potential antecedents and consequences of online community participation. The topics of such participation and online stores’ relationship management are multidisciplinary in nature and have received a great deal of attention in many fields. Thus, our theoretical framework drew on social identification and relationship marketing concepts, together with ideas from consumer behavior research [4,5]. Overall, community participation depends on external images of the online store and consumers’ perceptions of the online store’s performance; such factors lead to relationship investment and community identification, which, in turn, influence loyalty and the formation of consumer power.

## 2.1. Online community cultivation by online stores

In offline stores, buyers and sellers interact face-to-face, which offers a rich medium for transmitting information concurrently and through multiple channels, including verbal, non-verbal, social, personal, and paralinguistic cues. However, in online shopping contexts, consumers may have more uncertainty, because substantially less information is available through this medium. In addition, the spatial and temporal separation creates information asymmetries that may benefit the seller, prompting consumers to be wary of potential seller opportunism [12,20]. Such concerns may inhibit the development of buyer–seller relationships [3], but an online community may help sustain long-term relationships [23].

From an e-tailer’s perspective, an online community provides a supportive environment in which consumers can share their experiences and expectations. e-Tailers can then learn about consumers’ perceptions of past purchases and use them to improve their customers’ experiences [24]. Online communities offer quick responses to queries or information requests because of their direct communication capabilities: consumers obtain support and experience fellowship from other consumers [27]. By building an online community, e-tailers can provide a ‘‘family’’ of like-minded consumers [23,24]. Furthermore, members feel more comfortable and secure.

## 2.2. Antecedents of online community participation

## 2.2.1. Prestige

Prestige is the extent to which consumers perceive that people whose opinions they value believe that the online store is well regarded [28]. Social identity theory asserts that brand or store images link systematically with self-concepts and that consumers associate themselves with positive and negative attributes. If an online store’s external image is prestigious, customers should be more willing to participate in its online community. Therefore:

H1. Greater levels of prestige are associated with greater levels of online community participation.

## 2.2.2. Attribute satisfaction

Attribute satisfaction refers to a consumer’s cognitive satisfaction with attributes, such as services, products, or website features; it is a critical construct because it pertains to other important variables, such as system design, consumer loyalty [9], and website success [10]. Attribute satisfaction should encourage a customer to build a relationship with his or her community. Consumers frequently view stores or brands as viable partners and may even assign animate characteristics to them. Therefore, a satisfactory relationship with a particular brand or store can encourage customers to interact with like-minded consumers. In addition, Ahearne et al. [1] argue that satisfactory experiences with one party decrease the psychological distance between the self and the party and also drive members closer to the center of the group. Ultimately, positive experiences turn into supportive behaviors that benefit the store. Thus:

H2. Greater levels of attribute satisfaction are associated with greater levels of online community participation.

## 2.3. Consequences of online community participation

## 2.3.1. Community participation and relationship investments

A consumer-centric online community may serve as a strategic communication method that e-tailers use to respond promptly to queries about products, services, or transaction security. This seems particularly apt in an online shopping context because the lack of tactile experience and interpersonal communication limits the consumer’s ability to evaluate a product’s characteristics ex ante [20]. Building an online community thus strengthens consumers’ trust in the e-tailer and also reinforces their feelings about the e-tailer’s friendliness. Therefore, we posit that consumers may make additional relationship-specific investments in the store and form multifaceted ties that make them feel that they ‘‘know’’ the e-tailer at some level. Community participation thus helps encourage more relationship-specific asset investments [19]. Therefore:

H3. Greater levels of community participation are associated with greater levels of relationship investments.

## 2.3.2. Relationship investment and loyalty intention

According to transaction cost theory, specific relationship investment cannot be redeployed easily to other relationships; that is, they create a lock-in situation for the consumer [8,9]. For example, the time and effort invested in getting acquainted with an online store likely cannot be transferred to another store. This relationship encourages consumer retention [8]. Similarly, investments of time, effort, and other non-recoverable resources in a relationship create psychological ties that motivate parties to maintain the relationship. Thus:

H4. Greater levels of relationship investments are associated with greater levels of loyalty intentions.

## 2.3.3. Relationship investment and consumer power

The effect of any relationship-specific investment is to create locked-in conditions, reflecting prior socialization processes between the buyer and the seller [8,9]. For example, consumers who feel locked in to an online store might translate those feelings into a sense of dependence on the e-tailer. Building on prior work, we posit that a dependency situation may cause a power imbalance that may be detrimental to consumers who wish to influence the e-tailer. Therefore:

H5. Relationship investments have a negative impact on consumer power.

## 2.3.4. Community participation and community identification

Several studies have proposed that community identification involves both cognitive self-categorization and affective commitment [2]. Cognitive self-categorization occurs through consumers’ comparisons of their own defining characteristics with those that define the community [6]. Affective commitment takes this process a step further into feelings of attachment and belongingness [2,19]. Consumers who engage in various social activities and events have direct access to other members and can mediate the flow of resources in the community. Thus, participation in community activities makes them feel more like insiders. Therefore:

H6. Greater levels of community participation are associated with greater levels of identification.

## 2.3.5. Identification and loyalty intentions

When consumers identify with and become emotionally attached to a particular community (e.g., one associated with shopping for a brand or at an online store), they are likely to behave differently, e.g., by being open to customer-initiated marketing or more tolerant of lapses, because group identity motivates members to behave in ways consistent with identity maintenance [1].

Identification with a community results in relative immunity to negative publicity and even increased rivalry with and derogation of competitors. In addition, community identification can result in both extra-role behavior (e.g., acting as a consumer advocate and enthusiastic promoter of the store to others) and in-role behavior (e.g., encompassing sustained, long-term preference for a particular store through repeated purchases) [1,2]. Therefore:

H7. Greater levels of identification are associated with greater levels of loyalty intentions.

## 2.3.6. Identification and consumer power

The power of one social actor (e.g., a community member) over another (e.g., an e-tailer) depends on the power sources available to the former. Members with a highly defined sense of community identity may take on leadership roles, become defenders of the community, and be more adversarial toward competing communities [1,4]. Moreover, these members have easy access to other members and can mediate the flow of resources or information in the community. The importance of their role in the community thus strengthens their ability to leverage the community to influence the e-tailer’s decision making. Compared with other buyer–seller situations, community members can more easily use their strength to favor e-tailers with their preferred policies [16,22]. Thus:

H8. Greater levels of identification are associated with greater levels of consumer power.

## 2.4. Moderating effect of perceived e-tailer support

The role of e-tailer support is important in creating and fostering the necessary social climate that can build consumer– retailer relationships. The norm of reciprocity suggests that perceived e-tailer support prompts a sense of obligation to be concerned about the online store’s success [27]. Ahearne et al. [1] argue that when consumers believe that their provider is supportive, they may reciprocate by exhibiting behavior traits, such as enthusiasm for, cooperation with, and endorsement of the provider. Perceived e-tailer support also may fulfill socioemotional needs, which may mitigate the effect of community identification on consumer power. Thus:

H9. The impact of identification on consumer power is stronger for consumers who perceive less support from their e-tailer than for those who perceive more support.

## 3. Research method

For our data collection, we surveyed customers of four wellknown online stores in Taiwan. Each store offered items in similar categories, including general commodities (e.g., clothes, furniture), electronics (e.g., PCs, laptops, cameras), and fashion items (e.g., cosmetics, jewelry). Most of the stores’ customers live in Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, or mainland China, and each store has cultivated an online community to facilitate its customers information exchange. The communities are available worldwide, but the content is in Chinese, so users all likely understand that language. Because the four communities rely on asynchronous discussion boards, their information exchange capabilities are not in real time, though reactions to most posts occur within minutes.

By studying the retailers’ records, we could confirm that the exchanges typically involved customers answering others’ queries, offering suggestions about product usage, presenting their opinions about purchases, providing social support to unhappy members, and occasionally meeting face-to-face. Member-generated content was visible for all members and, through the site’s search engine, easily accessible at all times. As in any other voluntary social organization, the members varied in both their level of participation and their perceptions of e-tailer support.

## 4. Results

We adopted a two-step approach to test the models. First, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis to assess the measurement properties of the reflective latent constructs. Second, we performed structural equation modeling analysis to test the research hypotheses with LISREL 8.54.

## 4.1. Measurement model validation

We assessed our measurement model by evaluating the reliability of the individual items, the internal consistency between items designed to measure the same construct, and the discriminant validity between constructs. To determine the reliability of each item, we examined the significance and magnitude of its loadings. All items loaded significantly on their respective latent factors, and they all achieved standardized loadings of at least 0.60. We used two measures to evaluate the internal consistency of the constructs: composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE). The CRs ranged from 0.73 to 0.92, and the AVEs ranged from 0.58 to 0.79; thus all were above the recommended cutoff levels of 0.60 and 0.50, respectively and exhibited good internal consistency.

## 4.3. Tests of mediation and rival hypotheses

To further assess the model’s validity, we performed formal tests of mediation in the paths from community participation to loyalty and consumer power. Thus, we could determine whether additional direct paths, those not in our model, were significant. We compared the original model with one in which we added two more paths from community participation to loyalty and consumer power. The chi-square values between the two models did not differ significantly $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 4 9 , \Delta d f = 2 , p > 0 . 0 5 )$ , providing further evidence of the robustness of our model.

## 4.4. Moderating influences of perceived e-tailer support

Multiple-group analyses served to test our hypothesis regarding the moderating effect of perceived e-tailer support on the relationship between identification and consumer power. To measure perceived e-tailer support, we adapted a previously developed three-item scale: (1) the e-tailer really cares about consumers’ well-being, (2) help is available from the e-tailer if consumers need a special favor, and (3) the e-tailer is friendly and approachable. Respondents rated these items on seven-point Likert scales ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree. We calculated the composite score of each member and used a median split to identify the respective groups $( N _ { \mathrm { h i g h } } = 4 5 2 ; N _ { \mathrm { l o w } } = 4 6 1 )$ .

To test H9, we built separate structural models for two subsamples and conducted tests of moderation to determine whether the coefficients of the hypothesized paths differed. In a baseline model, we allowed the effect of identification on consumer power to vary across groups. In the second model, we constrained this effect to be equal across subsamples. The path from identification to consumer power weakened in the high-score subsample $\left( \gamma = 0 . 4 9 , \mathrm { S E } = 0 . 0 5 \right)$ compared with the low-score subsample $( \gamma = 0 . 7 4 , \quad \mathrm { S E } = 0 . 1 0 ) ;$ ; thus, H9 was supported $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } = 4 . 8 3 , \Delta d f = 1 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$

## 5. General discussion

Current theory fails to address the mediating mechanisms of the relationships between online community building and its outcomes in online shopping contexts. We showed that online community participation elicits relationship investments and community identification, which in turn influence loyalty intentions and the formation of consumer power. We also investigated external and internal community characteristics related to members’ online community participation (i.e., the online store’s prestige and attribute performance). Finally, we showed that the presence of an e-tailer’s support diminished the effect of community identification on consumer power.

We showed that community identification fully channels the effect of community participation on consumers’ decisions to repurchase, which also offers some support for the argument that members’ identification with a social group enabled by IT occurs primarily through ongoing, inter-member communication. However, our results showed that online community participation significantly enhanced consumer loyalty through relationship investments with the online store. This significant causal chain (community participation ! relationship investments ! loyalty) might arise because, by community building, managers not only gain more knowledge about consumers’ preferences but also can respond promptly to queries and go beyond routine information sharing. This suggests the need to extend existing theories of online customer relationship management by including online community building. Community building offers a critical means to demonstrate customer orientation when the firm’s goal is to encourage repeat purchase behavior [17].

Our study also found that online community participation resulted in the formation of consumer power through relationship investments and community identification. Identification plays an important mediating role in community participation and consumer power, perhaps because members who strongly identify with a community adopt leadership roles and gain easy access to other members and resources. In turn, their increased importance in the community strengthens their ability to influence the etailer’s decision making.

However, the presence of e-tailer support diminishes this effect; successful exchanges with customers require effective, high-quality customer support first. As Vargo and Lusch [25, p. 1] argued, ‘‘all economy is evolving into service economy,’’ so highquality customer support, rather than strong goods production or operations, increases share value. Our results help quantify this paradigm. From a managerial perspective, e-tailers might deliver superior e-service during routine transactions with little or no human contact, but strong customer support and excellent recovery strategies demand a human touch. Online stores should budget and allocate sufficient capital resources to enhance their customer support and offer superior service and recovery capabilities.

Contrary to our expectations, such relationship investments had a direct and positive effect on consumer power. In this situation, consumers perceive their claims on the online store as more legitimate, and thus they may be more assertive and consistent in their demands. Moreover, in virtual markets, consumers who are locked-in play an important value co-creation role for the company, which strengthens their influence on the firm. From another perspective, customers who feel heavily invested in a relationship are more likely than others to feel betrayed if they believe the online store has failed to meet their demands. Customers with high relationship investments devote more time, energy, and money to punish or inconvenience a firm that violates their sense of fairness. If they encourage relationship investments, online stores may inadvertently boost consumers’ power by embedding them deeply in the organization, providing potentially negative effects of community cultivation. In addition, a store’s image exerts the greatest impact on consumer participation in an online community, though this result may reflect the Chinese belief that people ‘‘gain face’’ by participating in a group or interacting with a well-known partner. In online shopping contexts, the lack of direct experience (e.g., by touching products) or interpersonal communication creates information asymmetries that benefit the e-tailer but also may make buyers more wary of seller opportunism [12,21]. Furthermore, this lack of experience may explain why consumers join or participate in an IT-enabled social group [14,15].

To enhance attribute satisfaction, managers might focus on hedonic and utilitarian factors that encourage consumer participation. Perceived enjoyment not only fulfills consumers’ entertainment needs [26] but also results in forward-looking positive emotions, because consumers engage in ‘‘prefactual appraisals’’ by imagining the pleasure of joining in community activities. In terms of utilitarian factors, the degree to which a community offers information that members perceive as useful also enhances attributed satisfaction [21]. A virtual community that serves as a source of information for members’ decision making or task accomplishment has an effect on the member–community relationship [15]. Finally, community openness may be another potential facilitator of attribute satisfaction. Openness creates a friendly atmosphere by allowing consumers to be free of the pressure to behave in certain ways and to express themselves according to their own inner feelings.

## 6. Limitations

This study suffers from the limitations common to any surveybased research. For example, we established that online community participation influences loyalty intentions and consumer power through both relationship investments and community identification in online shopping contexts. However, our examination of the effect of community participation included only a limited number of constructs; others may be important. In addition, we investigated the moderating effect of perceived etailer support, but other variables, such as member experience, also may affect the community identification–consumer power relationship. New members usually have a strong motivation to participate in user groups, perhaps driven by a personal motive, such as wanting to solve a problem encountered when purchasing from the online store. As a user participates, gets to know other members, and interacts with an e-tailer, the nature and extent of those interactions are likely to change. For example, if members believe they have been treated unfairly, they could organize a boycott of the store.
