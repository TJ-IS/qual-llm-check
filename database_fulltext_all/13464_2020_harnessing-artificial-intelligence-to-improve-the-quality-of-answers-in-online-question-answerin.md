# Harnessing Artificial Intelligence to Improve the Quality of Answers in Online Question-answering Health Forums

Reza Mousavi<sup>a</sup>, T.S. Raghu<sup>b</sup>, and Keith Frey<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, Virginia, USA; <sup>b</sup>W. P. Carey School of Business, Arizona State University, Tempe, Arizona, USA; <sup>c</sup>CommonSpirit Health (Dignity Health), Phoenix, Arizona, USA

## ABSTRACT

Quality of answers in health-related community-based question answering (HCQA) forums has been a concern for both users and forum administrators. We conducted a two-phase study to better understand the quality of answers in HCQA forums. First, we employed machine learning to examine the quality of health content. We validated our algorithmic quality ratings by comparing them with those of two physicians. Second, using data from Yahoo! Answers Health section, we examined the efect of the quality of the first answer on the quality of the subsequent answers. Our results suggest that the quality of the subsequent answers is impacted by the quality of the first displayed answer. We further show that the impact of the first displayed answer is larger when the answerers are more familiar with the forum but smaller when the forum provides tips for answering questions. Our study helps HCQA forums to improve the overall quality of answers by 1- creating an algorithmic solution that reliably measures the quality of answers, and 2- adjusting the order of existing answers to encourage higher quality subsequent answers. Our findings also extend the applicability of the order efect to online forums and provide evidence that experienced users would be more influenced by the order efect in such forums.

## KEYWORDS

Healthcare information systems; online communities; Q&A forums; machine learning; text mining; natural language processing (NLP); BERT; deep learning; hierarchical topic modeling; objectivity analysis; classifiers; online forums

## Introduction

Seeking information on the web is now a nearly inevitable part of our lives. According to Google, “around 7 percent of Google’s daily searches [are] health-related” [37]. Given the abundance of health-related resources available online, online diagnosers (users who seek health-related information online) have a variety of choices of where to seek and find information about health conditions. One of these options is a health-related communitybased question answering (HCQA) forum, where users can ask their questions and seek advice that is tailored to their needs from other users of the forum. Direct response to the question, availability, ease of use, emotional support and learning from others [9, 57], and usefulness are among the reasons why online diagnosers use these forums. With more engagement from the users, a wide array of HCQA forums have been emerging in the past two decades. Some of these forums are designed to cover topics related to specific conditions while many other forums cover any health-related topic. Online Supplemental Appendix A reports some of the popular HCQA forums, their main health topics, and their default mechanism for sorting answers. Although using these HCQA forums could help users seek information tailored to their particular needs, the quality of the answer content has been a major concern. For instance, Oh and colleagues [39] found that users rate the quality of health answers in Yahoo! Answers higher than do experts. Ben-Sasson et al. [3] compared the Yahoo! Answers users with clinicians by studying the answers that each group selected as the best answer in a set of questions and answers about autism spectrum disorder (ASD). They discovered that the clinician-selected best answers difered from user-selected best answers. These findings fuel concerns about the usefulness and potential adverse efects of HCQA forum content. Given the sensitive nature of healthrelated answers, HCQA forums should take all possible actions to help improve the quality of the answers that are posted on their forums. As such, they allow the users to select answers that helped them the most. Most of these forums also allow users to comment on other users’ answers. Although these steps may be helpful in improving the quality of the answers, we use analytic methods to suggest a design guidance that may help improve the quality of answers in HCQA forums. We deployed primacy efects from cognitive processing theories to show that the quality of the first answer posted in response to a question may work as an anchor for the quality of the subsequent answers. Therefore, we suggest that HCQA forums could seek answers from a more selective (and capable) pool of answerers to post the first answer and then post the Q&A thread to the public for more answers by other users. These forums could also consider delaying answer displays until a reasonably high-quality answer is posted.<sup>1</sup> They could further adjust their sorting scheme during answer gathering to assure that the highest quality answer, among all of the answers in a Q&A thread, is shown as the first answer. Under this scheme, the subsequent answers that will be posted later would have higher quality according to our findings.

To be able to adopt this design guidance, HCQA forums should be able to automatically determine the quality of the answers. Currently, HCQA forums mostly rely on the judgments of the users themselves to determine the quality of the answers. For instance, Yahoo! Answers enables the user who asked the question to choose the best answer from the answers posted, and enables other users to “thumbs up” (approve) or “thumbs down” (disapprove) each answer. Since this approach still relies on subjective opinions of nonprofessional users, the quality of answers in HCQA forums could largely deviate from that of health care professionals’ opinions. As inappropriate health-related recommendations could have serious efects on advice–takers, reliable measures that scale the quality of answers are necessary.

The quality of answers is a multifaceted construct that may depend on a variety of elements such as accuracy, completeness, relevance of the advice, and the credibility of the source. Previous studies show that the textual features can help in assessing the quality of online answers [16, 18]. Therefore, by employing machine learning and methods borrowed from text analytics, we developed six measures for assessing the quality of the answers posted on HCQA forums. We then externally evaluated the validity of these measures by comparing their evaluations with those of two independent board-certified physicians. We show that the proposed measures of quality can be used to predict the overall quality of the answers.

The next phase of our study is related to the use of the measures to potentially improve the quality of answers. The rationale is that the forums would implement automated quality metrics if there is potential to improve forum content. For instance, sorting the answers based on quality would enable online diagnosers to quickly find the best answer. More importantly, automated quality measurement on online forums can enable system redesign to enhance the quality of the posted answers. Given the lack of research in this area, we strive to answer the following questions:

● Does the quality of the first answer influence the quality of the subsequent answers?

● Is the relationship proposed in the previous question impacted by the level of experience of the answerers (i.e., would experienced answerers be more/less influenced by the first answer)?

● Can HCQA forums improve the quality of answers by providing helpful suggestions to users?

● What type of role HCQA forums can play in the relationship between the quality of the first answer and the quality of the subsequent answers?

To answer the research questions, we utilize the proposed quality measures to estimate the efect of the quality of the first answer on the quality of the subsequent answers. We provide evidence that the efect of the quality of the first answer over the subsequent answers would be greater if the answerer has more experience with the forum (answered more questions in the past). Furthermore, we show that HCQA forums can control the extent to which the first answer influences the subsequent answers by providing tips and suggestions to answerers. Furthermore, we create a unique set of duplicate questions by training a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network and check the robustness of our analysis.

Our research contributes to the information systems (IS) literature on online communities that investigates collaborative content creation [9, 21] and the emerging research on Q&A forums [26]. The findings in this study open a new line of investigation into the influence of contribution ordering on content quality in online forums. We introduce and validate approaches to measuring answer quality that lend themselves to automated deployment in online communities.

The organization of this paper is as follows. The next section reviews the current literature related to the quality of answers in online forums and presents the arguments for the hypotheses. We present our data and variables in the next section. We describe the empirical model and present descriptive statistics along with the results of our analyses in the following section. We then discuss our findings and lay out the implications of this study. Finally, we discuss the limitations and potential extensions of this study.

## Literature Review

## Evaluating the Quality of Answers

Initial attempts to evaluate the quality of answers in community-based question answering (CQA) forums relied on human intelligence. The first mechanism for evaluating answers was introduced by CQA forums themselves. In this approach, the CQA forum enables the asker to declare if she is satisfied with the given answers. In most CQA forums, the asker can select one answer as the best answer. As an extension to this approach, CQA forums introduced voting mechanisms through which users other than the asker can evaluate the usefulness of the answers. In most CQA forums, users can “thumbs up” (approve) or “thumbs down” (disapprove) the answers. Both of these approaches are part of a design paradigm called social computing, which is defined as “a computational integration of social studies and human social dynamics together with the design and use of ICT technologies in a social context.” [54, p. 79]

In subsequent attempts to evaluate the quality of the answers in CQA forums, the social computing paradigm has applied more rigorous approaches. For instance, Shah and Pomerantz [49] asked Amazon Mechanical Turk workers to rate the quality of a set of answers taken from Yahoo! Answers. In this approach, human intelligence is making judgments about the quality of the answers. Along with social computing, researchers have also looked at the features of the answers and the answerers to evaluate the quality. Jeon et al. [25] studied the efects of several factors including the length of the answers, the number of the answers in a given category, and the number of previous best answers by the answerer on the quality of the answer. Otterbacher [40] studied consumer reviews on Amazon.com by using 22 measures related to textual properties, authors’ reputations, and product characteristics. Overall, this stream of research relies on social computing, textual cues, and non-textual cues such as answerers’ reputation to evaluate the quality of the answers.

The next wave of studies that evaluated the quality of the answers in CQA forums deviated from the social computing paradigm and employed machine-learning techniques to assess the answers given in CQA forums. This recent set of studies has used text mining methods to automatically detect the answers that could be candidates for best answers. Agichtein et al. [2] used textual features, user information, and usage statistics to evaluate the quality of the answers posted on Yahoo! Answers. Liu et al. [33] employed Latent Dirichlet Allocation model to predict the best answers for new questions in CQA forums. Hu et al. [23] defined the problem of detecting a high-quality answer as a classification task and employed a multimodal Deep Belief Nets approach to perform the classification. This approach includes two steps. In the first step, a specially-designed deep network is developed to learn the unified representation using both textual and non-textual information. In the second step, the outputs of the previous step are used as inputs for a classifier to make predictions. In another study by Yao et al. [58], the quality of the answers is tied to the quality of the questions. Yao and colleagues developed algorithms that use the votes on the questions and answers as indicators of their quality.

The textual quality of user-generated content has been measured and deployed in IS research. Online Supplemental Appendix B reports a representative sample of IS studies that deployed text-based constructs. In this research, we therefore utilize a range of feasible measures to identify textual quality in HCQA forums.

## Efects of First Answer’s Quality

Our main research question is focused on the potential impact of the quality of the first answer on the quality of the subsequent answers. Although there is a glaring lack of studies that examine the potential impacts of sorting answers on the quality of the subsequent answers, psychology literature has long been studying the efects of item orders on the quality of survey responses [29]. Such efects are called “order efects” and have been reportedly observed during survey administrations. For instance, in a 2008 experiment by Pew Research center, when people were asked, “All in all, are you satisfied or dissatisfied with the way things are going in this country today?” immediately after having been asked, “Do you approve or disapprove of the way George W. Bush is handling his job as president?” 88 percent said they were dissatisfied, compared to 78 percent without the context of the prior question.<sup>2</sup>

Another form of order efects is observed in response alternatives [22]. According to Lavrakas [31], “[R]esponse order efect occurs when the distribution of responses to a closed-ended survey question is influenced by the order in which the response options are ofered to respondents” (31, p. 95). In general, there are two types of response order efects: primacy and recency [29]. Primacy refers to the phenomenon that occurs when the placement of a response alternative as the first alternative increases its likelihood of being selected. Recency refers to the case where placing a response alternative as the last alternative increases its likelihood to be selected [22]. Research shows that when choices are presented visually, the primacy efect is to be expected [29, 52]. There are two major reasons for the primacy efect. First, an alternative that is presented as the first option may establish a cognitive framework or standard for comparison that guides the interpretation of subsequent items. Second, an alternative that is presented first is subject to a deeper cognitive processing and therefore might occupy the mind of the respondent even when s/he is reading the subsequent alternatives. In other words, the first items “sufer less competition for time and space in immediate memory from other items” [46, p. 188]. Due to the latter efect, the respondent is less stimulated by the subsequent answers and may select them less frequently.

Another evidence supporting the importance of the first item in a list of items is provided in Galesic et al. [17]. Using eye-tracking technology to record the eye movements of online survey respondents, Galesic and colleagues observed what respondents did and did not look at while they answered questions. Their results suggest that respondents do in fact spend more time looking at the first few options in a list than subsequent options in the list. This study provides evidence for the previous claims that were made about the primacy efect.

Based on these studies, we propose that the first answer in HCQA forums would serve as anchor point for the subsequent answers. That is, the providers of the subsequent answers would rely partly on the content of the first answer when they provide their own answers. Therefore, we suggest that the quality of the first answer could positively influence the quality of the subsequent answers:

Hypothesis 1: Ceteris paribus, the quality of the first answer positively influences the quality of the subsequent answer(s) for a given question in a HCQA forum.

Given that HCQA forums often require users to register and create a user profile before they can post an answer, these forums would have some information about the answerers and their past activities within the forum. Therefore, it would be important for the forums to understand the potential moderating impacts of answerers’ characteristics on the relationship between the quality of the first answer and the quality of the subsequent answers. Previous research in this area has shown some potential impacts of answerers age [27] and education [28] on the order efect. The order efects in these instances manifest due to limitations of cognitive efort and comprehension. When such limitations exist, respondents are likely to exhibit satisficing behavior by relying more on cues and would be more impacted by the first item in the list (primacy efect). In other words, respondents are susceptible to response order efects.

To decide which moderating efects we can study in HCQA forums, we took a closer look at the type of information most of these forums collect. We can divide this information into two categories:

(1) Information collected at the time of registration with the forum

(2) Information about the past experience of the users within the forum

For instance, Yahoo Answers! would require a registration that asks for user’s gender, birthday, and phone number. WebMD requires a registration that asks for birthday and email address. healthcaremagic.com requires an email address for registration. It is evident that the amount of information requested at the time of registration varies across HCQA forums. Furthermore, it is not clear whether the users will provide accurate information at the time of registration and the HCQA forums have no known means to verify the accuracy of the information provided by users. Given that the users’ information at the time of registrations is limited, varies across forums, and may not be accurate, we do not intend to study the impact of such information on the relationship proposed in H1. Yet, we argue that since these HCQA forums require a type of registration and create a profile for the users, they can (and do) track the users’ activities within the forum. In particular, they can track how many previous answers or questions a user has posted. Such tracking could let the HCQA forum to develop a proxy for users’ experience level with the forum. For instance, all forums can determine, among the users, who are more experienced users and who are quite new to the forum. Given that forums track such information, we aim to understand if the quality of the first answer will influence experienced answerers (those who have posted answers in the forum in the past) more/less when they are posting their new answers. In addition to the empirical implications of the study of users’ experience in HCQA forums, such a study could help us extend the order efect literature that was originally established in survey design literature. In particular, survey respondents are not often given the chance to gain experience with the survey instrument, and therefore, the respondents’ past experience with the survey instrument was not a relevant question in that literature. However, in online forums, the users can repeatedly post their answers and gain experience. Hence, we believe that studying the potential impact of answerers’ past experience (repeated use of the forum) on the primacy efect is a relevant topic. In particular, this study would expand our understanding of order efects in online forums by comparing the extent to which experienced answerers and new users are impacted by the primacy efect.<sup>3</sup>

To lay out our study of the potential impact of past experience on the primacy efect, we borrow from the priming literature. According to Dennis et al. [12], priming is “the presentation of a stimulus designed to sub-consciously implant a concept in working memory that alters subsequent behavior.” Previous studies have shown that priming can influence users’ behaviors in recommender systems [1], consumers’ product choice [8], users’ privacy concerns [7], product advertising [47], advance selling [38], and brand positioning [14]. One particular type of priming is repetition priming, which is defined as “nonconscious form of memory that is accompanied by reductions in neural activity when an experience is repeated” [55]. Although priming has been widely studied in social science, repetition priming has not received similar attention. Repetition priming suggests that repeating a task will eventually require less neural activity to complete the task. In other words, repetition priming refers to “performance facilitation typically observed when a processing event is repeated.” [56] According to the psychology literature, practice is one of the main drivers of repetition priming [51]. When subjects practice a task, they learn how to find cues to perform the tasks using less neural activity [42]. When answering a question, such cues can be found in the question or the previous answers (specifically in the first answer in a list of answers since the first answer has the highest visibility). If, as per H1, subsequent answerers are impacted by the first answer, experienced answerers have been impacted by the first answer more frequently than novice answerers. Therefore, with the expectation that experienced answerers are more prone to repetition priming, we propose:

Hypothesis 2: Ceteris paribus, the impact of the quality of the first answer on the quality of the subsequent answer(s) is positively moderated by the answerer’s experience in the HCQA forum.

Aside from changing the mechanism for sorting the answers, HCQA forums could also provide helpful tips to their users to help them improve their content. For instance, the forums could deploy automated systems for checking the spelling and grammar of the answers as the users post content. Therefore, we are interested in examining if these tips would result in higher quality of answers. Intuitively, we expect that these tips would help users provide better answers. More importantly, we argue that the system features may (unintentionally) distract the answerer from the anchoring efect of the first answer. The distractive nature of the impact is akin to bufers employed in surveys to prevent order efects. Bufers are unrelated items that are presented between potentially afecting items and the subsequent items [6]. For instance in the example we mentioned earlier, we could add bufer questions (some items that are not related to the original items in the survey) between “Do you approve or disapprove of the way George W. Bush is handling his job as president?” and “All in all, are you satisfied or dissatisfied with the way things are going in this country today?” to minimize the impact of the first item on the subsequent item. The reasoning behind this strategy is that the intervening content will eliminate or at least significantly reduce the salience triggered by the first item. As such, if we introduce a series of “irrelevant” items then the respondent may forget the earlier part of the survey and it therefore will not “prime” subsequent responses [30]. When the HCQA provides tips and suggestions about writing style, spelling, and grammar to users (which occurs when the users are writing their answers which likely is after they were exposed to the first answer), these tips would serve as bufers and therefore reduce the impact of the first answer on the answer the answerer is about to post. Hence, we propose:

Hypothesis 3: Ceteris paribus, the positive impact of the quality of the first answer on the quality of the subsequent answer(s) is negatively moderated by the HCQA forum’s tips.

In order to test our hypotheses, we utilize a comprehensive data set that we obtained from Yahoo! Answers. We also collected data from other sources to augment Yahoo! Answers data. These sources include a professional healthcare Q&A website called askthedoctor.com and the National Library of Medicine (NLM). We used the data from askthedoctor.com as the labeled data that we used for building a classifier, while we used the data from NLM for health-related keywords. The details of our data collection, data processing, and data analyses are reported in the next sections. Figure 1 illustrates the steps we took throughout this process. We believe that the steps in Figure 1 can be replicated in other community-based Q&A forums. This process can also be automated and used by practitioners to generate desired outcomes.

## Data and Variables

## Yahoo! Answers

One of the pioneers of CQA forums is Yahoo! Answers. Launched in June 2005, it still remains a top 10 global site in web trafic as of 2019. Yahoo! Answers is a question & answering forum that enables users to ask questions and post answers to other users questions. Although the actual number of Yahoo! Answers users is not reported, a few years after its launch, it was estimated that Yahoo! Answers dominated the online Q&A forums by gaining 74.05% of the overall market share [24]. The following user of Yahoo! Answers provides a good explanation of its popularity among users seeking health advice:

“MOST people have the same problems with their health yr after yr after yr and are tired of going through this process. This is why they research their symptoms on the Web to see if MAYBE someone some where has the same thing and they can show the doctor so he might prescribe correctly or run tests to check and make sure it is right, then get the patient on the proper meds once and for all.”<sup>4</sup>

The health section of Yahoo! Answers (summarized in Online Supplemental Appendix C) has been the source of data in a variety of other HCQA forum studies [3, 5]. We collected all of the Q&As in Yahoo! Answers health section from July $7 ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ 2005 to June 2<sup>nd</sup> 2014. However, since Yahoo! Answers changed its answer sorting mechanism in March $2 0 ^ { \mathrm { t h } } 2 0 1 4 ^ { 5 }$ , we decided to include the data before this date in our analysis. This data set includes information about the questions (the content of the question, the date and time the question was posted, the topic of the question, and user id of the asker) and the answers (the content of the answer, the date and time the answer was posted, the user id of the respondent, and whether the answer was chosen as the best answer by the asker). During the period of our data (July 2005 through March 2014), Yahoo! Answers’ default sorting mechanism was chronological sorting<sup>6</sup>. That is, the first answer was shown on the top and the subsequent answers would be posted under the older answers. One of the common policies in online Q&A forums is closed-form policy. In the closed-form policy, once a question is posted, other users can answer it. When the person who asked the question is satisfied with one of the answers, s/he can close the question thread by selecting one of the answers as the best answer. The question and all of its answers will be available on the forum for future use but no one can provide a new answer after the asker selects the best answer. In this setting, the respondents are not aware of the best answer at the time of posting their answers, as the asker has not yet selected the best answer. Hence, the respondents see all answers in chronological order.<sup>7</sup>

## Measuring the Quality of Answers

To be able to test our hypotheses, we first need to examine the quality of answers. As noted previously, quality is a multifaceted concept. Given that we are interested in measuring quality in the textual form, we conducted an extensive literature review to identify diferent textual constructs that could potentially reflect the quality of answers (Online Supplemental Appendix B). To be able to verify if we were able to measure the quality of the answers, we needed to compare our quality scores with reliable, external quality scores. Therefore, we utilized two board certified medical doctors to independently score a random sample of 205 answers drawn from answers posted between March 2014 and June 2014. Below, we explain how we developed the constructs used to create an algorithmic approach to assess the quality of answers.

## Quality Measure 1: Depth of the Answer

The number of characters in the answer can indicate how much efort the answerer has put into answering the question. The depth (number of characters or the number of words) has been used in previous IS studies as well [19, 35]. Therefore, we use the depth of the answer as indicator of quality. To measure depth, we simply sum the number of characters in the answer.

## Quality Measure 2: Readability of the Answer

To evaluate the readability of the answers, we employed three commonly used measures of readability: Flesch Reading Ease (FRE), Gunning-Fog (GF), and Dale-Chall.<sup>8</sup> These measures assess the readability of an English text and have been previously used in IS literature [4, 18, 19, 50]. Higher FRE scores indicate that the material is easier to read. However, higher GF and Dale-Chall scores indicate that the material is more complex to read. Therefore, we inversed the FRE scores and then normalized all three scores to range from 0 to 1. Then we took the mean of the three normalized scores as the final readability score. A readability score close to 1 indicates that the material is more complex and reading it requires years of education. A readability score close to zero indicates that the material is easy to read even without significant education. We believe that the higher quality answers are most likely generated by answerers with higher levels of education. Therefore, their answers should also have a higher readability score (more complex to read). For instance, a medical answer that is created by a physician is more complex to read than an answer created by a high school graduate.

## Quality Measure 3: Objectivity of the Answer

According to Liu [32], textual information can be broadly categorized into facts (objective) and opinions (subjective). Given that medical recommendations should be based on objective facts rather than subjective opinions, a measure of objectivity of the text would help to assess the quality of the answers. Objectivity is found to be a good indicator of quality in the context of product reviews as well [18, 19, 20].

To measure the extent to which a review is objective, we used a Python package called “TextBlob”. This package uses a Naïve Bayes analyzer that is developed using Stanford University’s Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK). According to Loria [34], TextBlob first determines which tokens (words) would carry any sentiment (positive or negative). For instance, adjectives such as angry or cheerful would have a higher level of sentiment. Then it determines the likelihood of the sentence being more subjective (having more sentiment) or objective (having less sentiment). TextBlob has been used in empirical research previously [44, 45]. TextBlob’s subjectivity scores range from 0 to 1, with 0 being the least subjective (most objective) and 1 being the most subjective (least objective) document. Since we are interested in measuring the objectivity of answers, we converted the subjectivity scores obtained from TextBlob to objectivity scores using the following formula: Objectivity = 1 - Subjectivity. Therefore, our objectivity score also ranges from 0 to 1 with 0 being the least objective answer and 1 being the most objective answer.

## Quality Measure 4: Predicted Professional Answer (Classification)

In this approach, we determined which answers were similar to answers provided by a health care professional (e.g. doctors). Since the occupations or afiliations of the respondents are not known in Yahoo! Answers forum, we utilized supervised machine learning and state of the art natural language processing (NLP) methods such as Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) to build a classifier that could predict the probability of an answer being similar to an answer provided by a health care professional. Online Supplemental Appendix E provides a detailed description of our approach in developing this quality measure. The output of this quality measure is a binary class label (Professional/Non-professional) for each answer. We allocated a score of 1 to the answers that our classifier predicted to belong to the Professional collection and 0 to others. The logic is that if the answer belongs to the Professional class, it is similar to an answer provided by a health care professional and therefore is more likely to have higher quality. The answer will be considered a low-quality answer when it belongs to the Non-professional class.

## Quality Measure 5: Medical Terms (Keywords) Density

It is fair to assume that a medical text generated by a health care professional may have a higher ratio of count of medical keywords to count of total words. Therefore, an answer that resembles a professional answer is likely to have higher ratio of medical terms (or keywords). To count the number of medical terms used in each answer, we collected 27,455 medical terms from the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH®) Thesaurus. MeSH® Thesaurus is a controlled vocabulary produced by the National Library of Medicine (NLM) and is used for indexing, cataloging, and searching biomedical and healthrelated information and documents. The 2015 [version of] MeSH® includes the subject descriptors appearing in MEDLINE®/PubMed®, the NLM catalog database, and other NLM databases [53]. To find the medical terms in Yahoo! Answers data, we needed to check the presence of each medical term in each answer by comparing all of the medical terms with each word in each answer. Since such a task could be computationally expensive, we decided to employ Apache Spark™ which provides a scalable engine for large-scale data processing.<sup>9</sup> We used Scala programming language to hire a multi-node Spark™ cluster to determine the medical terms density scores for each answer. Intuitively the answers with a higher density of medical terms should have a higher quality.

## Quality Measure 6: Topic Similarity

For any answer to be considered a high-quality answer, it needs to be relevant to the question. To measure the relevance of an answer to a question, we first determine the main topics discussed in the question and the main topics discussed in the answer. Then we measure the similarity of these two sets of topics. To determine the main topics that were used in the questions and answers, we intended to use Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA). One main issue in LDA is the determination of the number of topics (usually referred to as k). There are diferent approaches for determining k. We decided to use a variation of LDA that does not require determining k. This method is called Hierarchical Dirichlet Process (HDP).<sup>10</sup> We used Python package “gensim” to perform HDP on our data. We used the default parameters in “genism”. For a text document, “gensim” will provide the list of topics as well as the weight of each word in the document per topic. After determining the list of topics for each question and answer, we used gensim’s cosine similarity function (“cossim”), which returns the cosine similarity between two sparse vectors, to measure the similarity between the answer and the question. This similarity score is a number between -1 and 1 (higher is more similar). Intuitively, a high-quality answer should be about the same topic(s) that was (were) discussed in the question. Therefore, higher similarity scores are desirable. 11

## Evaluating the Quality Measures

To evaluate the validity of our quality measure, we had to compare our quality ratings with a reliable, external quality rating. To do so, we utilized two physicians to rate the quality of a randomly drawn sample of answers. Then we compared their evaluations with the quality measure that we proposed. We report the details of this section in Online Supplemental Appendix F. After comparing the quality scores obtained from our algorithmic approach with those obtained from physicians, we conclude that our proposed quality measure is a good indicator of the quality of the answers provided in Yahoo! Answers health section.

## Robustness Check

Even though we have controlled for the quality of the questions and their topics in our models, one may argue that the quality of the answers (subsequent answers as well as the first answers) primarily depend on the quality and other characteristics of the questions. For instance, one may argue that questions that seek personal advice would receive totally diferent answers than do questions that seek objective medical advice. Although our eforts in controlling for such efects could have partially accounted for them, we still need to show what would have happened if the same question received diferent quality first answers. If the quality of the first answer indeed impacts the quality of the subsequent answers, we should be able to show that the quality of the subsequent answers for the same question would still depend on the quality of the first answer. The ideal way to examine such relationship is an experimental setting in which the researcher posts the same question two times followed by posting a high-quality answer for first question and a low-quality answer for the second (identical/duplicate) question. Then the researcher would be able to collect the subsequent answers posted by subjects (i.e. Yahoo! Answers users) under each one of the two Q&A threads (Figure 2). This experimental setting would tease out any potential impact derived from the questions. Given the size of data in our study, we realized that it is possible to see the same question posted more than once. Therefore, if we could identify pairs of duplicate questions in our data, we can identify a natural quasi-experimental setting without running a new experiment.

In this approach, we first need to find pairs of duplicate questions and then test if the quality of the subsequent answers is a function of the quality of the first answer for each pair. To detect duplicate questions in our data set and hence discover a natural quasiexperimental setting, we deployed a Siamese recurrent architecture which is an adaptation of Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network [36]. In this model, the semantic similarity between sentences will be assessed. This model can outperform carefully handcrafted features and other proposed neural network systems of greater complexity [36]. Given that this model is a supervised learning model, we have to train it using labeled data (duplicate pairs of questions). Fortunately, such training data set has been published by another Q&A forum called Quora. The data set includes over 400,000 pairs of questions and labels that indicate if the two questions within each pair are duplicate questions.<sup>15</sup> We deployed Keras deep learning API to train the LSTM network.<sup>16</sup> We used two-third of the original labeled data for training and the remaining one-third for validating the model. Our model had an accuracy of 84.12 percent on the Quora validation set. This level of accuracy is comparable to other models that were trained over the same training data set [15]. Furthermore, we created an additional sample of labeled pairs of questions using Yahoo! Answers data and evaluated the performance of the classifier using this sample. The results of this additional analysis are reported in Online Supplemental Appendix G. After training and validating the model, we applied it to our own questions data set to find the pairs of duplicate questions in our data set. We identified 742 pairs of questions that the model labeled as duplicate with 95 percent or higher confidence. We also manually checked the identified pairs to assure the classifier identified the duplicate pairs correctly. The next step was to check if the quality of the subsequent answers would be a function of the quality of the first answers for each pair of duplicate questions. To do so, we used the data presented in Table 3. For each pair of duplicate questions, we took the diference between the first answers (x) and the second answers (y). The distributions of x and y are shown in Figure 3. Then we used a linear ordinary least squares regression to estimate the coeficient for x.

## Discussion

## Measuring the Textual Quality of Answers

The quality of health-related content in online websites has been an important topic in recent years. Perhaps the pioneering study in this domain was conducted by Culver et al. [11]. They performed a study in which they examined the medical information provided in a health-related online discussion group, in terms of the professional status of the individuals providing information, the consistency of the information with standard medical practice, and the nature of the evidence cited in support of specific claims or recommendations. Their findings suggest that medical information available in online discussion groups may come from non-professionals, may be unconventional, may be based on limited evidence, and/or may be inappropriate. In a more recent study, Chung et al. [10] assessed the accuracy of the infant sleep safety recommendations provided on online websites. Chung and colleagues found that 43.5 percent of the 1300 reviewed websites provided accurate information about infant sleep safety, while almost 28 percent of the websites provided inaccurate information. Fairly similar results were reported in Scullard et al. [48]. In their study, Scullard and colleagues assessed the reliability and accuracy of medical advices that are returned by Google search engine. After analyzing the content of 500 websites that provided advice about children’s health, Scullard and colleagues discovered that 49 percent of the websites failed to answer the question, 11 percent provided incorrect information, and 39 percent provided accurate and reliable advice. Government websites were found to be the most accurate sources of information for health-related questions. In a similar study, Quinn et al. [43] examined the accuracy of information about breast cancer by analyzing the content of 500 websites. They found that 42 percent of the websites provided information that was inapplicable to the question asked. However, the accuracy of the applicable suggestions hinged around 80 percent.

Perhaps the most relevant study is a 2012 study by Oh et al. [39]. They assessed the quality of online health answers in Yahoo! Answers by relying on the ratings of three groups of participants: librarians, nurses, and users of Yahoo! Answers. Their findings indicate that there was a significant diference between those two expert groups (librarians and nurses) and users. Librarians and nurses rated the quality of answers significantly lower on most of the evaluation criteria than did the users [39]. Regarding the accuracy of the answers provided, nurses and librarians gave an overall rating of 2.6 (on a scale of 1-5) to the answers, while the users gave a 3.6 rating.

By borrowing from machine learning and text analytics domains, we were able to reliably measure the quality of answers in a typical HCQA forum. Being able to algorithmically and automatically measure the quality of answers in HCQA forums can have immense implications for the forums and their users. If the forums decide to measure and disclose the quality of answers posted on their forums, the users then will be able to find the answers that are likely to be more reliable. This would save them time and efort in finding the right (best possible in the forum) answer for their question. Adoption of such system by the forum would also motivate the answerers to put more efort in providing better answers given that the forum would measure the quality of their answers. This may decrease the number of answers posted on these forums, but it would encourage users to post better answers. For the online diagnosers (consumers of the answers), this is a good setup as they would find better answers without navigating through a number of not so good answers. Eventually, the forum would be able to build a reputation for good quality answers and therefore would be more attractive for the online diagnosers.

## Order Efects

According to the survey design literature, the order of the questions in a survey or the order of the alternatives in response to survey questions may influence the quality of responses. The two types of order efects are identified as primacy efects and recency efects. The primacy efect is often present when a visual format is used to present the questions or alternatives. The recency efect is usually present when a verbal format is used when administering surveys. Since HCQA forums present the questions and answers in visual form, it is expected that the primacy efect would be present. That is, the first answer in the list serves as an anchor for the subsequent answers. Therefore, we argue that the quality of the first answer has a direct relationship with the quality of the subsequent answers. Our results confirmed the presence of the primacy efect by revealing a direct relationship between the quality of the first answer and the quality of the subsequent answers, even after controlling for question-specific, category-specific, and time-specific factors. There are two explanations for the primacy efect. First, an alternative that is presented as the first option may establish a cognitive framework or standard for comparison that guides the interpretation of later items. Second, an alternative that is presented first is subjected to a deeper cognitive processing and therefore might occupy the answerer’s mind even when s/he is reading the subsequent alternatives. In other words, the first alternatives “sufer less competition for time and space in immediate memory from other items” [46, p. 188]. Due to this latter efect, the answerer is less stimulated by the subsequent answers and may select them less frequently [29, 52].

Furthermore, we tested the moderating impact of the answerers experience with the forum on the primacy efect. According to the repetition priming paradigm, repeating tasks (practice) could result in improved performance [56]. Therefore, we argue that if indeed the answerers are impacted by the primacy efect, the answerers who have had past experience with the focal HCQA forum would be even more impacted by the primacy efect due to the repetition priming efect. Our results confirmed our second hypothesis. That is, compared to new answerers, experienced answerers will be more impacted by the quality of the first answer when they are posting their own answers. The average marginal efect of a small increase in the quality of the first answer can increase the quality of the subsequent answers 8.5 percent more for the most experienced answerer (maximum value of Answerer\_Experience) than it does for the least experienced answerer (minimum value of Answerer\_Experience). We also examined whether assisting the answerers by providing tips would negatively moderate the relationship between the quality of the first answer and the quality of the subsequent answers. Per results reported in Table 2, the overall quality of the subsequent answers improved after the launch of the system. More importantly, the negative and significant coeficient for the interaction term in Model 3 indicates that the impact of the quality of the first answer on the subsequent answers, although still positive and significant, diminished after the launch of the system. The average marginal efect of the interaction term in Model 3 is equal to -0.020. This means that the average marginal efect of a small increase in the quality of the first answer can increase the quality of the subsequent answers 2 percent less when tips were enabled (Tips\_Enabled = 1) than it does when tips were not enabled (Tips\_Enabled = 0).

## Limitations

To provide an appropriate context for the interpretation of the results, we identify some key limitations of our study. First, we assumed that answerers read the previous answers before posting their own answers. If the answerers do not read the previous answers (the first answer in particular), the order efect would not be present in this data. Given that our analysis revealed a significant order efect present in Yahoo! Answers data (and given that the answers were chronologically listed during our study period), we believe that our assumption is a reasonable assumption. Our results could change depending on what percentage of answerers read the previous answer(s) before posting their own answer. If the majority of the answerers read the previous answer(s), then the estimates are unbiased. If only a small portion of the answerers read the previous answer(s), then the estimates will reflect the lower bound of the impact of the order efects. Second, we used the same measures of quality for all health categories in Yahoo! Answers. We acknowledge that some health categories may require more jargon-heavy answers than other health categories (e.g., answers related to cancer versus answers related to skin care). An extension of our study could refine these measures of quality based on specific health categories. For example, the weights of the six measures could be adjusted based on the health category. Third, when we built the classifier based on Yahoo! Answers data, we assumed that healthcare professionals do not post a health-related question on Yahoo! Answers. Although unlikely, it would be possible that healthcare professionals seek health-related information on Yahoo! Answers and post a question on this platform. We believe that the assumption is reasonable given that physicians form a very small percentage of the overall population of users seeking answers on Yahoo! Answers. Fourth, in classifying professional and non-professional answers, we relied on two diferent data sources. Any biases in the sources could cause the classifier to learn the diferences in the datasets rather than content diferences. We attempted to address this issue by having two physicians rate the answers professionally and then testing the classifier performance on this data. Although the performance of the classifier on this dataset declines to some extent, it still performs adequately well in separating professional and non-professional answers.

## Conclusion, Implications, and Future Research

Despite the innovative approaches in using text analytics to solve a variety of business problems [13, 59], measuring the quality of health content in health-related communitybased question answering (HCQA) forums has been a challenging task that has attracted many scholars from a variety of disciplines. Previous studies employed a variety of cues to evaluate the quality of the answers. However, the majority of these factors were based on non-professional evaluations of the users. In a few studies in which professional judgment was used to evaluate the quality of answers in HCQA forums, the procedure was not scalable to be employed for automatic evaluation of answers’ quality. To respond to this gap, we borrowed from machine learning and text mining literature to construct a classifier to automatically detect the similarity of a given answer to a professional answer. We also employed Apache Spark to develop another measure of quality, the density of the medical terms, which is defined by the ratio of the count of medical terms in an answer to the total count of terms (words). Furthermore, we deployed objectivity analysis to determine the objectivity of the answers and also deployed hierarchical topic modeling and cosine similarity to determine how relevant the answers are to the questions. We used traditional readability scores and the number of words in the answers as other indicators of quality. We then validated the six measures of quality by comparing their results with those created by two independent physicians.

In the second part of our study, we showed that the quality measures introduced in this study can be employed by HCQA forums to automatically evaluate the quality of the answers and the questions right after they are posted. These forums could adjust the order of the answers to motivate the desired outcome(s). If the forum’s objective is to encourage higher quality answers, a descending order of the answers based on their quality would help to achieve this goal. Presenting the highest quality answer as the first answer would typically lead to higher-quality answers and therefore may attract more online diagnosers to consume the resources provided in HCQA forums. Given the important role of the first answer in a Q&A thread, the forums could first seek answers from a select group of high-quality answerers. These high-quality answerers could be identified based on a manual evaluation of the answerers (for instance, the forums could hire healthcare professionals to provide the first answers) or based on an automated process. One example of such automated process can be identifying high-quality answerers based on the quality of their previous answers. We discovered that the top 1 percent of answers in terms of quality was posted by 1.3 percent of the answerers in Yahoo! Answers. We also found that more experienced answerers are more likely to post high-quality answers. The details of this analysis are provided in Online Supplemental Appendix I.

Our finding with regard to the third hypothesis could have important implications for the HCQA forums. For instance, if the first answer is the only answer and it is not a highquality answer, the forum could focus on providing more tips to the subsequent answerers to improve the quality of the subsequent answers. Conversely, if the first answer is indeed a high-quality answer, the forum can opt not to provide further tips to impact the quality of the subsequent answers.

With respect to the utility of online diagnosers, if possible, presentation of the highest-quality answer as the best answer would be desirable. Online diagnosers would have access to high-quality answers without spending time reading numerous answers by browsing the pages up and down. That is, for them, there are only a few high-quality answers to read. This way, the online diagnosers would find a better answer in a shorter period of time.

Although our study was about health topics, similar measures of quality could be developed for other CQA forums and be used for sorting the answers. For instance, in recent years, Yahoo! Answers started to use a new scoring system called “Relevance” to rate and order the answers for all of the topics available on its forum. Another application of machine learning algorithms in online forums could be found in stock message boards where the opinions of financial institutions could be perceived as professional collection and the regular users’ comments could form the non-professional collection. The suggestions provided by users for each stock could then be sorted based on the similarity of the suggestions to the professional collection. Aside from the practical implications for the online forums, this study has several implications from a theoretical perspective. First, although the order efect has been tested in an environment where the user had to choose an option from the possible alternatives, it has not been tested in a situation where the user needs to generate his/her own answer. Our findings therefore extend the applicability of the order efect to the online forums. Second, this study provides evidence that experienced users would be more influenced by the order efect. This finding extends our knowledge of the primacy efect by adding the experience of the audience as a factor in the equation. We discovered that the primacy efect could be augmented when the user is experienced with the forum. Given that almost all of the forums we reviewed allow only registered users to post content, the users’ past experience is already available to the forums. Therefore, the forums can factor in the users (answerers’) past experience when sorting the answers in the forum. Last, but not least, the forums can adjust the impact of the first answer on the subsequent answers by providing tips and suggestions.

This research can be extended in future. Primarily, a comparison between the labels from the output of our classifier (Quality Measure 4) with answers chosen as best answers by Yahoo! Answers’ users (Table 4) revealed that 3.24 percent of all answers were labeled as professional by our classifier and were also chosen as best answers by Yahoo! Answers users, while 77 percent of all answers were not best answers and were also labeled as nonprofessional by our classifier. Overall, the classifier has about 80 percent agreement with the Yahoo! Answers’ users.

However, the algorithm may be more discriminating in identifying professional answers. That is, 17.13 percent of the answers were selected as best answers but the algorithm labeled them as non-professional, while 2.65 percent of the answers were labeled as professional but not selected as best answers. This diference suggests that users’ chosen answers (best answers) are not necessarily professional answers. This finding is aligned with the findings in [3], where the researchers discovered that the clinicianselected best answers difered from user-selected best answers. We believe that studying the reasons for this discrepancy between users and the algorithmic solution could be an interesting extension of this research that may inform designing AI-powered solutions. Given the proliferation of these solutions, such studies would be timely.
