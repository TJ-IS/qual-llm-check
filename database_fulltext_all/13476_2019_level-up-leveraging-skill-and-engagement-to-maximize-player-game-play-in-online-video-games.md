# “Level Up”: Leveraging Skill and Engagement to Maximize Player Game-Play in Online Video Games

Yan Huang,<sup>a</sup> Stefanus Jasin,<sup>b</sup> Puneet Manchanda<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Tepper School of Business, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 15213; <sup>b</sup> Ross School of Business, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, Michigan 48109

Abstract. We propose a novel two-stage data-analytic modeling approach combining theories, statistical analysis, and optimization techniques to model player engagement as a function of motivation to maximize customer game-play via matching in the large and growing online video game industry. In the first stage, we build a hidden Markov model (HMM) based on theories of customer engagement and gamer motivation to capture the evolution of gamers’ latent engagement state and state-dependent participation behavior. We then calibrate the HMM using a longitudinal data set, obtained from a major international video gaming company, that contains detailed information on 1,309 randomly sampled gamers’ playing histories over a period of 29 months comprising more than 700,000 unique game rounds. We find that high-, medium-, and low-engagement-state gamers respond differently to motivations, such as feelings of effectance and need for challenge. In the second stage, we use the results from the first stage to develop a matching algorithm that learns (infers) the gamer’s current engagement state “on the fly” and exploits that learning to match the gamer to a round to maximize game-play. Our algorithm increases gamer game-play volume and frequency by 4%–8% conservatively, leading to economically significant revenue gains for the company.

## 1. Introduction

The video game industry is a large and important part of the overall media and entertainment industry, with 2017 global revenues at \$116.0 billion (Wijman 2017), up by 10.7% from 2016. The biggest drivers of growth have been online game mode, for example, multiplayer mode (Quandt and Krger 2013), and online usage business models and distribution (Gaudiosi 2012), which are all facilitated by technological advances. Despite the size of the market and rapid growth, there has not been as much systematic research on consumer (gamer) behavior in the video game industry. This is in contrast to other sectorsof the media and entertainment industry, such as the music, movie, and book publishing industries, which have been extensively studied by researchers in the information systems community (e.g., Brynjolfsson et al. 2003, Duan et al. 2008, Danaher et al. 2014). Most of the existing studies on various sectors of the media and entertainment industry focus on consumers’ purchase decisions. This is likely because, in many of those sectors, consumers make one-time purchases of products, and the objective of the producers is typically to maximize the (one-time) sales revenue. In the video game industry, by contrast, in addition to consumers’ initial purchases, their postpurchase gameplay activities are very important for video game companies’ revenue.

Player engagement and continuous game-play are strongly correlated with a video game’s financial success (GameAnalytics 2015). This is because players’ game-play frequency and volume can positively contribute to the game revenue under almost all types of revenue models used by video games. For games with in-game purchase features, the more rounds a player plays, the more the player is likely to spend on in-game purchases, leading to higher game revenue; for games with in-game display ads, the more rounds a player plays, the more ads the player is exposed to, leading to a higher click-through number and, thus, higher game revenue. For games that do not have either in-game purchases or in-game advertising features, a higher game-play frequency/ volume is still likely to positively contribute to game revenue because highly engaged players are more likely to upgrade to the premium/subsequent version of the game (empirical evidence for this is provided in Section 6.2 of this paper). In addition to the direct impact of players’ engagement and game-play on game revenue mentioned, existing players’ high game-play frequency and volume can also help attract new players through buzz, visibility, and network effects. In practice, video game companies use daily/weekly/monthly active users (DAUs/WAUs/ MAUs; that is, the number of unique users on a day/ in a week/in a month) as key performance indicators of their video game products. Game-play frequency and volume are closely related to these parsimonious performance measures in that a higher game-play frequency/volume typically corresponds to a larger DAU/WAU/MAU and, thus, indicates a better game product performance.

Player engagement and game-play activities can be affected by many aspects of a video game, such as content, mechanism, and interface. Apart from these features, which have been determined before the release of a video game, an important factor that plays a significant role in influencing player engagement, especially in online multiplayer video games, is how players are assigned to available game rounds. The assignment algorithm determines with/against whom a player plays in each round, which directly influences the player’s game-play outcome in the current round and indirectly affects the player’s future engagement and participation. Joost van Dongen, a lead programmer and cofounder at Ronimo Games, notes in his blog that “Matchmaking is a highly important part of most modern multiplayer games. . . . It’s also a difficult topic to analyze and test before launch.”<sup>1</sup> Traditional matching algorithms that are used in most online video games do not utilize the detailed gamer playing history; they either match players randomly or use simple assignment rules, such as matching players with similar skill levels. Given the data-rich environment of (online) video games, video game companies have begun to realize that it may be possible to create better matching rules. However, there has been little systematic research investigating how behavioral player dynamics can be leveraged to improve matching assignment.

In this paper, we propose a novel data-analytic strategy that uses correlates of an unobservable (player engagement) and evaluates its impact on an observable (game-play frequency and volume). In particular, we first identify factors in players’ past game-play experiences that affect their engagement state and its evolution over time. Based on the impact of these factors, we propose a new matching algorithm that maximizes game-play over a specified time period. We do this using a two-stage modeling approach that combines theory, statistical analysis, and optimization techniques. In the first stage, we build a hidden Markov model (HMM; e.g., Netzer et al. 2008) based on theories of customer engagement and player motivation to capture the transition of players’ latent engagement state and state-dependent game-play behavior. We apply the HMM to a longitudinal data set made available to us by a major international video gaming company for a first-person shooter game.<sup>2</sup> The first-stage estimation results suggest that players can be classified into high, medium, and low engagement states with different motivations impacting each state. For example, effectance (proxied by per-period total score) has a positive effect on players in a low or high engagement state but not on players in a medium engagement state, challenge (proxied by withinperiod score variation) positively affects the engage ment level of players who are currently in either a low or medium engagement state but negatively affects the propensity of players in a high engagement state to stay highly engaged, and curiosity (or the lack thereof, proxied by time since sign-up) decreases the engagement level over time, with the rate of decrease being higher for less engaged players. In the second stage, we use the estimated HMM parameters to generate predictions around specific gamer behavior and then match each gamer to a game round that is expected to yield the most collectively desirable outcome. Spe cifically, we develop a novel matching algorithm that first learns (or infers) the current engagement state of a player using the player’s past game-play history and then assigns the player to a new round to maximize future engagement. We compare our approach with approaches that are commonly used in the industry (matching players randomly and matching players based on skill level). We show that our pro posed matching algorithm provides real-time matches and improves game-play frequency and volume (conservatively) by 4.04% and 7.79%, respectively. We also show that this improvement translates to economically significant outcomes for the firm.

Our paper makes three main contributions. First, it is one of the first studies that uses real-world data to empirically examine consumers’ (gamers’) postpurchase behavioral dynamics in the fast-growing online video game industry, where postpurchase engagement plays an essential role in the success of the product (i.e., the online video game). Our analysis of real-world data confirms the roles of effectance, challenge, and curiosity in keeping players engaged and reveals that these effects are heterogeneous across players at different engagement levels. These insights enhance our understanding of player engagement and motivation. Second, our proposed algorithm provides real-time player-matching outcomes that result in economically significant improvements for the firm. Our algorithm is general enough to be used in experiential settings in which engagement states vary across customers and evolve in a dynamic manner. Finally, from a methodological point of view, our two-stage data-analytic modeling approach combines theory, statistical methodology (to understand gamer behavior), and dynamic optimization (to derive firm policy). In doing so, we demonstrate how theory, statistical modeling, and optimization techniques can be integrated seamlessly to generate business insights and assist decision making.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the relevant literature. Section 3 describes the institutional setting and provides an overview of the data along with some “model-free” data patterns. We discuss the development of HMM in Section 4 and the associated results in Section 5. Section 6 describes the proposed matching algorithm and its performance. We conclude our paper in Section 7.

## 2. Literature Review

Our paper is related to multiple streams of literature. First, it is related to the emerging literature on the video game industry. There have been several studies on the structure of the video game markets (Nair 2007, Liu 2010, Zhu and Iansiti 2012, Anderson et al. 2014) in which the market for video games is typically characterized by an oligopolistic structure with both direct and indirect network effects. A number of papers examine other important aspects of video games, including the business models for video games (Roquilly 2011), “freemium” pricing strategy (Wu et al. 2013, Niculescu and Wu 2014), word-ofmouth effects (Zhu and Zhang 2010, Hao et al. 2011, Dou et al. 2013), and piracy issues (Lahiri and Dey 2013). Most of the existing studies focus on customers purchase decisions; to our best knowledge, with the exception of Kwon et al. (2016), who study the consumption pattern of mobile social apps, there has been little research that uses individual-level behavioral data to study the evolution of player engagement postpurchase.

Second, researchers from media psychology, communications, and computer science have investigated motivations for playing computer games, mostly using theoretical, experimental, and qualitative approaches. For example, Malone (1981) applies intrinsic motivation concepts to computer games and identifies three basic motivational categories: fantasy, challenge, and curiosity. Later studies find that other psychological constructs, such as in-game autonomy and competence (Ryan et al. 2006), are important motivating factors for video game consumption. It has also been shown in the literature that rewards (such as experience points and wealth) help motivate players and create loyalty (Bartle 2004, Amabile and Kramer 2011, Deterding 2012, Hofacker et al. 2016). In this paper, we develop an HMM based on theories of motivation, such as the self-determination theory (SDT; Deci and Ryan 1985, Ryan et al. 2006), Malone’s framework for computer game motivation (Malone 1981), and theories of customer engagement (Brodie et al. 2011), and then empirically evaluate the effects of various motivational factors on player engagement and gameplay with real-world data. Our longitudinal study of player behavioral dynamics complements and extends existing theoretical and survey-/experiment-based empirical research of gamer behavior in the literature and provides novel insights into the evolution of player engagement.

Methodologically, the empirical part of this paper adds to the growing literature that uses HMM to analyze business- and management-related questions, such as customer relationship management (Netzer et al. 2008) developer learning dynamics in open-source software projects (Singh et al. 2011), collaborative filtering (Sahoo et al. 2012), attribution in multichanne advertising (Abhishek et al. 2015), healthcare information technology (Yan and Tan 2014, Ayabakan et al. 2016), and user voluntary contributions in online communities (Chen et al. 2018). We believe that the HMM framework is well suited for our context because one of the goals of this research is to capture an important but hard-to-measure latent construct— engagement—and evaluate its impact on an observable outcome variable—game-play frequency and volume We incorporate covariates into the transition matrices of the HMM, which allows a rich description of the evolution of a player’s game-play behavior over time The HMM we propose also provides interpretability because it represents a quantitative model that corresponds to theories underlying gamer behavior. The optimization part of this paper adds to a large body of literature on dynamic optimization with partial ly observable states, especially that of the partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP; see Krishnamurty (2016) for an overview). Existing approaches to solve POMDP are not easily applicable to our problem for at least two reasons. First, most existing work assumes a stationary transition matrix whereas the probability of transitioning from one engagement state to another state in this paper is not stationary (it depends on many factors, e.g., how long the player has stayed in the system). Second, unlike in a typical POMDP setting in which the decision maker only needs to choose one action per period, a player may play multiple rounds in one period, which requires multiple game assignments. In other words, we are dealing with a setting in which the decision maker has to make sequential decisions in each period, and the state at the beginning of the next period is determined by the col lective outputs of the sequential decisions made in the current period. To the best of our knowledge, this setting is new to the POMDP literature. In sum, because efficiency and speed of the matching algorithm are of ut most importance in our application context, we depart from standard POMDP techniques and develop a novel algorithm that combines (approximate) learning with (approximate) matching.

## 3. Research Context, Data, and Model-Free Evidence

As noted earlier, the data that we use were provided by an anonymous major international video gaming company for a first-person shooter game. In the game, players take on the persona of various military roles, such as assault, reconnaissance, and engineer, and play on maps of urban streets, metropolitan areas, and open landscapes. The game can be played on a game console (e.g., Xbox 360) or on a regular personal computer (PC). Players typically play in teams,<sup>3</sup> and the objective may be to capture a flag, kill the rival teams a certain number of times, or attack/ defend stations depending on their chosen game mode. Players earn a score (points) individually in each round they play by injuring enemies, killing enemies, or completing tasks, such as capturing the flag The cumulative score a player collects determines the player’s “rank.” Each rank has a set cumulative score that must be earned to “level up,” that is, reach the next higher rank. The cumulative score is nondecreasing—players can only move from a lower rank to a higher rank, not the reverse. After leveling up, players can unlock the new rank’s specializations or weapons. To join a game round, players can choose either “quick match” or “server browser.” If quick match is selected, players simply choose a game mode and one of the associated maps and are then matched by the company to a currently available game round. If server browser is selected, players can browse servers and look for a specific game round to join; that is, the matching choice is made by the player, not the company. Although the video game company does not record which method a player chooses to join a round, its internal data suggest that the proportion of players who choose quick match is much higher for console players than for PC players.

The data set includes the complete game-play histories of 1,309 players (focal players) from October 2011 (product launch) to March 2014. These players played 710,212 unique game rounds in this period, with the total number of players playing these rounds at 9.5 million. The round-level information in the data includes map, game mode, the date on which the round began, the duration of the round, the number of unique players in the round, the maximum number of players in the round at any given time, and the maximum number of players allowed in the round at any given time. For each player in each record, we observe the player’s rank at the star and end of the round, how long (in seconds) the player spent in the round, and the total score and combat score the player earned in the round. We are only given the user IDs for the 1,309 sampled focal players. For nonfocal players, we observe neither their user IDs nor their complete playing history. In addition, there is little overlap in the game rounds played by different focal players; 0.2% of the rounds in the sample have more than one focal player playing in them. For these reasons, we do not observe the “game-play network” among players and, thus, cannot model it explicitly. However, for each round in which one or more focal players played, we observe the ranks of all plavers in that round. We can use these observed ranks to construct variables that describe with whom the focal player played in that round, which can impact focal players’ future game-play behavior. Player-round-level variables for focal players are summarized in Table 2 The variables in Table 2 are defined as follows: rank at start/end represents the focal player’s rank at the start/end of the current round, player seconds represents the number of seconds the player spent in the round, total score represents total points earned from the round, and combat score represents points earned from killing other players.

An alternative modeling approach is to first aggregate a player’s game-play behavior and game-play outcome to the daily or weekly level and then study how a player’s daily/ weekly game-play outcome affects the player’s gameplay activities in future periods. This approach has multiple advantages; it not only resolves the data issue described, but it also simplifies the analysis because a player’s game-play process can be modeled as a discrete-time process instead of a continuoustime process. We adopt this “aggregate approach” and define a period as one week. We choose to aggregate the player’s game-play information to the weekly level rather than the daily level because the mean and median of the probability of playing on a given day across all 1,309 players are 0.098 and 0.063, respectively, resulting in too many zeros in the daily-level data. This can adversely affect the identification of the HMM parameters. The summary statistics of the weekly game-play activities and outcome variables are provided in Table 4. For each player, we consider only the weeks after the player registered for the game and before the player upgraded to the next version of the same game. This is because it is evident from the data that once a player upgrades to the new version of the game, the player rarely returns to the older version of the game. ## 4. Theory and Model Development

In the first stage of our two-stage data-analytic ap proach, we construct a model of player engagement based on theories of customer engagement and gamer motivation and then use the real-world player gameplay data described to calibrate the model.

## 4.1. Player Engagement and Factors Affecting Player Engagement

The term “engagement” has been used in the social science, management, and practitioner literature. Brodie et al. (2011, p. 260) review the definitions of engagement in all these research areas and propose a general definition of customer engagement as “a psychological state that occurs by virtue of interactive, cocreative customer experiences with a focal agent/ object.” They further point out that engagement exists as a dynamic, iterative process and stress that other relational concepts (e.g., involvement, loyalty, and participation) are antecedents and/or consequences in the iterative engagement processes. In the context of video gaming, we consider engagement as an unobservable psychological state; “participation” (i.e., game-play) is both a consequence of current and an antecedent of subsequent player engagement processes. The distinction between engagement and participation and the relationship between these two concepts motivate the use of an HMM to model players’ game-play behavior. Players’ engagement level can be modeled as the latent state variable in the HMM, and the latent engagement state (stochastically) determines player game-play activities, which are observable to researchers. Game-play outcome then affects players’ future engagement state. The HMM framework also allows us to incorporate the player motivations discussed in the psychological and behavioral literature and empirically evaluate the effects of those motivational factors on player engagement evolution.

Next, we discuss how player engagement evolves and what factors affect this process based on theories of motivation, especially those that have been applied to video gamers. In this paper, we focus on the motivational factors that (1) vary across game rounds and (2) are related to game-play outcomes and, thus, can be affected by the matching algorithm. Recall that our player game-play data come from a single video game. For factors that are determined by the content and mechanism of the video game, such as fantasy (Malone 1981), autonomy, and relatedness (Ryan et al. 2006), there is no variation in player experience with those aspects of the game in the data, which does not allow identification of their effects on player engagement (i.e., they are all absorbed into the intercept of the state transition propensity discussed in Section 4.2.1), and the matching algorithm cannot influence player experience in those dimensions either. The motivational factors that satisfy conditions 1 and 2 above are discussed in detail.

One of the motivation theories that have been applied to studying the motivations for individuals to engage with video games is SDT (Deci and Ryan 1985). Ryan et al. (2006) applied SDT to the video gaming context and found that games are motivating to the extent that players’ psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness are satisfied. “Competence” is described as “the most important satisfactions provided by games, as they represent arenas in which a person can feel accomplishment and control” (see Ryan et al. 2006, p. 350). The need for competence involves two aspects: feelings of effectance and needs for challenge.

4.1.1. Effectance. White (1959, p. 298) defines “effectance” as a “tendency to explore and influence one’s environment.” He argues that successful mastery attempts or positive outcomes of one’s behavior generally lead to feelings of efficacy or intrinsic pleasure, which, in turn, increases or maintains effectance motivation. Applying this argument to the motivation for players to play a video game, we expect that success in game-play has a positive impact on player engagement (Ryan et al. 2006). Games provide immediate feedback on in-game actions, a view on the progress one has made in a game, and the position of the player toward the player’s goal in the game (Richter 2015). In the specific game we study, success or positive gameplay outcome is best summarized by the total score a player receives from each game round (round level). Because our empirical analysis is at the period (week) level, we use the per-period total score to proxy for feelings of effectance in game-play. As mentioned in Section 3, there are other outcome variables, such as combat score, number of kills, and so on; however, total score is arguably the most comprehensive measure of success because it is almost a perfect linear combination of all other outcome variables. Players making progress and moving toward their goal in the game are reflected in their leveling up in rank. As mentioned in Section 3, players’ rank in the video game we study is determined entirely by their cumulative score; therefore, we do not consider player rank directly because it contains redundant information. Instead, we consider another rank-related variable, time staying at the same rank, as a proxy for the lack of effectance.

4.1.2. Challenge. The need for challenge is the other aspect of the competence motivation. In one of the first papers that studied computer game player motivations, Malone (1981) argues that challenge involves uncertain outcomes. Sources of outcome uncertainty include variable difficulty level, hidden information, randomness, and so on. It is important to set the right level of challenge in video games: if the challenge is underwhelming, players get bored with the game, leading to lower engagement; if the challenge is overwhelming, players get frustrated and lose the sense of efficacy, leading to lower engagement (Przybylski et al. 2010). In our empirical analysis, we use the standard deviation of the scores a player receives across different rounds the player plays in a period to proxy for the level of challenge the player faces (in terms of outcome uncertainty).

4.1.3. Curiosity. Another intrinsic motivation of video gaming mentioned in Malone (1981) is curiosity: the degree to which the focal object/product can continue to arouse and then satisfy curiosity. Malone points out that people are motivated to bring to all their cognitive structures three of the characteristics of well-formed scientific theories: completeness, consistency, and parsimony. When individuals are presented just enough information to make their existing knowledge seem incomplete, inconsistent, or nonparsimonious, they are motivated to learn more to make their cognitive structures better formed, which leads to higher engagement. In the video game context, as players play the game for a longer period of time, they accumulate more and more knowledge about the details of the game; as a result, their curiosity about the video game gradually decreases, which may lead to lower engagement. In our empirical analysis, we use time since sign-up to proxy for the players’ (drop in) curiosity about the video game.

## 4.2. The HMM of Player Engagement

Based on the theories discussed, we develop an HMM of player engagement (Figure 3). In the model, players engagement level is unobservable to researchers, and the transition between different engagement levels is modeled as a Markovian process. Players’ hidden engagement state stochastically determines their future participation decisions (process 2 in Figure 3) The transition of engagement state is affected by players’ game-play outcome, which reflects the satisfaction of players’ needs for effectance, challenge, and curiosity (process 1 in Figure 3). As with other HMMs, the proposed HMM of player engagement consists of three major components:

1. The initial engagement state distribution π<sub>i</sub>. π<sub>i</sub> is a K-element vector (K is the number of possible levels of the engagement state) with the kth element (π k ) representing the probability that player i’s engagement state in period 1 is k ${ \bar { ( \pi _ { i } ( k ) = \operatorname { P } ( { \bar { S } } _ { i 1 } = k ) ) } }$ .

2. Engagement state transition (process 1 in Figure 3). The transitions between the latent engagement states are a sequence of Markovian transitions $( Q _ { i , t , t + 1 } ) .$ which express in a probabilistic manner how the game-play outcome in the previous time period affects the transition of player engagement state. $Q _ { i , t , t + 1 }$ is a $K \times K$ matrix with the element in row $k ,$ column $k ^ { \prime }$ representing the probability of a player transitioning from state k in period t to k in period t 1.

3. State-dependent game-play activity (process 2 in Figure 3). The state-dependent game-play activity in our research context includes two components: (a) the probability that a player plays the game in period $t ,$ conditional on the player’s current hidden engagement state $( P ( d _ { i t } = \bar { 1 } | S _ { i t } = k )$ , where $d _ { i t }$ is a binary variable with 1 indicating individual i plays at least one round in period t), and (b) the conditional distribution of the number of rounds that the player would play if the player decides to play in period t $( f ( r | S _ { i t } = k ) )$ ). Although neither (a) nor (b) is directly observable, the observed user participation decisions in each period are realizations of these probability distributions.

Another stochastic process in our model, which is not part of the HMM, is process 3 in Figure 3. This process captures how the profile of coplayers in a game round affects the focal player’s game-play outcome. Process 1 captures how players’ engagement state evolves as a function of their game-play outcome, whereas process 3 links the matching algorithm to game-play outcome. Note that this process is not affected by the latent engagement state. (See Online Appendix B for evidence supporting this assumption.) The matching algorithm influences the evolution of player engagement state by affecting coplayer profile and game-play outcome.

In reality, some players may be inherently more interested in or attached to the video game than others. Therefore, the transition of the hidden engagement level may vary across individuals. To capture such unobserved individual heterogeneity, we assume that there are M classes of players. Here we use $C \in \{ 1 , 2 , \dots . . . , M \}$ to denote the class to which an individual belongs. Player-level heterogeneity is captured by discrete classes rather than continuous distribution because discrete classes are easier to deal with when it comes to designing the matching algorithm. (One of our robustness checks presented in Section 5.5 assumes continuous heterogeneity.) The initial state distribution and state transition probabilities discussed are then class specific, and we modify their corresponding notations to $\pi _ { i } ^ { m }$ and

$Q _ { i , t , t + 1 } ^ { m } ;$ however, for identification purposes, we assume that the state-dependent playing activities are not class specific. We next elaborate on the specification of the three components of the HMM and process 3.

4.2.1. Engagement State Transition. The state transition matrix $Q _ { i , t , t + 1 } ^ { m }$ is defined as

$$
Q _ {i, t, t + 1} ^ {m} = \left[ \begin{array}{c c c c} q _ {i t 1 1} ^ {m} & q _ {i t 1 2} ^ {m} & \dots & q _ {i t 1 K} ^ {m} \\ q _ {i t 2 1} ^ {m} & q _ {i t 2 2} ^ {m} & \dots & q _ {i t 2 K} ^ {m} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ q _ {i t K 1} ^ {m} & q _ {i t K 2} ^ {m} & \dots & q _ {i t K K} ^ {m} \end{array} \right].
$$

Here $q _ { i t k k ^ { \prime } } ^ { m }$ represents the probability that individual i’s engagement state transitions from k to $k ^ { \prime }$ at the end of period t. The transition probabilities are affected by individual $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ decision to play in period $t \left( d _ { i t } \right)$ and a set of time-varying covariates (denoted as $X _ { i t } )$ , including the total score earned in the current period, the standard deviation of the scores earned in the same period, the number of weeks in which the player has played the game since the player’s last rank increase, and the number of weeks passed since the player registered for the game. As noted in Section 4.1, we include these variables because they capture the key motivational factors identified in the theoretical literature. We assume that if individual i does not play in period $t \left( d _ { i t } = 0 \right)$ , then individual i will stay at the same engagement state in the next period $( S _ { i t + 1 } = S _ { i t } )$ that is, $\bar { Q } _ { i , t , t + 1 } ^ { m } = I _ { K }$ . (We show in one of our robustness checks—see Section 5.5—that relaxing this assumption does not affect our results.) This assumption helps us considerably in implementing the matching algorithm. If the individual plays in period t, that ${ \mathrm { i } } \mathbf { s } ,$ $d _ { i t } = 1$ , the player’s latent propensity to move from one engagement level to another in period $t \left( Z _ { i t } \right)$ can be expressed as

$$
Z _ {i t} = X _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \eta_ {k} + \epsilon_ {i t},\tag{1}
$$

where $\eta _ { k }$ is a k-specific vector with each element representing the marginal effect of the corresponding element in the vector on the latent transition propensity, and $\epsilon _ { i t }$ captures the unobserved factors that also affect the engagement state transition. In the main model, we impose the restriction that players engagement states can only transition between adjacent states: when the propensity for transition crosses the threshold for transitioning to a higher (h) state $( \mu _ { h k } ^ { m } )$ , the engagement state moves up by one; when it falls below the threshold for transitioning to a lower (l) state $( \mu _ { l k } ^ { m } )$ , the engagement state moves down by one. Notice that $\eta _ { k } , \mu _ { h k } ^ { m }$ , and $\mu _ { l k } ^ { m }$ are all k-specific, allowing for the possibility that the same values of $X _ { i t }$ lead to different transition propensities at different engagement-state levels. To capture the possibility that players in a certain class (or classes) are inherently more interested in the game and, thus, more likely to stay engaged than other players, we also allow the thresholds to be class specific $( \mu _ { h k } ^ { m }$ and $\mu _ { l k } ^ { m }$ are m specific). We assume the same $\eta _ { k }$ for different classes, indicating that the effects of game-play outcome on the latent state transition propensity are common across classes. We further assume that $\epsilon _ { i t }$ follows the type I extreme value distribution, and thus, the transition probability takes an ordered-logit form. The elements in the $Q _ { i , t , t + 1 } ^ { \bar { m } }$ matrix can then be written as follows. If $d _ { i t } = 0 .$ , then

$$
q _ {i t k k ^ {\prime}} ^ {m} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \text {if} k = k ^ {\prime}, \\ 0 & \text {otherwise (o.w.)}. \end{array} \right.
$$

If $d _ { i t } = 1$ , then

$$
q _ {i t k k ^ {\prime}} ^ {m} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 - \frac {\exp (\mu_ {h k} ^ {m} - X _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \eta_ {k})}{1 + \exp (\mu_ {h k} ^ {m} - X _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \eta_ {k})} & \text {if k^{\prime} = k + 1}, \\ \frac {\exp (\mu_ {l k} ^ {m} - X _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \eta_ {k})}{1 + \exp (\mu_ {l k} ^ {m} - X _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \eta_ {k})} & \text {if k^{\prime} = k - 1}, \\ \frac {\exp (\mu_ {h k} ^ {m} - X _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \eta_ {k})}{1 + \exp (\mu_ {h k} ^ {m} - X _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \eta_ {k})} \\ - \frac {\exp (\mu_ {l k} ^ {m} - X _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \eta_ {k})}{1 + \exp (\mu_ {l k} ^ {m} - X _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \eta_ {k})} & \text {if k^{\prime} = k}, \\ 0 & \text {o.w.,} \end{array} \right.
$$

where $\mu _ { h k } ^ { m } = \infty$ for $k = K$ and $\mu _ { l k } ^ { m } = - \infty$ for $k = 1$

Note that we impose a number of assumptions on the state transition part of the HMM. A truly “general model” would require relaxing all these assumptions. However, such a model would be extremely difficult, if not impossible, to estimate. The strategy we take here is to use a model with a specific structure (with reasonable assumptions) that captures the most important aspects of player behavior as the main model. In the robustness checks (see Section 5.5 for details), we relax one assumption at a time and show the robustness of the results with respect to those assumptions.

4.2.2. The Initial State Distribution. The initial state distribution is typically set to the stationary distribution of the state transition matrix if an HMM has a time-invariant transition matrix (MacDonald and Zucchini 1997). However, the state transition matrix in our model contains time-varying variables and players’ playing decisions in each period. We calculate the stationary distribution of the transition matrix by solving the equation $\pi _ { i } ^ { m } = \pi _ { i } ^ { m } \bar { Q } _ { i } ^ { m }$ under the constraint $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { K } \pi _ { i } ^ { m } ( k ) = 1 } \end{array}$ , where $\bar { Q } _ { i } ^ { m }$ is the transition matrix with all time-varying covariates set to zero, and under $d _ { i t } = 1$ because our data are not left truncated.<sup>4</sup>

4.2.3. The State-Dependent Game-Play Behavior. Given a player’s state, the player’s participation is assumed to be conditionally independent. We use a hurdle model to capture players’ state-dependent game-play behavior. That is, the binary outcome of whether a count variate has a zero or positive realization is governed by a binomial probability model. If the realization is positive, the “hurdle is crossed,” and the conditional distribution of the positives is governed by a truncated-at-zero data model. In our context, we assume that, in each period, a player first decides whether to play the game $d _ { i t } ,$ which depends on the engagement state the player is in and the realization of the random error. Then, conditional on $d _ { i t } = 1 ,$ the player decides on how many rounds to play. For different engagement state $k ,$ the probability of playing and the distribution of rounds of playing are different. We denote the probability of playing in engagement state k as $p _ { k } = \exp ( \alpha _ { k } ) / ( 1 + \exp ( \alpha _ { k } ) )$ and assume the number of rounds played in a period follows a k-specific log-normal distribution $[ \ln ( \dot { \boldsymbol { r } } _ { i t } ) | S _ { i t } = k ] \sim \mathcal { N } ( \gamma _ { k } , \dot { \sigma } _ { k } ^ { 2 } )$ . To ensure identification of the states, we restrict $\alpha _ { k }$ and $\gamma _ { k }$ to be nondecreasing in k. We further define $a _ { i t } = \left( d _ { i t } , r _ { i t } \right)$

4.2.4. Factors Affecting Game-Play Outcome (Score). Because our ultimate goal is to design a matching algorithm that maximizes a player’s game-play frequency and volume, we also need to identify which round-level characteristics can affect game-play outcome, quantify their impact, and incorporate them into our matching algorithm. More specifically, we want to establish a connection between a focal player’s gameplay outcome (round-level score) and the characteristics of the focal and other players in the round (process 3 in Figure 3). Thus,

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\ln s c o r e _ {i r} = \delta_ {0} + \delta_ {1} A v g R a n k _ {r} + \delta_ {2} P e r c e n t i l e _ {i r}} \\ & {\qquad + \delta_ {3} A v g R a n k _ {r} \times P e r c e n t i l e _ {i r}} \\ & {\qquad + \delta_ {4} \ln (A v g S c o r e _ {i r} ^ {P R E V})} \\ & {\qquad + \delta_ {5} \ln (T t l S c o r e _ {i r} ^ {P R E V}) + \xi_ {i r}.} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Here score is the score player i receives in round $r ;$ AvgRank is the average rank of all players in the game round, which captures the mean competence level of players in the round; $P e r c e n t i l e _ { i r }$ is the percentile of the focal player’s rank at the beginning of the round among all players in round $r ,$ which reflects the relative standing of player i in the round in terms of the player’s skill/experience level; $A v g S c o r e _ { i r } ^ { P R E V }$ and $\stackrel { \bullet } { T } t l { \cal S } c o r e _ { i r } ^ { P R E V }$ represent player $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ average score per round in the most recent period (not including the current period) in which the player played the game and the total score the player received in that period, respectively. These two covariates are introduced into the regression to capture players’ short-term performance level, and they differ from the rank-based variables that capture players’ long-term cumulative performance level. The log transformation is applied to variables that are highly skewed. Note that the regression model is independent of the HMM and can be separately estimated. Also note that this analysis is at the round level, not the weekly level. Although we consider only a few player characteristics in this model, our modeling approach can be readily extended to incorporate more player characteristics (but at the expense of complicating the matching algorithm).

## 4.3. The Likelihood of an Observed Sequence of Actions

Let $\boldsymbol { S _ { i } } = ( S _ { i 1 } , S _ { i 2 } , \ldots , S _ { i T _ { i } } )$ denote player i’s hidden state sequences and $a _ { i } = ( a _ { i 1 } , a _ { i 2 } , \ldots , a _ { i T _ { i } } )$ denote an observed game-play activity sequence. Then $T _ { i }$ refers to the number of periods between the date when player i signed up for the game and the time when the player upgraded to the next version of the game or the last date observed in the data, whichever happened first. Given that any two consecutive observed actions are linked only through the hidden states, the probability of observing the sequence $\mathbf { \alpha } _ { a _ { i } }$ conditioned on the states in each period can be written as

$$
P (\boldsymbol {a} _ {i} | s _ {i}) = \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T _ {i}} P (a _ {i t} | S _ {i t}).
$$

The joint likelihood of a sequence of activities $a _ { i }$ for a player of class m is given by the sum over all possible paths individual i could take over time among the underlying states

$$
\begin{array}{l} L (a _ {i} | C = m) = P (a _ {i 1}, a _ {i 2}, \ldots , a _ {i T _ {i}} | C = m) \\ \qquad = \sum_ {s _ {i 1} = 1} ^ {K} \sum_ {s _ {i 2} = 1} ^ {K} \ldots \sum_ {s _ {i T _ {i}} = 1} ^ {K} \left[ P (S _ {i 1} = s _ {i 1} | C = m) \right. \\ \qquad \cdot P (a _ {i 1} | S _ {i 1} = s _ {i 1}) \prod_ {t = 2} ^ {T _ {i}} P (S _ {i t} = s _ {i t} | S _ {i t - 1} = s _ {i t - 1}, \\ \qquad \qquad C = m) \prod_ {t = 2} ^ {T _ {i}} P (a _ {i t} | S _ {i t} = s _ {i t}) \Bigg ]. \end{array}
$$

Following MacDonald and Zucchini (1997), we can rewrite this equation in a matrix product form that simplifies computation:

$$
L (\boldsymbol {a} _ {i} | C = m) = \pi_ {i} ^ {m} \tilde {A} _ {i 1} Q _ {i, 1, 2} ^ {m} \tilde {A} _ {i 2} Q _ {i, 2, 3} ^ {m} \dots \tilde {A} _ {i, T _ {i} - 1} Q _ {i, T _ {i} - 1, T _ {i}} ^ {m} \tilde {A} _ {i, T _ {i}} \mathbf {1},
$$

where 1 is a $K \times 1$ vector of ones and $\tilde { A } _ { i , t }$ is a diagonal $K \times K$ matrix whose kth diagonal element $( \bar { a } _ { i t | k } )$

denotes the likelihood of observing $a _ { i t }$ given that player $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ engagement state in period t is $k ,$ that is,

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\tilde {a} _ {i t | k} = \left\{\frac {\exp (\alpha_ {k})}{1 + \exp (\alpha_ {k})} \frac {\phi [ (\ln (r _ {i t}) - \gamma_ {k}) / \sigma_ {k} ]}{r _ {i t}} \right\} ^ {d _ {i t}}} \\ & {\qquad \cdot \left\{\frac {1}{1 + \exp (\alpha_ {k})} \right\} ^ {1 - d _ {i t}}.} \end{array}
$$

Let $\tau ^ { m }$ denote the fraction of the player population that belongs to class m such that $\textstyle \sum _ { m = 1 } ^ { \bar { M } } \bar { \tau ^ { m } } = 1$ . The unconditional likelihood of individual i’s sequence of activities $a _ { i }$ is $\begin{array} { r } { L ( { a } _ { i } ) = \sum _ { m = 1 } ^ { M } \tau ^ { m } L ( { a } _ { i } | C = m ) } \end{array}$ . The likelihood of observing all individuals’ sequence of activities is

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & L (a) = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \tau^ {m} L (\boldsymbol {a _ {i}} | C = m) \\ & \qquad = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \tau^ {m} \pi_ {i} ^ {m} \tilde {A} _ {i 1} Q _ {i, 1, 2} ^ {m} \tilde {A} _ {i 2} Q _ {i, 2, 3} ^ {m} \\ & \qquad \dots \tilde {A} _ {i, T _ {i} - 1} Q _ {i, T _ {i} - 1, T _ {i}} ^ {m} \tilde {A} _ {i, T _ {i}} \mathbf {1}. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

## 5. Estimation and Results

Maximum likelihood estimation is used to estimate the HMM. We randomly sample 1,100 of the 1,309 players for whom we have the complete game-play history to calibrate the model and use data about the remaining 209 players to validate the model. To arrive at the “best” model, we estimate HMMs with different combinations of latent classes and engagement state levels. We first estimate a series of homogeneous HMMs with up to four possible engagementstate levels. The estimation results suggest that the model fit and predictive performance do not improve significantly beyond three engagement levels. Next, we expand the model by incorporating up to four latent classes into the three-engagement-state model. In the heterogeneous HMM models, we allow only the threshold parameters $\mu$ to be different across classes. However, as a robustness check, we also estimate a model that allows the parameters associated with the covariates in the $X _ { i t }$ vector to be class specific. Finally, we estimate a nondynamic model (without any time-varying covariates) with three latent states. This model does not allow individuals to move among the engagement states over time. Table 5 summarizes the fit statistics of this set of models.

## 5.1. Model Comparison

The results reported in Table 5 show that all dynamic models perform better than the nondynamic model. This confirms that players’ engagement state evolves over time. Not surprisingly, as we allow for more engagement-state levels, the in-sample model fit improves. Among the models with three engagement states, the in-sample log-likelihood and Bayesian in formation criterion (BIC) improve as the number of classes increases. However, after the number of classes goes beyond two, the in-sample fit improvement is rather small when we add additional classes to the model. Moreover, the “two classes, three states” model performs almost as well as the “four classes, three states” model out of sample, and the “three classes, three states” model has a worse out-of-sample fit than the “two classes, three states” model and the “four classes, three states” model. Hence, we select the “two classes, three states” model as our main model and base our discussion of HMM parameter estimates on this model.<sup>5</sup>

## 5.2. HMM Results

The estimation results for the “two classes, three states” HMM are reported in Table 6. In the table, we label the three engagement states as “low,” “medium,” and “high.” The first set of parameters reported in the table governs the state-dependent gameplay behavior. The state-specific intercepts in the logit probability of playing (α ) are <sup>−</sup>3.212, 0.936, and 4.542 for the low, medium, and high engagement states, respectively. These numbers can be translated into probabilities (via $e ^ { \alpha _ { k } } / ( 1 + e ^ { \alpha _ { k } } ) )$ ) of playing in a given week at 0.039, 0.718, and 0.989 for the low, medium, and high engagement states, respectively. The gap in the probability of playing is a lot larger between the low and medium engagement states than between the medium and high engagement states. Interestingly, when it comes to the number of rounds a player wil play if the player decides to play in a given period, the difference between the medium and high engagement states is larger than that between the low and medium engagement states; the conditional means of the logarithm of the number of rounds played for the low, medium, and high engagement states are 1.521, 1.791, and 3.334, respectively. The estimated variances of the logarithm of the number of rounds a player would play for the low, medium, and high engagement states are 1.093, 1.013, and 0.713, respectively (calculated by taking the exponential of ln $( \sigma _ { k } ^ { 2 } ) )$ . Combining the mean and variance estimates for the low-, medium-, and high-state log-normal distributions, we can approximate the expected number of rounds a player would play in a given week: 7.9 for the low, 9.9 for the medium, and 40 for the high engagement states.

The second set of parameters of interest consists of the parameters governing the state transition process $\eta ^ { \prime } s$ and the threshold parameters $\mu ^ { \prime } s . \eta _ { . , k }$ capture the effects of the time-varying motivation proxies we consider in this model on the transition of the engagement state. First, somewhat surprisingly, we find that obtaining higher scores does not always keep the players engaged. The positive effect of the total score on the propensity of moving to/staying in a higher engagement state exists only when players are in either the low or high engagement state. When players are in the medium engagement state, a higher total score can slightly reduce the player’s propensity to move to a higher engagement state. Also, the magnitude of the positive effect of the total score is larger for players who are currently highly engaged than for those who are in the low engagement state. The standard deviation of the scores a player receives from different rounds the player plays in a period also has differential effects on an individual’s propensity to move toward a higher engagement state. For players in the low or medium engagement state, higher variations in the game-play outcome actually increase the propensity to move to a higher engagement state. However, the effect of such variation is negative for individuals who are in the high engagement state. If we look at the joint effects of the total and standard deviations of scores a player receives in a week on the player’s engagement state evolution, we see that for players who are currently highly engaged, continuous reinforcement of their sense of effectance is important to keep them engaged, and the sense of being challenged discourages their continuous play. This may be because highly engaged players are eager to establish their records in the game and pay more attention to their performance and progress in achieving the game goal; therefore, the feeling of effectance outweighs the need for challenges. For players who are in the low engagement state, both feelings of effectance and sense of being challenged can motivate their future participation. One potential explanation for this finding is that achieving high performance can rearouse their interest in the game (Lin 2009). In addition, without hurting the total score, a moderate level of challenge can also motivate their future participation. Players who are in the medium engagement state enjoy being challenged even at the expense of receiving a lower total score, indicating that those players care more about encountering challenges than mastering challenges (Gecas 1989).

The number of weeks a player has played the game without improving the player’s rank (ln(no. of weeks same rank)) negatively affects the player’s propensity to move toward higher engagement states. This finding supports the theoretical prediction that the lack of effectance (proxied by lack of progress) discourages players from staying engaged. It is worth noticing, though, that this negative effect is not statistically significant in the case of the low engagement state but is significant for individuals in either the medium or high engagement state. The intuition behind this finding is that highly engaged players play the game more and, thus, expect more frequent rank improvements. If they fail to level up, they get more frustrated. In contrast, those who are in the low engagement state probably do not have as strong a desire to level up, and thus, staying at the same rank may be less frustrating to them. Note that the effects of per-period total score and the number of weeks staying at the same rank are not always in the opposite direction, as theories suggest. Although they are both proxies for the sense (or lack) of effectance, the former is more of a short-term measure, whereas the latter is a cumulative measure. The difference of their effects on player engagement calls for further examination of different aspects of effectance motivation in the context of video gaming. The number of weeks since a player started playing the game is estimated to have a negative impact on the player’s propensity to move toward a higher engagement state, confirming that the decrease in the sense of curiosity negatively affects engagement. Also, the size of the negative effect is larger for individuals in a lower engagement state than for those in a higher engagement state. This is not surprising because individuals who are in the high engagement state are still very interested in and highly engaged with the game, and the same increase in the familiarity with the video game may not make them quickly become bored with the game compared with players in the low engagement state.

The remaining parameters are the class-specific parameters. First, the estimated value of τ is 0.648, which corresponds to a proportion of $e ^ { 0 . 6 4 8 } / ( 1 + e ^ { 0 . 6 4 8 } ) =$ 0.657 of players who belong to class 1. As we see in the Table 6, both the $\mu _ { l k . }$ , the threshold of moving down to a lower engagement state, and $\mu _ { h k } ,$ , the threshold of moving up to a higher engagement state are smaller for class 2 players than for class 1 players. This indicates that given the same past game-play outcome, class 2 players are less likely to drop down to a lower engagement state and more likely to move up to a higher engagement state. In other words, class 2 players are more inherently interested in the game and tend to stay engaged for a longer period of time, all else being equal.

## 5.5. Robustness Checks

In this section, we describe a series of robustness checks that we carried out to ensure that our empirical results are reliable. For the sake of brevity, we describe these tests briefly—the detailed results for each robustness check are provided in Online Appendix C. Overall, we find that our results are generally reliable to various sets of player data and model structures.

1. Cohort differences. The video game company classified the players in our sample into two cohorts based on the time they started to play the video game. Players who registered between October 25, 2011, and November 24, 2011, are defined as cohort 1, and those who registered between January 1, 2012, and January 31, 2012, are defined as cohort 2. Cohort 1 players purchased the game within one month of its release and can be viewed as “early adopters.” Cohort 2 players can be viewed as “followers.” To see whether the game-play behavior of cohort 1 players is not different from that of cohort 2 players, we estimate the “two classes, three states” HMM with the game-play data for only individuals in cohort 1 (here we allow two classes within cohort 1). The estimation results based on the cohort 1 subsample are similar to the estimation results based on the full sample, which suggests little heterogeneity in the evolution of the engagement state between cohort 1 and cohort 2 players. We also examine whether individuals’ latent class membership is correlated with the cohorts as well as other player time-invariant characteristics and find that cohort 1 players seem to have a lower probability of belonging to class 1, the class whose engagement level tends to be lower, compared with cohort 2. However, the difference does not seem to be material.

2. Console players only. Our main model does not explicitly control for potential behavioral differences between PC players and console players. Because console players are more likely to use quick match, we estimate the “two classes, three states” HMM with data associated with console players only. The estimation results remain largely unchanged.

3. Alternative modeling assumptions. We further estimate several variations of our main HMM, including (q) a model that uses the cumulative playing time to proxy for curiosity, (b) a model that allows players to drop from the high engagement state directly to the low state to capture the possibility that they suddenly lose interest in the game completely, (c) a model that allows class-specific $\eta ^ { \prime } s ,$ (d) a model that assumes continuous player heterogeneity, and (e) a model that considers the potential direct impact of the matching outcome on player engagement evolution. (Recall that, in the main model, we assume that matching outcome only has an indirect effect on player engagement; that is, matching outcome affects gameplay outcome, and game-play outcome, in turn, influences player engagement.) This set of robustness checks generally confirms that our empirical results are robust to alternative modeling assumptions and choices.

## 6. Matching Algorithm

In the second stage of our two-stage data-analytic approach, we develop a matching algorithm based on the estimated HMM to maximize overall player game-play volume and then numerically test its performance against two benchmark algorithms that are commonly used in practice. Note that estimation of the HMM and optimization of the matching algorithm are done sequentially.

6.1. The “Learn-and-Exploit” Approach to Matching The video game company’s ultimate objective is to maximize game revenue. However, we cannot use maximizing game revenue as the objective of our matching algorithm directly because it would require developing a comprehensive model that captures the relationship between matching outcome and game revenue, which is not feasible without player purchase data. Instead, we choose to maximize the ex pected number of rounds played by a player during a fixed period of time beginning from when the player starts to play the game (i.e., game-play volume) as our objective. The rationale behind this choice is that a player’s game-play volume is likely to be strongly positively correlated with game revenue regardless of the revenue model of the video game; therefore, the objective of maximizing game-play volume is well aligned with the objective of maximizing revenue. In Section 6.2, we provide evidence for the positive relationship between game-play frequency/volume and game revenue. Note that we model this optimization as a finite-horizon nondiscounted problem because the length of the life cycle of a video game is typical two years.

6.1.1. Motivation and Intuition for Learn-and-Exploit Matching. We call our algorithm “learn-and-exploit matching.” This algorithm is motivated by the empirical result that players in a higher engagement state are more likely to play the game and to play more rounds, on average. Therefore, maximizing a player’s gameplay activity is equivalent to maximizing the probability that the player stays in/moves toward the high engagement state in every period. The results presented in Section 5 suggest that a player’s heterogeneity and engagement state lead to different responses to game-play outcomes. Hence, an effective algorithm should take into account both the player’s current engagement state and the player’s class. In Table $6 ,$ the coefficient of the term ln stdscore is positive for the low-/medium-state players and negative for high-state players, indicating that variation in game-play outcomes keeps low-/mediumstate players engaged but discourages high-state players from staying engaged. Therefore, when maximizing the probability of low-/medium-state players transitioning to a higher engagement state, we need to find a balance between maximizing total score in each period and maintaining a reasonable level of variation across the scores a player receives in different rounds played in the same period. In other words, we need to find a balance between satisfying players’ needs for competence (high total scores) and challenge (standard deviation of scores)—two of the fundamental motivations driving player engagement. This motivates us to develop a matching algorithm that alternately matches a low-/medium-state player to a round that guarantees a high expected score and a round that leads to a lower expected score to create sufficient variability across the scores collected during a period.

6.1.2. Four Steps of Learn-and-Exploit Matching. Let $G _ { r t }$ denote the set of candidate rounds for the rth round in week $t ,$ and let Score denote the deterministic part of the scoring function (Equation (2), excluding $\xi _ { i r } )$ . Throughout the remainder of this section, we use ${ \overline { { S c o r e } } } ( g , i )$ to denote the deterministic score that player i gets if player i is assigned to candidate round $g .$ The matching algorithm consists of four steps. Suppose that we are currently at the beginning of week t. In the first step, we learn (infer) player i’s engagement state at the beginning of last week to help us infer player i’s engagement state at the beginning of the current week. To do so, we propose an approximate learning scheme that infers the true engagement state of player i at the beginning of week t 1 using only the number of rounds player i played in that week. The intuition is as follows: if player i plays a lot of rounds during week $t - 1$ , player i is likely to start week t 1 with a high engagement state; ${ \mathrm { i f } } ,$ in contrast, player i only plays a few rounds, player i is likely to start with a low engagement state. Thus, the number of rounds played in the previous week has a good predictive power for inferring player $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ engagement state during the current week. In the second step, we update our belief regarding player i’s true class. It is worth noting that, in theory, we can calculate the exact posterior probability that player i belongs to a certain class conditioned on all the observed outcomes starting from week 1. Although this is conceptually straightforward, it is computationally very intensive. Because both speed and simplicity of a matching algorithm are of utmost importance to many game companies, instead of performing an exact inference calculation, we resort to an approximate inference scheme based on the idea of maximum likelihood. In particular, we act as if player $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ true engagement state at the beginning of week $v \leq t - 1$ is given by $\hat { s } _ { i v } .$ . In the third step, we infer the most likely engagement state at the beginning of week t given $\hat { { s } } _ { i , t - 1 } , { \cal O } _ { i , t - 1 } ,$ , and $c _ { i t }$ . In the fourth step, we use both $\tilde { s } _ { i t }$ (our inferred engagement state at the beginning of week t) and $c _ { i t }$ (our most up-to-date guess on the true class of player i) to make a matching decision.

Moreover, although the current algorithm alternates between odd and even rounds, this is not the only way to create variation in outcomes; other forms of randomization can also be used. We use this alternating odd–even mechanism simply as an illustration of one such mechanism. Finally, in its most general form, the value of $\Delta _ { t } ( \cdot , \cdot )$ can be different for different t. However, increasing the number of different $\Delta$ values increases the complexity of the corresponding off-line optimization. In the performance simulation discussed in the following, we only test the algorithm in which we use the same Δ values at all t (even then, we show that our algorithm already achieves a significant improvement over the benchmark algorithms).

6.1.3. Remarks on Learn-and-Exploit Matching. It is important to understand some of the elements that characterize our learn-and-exploit matching algorithm. First, as noted, we use approximate learning/ inference schemes to infer a player’s engagement state (in the first step) and class (in the second step). Second, we match one player at a time.<sup>6</sup> Third, we do not allow for the possibility of deferring matches; that is, we do not wait to find “better” rounds for the focal player match. Fourth, we assume that the matching outcome impacts the performance of only the focal player (the one who is being matched). Finally, we assume that multiple rounds are available for matching whenever the focal player wants to play. The data suggest that the range of available rounds is from 100 to 3,000. The more rounds available, the better is the match. In our performance comparison, we set the number of available rounds to the lowest number (100). Relaxing any of these assumptions is likely to provide better matches and, therefore, a higher level of improvement. Thus, the results we present are conservative and should be interpreted as the lower bound in terms of improvement in gameplay frequency and volume. It is also worth pointing out that in cases in which the connection between player activity and game revenue is clearly defined or a higher game-play frequency/volume does not lead to a higher game revenue, alternative objective functions may be considered. For example, one may argue that for games whose main revenue sources are in-game advertisements or in-game purchases, the objective of the matching algorithm should be to maximize total playing time. We experiment with this alternative matching objective in our context. Specifically, we use an algorithm similar to the learn-andexploit algorithm with a caveat that the parameters $\{ \Delta _ { t } ( k , c ) \}$ <sub>}</sub> are now optimized to maximize total playing time. We find that the new matching algorithm with the objective of maximizing the total playing time is almost identical to our original learn-and-exploit matching, likely because the total number of rounds a player plays and the player’s total playing time are highly correlated (with a correlation of 0.964).

## 6.2. Performance Comparison

To evaluate the performance of our algorithm, we consider two benchmark algorithms that are most commonly used in multiplayer online video games: random matching and closest-rank matching. For the first algorithm, during each round, it matches a player to a candidate round randomly. It is the simplest algorithm among all because it does not take into account either the observable characteristics of the player (such as the player’s rank) or the unobserved characteristics of the player (such as the player’s class and engagement state). As for the second algorithm, it matches a player to a candidate round whose average rank of all existing players is closest to the player’s own rank in absolute value. This algorithm is motivated by the belief that a player’s engagement will be high if the player plays in a round with other players of similar skill levels. In our numerical experiments, we measure the number of rounds played by an average player during a period of 104 weeks or two years. To average out the uncertainty in the system $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ class membership, realization of the random shocks in the score function, etc.), we perform 500 independent Monte Carlo simulations. The sequence underlying our numerical simulation is as follows. We first randomly generate a set of 500 “instances” of the average player. The player instance in each simulation round is characterized by the player’s class (generated randomly according to the estimated prior probability in Section 5) and joins the system in week 1 (thus, the players’ initial ranks are all zero). In each simulation round, we simulate a sequence of the player’s activities for 104 consecutive weeks under each matching algorithm. Finally, we output the average number of game rounds played in each week across the 500 simulated instances. The details of the simulation procedure can be found in Online Appendix D.

We report the results of our numerical experiments in Tables 8 and 9. The plot of average number of rounds played per week for the three algorithms can be seen in Figure 5. Four aspects are worth highlighting. First, in terms of the number of rounds played during a two-year period, random matching performs the worst among all the algorithms tested. This is perhaps not too surprising given that random matching completely ignores all player characteristics, observable and unobservable. Second, the idea that a player enjoys playing with peers who have a similar skill level may not be completely unfounded. Both the numbers in Table 8 and the plot in Figure 5 suggest that closest rank matching outperforms random matching, especially during the first 30 weeks. During the later weeks, random matching and closest-rank matching seem to have a relatively comparable performance. Third, in a two-year span, learn-and-exploit matching increases the expected number of weeks of playing and the expected number of rounds played by 4.04% and 7.79% of the best numbers that random matching and closest-rank matching produce, respectively, which highlights the potential benefit of using our HMM results together with learning the player’s unobservable characteristics in matching assignment. Fourth, Table 9 and Figure 5 show that most of the improvement of our algorithm over the other two benchmark algorithms happens in later weeks because there does not seem to be much improvement during the first 30 weeks. The intuition behind this result is as follows. During the early periods, most players are still in a high engagement state, and matching assignments have little impact on their state transition. In later periods, players’ engagement level is significantly affected by the loss of curiosity resulting from the longer period since registration. At the same time, the effects of the two main factors considered in the learn-and-exploit matching algorithm, that is, the total scores a player collects in a period and the standard deviation of scores the player receives from individual rounds the player plays in that period, become stronger. This provides the “bump” in play probability and the number of rounds played in later weeks (conditional on play). Note that the learn-and-exploit matching algorithm generates matches with more similar ranks (leading to a smaller rank range) than the algorithm used by the company for our data (see Online Appendix E for details).

To measure the economic impact of the improvements in the total number of rounds played and number of active weeks (defined as weeks in which a player played at least one round) reported, we use the link between these two numbers and a player’s propensity to upgrade to the next version of the game. In the following logistic regression, we use the total number of rounds played and/or the number of active weeks to predict whether a player will eventually upgrade to the next version of the game. The results (Table 10) suggest that both the total number of rounds played and the number of active weeks positively affect the probability of upgrade. Because of the high correlation (0.90) between ln(no. of active weeks) and ln(no. of rounds played), we can assess the impact of changing only one of them at a time. We plug in the improvement numbers from earlier (4.04% in active weeks or 7.79% in rounds played) and find that there is a 1.3% increase in the upgrade probability. Given that the game had 9.5 million players and the average cost of the upgrade was \$60, this additional play results in increased revenue of about \$7.4 million. This represents 1% of the total revenue from this game to date and (as noted) is a very conservative estimate from both the substantive and the methodological points of view.

## 7. Discussion and Conclusion

In the online video game industry, although it is commonly believed that players’ engagement level is directly affected by their game-play experience and outcomes, empirical research testing such beliefs remains limited. Existing empirical research examining player motivation are mostly based on data collected from surveys or laboratory experiments at a specific point in time. There has been little empirical research that uses panel data about real-world players’ gameplay history to examine the dynamics of player engagement and game-play activities over time. The current study fills this gap in the video game literature. We propose a novel two-stage data-analytic modeling approach. In the first stage, motivated by existing theories of engagement and motivation, we construct an HMM of player engagement and gameplay dynamics and estimate it using a longitudinal data set provided by a major international video gaming company. Our empirical results are consistent with existing theoretical and qualitative studies on the role of effectance, challenge, and curiosity in keeping players engaged. Moreover, although existing research has been focusing on identifying factors that affect game-play and explaining why those factors may affect game-play, our HMM details how those factors affect game-play through affecting the latent engagement state and reveals significant intertemporal heterogeneity in their effects. These results have important managerial implications not only for the design of the player-matching algorithm, which is the focus of this paper, but also for the content and mechanisms of video games. For instance, the finding that there is a significant decay in game-play activities over time suggests a need for introducing surprises, such as new maps and new game modes, to offset the decrease in player curiosity as players become more and more familiar with the game. In addition, the differential effects of the gameplay outcome on the engagement of players at different engagement levels identified in this study call attention to the possibility of varying game difficulty based on players’ engagement level. For multiplayer video games, game difficulty can be adjusted by matching players to game rounds with stronger/weaker players; single-player games can track players’ game-play activity to infer players’ engagement state and adjust the level of difficulty or the degree of outcome uncertainty of the game rounds. Because the lack of progress toward moving to a higher rank has a negative impact on game-play, video game developers need to carefully choose the difficulty level to ensure that progressing to a higher rank is achievable with a reasonable amount of effort

In the second stage of our two-stage data-analytic approach, we use the estimates from the first stage to develop and calibrate a dynamic matching algorithm, learn-and-exploit matching, that incorporates each player’s time-invariant inherent interest in the game and the player’s time-varying engagement level, both inferred from the player’s individual-level game-play history. This algorithm uses different policies to assign players in different (predicted) engagement states. We benchmark our proposed algorithm against two algorithms most commonly used in the industry. Our numerical experiments show that by incorporating players engagement state and their latent interest level into the assignment algorithm, the video game company can improve player game-play frequency and volume. This improvement is economically significant in terms of increasing the firm’s revenue.

In conclusion, our paper is one of the first studies to use observational data to examine the evolution of gamer engagement state based on motivational factors. The novel two-stage data-analytic modeling approach we develop demonstrates how theories, statistical modeling, and optimization techniques can be seamlessly integrated to generate business insights and assist decision making in real time. We think that our approach—using observables to model the evolution of engagement (an unobservable) and its link to business outcomes that can be optimized—can serve as a general template for modeling customer–firm interactions in which engagement plays a big role.

That said, our study has several limitations. First, we examine only one aspect of the play experience— matching—to increase engagement while ignoring factors such as game interface and reward structure. Second, we cannot formally establish the connection between player engagement/game-play and game revenue. Third, we cannot incorporate social interactions among players into our model. Most of these limitations are a function of the data available to us. However, we believe that our findings about player motivation and engagement state evolution, which are based on the theoretical literature of engagement and motivation, are not unique to the specific video game we study in this research. Our HMM modeling framework is also applicable to studying player behavioral dynamics for other video games and/or optimizing the design of other aspects of a video game (e.g., content, mechanisms, etc.). From the practical standpoint, aside from the need to retune the parameters, our proposed algorithm is readily applicable to other multiplayer first-person shooter games, which accounts for a significant percentage of the revenue in the video game industry. Finally, our proposed matching algorithm does not incorporate the entire context in which matching is done (e.g., it does not consider how the matched player directly impacts the game-play outcome and state transition of all players in the matched round and the possibility of dynamically updating the model estimates and optimizing the matching algorithm) because our objective is to show how the firm can leverage engagement in matching to obtain better outcomes in real time. We hope that future research will address these limitations.
