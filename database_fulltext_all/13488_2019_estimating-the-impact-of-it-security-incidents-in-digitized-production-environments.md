# Estimating the impact of IT security incidents in digitized production environments

Olga Bürger<sup>a</sup>, Björn Häckel<sup>b</sup>, Philip Karnebogen<sup>c,⁎</sup>, Jannick Töppel<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Research Center Finance & Information Management, University of Bayreuth, 95447 Bayreuth, Germany

<sup>b</sup> Fraunhofer FIT – Project Group Business and Information Systems Engineering, University of Applied Sciences Augsburg, 86159 Augsburg, Germany

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: IT security Cyber attacks Bayesian networks Value creation

## A B S T R A C T

Owing to digitalization, manufacturing companies increasingly integrate IT services – such as control systems – into their production environments. This increases the flexibility of production and allows them to ofer new data-based services (e.g., predictive maintenance). However, stepping up production-IT system connections also lead to an increased reliance on the availability of IT services as a means to value creation, both in internal production processes and at the customer interface. More interconnectivity also increases network complexity, and thus favors the rapid spread of cyber-attacks within the information network. The potential for damage is massive, as disruptions to IT services can harm the deliverability of both, connected IT services and production components. Despite existing studies on IT security, little has been written on ways to estimate the impact that availability incidents have on digitized production environments based on the IIoT – for example, smart factories. To help close this research gap, we provide an approach that enables users to simulate cyber-attacks and measure the impact of such attacks on value creation in digitized production environments. We compare the features of our model with our specific design objectives and competing artifacts, present our prototype and the results of a sensitivity analysis for selected model parameters, and illustrate the applicability of our model using the real-life case of a German manufacturing company. Our results indicate that the degree of interconnection in digitized production environments is the most important influencing factor when estimating the impact of an IT avail ability incident on value creation.

## 1. Introduction

Traditionally, the value creation process in manufacturing companies involves a flow of processes which, step-by-step, turns input materials into a final product [1]. Increasingly, however, digitalization is forcing manufacturers to adapt their value creation processes. Changing market and customer requirements compel manufacturers to “connect […] the unconnected” [2] and incorporate production and IT systems into smart factories or the Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT). By doing so, companies aim to innovate their business models and streamline the organization and management of value chains. This enables highly flexible and resource-saving production, prioritizing product individualization over mass production [2]. Consequently, manufacturers increasingly integrate IT-related services, such as autonomous control systems, into their production systems in order to monitor and optimize the production processes. The result is that production facilities, which originally involved autonomous production systems. are now increasingly IT-based, interconnected, and integrated into business IT systems. Yet, companies do not only connect their production facilities to the internet in order to create cyber-physical production systems (CPPSs) [3]: they also interconnect their value creation processes with external market players and customers in order to optimize production and business processes and create innovative solutions – or even new business models. Overall, we are witnessing a marked shift as manufacturers change from product makers to solution providers by, for example, ofering data-based product-service bundles, usage-based provider business models, and digital platforms [4]. Thus, value creation among manufactures increasingly depends on IT or data-based services which support their new value creation processes. Moreover, the specific value added to the product is increasingly found in services provided to the customer, rather than in the physical product itself [5].

These changes in the value creation process lead to new challenges, in particular, those resulting from the new risks to IT security. Boundaries are being eroded, not only between operational systems and IT systems but also between companies. This increases complexity in digitized production environments which consist of two interconnected main networks: an information network and a production network. The resultant increase in interconnectivity in digitized production environments creates an increased attack surface and more opportunities for the propagation of attacks [5]. Higher interconnectivity between the information and production networks, in particular, also increases the potential for damage as value creation is highly reliant on the avail ability of IT services, both within production processes and at the customer interface [6]. The main diference between cyber-attacks and conventional machine failures caused by, for example, technical mal functions, is the uncertainty about the attacker's behavior. A simple machine breakdown is usually a specific event followed only by consequential damage within the production network. In contrast, a cyber attack can spread through the whole information network [6] and cause extensive damage within both the information and production net works.

To protect their business against cyber-attacks, companies should thoroughly assess the potential for damage, and develop an approach which enables them to simulate both (a) the spreading efects of in dividual cyber-attacks within the information network and (b) the consequential spread of damage within both the information and production networks. The example of services running on an external cloud highlights the potential efects on the information network. If the cloud is attacked, services running on it cannot function, which means the damage is spread to other components in the information network [7]. Whereas in the past, researchers have widely investigated the question of how to model multi-stage attacks [8–13], there remains a lack of approaches which enable companies to measure the impact that cyberattacks would have on value creation [14]. Although certain approaches investigate disruption of the value stream in operation research, they have not yet been used to measure the impact of cyber attacks on complex networks, such as smart factory or IIoT, nor have they taken the spread of damage into account. One of the few exceptions is a study by Häckel et al. [15], which provides an approach for assessing IT availability risks in smart factory networks, and which involves graph theory, matrix notation, and value-at-risk. Yet while ’ their approach considers dependencies between the information and production networks. it ignores the spread of damage to the information network. In contrast, Häckel et al. [16] model the possible spreads of attacks in the information network of a smart factory, but do not consider corresponding damage which may occur in the production net work. This, however, is important due to the increasing share of services in the value network of manufacturers [5]. Our research aims to support decision-makers in their attempts to protect digitized production environments by estimating the impact of availability incidents. Hence, we pose the following research question:

How can companies estimate the impact of IT security incidents which harm availability in digitized production environments?

In order to address the current research gap, we develop a model to assess the IT security risk of availability incidents in digitized production environments and support decision-making. We use Bayesian Networks, Bayesian Attack Graphs, and stochastic distributions for risk analysis in digitized production environments to illustrate how an attack can spread within the information network, and to measure the associated damage within both the information and production networks. The paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we provide an overview of relevant literature and determine design objectives for our approach. In Section 3, we introduce Bayesian Networks and Bayesian Attack Graph as the main modeling methods and develop our model for the assess ment and analysis of availability incidents in digitized production en vironments. Section 4 reports on our evaluation activities, following methods suggested by Sonnenberg & vom Brocke [17]. We conclude by discussing our contribution to research and practice, the limitations of our study, and potential areas for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background and design objectives

## 2.1. Digitalization in production environments and hybrid value creation

Driven by recent trends and initiatives in digitalization – for ex ample, CPPSs, smart factories, and IIoT – manufacturing companies are increasingly integrating IT services into their production environments. These services improve companies' ability to collect data, control the production processes, and generate data for new applications. Thereby, (I)IoT can be defined as a variety of smart things, such as tags (including radio frequency identification (RFID) tags), sensors, actuators, mobile phones, etc., which can interact with each other to reach common goals [18]. For example, RFID and wireless sensor networks (WSN) enable the advent of CPPSs – platforms that autonomously compute, control, and monitor physical production processes, and which thus form the foundation of the IIoT. From a business model perspective, manufacturing companies must (a) adapt their business models by developing new service oferings based on hybrid value creation for their customers, and (b) engage in digitized value network in order to retain competitiveness or lever revenue potentials [19]. ‘Hybrid value creation’ can thus be defined as the creation of added value via the combination of specific resources, capabilities, intelligent digital technologies, and the internet-based interconnection of companies and customers through integrated, data-based product-service bundles [4].

Prior research provides various approaches with which to analyze the structure of digitized production environments based on the IIoT, such as smart factories. For (I)IoT applications, for example, a threelevel architecture [20] with the perception, network, and application levels is a widely used model [20,21]. In this case, the perception level aims to collect data using sensor technologies (e.g., RFID tags and sensor networks) [22] and controlling objects. The network level enables information exchange by processing and transferring data [21] gathered by the perception level to applications. Finally, the application level supports business services [23] and decision-making by analyzing received data [21]. In the case of smart factories, prior research outlines that their architecture is predominantly comprised of two interconnected networks: a production network, including production components such as industrial robots or machines, and an information network, consisting of hardware, software, and service components [15]. Service components in the manufacturing context include IT services, such as enterprise resource planning (ERP), and manufacturing execution systems (MESs). In line with prior research, we model Digitized Production Environments as a network (DPE network) consisting of two interconnected networks: a production network, with production com ponents involved in deploying a physical product, and an information network, with service components that support the value creation process. We follow Gössinger [24] and characterize IT services incorporated in production environments according to the potential dimension (i.e., a service is the ability to deliver a specific performance), the process dimension (i.e., the deployment and utilization of a service occur simultaneously), and the outcome dimension (i.e., a service is an intangible product). In addition, ofering hybrid product-service bundles requires further IT services which considerably enhance the classical manufacturing information network.

Companies' goal of digitalizing their production environments in order to enable highly flexible and resource-saving production, and to ofer innovative digital solutions which accompany physical products [2], increases the complexity of smart factories and IIoT networks. Firstly, integrating IT-related services, such as autonomous control systems, into production systems in order to monitor and optimize the production processes leads to a stronger interconnection between information and production networks [15]. Moreover, connecting the ‘own’ network with external market players (e.g., value creation partners) – which is now a prerequisite for hybrid value creation – also increases the network complexity. To handle this complexity, a structured modeling approach is required. This approach must involve transparency about network structure, depicting the network compo nents and dependencies between them. Thus, we formulated the fol lowing design objective:

DO1 Network modeling: the approach should enable the modeling of digitized production environments based on the IIoT, such as smart factories. A more specific example is a DPE network consisting of in terconnected information and production networks. The modeling approach should enable both a graphical representation of the DPE net work, in order to visualize its structure, and a formal representation of the DPE network, in order to analyze and compare diferent network designs (e.g., simulation-based analyses). Moreover, the approach should be able to handle an increasing number of components, and the dependencies between them.

## 2.2. IT security in digitized production environments

Regarding the diferent types of cyber-attacks, we base our study on the well-known CIA triad [25] which defines three main information security goals: confidentiality, integrity, and availability. Confidentiality is ensured when only authorized parties can view the data [26]. Integrity means that the data is complete and that it refers correctly to an underlying situation or fact [26]. Finally, availability is ensured when a system is accessible and usable when required [26]. Although the implementation of IT services in production environments addresses all three security goals, in our model, we focus on availability incidents which threaten a value creation process and can result in production delays, complete stops in production [25], or impairments of service delivery [5]. Thus, availability incidents can harm the value added through both production processes and services. Owing to the real-time requirements of industrial productions systems, their availability must be ensured in order to prevent a production delay which results in the loss of productivity and revenue [2]. For example, production systems should be able to make autonomous decisions, such as routing and scheduling decisions in the production process, which increases the importance of their real-time availability [27]. Finally, IT services mostly run on cloud-based infrastructures whereby availability is also defined as a major task [7], in particular, for IT services provided to the customer within the hybrid value creation. We are aware that the security goals of confidentiality and integrity should also be considered to ensure a comprehensive IT security management. However, modeling all CIA triad incidents is a very complex task and would overload this paper, as diferent approaches are needed to measure and analyze the impacts of these incidents. Consequently, we focus on modeling avail ability incidents as a first step in reducing the model's complexity, since availability is considered the most important security goal in digitized production environments [2].

Prior research $( \mathbf { e } .  g . , \ [ 2 , 1 8 , 2 0 , 2 8 ] )$ states that the digitalization of production changes IT security risks, as it increases the attack surface, the probability that attacks will be successful, and their impact. Thus, estimating the impact of IT security risks in digitized production en vironments becomes a challenge, as companies have to consider two efects. Firstly, cyber-attacks can spread within the information network thanks to the high level of interconnectivity between service components. Secondly, the damage caused by a cyber-attack not only reduces the functionality of the attacked network node itself but also can spread within both the information and production networks due to their interconnectivity. Thus, companies need an approach that addresses (a) possible attack vectors and routes within an information network and (b) the subsequent spread of damage within both information and production networks. Thus, we formulated the following design objectives:

DO2: attack modeling: the modeling approach should enable the modeling of cyber-attacks which harm the availability of IT service components, and their possible spread efects within an information network. Moreover, it should enable users to estimate (1) the probability of a successful attack based on the initial probability of a random IT service being successfully attacked, and (2) the probable spread of an attack and the likelihood that it will harm connected IT service components.

DO3: damage modeling: the modeling approach should allow users to estimate the damage which might result from IT availability incidents within the information network. Moreover, it should enable them to assess damage propagation within both the information and production networks in order to estimate the loss of value creation.

Existing approaches consider one of the two perspectives (attack modeling or damage modeling), but rarely integrate the two. Thus, methods such as graph theory [29], Bayesian Mathematics, Petri Nets [16], or other stochastic approaches [12] are already used by researchers to simulate cyber-attacks, in particular, attack sequences and patterns $[ 8 , 1 0 - 1 3 , 3 0 ]$ . For example, Poolsappasit et al. [11] use Bayesian Networks to investigate diferent attack paths in computer networks. However, they do not consider the impact of such attacks. In contrast to attack modeling, damage modeling has received less academic attention. Frigault et al. [9] show how single attacks on network components may cause major damage to the entire network by exploiting the dependency structure of the connected components. Nguyen & Nof [31] analyze how significant damage can be caused by disruption propagation within a complex network. However, they do not consider cyber-attacks and their propagation. One of the few exceptions is a study by Häckel et al. [15], which provides an approach for assessing IT availability risks in smart factory networks, considering both the attack and the spread of damage. However, the authors consider the spread of damage only within the production network, not the information network. To address this research gap, we develop a quantitative model that enables multi-stage attack analyses by explicitly considering the consequential damage that results from single attacks.

## 3. The model

In this section, we introduce Bayesian Networks and Bayesian Attack Graphs as the underlying method for our model. Thereby, we outline how the method can be used to model availability incidents in networks more generally. Thereafter, we define the key components of our model, along with their characteristics. These are used to model digitized production environments (DPEs) based on the IIoT, for example, smart factories, which constitute a DPE network consisting of interconnected information and production networks. Finally, we determine the key figures necessary to measure the availability and the deliverability of DPE network components.

## 3.1. Bayesian networks and Bayesian attack graphs for risk analysis

In our model, we mainly build on Bayesian Networks (BNs) and Bayesian Attack Graphs (BAGs) to address uncertainty about the primary target of an attack, and the possible paths an attacker might take in a network to reach his target. BNs are widely used to assess risks or vulnerabilities in IT networks by considering the potential of cyberattacks or other kinds of cybercrime [10–12,30]. In particular, their ability to build relationships between explicit variables and visible dependencies is very popular in modern risk management [12]. Thanks to their modular structure, BNs are also easy to use and update [32]. BNs enable networks to be graphically represented as nodes and connecting edges. This enables users to visualize the dependencies between the network components, which can then be analyzed. In the following, we describe our use of BNs and BAGs in the model.

Consider a finite set $C = \{ C _ { 1 } , . . . , C _ { n } \}$ of discrete random variables (components) where each variable $C _ { i }$ may take on values from a finite set (e.g., {Attacked, Not attacked}). On a mathematical level, BNs are directed acyclic graphs that encode a joint probability distribution over a set of random variables C. Formally, a BN for C is a pair $B = \langle G , \theta \rangle$ The first element $G$ is a directed acyclic graph whose nodes represent random variables $C _ { 1 } , . . . , C _ { n }$ and whose edges represent dependencies between the random variables. Further, θ denotes a set of parameters which characterize the BN, including $\theta _ { C _ { i } | \{ C _ { i } ^ { - } \} } = P _ { B } ( c _ { i } | \{ C _ { i } ^ { - } \} )$ for each value $c _ { i }$ of $C _ { i } ,$ where $\{ C _ { i } ^ { \phantom { } - } \}$ denotes the realization set of all parents (direct predecessors) of $C _ { i }$ in G. The formal definition of the probability distribution of BNs reads as follows:

$$
P _ {B} (c _ {1}, c _ {2}, \dots , c _ {n}) = \prod_ {j = 1} ^ {n} P \left(c _ {j} | \{C _ {j} ^ {-} \}\right).\tag{1}
$$

Described in a less formulaic way, BNs help us to understand how, with a certain probability, a change of state at node A afects a change of state at node B, assuming that there is a functional dependency between nodes A and B (A is “parent - node” or, more accurately, a directly connected predecessor of B). The first network component to be attacked is often not the attacker's primary target, and is only used to access the network and cause further damage to its peripheral compo nents, as the example of the Stuxnet Worm demonstrates [33]. We, therefore, use BAGs, which are based on BNs [13], to address uncertainty about the primary target of an attack. The use of BAGs is a common method for modeling complex multi-step attacks in networks. It identifies potential weak points [30,34] and possible paths an attacker may take through a network to reach a target. BAGs further enable predictions to be made about complex attack scenarios. They do so by changing the network's properties to create and reveal various diferent ways that an attack might harm the network [11]. In a standard BAG, every network component can be used for initial attacks and there is a certain probability that no predecessors will be attacked, especially if we consider components with an internet connection, which is natural in digitized production environments. Furthermore, the probability of a specific component being attacked increases if a direct predecessor has already been attacked, which captures possible spread efects [13]. In order to simulate attack paths, we use condi tional probabilities, as detailed in the following.

Assuming that B is a BN with n random variables $C _ { i }$ ∈{Inital attack,Spread attack,No attack}, we define the basic probability of an initial attack occurring in a predefined time period $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } }$ , a month or a quarter) and targeting component $C _ { i }$ with $p _ { i } ^ { \dot { i } n \dot { i } t a l } = P ( C _ { i } = I n i t a l \ a t t a c k ) .$ and the spread probability with $p _ { j i } { } ^ { s p r e a d } = P ( C _ { i } = S p r e a d$ attack| $C _ { j } = \{ I n i t a l$ attack, Spread attack}). This characterizes an attack's conditional spread probability from $C _ { j }$ to $C _ { i } ,$ where $C _ { j }$ denotes a direct predecessor of component $C _ { i \cdot }$

Moreover, we define this set of events as attack = {Inital attack,Spread attack} and determine the events' conditional probability using the following formula:

$$
P (C _ {i} \in \text { attack } \mid \{C _ {i} ^ {-} \}) = 1 - (1 - p _ {i} ^ {\text { initial }}) \prod_ {\{C _ {i} ^ {-} \}} (1 - P \Big (C _ {j} \in \text { attack } \mid \{C _ {j} ^ {-} \} \Big) p _ {j i} ^ {\text { spread }}),\tag{2}
$$

wherein $\{ C _ { i } ^ { \phantom { } - } \}$ denotes the set of all direct predecessors' components $C _ { j }$ of component $C _ { i }$ . In this sense, a BAG can be defined as BN $B ^ { \bar { A } \bar { G } } = \langle G , \theta \rangle$ with $\bar { \theta } = \{ p ^ { i n i t a l } , P ^ { s p r e a d } \}$ , where $p ^ { i n i t a l } \in [ 0 , 1 ] ^ { n }$ and $P ^ { s p r e a d } \in [ 0 , 1 ] ^ { n \times n } .$ This model can increase its basic attack probability if one of a component's predecessors is attacked [9,12]. In words, Eq. (2) describes the increase in the probability that a network component will be attacked if any of its predecessors have been attacked (cf. product notation part in Eq. (2)). We use this characteristic as single attacks on predecessors can have major efects on successors, even though these successors will have other predecessors which have not been attacked. Since we focus on modeling the impact an attack will have on the value creation process, we do not diferentiate between internal threats, such as social engineering attacks, and external threats, such as a distributed denial of service (DDoS) attack. Companies can account for these diferences by adjusting the probability values of initial and spread attacks.

Furthermore, they can consider existing IT security measures in the network when parametrizing the spread probability $p _ { j i } ^ { \ s p r e a d }$ . Low values for $p _ { j i } ^ { ~ s p r e a d }$ indicate strong security measures which decrease the spread probability of an attack. High values for $p _ { j i } ^ { ~ s p r e a d }$ convey weak security measurements and provide a high spread probability after the initial attack. In line with related research $[ 1 0 , 1 1 , 3 0 ]$ , we make the following basic assumptions when using our model:

• The network structure does not change during a simulation.

All attackers have the same expectations of success or failure, regardless of their skills or technical background.

However, BNs and BAGs also have certain limitations. One limitation that is often mentioned is the feedback loops' inability when designing the underlying network. Consequently, it is not possible to describe a mutual dependency between components. When loops still occur throughout the network, the less important arcs are mostly ignored [32] and only the dominant direction of an information flow is considered [18]. However, in the context of IT security, BNs follow the monotony principle, which states that attackers never relinquish rights once obtained and their attack paths must, therefore, be acyclic [11–13]. Since our approach aims to model the spread of cyber-attacks and the associated damage, we follow the literature and focus on modeling meaningful attack pathways rather than considering a bidirectional information flow between components. Nevertheless, the lit erature presents initial suggestions of probabilistic graphical models with cycles for feedback loops, which are unfortunately dificult to parameterize [31,35]. Another limitation concerns modeling network with many nodes $( \mathrm { i . e . , } > 1 0 0 \mathrm { n o d e s } )$ , with the property of each network node having a multitude of diferent states. The analysis of such networks can be a highly complex or time-consuming [12]. However, approaches which provide an approximate result can help deal with this challenge [12]. Despite these limitations, BNs suit our approach as they allow the user to describe the dependencies in DPE networks and to understand how attackers can move through these networks. The fol lowing sub-chapters introduce availability as a measure of whether the technical IT network is intact, and deliverability as a measure of the network's ability to add value.

## 3.2. Modeling a DPE network

## 3.2.1. Key components for modeling a DPE network

In order to model IT security risks for availability incidents in a DPE network, we need to identify the key types of components and their characteristics as basic nodes and edges within the BAG. To do so, we follow prior research on smart factory and IIoT architectures.

Service components. We define the nodes service components as IT services within the information network of a smart factory or within the network and application layer of an IIoT. Service components support production components and can represent software such as manufacturing execution systems (MES) or enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems [20,23].

Information flow. We define the edge information flow as informational dependency between two diferent components, which is symbolized with an edge running in the direction of the information flow. This means that two components in our model can share information or data (e.g. via 4G, Wi-Fi, or Bluetooth [23]) only when they are linked with an information flow edge.

Production components. We define production components as nodes within the production network of a smart factory, or the perception layer of an IIoT, which are mainly involved in producing a physical product. Thereby, we conceptualize whole assembly lines and plants as production components, rather than as single production entities like robots or single machines. We also categorize production facilities – such as warehouses – and construction and purchase departments involved in deploying a physical product as production components [23]. Since production components are not part of the IT network and, thus cannot be directly harmed by a cyberattack, it holds for all production components $C _ { i } ^ { P }$ that $p _ { i } ^ { \dot { m i t a l } } = 0$

Production flow. During the production process, a product runs through various production components as it develops from a combination of primary products to a final product. Based on Porter [1], we define the edge production flow as the input-output dependency between production components with an edge along the direction of the production flow. Although the need for flexibility transforms production chains into modular entities, it is still true that linear production processes create value as the value diference between inputs and the final products [36]. Since production flows are not part of the IT network and, thus, cyber-attackers cannot use them for propagation, it holds for all production components $C _ { i } ^ { P } , C _ { j } ^ { P }$ that $p _ { j i } { } ^ { s p r e a d } = 0$

The key components introduced above represent one possible way of modeling a DPE network. Companies can adjust our components or use other approaches to enable them to consider specific requirements. For example, they can model the information flow as a service com ponent, which would allow them to consider the information flow as attackable in order to estimate the impact of attacks, such as man-inthe-middle attacks or spoofing.

## 3.2.2. The availability of service components (attack modeling)

Since availability is one of the main goals of information security [25] and is essential in digitized production environments, we model a cyber-attack on a network component as an attack on the component's availability. Let G a BAG with n ∈ ℕ components (nodes) $C _ { i } .$ For these components, we follow Häckel et al. [15] and define the binary variable $A _ { i } \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ representing the availability of a component $C _ { i }$ and set $A _ { i } = 0$ if a successful attack has occurred. Although there may be scenarios in which a small, temporary reduction in availability can be tolerated (e.g., the short unavailability of a system's marginal component may not spread unexpectedly to other important components), we model a component's availability as a binary variable. We assume that short-term failures can be compensated and, therefore, we only consider long-term failures in this definition. Owing to the BAG's interdependencies, we obtain the conditional probability for the availability $A _ { i } 0 \mathrm { f }$ the component $C _ { i } ,$ based on the availability of the predecessors, by applying Formula (3) as follows:

$$
\boldsymbol {P} \left(\boldsymbol {A} _ {i} = \boldsymbol {1} \mid \left\{\boldsymbol {A} _ {i} ^ {-} \right\}\right) = \boldsymbol {1} - \boldsymbol {P} \left(\boldsymbol {C} _ {i} \in \text { attack } \mid \left\{\boldsymbol {C} _ {i} ^ {-} \right\}\right),\tag{3}
$$

where $\{ A _ { i } ^ { - } \}$ denotes the set of availability values for all direct predecessors of the component $C _ { i } .$ . With the use of BAGs, we can see that an availability incident at a component increases the probability of availability incidents occurring at all connected components and directly influences the afected component's functionality. The associated loss of availability can then spread and harm further components. Particularly in complex, interconnected networks that undergo multiple redundancies, an availability incident at one component can threaten the connected components. Since production components are (usually) not part of the IT network (cf. definition above), the availability of production components is always 1. Companies which have already implemented mitigation measures can fine-tune our model by, for example, adjusting the probabilities of initial and spread attacks for particular components as defined in Eq. (2) – for example, when systems are redundant or if a fail-safe mechanism is in place [37].

## 3.2.3. The deliverability of service and production components (damage modeling)

With the availability modeling approach presented in Section 3.2.2, we only model the propagation of the attacker and not the (spread) damage which occurs within digitized production environments. We now develop a procedure for measuring the impact of a cyber-attack on the value creation process within a DPE network. We therefore assume that a component's availability directly impacts its deliverability, meaning that, when a component is successfully attacked and not available $( A _ { i } = 0 ) ,$ , its deliverability is also afected. Since we assume that all DPE network components are connected, the non-availability and therefore the limited deliverability of one component can afect the deliverability of all its successors' components, even though their availability is not afected. In the following, we define the deliverability of a service component $D _ { i } ^ { S } \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ and the deliverability of a production component $D _ { i } ^ { P } \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ . In line with our definition of IT services, we assume that services can only be considered deliverable when their availability is not damaged $( A _ { i } = 1 )$ . This leads to the assumption that the deliverability of services can only take binary values. The value $D _ { i } ^ { S }$ of a service i will be 0 if the service is not available at a certain time, or 1 if it is available and all its predecessors have a full availability $( A _ { j } ^ { S / }$ $^ P = 1 )$ ). Services that are successfully attacked decline in their avail ability from 1 to 0. The deliverability $D _ { i } ^ { s }$ of a service component i depends on its availability $A _ { i }$ (if a service component has been attacked, the service stops), the availability of all direct predecessors (if a predecessor has been attacked, the service lacks the necessary data or information input and also stops), and the deliverability of all direct service component predecessors (if a predecessor has not directly been attacked, but were afected by the spread damage), and can be defined as:

$$

Porter [1] also stated that a component creates value by transforming input resources into higherlevel products. ## 4.1. Feature comparison and competing artifacts (Eval2)

In line with Eval2, we validated the design specifications of our model against the design objectives derived from the literature, and against competing artifacts (i.e., using the feature comparison method). In order to validate whether the design specification of our model suitably addresses the research question stated in Section 1, we discuss its characteristics with reference to the design objectives derived from the literature. We also compare the features of competing artifacts to the design objectives in order to assess whether our model contributes to existing knowledge. For competing artifacts, we selected approache from the field of IT security which cover the modeling of IT security incidents in digitized production environments based on the IIoT, such as smart factories. We briefly described these approaches in Section 1 and 2 when identifying the research gap. Since this analysis may not include all existing approaches, we aim to cover selected studies which represent the most recent relevant developments, and which best fit our research question. In the sensitivity analysis, we considered three main influencing factors which afect the impact of an availability incident. In the course of IoT solutions, (1) the degree of interconnection will increase in the next few years and change the severity of IT security incidents [41]. Further, (2) the number of IT services will also increase and, thus, so too will their share of the total value added [42]. Finally, (3) the impact of an availability incident mainly depends on the spread probability of cyber-attacks [43]. We examined how these three factors influence the impact of an availability incident. In our analysis, we addressed the degree of interconnection (1) by modeling networks with diferent maximum input grades (MIG). MIG refers to the maximum number of incoming directed edges a node may have in the network, which, in our example, denotes the sum of the incoming service or production components. This allows us to analyze networks with different degrees of interconnection, examining the spread efects. Thereby, a higher MIG provides more opportunities for attackers to move within the information network and, at the same time, increases the dependencies of the individual components, which can lead to a greater spread of con sequential damage. Based on the MIG, we generated random DPE net works, whereby the decision as to whether the component $C _ { j }$ has an incoming edge from $C _ { i }$ with $i < j$ is based on the Bernoulli distribution with probability $p = 0 . 5$ if the current input degree of $C _ { j }$ is smaller than MIG. Next, we modeled the share of services (2) in the network and the classification of each component (as a service component or a production component) as an urn problem by using the hypergeometric distribution. The “urn” thereby describes a set of classified components (production or service) from which the classification of each node is then randomly drawn (pull without putting back) and the component type is determined. The share of service components and the total number of components define a unique urn model, which can be used as a random generator to classify components. Further, we varied the spread probability (3) $p ^ { s p r e a d }$ of service components and assumed $p _ { j i } { } ^ { s p r e a d } = p _ { k l } { } ^ { s p r e a d }$ for all $j , i , k ,$ 1 with $i \neq j$ and $k \neq l .$ Thereby. low se: curity levels within the network lead to a high $p _ { j i } ^ { \ s p r e a d }$ which, in turn, leads to an increased attack probability for all components of the net work (see Eq. (3)).

As soon as an attacker enters the information network, the probability that he will continue this behavior increases. This, in turn, in creases the probability of an attack on all subsequent service components in the information network. Fig. 3 shows the resulting analytic attack probability $P ( A _ { i } = 0 | \{ A _ { i } ^ { - } \} )$ as a function of the number of predecessors of component $C _ { i }$ which have already been attacked. Based on the safe assumption that the attacker intends their attack to spread, we can say that the attack probability exhibits logarithmic growth. The danger of an infection by the attacker thus increases if many predecessor components in the network have already been infected. This fact is essential to the following analysis because, as the network's complexity increases, more and more scenarios arise in which extremely high values for $P ( A _ { i } = 0 | \{ A _ { i } ^ { - } \} )$ ) are achieved, thanks to the high number of dependencies.

## 5. Conclusion

## 5.1. Contribution

From an academic perspective, our study contributes to knowledge of IT security in digitized production environments. As our feature comparison in Section 4.1 shows, competing artifacts focus on the modeling of either cyber-attack spread [12] or damage spread [15], while our approach integrates both perspectives. Moreover, in contrast to approaches which focus on modeling physical damage to production networks [16], our approach considers spread efects within both information and production networks, which means it can account for the increasing share of IT services in the value creation process of manufacturers. With this approach, we lay the basis for further theoretical and empirical investigations of ways to measure the impact of cyber attacks in digitized production environments.

Practitioners can use our approach as a first step in the analysis of how the digital transformation of their production environments afects their IT security risk landscape. Our model enables the user to visualize and formalize a DPE network in order to cover interdependencies within the network, and, thus, increase the transparency. It can also be used to document the current structure of the DPE network. As shown in Sections 4.2 and 4.3. practitioners can implement our model as a software prototype, and simulate scenarios to analyze various DPE network designs – for example, designs related to the degree of interconnection. They can also use it to identify the most vulnerable components in their value creation process. Since the increasing complexity of DPE networks makes it dificult to analyze the efectiveness of individual mitigation measures, our model can help practitioners allocate limited IT security budgets to available mitigation measures in an economically reasonable way. Based on our model, decision-makers can simulate availability incidents for various mitigation measures and calculate the associated impact on the deliverability of the DPE network. By comparing the expected loss before and after the implementation of a mitigation measure, a decision-maker can estimate the measure's economic benefits in terms of the reduction to the expected damage potential. Our model can also help to analyze diferent alternatives for the implementation of IT services (e.g., in-house IT services vs. IT outsourcing) and assess the associated financial con sequences from a risk perspective – for example, by supporting the calculation of changes in the expected loss. Since most IT services in digitized production environments run on cloud-based infrastructures, companies can use our model to support cost-benefit analyses for decisions on cloud outsourcing. Therefore, those in charge of making decision should set parameters for a concrete scenario in which to make said decisions. For example, individual service components provided by an external provider may be more or less likely to succumb to initial or spread attacks than those associated with services provided by an inhouse cloud. In this case, decision-makers can estimate the possible economic impact of diferent outsourcing decisions (e.g., external or private cloud) from an IT security-risk perspective. Finally, they can also perform a detailed risk analysis – for example, examining the risk for each machine within a smart factory.

## 5.2. Limitations and outlook

Although our approach provides initial insights into IT security risk analysis for availability incidents in digitized production environments, and although it serves as a starting point for further investigations, our approach has certain limitations. Companies need to assess the model's parameters before using our model in a real-life production environment. For example, the values of attack probabilities should be adjusted according to existing mitigation measures (e.g., network redundancies, fail-safe mechanisms, or air gaps), industrial sector or market share (e.g., companies in selected industry sectors, or companies with specific know-how, might be at higher risk of attack). Since we aim to model the impact an attack has on the value creation process, we do not differentiate between various types of attack. Companies might account for the diferences between internal threats, such as social engineering attacks, and external threats, such as DDoS attacks, by adjusting the values for attack probabilities. Owing to the lack of empirical data, we use estimates derived from the literature to determine the parameters and dependencies of our model. Further risk estimation research – which studies actual events that have occurred, and their impact on availability – could make an important contribution, as this would contribute to broadening the empirical basis for parameterizing our model. Moreover, empirical data provided by further research could be compared with the simulation results from our model in order to prove our model's validity. However, collecting empirical data is extremely dificult in this context, as companies usually have strict confidentiality agreements and have serious concerns about reputation loss should news of a successful attack go public. Furthermore, the Bayesian networks that provide the underlying concept for our model are designed as acyclic graphs, meaning that all Bayesian networks are directed graphs (i.e., no loops within the graph). Since the IoT is understood to be a complex and strongly heterogeneous network [20]. wherein in. formation is often shared bi-directionally, further research on approaches which allow the bidirectional modeling of information flows is needed. In our analysis, we also consider networks to be static, yet network structure can change, and the dynamic nature of IoT-based networks might be an interesting research subject. Our research focuses on availability incidents, and further research including confidentiality and integrity incidents might reveal how these afect the value creation process. Moreover, cyber-attacks not only cause outages of production IT systems but also of machines. Such attacks may lead to ineficiency in the system, rather than functional loss. In these cases, ineficiency results from the machines' downtime. Adding the heterogeneous recovery time for IT systems or machines to our model might help to better represent the real-life system. As things stand, the time component is indirectly accounted for via the service-component classification (less time-critical. moderately time-critical. and highly time-critical).

Finally, our model only illustrates the value creation process for a single product (line) or service, and, in future studies, should be adjusted and applied to multiple products in real-life large-scale networks. Despite these limitations, our work provides an initial approach for the analysis of IT security risk in the value creation processes in highly interconnected, digitized production environments. Thus, our study provides a solid approach that can serve as basis for further research aimed at closing this key research gap.
