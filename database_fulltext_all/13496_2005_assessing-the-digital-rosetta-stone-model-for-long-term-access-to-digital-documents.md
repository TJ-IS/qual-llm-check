# Assessing the Digital Rosetta Stone Model for Long-Term Access to Digital Documents

ALAN R. HEMINGER AND DON M. KELLEY

ALAN R. HEMINGER has been an Associate Professor of Information Resource Management at the Air Force Institute of Technology (AFIT) since 1994. Prior to that, Dr. Heminger was an Assistant Professor of Management Information Systems at Indiana University School of Business from 1989 to 1994. In addition to teaching courses, advising theses, and conducting research, Dr. Heminger has provided many consultations to various Air Force units, including the office of the Air Force Chief Information Officer, Air Force Communications and Information Center, Air Force Material Command, Air Force Research Laboratory, and Air Force Special Operations Command. He received a B.A. in Philosophy from the University of Michigan in 1966, an M.S. in Educational Psychology at California State University–Hayward in 1978, and a Ph.D. in Management Systems at the University of Arizona in 1989.

DON M. KELLEY is an active duty officer in the United States Air Force. He undertook research related to this study in partial fulfillment of the requirements for a Masters in Information Resource Management, which he earned in March 2001.

ABSTRACT: Information that is stored digitally is at risk for being irretrievably lost if either the methods used to retrieve the bitstream or the methods used to interpret the bitstream are lost. The Digital Rosetta Stone (DRS) model was developed as a conceptual framework for capturing and maintaining the methods necessary to retrieve digital information stored on obsolete media and to properly interpret it, though the software used to create it may also be obsolete. However, the community of those professionals concerned with this issue had not yet assessed this conceptual model. This study used the Delphi method to explore these issues with those responsible for maintaining access to digital data. Overall, the Delphi group expressed concerns about the practicality of developing the DRS, but agreed that it is an important concept that should be explored further. If found to be technologically feasible and economically desirable, the DRS could well lead to a long-term solution for recovering information that would otherwise be impossible to recover.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: access to digital documents, digital storage, permanence of stored information, technological obsolescence.

THIS STUDY ADDRESSES ACCESSING INFORMATION in computing devices that are many generations behind the current technology. People have been storing information since the dawn of human history. However, only relatively lately have people begun to store information in digital format. What makes this important is that, for the first time, we are beginning to store much of our historically important information in a way that cannot be read without specific, often esoteric, technologies that we may well lose.

The U.S. National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) was created as a repository for government documents and other historically significant materials. It is required to accept “sufficiently historical or otherwise valuable records” (44 USC § 2107). However, “NARA faces increasingly enormous quantities of records” [2, p. 2]. As if sheer volume were not enough of a problem, NARA is also receiving an increasingly diverse load of (digital) information created using ever-increasing varieties of software and stored in increasingly diverse media. This predisposes information to the threat of being permanently lost, even if it is under NARA’s watchful eye. The Archivist of the United States put it eloquently when he said, “It will be worse than sad if the marvelous technologies that are giving us a new information age outrun our ability to keep a record of it” [2, p. 3].

The increasing pace of technological development also creates accelerating technological obsolescence. It is this accelerating obsolescence that is threatening our knowledge of methods used to retrieve and properly display our stored digital history [2, 3]. Accelerating obsolescence is the quickly shortening life span of widely used storage and computing devices (including both hardware and software). The life span shortening occurs simply because the time between technological generations is becoming shorter. The impetus for this race occurs when companies (both producing and consuming) get caught up in a paradigm of needing to push the technological envelope in order to stay competitive. Many see an accelerated rate of increase in this race.

Even if a decision is made to move all stored information to a new generation of storage technology, we simply cannot move all of our data to a current form in a current storage environment before the next generation of technology makes the current one obsolete. As one systems librarian puts it, “All those state-of-the-art machines, software packages, and compression techniques seem old before the boxes and shrink-wrap even hit the landfill” [8, p. 56]. Regardless of the chosen solution, the problem is compounded by an exponentially growing data set.

Limited resources preclude efforts to preserve all available information objects. Document retention schedules set up by the U.S. government recognize the value of maintaining legal documents for long periods of time. However, documents deemed less significant are usually destroyed or kept for only a short while. The criteria for determining document value can change depending on many and varied factors such as world events or high-profile lawsuits. This can lead to information being lost that is later deemed important but unrecoverable. In trying to upgrade archival data at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), Zuzga [14] realized that there were some tapes that, although not currently valuable, may be of extraordinary value in the future.

## Searching for Solutions

A NUMBER OF PEOPLE HAVE PROPOSED strategies to address the threat to our stored digital information [11, 13]. NARA’s and others’ efforts to date have been labor intensive and expensive. However, there is also a lack of agreement in the community as to which is the best way to proceed [5].

Several methods—migration, refreshing, technology museums, and so on—have been proposed by different researchers [11, 13]. Unfortunately, there is no agreedupon single strategy that will satisfy all of the above criteria [5, 8]. A survey of some of the methods that have been designed for long-term access is presented here.

## Media-Based Approaches

One strategy is to make a copy of each digital document on some other media, such as paper. This approach might be at least technically doable if all digital documents were simply electronic versions of paper documents. However, more and more digital documents are not printable in static form, because they contain nonprintable aspects, such as sound or video, or contain more complex data relationships, such as queries against a database. Nonetheless, for those digital documents that can be printed, suggestions have been made to use this as a means for long-term record storage. Following are some specific strategies.

## Printed Hard Copy on Paper

Because paper tends to be more stable than magnetic, optical, and other electronic media, some have proposed printing onto paper any of the information to be saved. A major flaw to printing everything out is that there is simply too much to print. Another flaw is that printing the information explicitly puts it in a nonelectronic medium that is time-consuming to copy, manage, store, and so on. The storage requirements for the roughly 250 megabytes worth of unique data for every man, woman, and child on the earth would require 50 billion trees per year [12].

## Micrographics

This solution, promoted by Willis [13], seeks to remedy the problem of requiring vast amounts of paper resources. This strategy is similar to printing information on paper except the medium is plastic and the information is miniaturized. Some of the major advantages are that it is already used as an archival medium with well-documented standards, it is easy to read, and it can store a high resolution of detail [13]. There are some disadvantages, however. The medium has to be physically handled to access the information. It can become scratched in storage or use. The copy quality is degenerative (it loses about 10 percent of resolution). There are also some problems with the transfer process [13]. Items “born digital” are those things that are created electronically and may not be directly transferable to microfilm. These digitally born objects may include video, audio, and databases as well as many other object types.

## Nickel Slugs

Because paper and plastic tend to deteriorate when handled and are subject to limited environmental conditions, some have suggested engraving the digital information on nickel slugs [7, 11]. This, as well as any other method to transfer the information to nonelectronic media, makes it far more difficult to access the information. Since the information is stored by engraving the information, these metal slugs offer the unique attribute of lasting for thousands of years. The storage capacity for the HD-ROM, produced by Norsam Technologies Inc., is 200 gigabytes per disk and expandable to the petabyte range. At this rate, though, it would take 10,000 of these disks just to preserve one year’s worth of data. However, although engraving information on nickel slugs may address the longevity of the medium, it still does not guarantee the ability to recover the bitstream or to interpret that bitstream once recovered. Not all strategies are medium dependent, however.

## Standards-Based Approaches

Several strategies regarding a standards-based solution have been posited. The major tenet of each of these strategies is that it is easier to maintain access to data if only one standard or a few standards are used. The universal preservation format (UPF) was proposed by David MacCarn at the WGBH Education Foundation in Boston. The UPF is designed to reduce the confusion caused by the increasingly large number of formats available. It also includes machine-independent algorithms encapsulated within the stored media. Two strategies, the Bento specification and the open media format, are media technologies that are similar to the UPF concept. The major disadvantage of using a single format for storing all digital information is that “no computer technical standards have yet shown any likelihood of lasting forever—indeed most have become completely obsolete within a couple of software generations” [1, p. 3]. The Time Capsule File System, proposed by Zuzga [14], is a similar approach to the UPF. It specifies a format that is “very similar to the RFC-822 format used for electronic mail” [14, p. 16]. It suffers from the same drawback as the UPF in that no single standard is likely to apply to technologies developed in 20 years and beyond. Some time into the future, scientists may find ways to process information based on an octal number system instead of binary.

## Other Approaches

## Technology Museums

Another approach is to store every generation of technology, keep the machines in working order, and run them with skilled operators. This is referred to as a technology museum. This approach would benefit by extending the longevity of computer systems and their original software to keep documents readable [11]. “Because originals are so important, [NARA has] a kind of museum of equipment that will work or can be modified to work” [2, p. 2]. This works on a small scale and for a short time, but for MIT, which has a tremendous amount of information, the museum concept has not been as successful as MIT had hoped [14]. A disadvantage of a technology museum is that “the hardware and software for digital media change so rapidly that it would be impossible to keep an up-to-date . . . museum” [8, p. 60]. Bearman [1] agrees with Rothenberg [11] that there are problems with technology museums. In fact, Rothenberg argues that “even if obsolete computers were stored carefully, maintained religiously, and never used, aging processes such as [metal migration and dopant diffusion] would eventually render them inoperative; using them routinely to access obsolete digital documents would undoubtedly accelerate their demise” [11, p. 13].

## Refreshing

This strategy is the one that is probably most often employed [8]. It “involves transferring digital materials to a new medium, for instance, changing from 5¼-inch floppies to CD-ROM, or from CD-ROM to DVD” [8, p. 58]. NARA has procedures in place for refreshing: “Whenever any of the digital media in our custody shows signs of deterioration, or whenever they reach 10 years of age, we recopy the records to new media” [2, p. 3]. While this approach addresses the media instability problem, it does not fundamentally address formatting problems. This method ensures that we will be able to access, for example, a WordStar 1.0 document, but without software to interpret the document, the file will be useless.

There is a major problem with refreshing as a best strategy, however. “Disk capacity has improved 1,000 fold in the last 15 years, consistent with Moore’s Law, but the transfer rate MBps has improved only 40x in the same time” [4, p. 3]. This means that disk capacity has a growth rate of 25:1 when compared with the transfer rate of data. The effect of this phenomenon is that our ability to store information is far exceeding our ability to transfer it to the next technological generation.

## Migration

Migration involves updating the format of the old digital object into what is currently used. Returning to the WordStar 1.0 document example, migration involves translating the information and storing it in Word 2000 format. This method is used frequently, but Rothenberg [11] points out that trying to undertake such a strategy results in labor- and time-consuming tasks that may still result in less than complete conversion. In other words, migrating all stored data to each new generation becomes increasingly infeasible, introduces the possibility for new losses, and quickly borders on the impossible.

## The Hybrid Approach

This strategy, proposed by Don Willis, among others, suggests that for information that is not “born digital,” preserving both the electronic version and a micrographic version mitigates the disadvantages of each individual method [13]. This approach is not without its own unique drawbacks. It would, in essence, triple the amount of already exponentially growing information—one set of information would be the original, the second set the digital copy, and the third set the micrographic copy. Using standard compression methods, there are still about 240 terabytes of printed information yearly. Even though this is a tremendous amount of information, it is a tiny amount of the total information produced yearly.

## Encapsulation

Rothenberg [11] proposes that metadata and other information be encapsulated, or stored with, the digital information object. The other information would include the original executable software and operating system along with any other pertinent data files. One factor that is both an advantage and a disadvantage is the inclusion of the software. On one hand, the encapsulated digital object would have the appropriate software to access the data. On the other hand, because every digital object would require its own copy of the software and operating system, it would require as many instances of the software as digital objects—even if all of the digital objects were at a single repository. Current operating systems require several hundred megabytes worth of hard drive space, and typical digital object software also requires hundreds of megabytes. Storing this nearly half of a gigabyte for one file that can range in the tens of kilobytes seems inefficient. With the exponential data growth, maintaining individual copies of massive software sets seems infeasible.

## Emulation

This strategy uses hardware and software emulators to access information stored on obsolete media and in obsolete formats. Rothenberg [11] suggests that there are several benefits to the approach, and argues that this is the best, if not the only, approach. The first benefit is that the emulators only need to be developed one time. However, this brings up the question, “What happens when the system the emulator runs on becomes obsolete?” A second benefit is that using the emulation of original software and hardware is the only way to accurately recreate the original digital environment. This will give the digital information object the same “look and feel” it would have had using the obsolete technology. The negative aspect is that each emulation would also have to be maintained. Zuzga [14] argues that there would be a “serious continuing cost” if emulation was used.

One or more of these or some other strategies may well be put in place to help retain our digital heritage. However, even with this, there will be a need to recover stranded digital information.

## Capturing Metadata

Another strategy for knowledge preservation is to capture the information about storage devices and software algorithms when it is readily available, and use that information later to recover and display digital documents. Generally, this means to collect the metainformation about the hardware and software used to create a document while the storage device and software are still in general use. If one waits until the technology is no longer current, it may be too late. For example, when trying to recover the information stored on an eight-track punched paper tape, Robertson [10] was not able to map out the entire storage technique, even though the technology was relatively recent and the company that designed the technique was still a current industry leader. If the relatively simple task of being able to read paper tape is difficult, then the task of reading magnetic and other high-technology media without the proper know-how is truly daunting. Now, imagine the problem if it occurs 100 years later.

## The Digital Rosetta Stone

ROBERTSON [10] EXPLORED THE LONG-TERM ACCESS PROBLEM and suggested an approach to retrieving and interpreting data stored on obsolete media, based on capturing the metadata. He called this model the Digital Rosetta Stone (DRS). Robertson’s model was conceptual in nature and did not include details of how best to implement it. This study will start with Robertson’s model, and using the Delphi technique to gather information from experts in the field, will explore features of this model, and add detail to its conceptual framework.

## Focus of the Digital Rosetta Stone

Recognizing that many strategies for systemic knowledge preservation are likely to be employed by many groups, the DRS is designed for those instances where those other strategies fail. The DRS does not attempt to address recovery of information from media that has degraded beyond the point of being readable. The DRS was designed to be a last-ditch effort to recover stranded information, on storage devices where the bitstream is present, but the knowledge of either how to recover the bitstream or interpret the bistream has been lost. It is therefore to be used as a digital archaeology tool—recovering information that, until now, has been beyond reach.

## Digital Rosetta Stone Components

The DRS is composed of three major components that are necessary to access digital information stored on obsolete storage devices or in obsolete formats—knowledge preservation, data recovery, and document reconstruction [10] (Figure 1). Developing each of these processes accurately is critical to the success of the DRS. The first major process, knowledge preservation, is addressed by the metaknowledge archive (MKA).

## Metaknowledge Archive

Robertson [10] proposed developing a repository of information necessary to both recover the data and reconstruct the document, which he calls a metaknowledge archive. This archive would be created through the act of knowledge preservation and would form the foundation for the other processes of the DRS model. In fact, without this MKA, a file stored on an obsolete medium or in an obsolete format would be completely useless, even if the bits were preserved [14]. We know the most about a digital object at the time we create it, and we cannot know for sure how we will want/need to access it in the future. Therefore, we should capture as much information about the digital object as we can at the time of creation.

Knowledge preservation is the process of collecting the information on the data storage and formatting techniques used by the designers and builders of information storage and processing devices. This includes the technical aspects of what constitutes a bit of information on this device, how it is arranged on the device, and how it is accessed. Information is also collected from systems and applications software that identifies the file structures, along with all information necessary to recover and read the stored digital document. The MKA would be developed over time by a proposed Digital Rosetta Stone Office (DRSO), and would be made available to technicians to use to recover digital documents. The MKA obviates the need for the original designers and creators to be present for data recovery. The Defense Information Infrastructure Common Operating Environment (DII COE) developed and operated by the Defense Information Systems Agency (DISA) is one example of how software and hardware functionality is mapped out and categorized. Although it does not capture exactly the same information that would be in the MKA, the DII COE works much in the same fashion as the DRSO would in order to create and maintain the MKA. Rothenberg [11], Bearman [1], and Pace [8] all stress that the effort necessary for an effective MKA is significant. Storage techniques can be quite complex, not to mention that current and cutting-edge technologies are fiercely protected—companies stake out and defend proprietary market advantages to protect their profit [9]. One side benefit of developing the MKA is that it could also help in other cases by providing information on standards or hardware and software used and pointing people to places where appropriate hardware and software can be found.

## Recovering the Bitstream

Armed with the knowledge of storage techniques, recovery technicians can begin. Data recovery is the process of retrieving the bitstream from the outdated and obsolete medium and moving it to a current storage device. If necessary, the information in the MKA could be used to create a new medium access device. The access method may be altogether different than the original device used. For instance, instead of building a CD-ROM drive to recover a bitstream, the DRSO workers might use a high-resolution scan of the CD and software to interpret the image [10]. This may help if the media is fragile and may not survive traditional data access methods.

## Interpreting the Bitstream

Once the bitstream is accessible to the modern computing environment, document reconstruction can take place. This is where the bitstream—manipulated using the knowledge of formatting techniques—is displayed as the original digital information object. Depending on how well the MKA has accurately and thoroughly captured all of the storage and formatting techniques, the reconstructed document can be an exact representation of the original document.

## Output

The result of going through each of the stages of the DRS would result in a recovered digital information object. Given the variety of file formats, the reconstructed object could be an encapsulated document containing metadata or a simple ASCII text file. This flexibility gives the DRS the hardiness to be a long-term solution.

## Research Question

THE RESEARCH QUESTION TO BE EXPLORED in this study is: “Is the DRS model a potentially useful method for maintaining long-term access to digital documents?” To answer this question, the following subquestions were developed, which were sent to the study participants for round one of the Delphi group.

1. What are the strengths of the DRS model?

2. What are the areas in the DRS model that need improvement?

3. What is missing from the DRS model?

4. How does the DRS model compare with other models in relation to maintaining long-term access to digital documents?

5. What are the underlying assumptions of the DRS model?

6. What steps are necessary to begin implementation of the DRS model?

7. Who should undertake development and implementation of the DRS? And why?

8. Do the experts have anything else to contribute that does not fit in the previous questions?

## Methodology

THE METHODOLOGY FOR THIS RESEARCH was a Delphi study. It involved a group of experts who provided their opinions and insights on a certain topic, thus it used nonprobability sampling. The ideas generated were then analyzed and condensed to determine a level of consensus. The Delphi technique was performed in a series of rounds. This iterative process of rounds and analysis continued until a consensus or stabilization point was reached. Stabilization indicates that interround answers have not changed beyond an appreciable amount. The opinions were typically annotated using a Likert-type scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” and from “very important” to “not important” [5, 6]. Because this research was inductive in nature, group consensus was not the only measure of “success.” The idea generation in and of itself was also useful to the DRS—the ideas submitted by experts in the field provided important insights into the strengths and weaknesses of the model.

## The Study Population

The population of interest in this study was the group of people called digital archivist experts (DAEs), whose knowledge about the subject area is key to exploring the potential of the DRS model. Those who constitute the digital archivist community include information technology (IT) specialists who are responsible for maintaining long-term access to digital information and are primarily librarians, digital archivists, and academicians. Individuals in this community may be found in a wide variety of industries and government agencies. Key technology-makers were considered because of their effect on technology. The organizations under consideration were asked to decide who was most suited to participate in this study.

For purposes of the technique, the individuals in the group knew which organizations were participating but did not know which organization made which comments—anonymity has been shown to increase creativity and idea generation [6].

## The Delphi Study, Round One

A description of the DRS model was sent out to each member of the group, along with instructions on how to participate in the study. They were asked to answer a set of questions about the DRS. These questions were the subquestions developed from the research question. The goal of this round was to generate as many ideas about the DRS as the experts felt appropriate. These ideas formed the basis for beginning to develop a consensus. The initial round took five weeks, after which the results were collated and organized for further group consideration in the second round.

Responses were received from seven of the eight participants that had agreed to take part in the study. There were a total of 66 statements, 54 of which were deemed to be unique. The responses were aggregated, with duplicate ideas merged.

## The Delphi Study, Round Two

In the second round, the members of the Delphi group were asked to assess the aggregated results from round one. They were also given an opportunity to refine their statements regarding the DRS model.

The opinions expressed during the second round were recorded in the form of Likerttype scales, with the opportunity at the end of each question to add additional comments as deemed appropriate. The experts submitted opinions about the issues in two ways. The first opinion related to whether or not the expert agreed with the statement. The second opinion dealt with whether or not the statement was important to the DRS. The opinions were recorded using a five-point Likert-type scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” for one scale and from “not important” to “very important” for the other. However, during analysis, the five divisions for each scale were reduced to three for simplicity in understanding the relative views of the participants. Thus, ratings 1 and 2 at the “strongly disagree” end of the scale were combined as low agreement, and ratings 4 and 5 at the “strongly agree” end of the scale were interpreted as high agreement, leaving item 3 in the middle to be interpreted as unsure. A similar process was used for the five levels of importance. Thus, ratings 1 and 2 at the “not important” end of the scale were interpreted as low importance and ratings 4 and 5 at the “very important” end of the scale were interpreted as high importance. Thus, each of the statements has two opinion parts: statement agreement and statement importance, with three options for each. This results in nine possible responses for each statement.

One of the goals with a Delphi study is to strive for consensus among the group members on the various issues under consideration. Consensus is a measure of how much people agree with one another. For this study, consensus for agreement and high importance was defined as a median of half a point above the middle of the Likert scale or higher. Consensus for disagreement or low importance was defined as a median of half a point below the middle of the Likert scale or lower. A group consensus that was in the middle of the Likert scale resulted in an unsure rating, for either importance or agreement. There was no distinction between a group consensus of unsure and nonconsensus. Nonconsensus was, for all intents and purposes, defined as a consensus of unsure.

Question 1: What are the strengths of the DRS model?

• It recognizes the importance of retaining access to objects, even as the technology for storing them becomes obsolete.\*

• It recognizes the importance of the digital object’s original characteristics.

• It allows for access, even if no readers for such a medium exist.\*

• It lays out a methodology to maintain the ability to reliably retrieve and reconstruct digital documents.

• It has the idea of a central registration of document types and specifications.\*

• It addresses fundamental issues of technically translating documents over time.

## Question 2: What are the areas in the DRS model that need improvement?

• The MKA should have its data distributed instead of centralized.

• The DRS has too narrow a view of what constitutes data recovery—that is, it should include a short-term perspective as well.

• It does not describe how to handle media degradation and media failure.

• Where possible, the DRS should integrate well with archiving.\*

• It needs to spell out a methodology for commercial cooperation.

• It needs to develop functional standards for chronological interoperability.

• The metaknowledge criteria need to be further developed.\*

• The DRS should focus more on the behavior of the software that interprets the bitstream rather than on the format of the physical medium.

## Question 3: What is missing in the DRS model?

• The need for self-describing metadata.\*

• It does not address media instability.

• The awareness of other long-term access efforts and its compatibility with them.\*

• An analysis of the cost-effectiveness of different approaches.

• It does not address what to do with the data after recovery.

• It does not address the problem of authenticity, or integrity, of the original document.\*

• It lacks the archival distinction between a document and a record.

• It does not address the context or order of the document in a collection.

• It does not address any legal-related issues such as intellectual property rights.

• It does not address verification and validation of the translation.\*

• It misses the importance that software plays in interpreting the digital documents by the fact that the behavior of such software is not implicit in a digital artifact’s format.\*

## Research Questions Answered

BASED ON HIGH LEVELS OF AGREEMENT of statements submitted by the Delphi group, the following answers were provided to the research questions.

## What Are the Strengths of the DRS Model?

There was strong agreement across the Delphi group that a main strength of the DRS is in recognizing the importance of retaining access to objects, even as the technology for storing them becomes obsolete. The members of the group recognized that we are facing a likely scenario in which we will not be able to access important digitally stored information at some time in the future, and it is important that we start now to give some thought as to how we can prepare for that day. They also agreed that a strength of the DRS is that it would be able to access such documents even though the technology that was used to store that information might no longer exist. There was also agreement that it would be good to have a central registry of document and storage types along with specifications, so that we will have a known place to go to when the day comes that we must locate the knowledge necessary to recover digital documents from obsolete media.

## Which Areas in the DRS Model Need Improvement?

Many in the Delphi group believe it is important that the development of the DRS should be integrated with archiving activities, so that as the documents are recovered, they can be appropriately prepared for archiving. A number of participants expressed concern that DRS does not address how to handle media degradation or failure. However, as it was designed, DRS was not intended to address this problem. As development of a DRS is undertaken, it may be important to address this issue to see if it should be incorporated into the DRS. There was also a belief that the criteria for the MKA will need to be further developed. This is a point that we agree with, because the criteria for the MKA will be crucial for developing the kind of system that will be likely to be successful in real-world situations. The group generally also supported the importance of emphasizing the behavior of software during retrieval of the bitstream and its interpretation.

## What Is Missing from the DRS Model?

The group identified a number of items that they believed were missing from the current DRS. These include a clear link to other long-term access efforts so that they can be developed in compatible ways. They also noted that the current DRS does not address the issues of authenticity or integrity of the original document, nor verification and validation of the translation. It is not clear at this point how these could best be brought into the model, but they certainly could be important issues that could have relevance to the task of maintaining long-term access to digital documents. Several felt that DRS misses the importance that software plays in interpreting the digital documents by the fact that the behavior of such software is not implicit in a digital artifact’s format. Others felt that the DRS should include the solution of self-describing media. In both of these areas, knowledge of the capabilities of self-describing media and the role of software in document reconstruction could be addressed by including support for them in the MKA.

## How Does the DRS Model Compare to Other Models in Relation to Maintaining Long-Term Access to Digital Documents?

The other schemas appear to be more focused on document preservation than bitstream recovery and document reconstruction. Document preservation is, of course, an important goal, one that is affected by such issues as media degradation. However, a preserved document that we have lost the ability to read or view leaves us in the position of having preserved ancient hieroglyphics, but not being able to read them.

## What Are the Underlying Assumptions of the DRS Model? If the DRS Makes the Assumption, Is It Valid? All Assumptions Identified for the DRS Were Considered Valid

The group agreed to several underlying assumptions about the DRS. They agreed that we are in a situation that requires that requires digital archaeology. They agreed that we are at risk of losing access to significant digital documents because the methods used to create them and store them will become lost to us. We should prepare now for that event. They agreed that, where possible, we should seek to be able to recover documents in their native format, since that is how they were created. They also emphasized that while some digital documents will be textual, it is important to address the issues related to broader categories of digital documents, such as sound and video, and complex information storage, such as databases and the queries that are used to pull information from them. Finally, they agreed with the underlying assumption that it will be important for the public and private sector to work together to successfully address the issues of gaining access to some of our documents stored in obsolete ways on obsolete storage media.

## What Steps Are Necessary to Begin Implementation of the DRS Model?

The group recommended that, first, a feasibility study should be performed. This study should include a total life cycle of the DRS, and should include technical, economic, and cultural feasibility. Part of the front-end work for the DRS would be to clarify how nontextual documents would be recovered. To do all of this would require building a consortium of experts to further build the model, to make it practical to implement. With these conditions, the group agreed that the DRS warrants significant investigation at this time.

## Who Should Undertake Development and Implementation of the DRS? And Why?

The group agreed that it would be advisable to create a consortium of those who store, archive, and use information. Although the ability to maintain long-term access to digital information is important to many groups and organizations, the consortium would be most likely to begin with those groups and organizations that recognize the looming problem, and are willing to begin working now to address the problem. Both private and public organizations should be represented in such a consortium.

## Do the Experts Have Anything Else to Contribute That Does Not Fit in the Previous Questions?

The group agreed that this concept needs to be brought into contact with others where substantive work in this field is being done. Among those who would be appropriate to involve would include archivists, such as those at NARA and major libraries who have a mission now to preserve information. It would also likely include people working for private organizations that have a stake in preserving digital information for many and varied reasons. Organizations such as producers of movie and video content would have reasons to become a part of such an effort.

## Discussion

THIS RESEARCH REPRESENTS AN INITIAL ASSESSMENT of the DRS model by the expert community. As recommended by Robertson [10], it presented the model to the archi-

val community and other interested parties. Some of their overall statements in the first round suggested that the overall impression of the DRS was negative. They addressed problems with the practicality of such an undertaking and whether the DRS’s focus may be misguided. However, when asked to address the research questions, their answers proved to be realistic but hopeful.

The group agreed that the DRS recognizes the importance of retaining access to digitally stored documents, and that the DRS could be a possible solution to at least part of the long-term access issue. They noted that many models deal with preservation and associated methods to ensure bit survival. The DRS assumes preservation of the bitstream on the storage medium, and focuses on how to recover and interpret that bitstream. They believed that the preservation and access community should continue to explore the concepts of the DRS with existing and potential preservation strategies in mind. Symbiosis between these preservation strategies and the DRS could then be nurtured.

The group also recognized the importance of having the right information in the MKA. Therefore, careful and thorough development of the MKA would be a necessary step for a successful DRS. Perhaps hardware and software engineers can uncover this metaknowledge through the research of old technical and scientific journals, as well as the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office or standards-based groups. Among the information that should be stored in the MKA, according to the group, would be knowledge of how best to handle and store the media until bitstream retrieval has occurred. Oftentimes, ignorance in the handling of sensitive objects can undo years of preservation. This is one area where working with preservation specialists could reduce the amount of media failure and slow the process of media degradation caused by abuse or neglect.

In developing the DRS, the experiences gained from others who have worked on access strategies needs to be taken into account. This could help overcome unforeseen obstacles in DRS development or help to build a more robust DRS.

The group suggested that self-describing media could help fill in for the MKA if there are any gaps or inconsistencies and could reduce reliance on a concept such as the DRS. This is where the medium itself has written instructions on how to recover the information. They also raised the issue that when reconstructing the original document, unless there is a human-readable copy or other known stored instance of it, verifying that the output is exactly the same as when viewed using the “native software” will be difficult. Of course, if one already has a readable copy of the document, the need for reconstructing it from the bitstream seems to be moot.

Efforts to develop the DRS and MKA will be expensive and time-intensive. A consortium would need to be developed to conduct extensive research in order to build a robust solution to the long-term access problem. It is therefore necessary to know what resources will be required, and when to best manage development and implementation. Because the DRS would be the link to our obsolete digital history, once developed, it would need to undergo significant testing and constant upkeep.

Involving a consortium of those who use and store information will benefit the development and implementation of the DRS. As previously mentioned, pitfalls can be avoided and a well-designed solution crafted by those who have worked on previous access or preservation strategies, as well as others who are the beneficiaries of the DRS process. Because the framework needs to be further developed, the DRS project needs to be brought into contact with those, as mentioned above, to clarify and develop the DRS. If the DRS is developed in isolation, it may not be as comprehensive as it needs to be.

## Conclusions

THE RESULTS OF THE DELPHI STUDY suggest that the DRS, or something like it, warrants further study as a means of maintaining access to the increasing volume of digital documents that we continue to create, and that we know are at risk due to technological obsolescence.

There is a limitation to the use of the Delphi technique in that it does not guarantee truth. Although it strives for consensus among experts, experts are not always right. It may be discovered, in time, that other new technological solutions or standards may better mitigate the risks that stranded data face.

As with Robertson’s study [10], this research does not test the technological feasibility of the DRS. It may turn out that other strategies may be more cost effective, or more popular with those who will be undertaking to save access to our digital data. That being said, this group of experts has agreed that the DRS warrants further investigation and it needs to be brought into contact with others where substantive work in this field is being done. An important next step may be to design and build a prototype of the DRS and demonstrate its technological feasibility with the help of software and hardware technologists. Showing its practicable efficacy could then lead to a fullscale development and implementation of the DRS.
