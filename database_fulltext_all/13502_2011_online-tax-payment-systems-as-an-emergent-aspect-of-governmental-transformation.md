# Online tax payment systems as an emergent aspect of governmental transformation

Thomas F. Stafford<sup>1</sup> and Aykut Hamit Turan<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Memphis, Memphis, TN, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Namuk Kemal University, Tekirdag˘, Turkey

Correspondence: Thomas F. Stafford, Fogelman College of Business and Economics, University of Memphis, Memphis, TN, U.S.A. E-mail: tstaffor@memphis.edu

## Abstract

The achievement of governmental transformation through the use of electronically delivered services is a worthy goal that requires significant planning and research to achieve. In order to reach transformational paradigm shifts in governmental operation, it will first be necessary to understand and optimize present governmental e-Service provisions. Of these, the revenue function of taxation is paramount. This paper describes factors related to the use and acceptance by accounting professionals of information technology intended to facilitate electronic tax filing systems. Though tested in the context of governmental tax management systems in Turkey, our findings on the use and acceptance of e-Tax systems are relevant and applicable to a great number of nations and contexts as the ongoing electronic transformation of the governmental revenue system contributes to efforts to transform governments through alternative services delivery venues and channels. We discover that intention to use automated systems as part of the governmental treasury function transformation is hindered by factors that mediate actual plans to do so, mostly in terms of normative pressures and perceptions of behavioral control, which training and education may well ameliorate. Hence, transformation of the treasury function in Turkey is only partially complete and will require additional support, direction and training on the part of the government in its interactions with the tax professionals who interact with the emergent automated system.

## Introduction

In the processes of governmental transformation through information and communication technologies (ICT), a significant sought benefit has been the realization of cost reductions and enhanced service levels (Foley & Alfonso, 2009) and a defining success factor has been the motivation of users and their subsequent frequency of use of such transformational e-Government systems (Gilbert et al., 2004; Hu et al., 2009). While the e-Government concept has passed from the novelty stage to the process of development and implementation in nations around the world, research is needed more than ever to guide the process of design, delivery, adoption and evaluation of subsequently delivered governmental e-Services in the transformation process (Esteves & Rhoda, 2008; Mitra & Gupta, 2008; Wang & Liao, 2008). Yet, such transformations are not always smooth or easy, and this study highlights some of the challenges present in the automation of a key aspect of governmental transformation – to wit, the revenue function. The focus of this paper is on the electronic conversion of the taxation system as one part of a transformational program underway in the nation of Turkey, where governmental transformation is being undertaken as a significant aspect of the nation’s European Union candidacy effort (EU, 2003, 2006).

Definitions of e-Government span a continuum from a basic characterization of ‘service delivery via Internet’, to ‘widespread use of Information and Communication Technologies in the public sector’, which is a much broader conceptualization ( Janssen et al., 2004; EU, 2006). Perhaps the most pertinent definition of the transformational processes offered in e-Government implementations comes from the Blackwell Dictionary of Management Information Systems, where e-Government is considered to involve the significant improvement and streamlining of a range of governmental service, notably including document access and filing and payment of fees, such as taxes and licensing charges (Ciborra, 2005). Indeed, organizational perspectives on control theory in the provision of e-Government typically highlight the centrality of electronic treasury operations as a crucial enabling process in the evolution toward transformational governing styles (Hood, 2006). By taking such treasury operations to electronic delivery channels in Turkey, many of the issues experienced in traditional interpersonal channels – such as the prevalence of bribes, poor service, favoritism and other trappings of the interpersonal bureaucracy – are avoided in this transformational move to electronic channels (Yılmaz & Kılavuz, 2002).

For that reason, in this study we focus on the process of electronic tax filing and payment as a potentially transforming aspect of e-Government, taking the all important treasury operation out of the interpersonal realm and evolving toward automated and electronically mediated delivery and service channels. Electronic tax reporting – called e-Tax – refers to the process of filing tax returns and supporting documentation with computers or other electronic devices, across either publicly switched or dedicated lines, or via the Internet (Pant et al., 2004). As noted, the move toward electronic tax management in Turkey overcomes many of the shortcomings found in traditional face-to-face bureaucracies, specifically related to inefficiencies and corruption (Yılmaz & Kılavuz, 2002).

Hence, in this case, the automation of the tax function can be seen as the transformational catalyst for changing governmental processes through which taxation is handled, as well as the manner in which the citizenry interacts with the government in that regard. A key aspect of transforming the Turkish tax system lies in the high degree of tax evasion and tax exile that arises from inefficient and error-prone human-mediated processes (Tecım, 2008). Hence, e-Tax systems transform governments such as that of Turkey’s by not only automating a vital treasury function, but also by providing more accurate controls on corruption and evasion in the subsequent service delivery processes.

In the United States, the Internal Revenue Service instituted electronic filing as early as 1986, with dedicated Internet filing being introduced in 1995. In the European Union, where economic and social challenges have led the drive toward transformational e-Government (EU, 2003), in recent years the member governments have been able to save million of hours of personnel time and millions of euros in costs through leveraging e-Tax systems as part of transformational e-Government implementations (Anon, 2005). e-Tax systems are also quite useful to developing and emerging nations, in circumstances where government offices are not physically collocated or accessible to citizens (Hu et al., 2009). Such might be the case for the most recent additions to the European Union as well as several prominent candidates for membership.

This is the specific context and contribution of our study: demonstrating the usefulness of e-Tax systems as an aspect of transformational government initiatives enacted by a European Union candidate nation. This contribution will be achieved through a study of Turkish Certified Public Accountants (CPAs) and the process of their acceptance and subsequent use of e-Tax systems instituted by the Turkish government for purposes of streamlining the governmental revenue function. As part of this study, e-Tax system ‘usefulness’ will serve as a key variable predicting system usage in a model of acceptance which we adopt for application in this context (e.g., Davis, 1989; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000; Fu et al., 2006). In this study, the specific application of models of accep tance for e-Tax systems utilized in studying e-Government initiatives in Asian nations will be extended and adapted to the developing e-Government services market represented by Turkey. The e-Tax initiative in Turkey is considered particularly important, as it is being enacted as part of the overall preparations by the Turkish government for membership application to the European Union. in support of specific calls to innovate technologically on the part of the EU (EU, 2003, 2006).

Scholars and policy analysts, alike, recognize the necessity of understanding transformational government processes (Hood, 2006). In view of the need to examine the implementation differences between developed and emerging nations on e-Governance (Anon, 2007) and in light of the growing desire of government policy makers to compare their own transformational processes to those of other nations ( Janssen et al., 2004), e-Government research that considers the established transformational practices of advanced nations in the context of emerging nations can be particularly useful. We investigate the implementation and acceptance of e-Tax systems among CPAs in a prominent emerging nation for purposes of understanding the usefulness and critical implementation factors that may pertain to similar nations and circumstances elsewhere in the increasingly globalized world.

Disseminating online governmental services has a number of obstacles including safeguarding the trust of and confidence in online governmental channels, the enablement of widespread public access to online services, and the achievement of interoperability among government entities in terms of digital information exchange (CEC, 2003). Hence we need to know more about implementation and subsequent acceptance of such technologies by their intended users.

This study is concerned with how new tax management information technologies are implemented by CPAs faced with the modernization of a government tax management system as a facet of governmental transformation in the information age. This goal is achieved through the study of electronic tax payment systems implemented by the Turkish Ministry of Finance. Our goal is to understand the factors that promote use and acceptance of such transformational systems among the emerging nations of the developing world, in service of the frequently stated need for evaluative research on e-Government processes across national and cultural contexts (e.g., Janssen et al., 2004; Hung-Pheng et al., 2005; Esteves & Rhoda, 2008; Mitra & Gupta, 2008; Wang & Liao, 2008). The context of our investigation involves the nature and evolution of e-Tax systems as an aspect of transformational government in an emerging nation seeking European Union membership and leveraging e-Tax systems as part of that initiative; the related contribution lies in understanding the process and power of such transformational initiatives for use in other emerging nations, in the theoretical context of technology acceptance theory, both for purposes of understanding empirical factors leading to successful acceptance of technological e-Government initiatives, as well as the practical and managerial implications leading to the achievement of governmental efficiencies and supra-governmental benefits such as those associated with obtaining membership in organizations such as the EU.

The paper proceeds as follows: first, we discuss e-Tax and electronic payment systems as a critical aspect of the control system implicated in transformational governance (e.g., Hood, 2006). Then, we introduce the theoretical platform for investigating this governmental e-Service, discussing technology acceptance and planned behavior models as useful templates for understanding citizen acceptance of electronic services (e.g., Fu et al., 2006). Following this, we report on the process and results of a country-wide online survey of CPAs in a nation presently implementing an e-Tax system, the data from which is subjected to structural equation modeling for analysis. Lastly, implications for the adoption, acceptance and use of e-Tax systems by CPAs and their tax preparation representatives in emerging and developing nations are discussed as an element of evolving transformational government practices.

## e-Taxation and electronic tax payments

Electronic taxation is a form of tax assessment, collection and payment that does not require tax payers to physically interact with the tax authorities, instead enabling them to review, file and pay taxes via new ICT (cf. Gellis, 1991; Petersen & Washington, 1993; Delucia, 2000; Wang, 2002; Fu et al., 2004; Turner & Apelt, 2004; Fu et al., 2006; NSDL, 2007). e-Tax systems are intended to streamline revenue collection and documentation services (Ciborra, 2005). Such systems are typically used more often by private citizens for personal tax returns than by industry for business tax filings, though that trend is changing in recent years (Pant et al., 2004).

e-Tax as an aspect of transformational e-Government services represents a critical new electronic government application and a focal perspective for public service organizational control systems (Hood, 2006). In circumstances where national tax authorities spend amounts that reach as much as 4% of received revenues in taxation, the motivation for finding increased services efficiencies through transformational electronic channels is high (Pant et al., 2004).

According to Dalziel (2004), online government users use websites to search for information and download software applications to support online government services, but tax-based service interactions are more complex, as they involve both traditional search and download activities as well as file uploading services in support of tax filing and payments (Fu et al., 2006). Although the concept of electronic tax payment originated in the U.S.A., other technology-enabled nations have also moved quickly to utilize the modality, including Australia, Canada, England, Germany, India, Singapore and Taiwan (Turner & Apelt, 2004).

e-Taxation, as used here, is specifically defined as the calculation, realization and payment by citizens to a government of accrued taxes via computers connected to the Internet or other telecommunication means. Such electronic tax systems facilitate the electronic pre paration and filing of tax declaration forms, followed by electronic payment channels for fulfillment of tax liabilities. This can be achieved through system features as sophisticated as Internet-enabled interactive bank accounts or as simplistic as ATM bank machines. Online e-Tax payment methods involve online systems equipped with security infrastructure, such as banks employing special software running on their extranet to support e-tax payment services, in view of the increased accountability issues associated with the provision on online public services (Heeks, 2007). Online e-Tax payment systems help governments efficiently cope with tax losses and tax aversion on the part of its citizens, which tends to increase tax revenues; at the same time, taxpayer users of the system also benefit in terms of time and cost saving, resulting in simpler and easier tax payment processes (Centeno, 2004).

Even so, the predominant method of tax payment in developed nations around the world remains the manual tax return, despite the advantages inherent in e-Government approaches to tax preparation, filing and payment (Wang, 2002). Hence, it is important to understand why tax payers would use or not use such electronic systems, in view of the efficiencies, savings and conveniences associated with electronic delivery channels.

## Emerging global ICT support for tax management systems: a synthesis

Governments, worldwide, have been utilizing information technology for some time in a variety of instances, but as ICT and computing power grows exponentially (Seyal et al., 2002), this new trend provides great opportunities for tax agencies to improve service quality and to reduce service costs simultaneously (Fu et al., 2004). Among nation members of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, electronic tax payment systems are being increasingly used as part of their e-Government applications (Turner & Apelt, 2004). The U.S.A. pioneered in the development of e-Tax systems, having demonstrated first trials in 1986 (Turner & Apelt, 2004). As of the millennium, 50% of U.S. tax returns were filed electronically, and the goal of the transformational process is to reach 80% of returns filed through electronic channels.

Singapore is another early pioneer, piloting an electronic filing system in 1987, with national rollout in 1999, while Canada introduced a system in 1990. The U.K. launched a system in 2000, and 30% of Norwegian citizens were filing electronically by 2004 (Turner & Apelt, 2004). Recent results show great advancement for e-Tax implementations in Taiwan (Wang & Liao, 2008), as well as Hong Kong (Hu et al., 2009) while researchers in Belgium ( Janssen et al., 2004), Spain (Esteves & Rhoda, 2008), India (Mitra & Gupta, 2008), South Korea (Anon, 2005), the U.S.A. (Pant et al., 2004) and the European Union (Gilbert et al., 2004) are all demonstrating not only advancement of the e-Tax process, but calling for comparisons across national boundaries for purposes of program evaluation.

The rise of the Internet has contributed substantially to this e-Tax evolution ( Janssen et al., 2004), though classic e-Tax definitions consider dedicated switched circuit lines to be as viable as anything else when it comes to electronic filing processes (Pant et al., 2004). We think that since dedicated Internet filing has become a mature practice in the advanced nations (Pant et al., 2004), it will be useful to examine emerging nations with strong Internet capabilities for indications of where the next major e-Tax implementation will take place. One such nation is Turkey.

The case of Turkey: e-Tax systems in emerging nations In Turkey, much of the total taxation revenue base derives from a Value Added Tax; other sources include a Special Individual Consumption Tax, standard Income Tax and a Corporate Tax on company revenues (GI<sup>˙</sup>B., 2007). The general goal of the online tax strategy in Turkey is to simplify tax laws, regulations and filing procedures in order to reconcile Turkish practices with those of the European Union. The regulations governing this process in Turkey were outlined by the Higher Planning Board in a preliminary national development plan (DPT, 2005) for the Presidency of Revenue Administration of the General Directorate of Revenue. This plan specified initiatives such as the diffusion of electronic tax services to individual citizens (including the issuance of individual tax numbers via the Internet), the implementation of automation for tax administration (including the development and implementation of electronic tax declaration and electronic tax payment systems), and the electronic transfer of tax collection funds to taxation authority accounts (Yaranli, 2005).

The intended outcome, in general, was the creation of a robust and reliable governmental decision support and service delivery system for the use of General Directory of Revenue managers, for tax authority clerks and for Office of the District Treasurer to enable better service delivery and communication with individual tax payers. The system is also intended to reduce unregistered economic activities that are very common in Turkey (Apak, 2004).

The work of automating Turkish tax authorities began in 1998, and was projected to be complete by the end of 2008, though the work is still underway at this writing, emphasizing the wisdom that ambitious transformational government initiatives often outstrip the practical capabilities of practical deployment of the underlying governmental e-Services (Kolsaker & Lee-Kelley, 2007). That notwithstanding, by the year 2000, 155 Turkish tax authorities were converted to full automation – representing contact points with nearly half of the total tax payers in the country, and spanning 22 provinces and 10 urban municipalities (ABGS, 2003). By 2006, automation of tax authorities numbered 430 and included the tax authorities for every city center.

Turkey has shown impressive development of e-Government services, ranking 27th among all nations studied in the Seventh Annual State and Federal E-Government Study (West, 2006). However, as the Turkish government rapidly moves from paper-based services to technology-based services, they need to understand their citizens’ technology acceptance motivations and look for ways to encourage adoption of e-Government services. Internet service is widely available and e-Government services are on offer to the majority of the Turkish population. The issue is what will motivate citizens (Horton et al., 2001) to take advantage of the transformational e-Service that have been offered?

## Theoretical background

User acceptance of ICT is a crucial factor in information systems success (Money & Turner, 2004). Numerous individual, organizational and technological variables have been investigated to determine users’ acceptance of information technologies, yet researchers continue to seek high quality measures of user acceptance (Davis, 1989). Meanwhile, little has been done on the investigation of citizen acceptance of e-Government technology in the specific form of e-Tax. Governments that implement such e-Tax systems have an urgent need to understand why people accept or reject e-Government applications, so that they may better plan in order to enhance e-Government services and develop better applications for their citizenry.

Although psychology-based theories such as Technology Acceptance and Planned Behavior have been widely validated as powerful and parsimonious frameworks to assess IT adoption by business users (see Venkatesh et al., 2003 for a complete review), similar theories show promise for application in contexts outside of business settings, such as that of the governmentally provided e-Tax system (Wang, 2002). Some work has been done on the topic, much of it focused on a select few nations, with Taiwan standing out as a notable exemplar. For example, research on e-Government in Taiwan has been rather fulsome; Fu et al. (2006) studied taxpayer intentions in Taiwan with an online survey, Chang et al. (2005) examined the taxpaying behaviors of Taiwanese university students, using the IS Success model (e.g., Delone & McLean, 1992), and Hung et al. (2006) engaged in a widespread survey of Taiwanese taxpayers with the assistance of the Taiwanese Ministry of Finance.

There have been some reviews of the literature on e-Government services. Yildiz (2007) provided a general literature review of e-Government research, while Titah & Barki’s (2006) study represents perhaps the most extensive survey of the literature on e-Government adoption currently available. And some work has been done on e-Government in the West, but much of it has been qualitative and exploratory. Hansford et al. (2006) executed a series of 10 depth interviews on the topic among British taxpayers, while Barnes & Vidgen (2006) used qualitative methods to assess taxpayer reactions to the British Inland Revenue Website. Turner & Apelt (2004) used textual analysis and depth interviews to study a diffusion of innovation perspective of Australian e-Tax systems and Heeks & Bailur (2007) performed a content analysis of Western e-Government research publications. A notable quantitative study, Horst et al. (2007) tested technology acceptance for e-Government services in Holland with structural equation modeling.

## Specific acceptance theory considerations for e-Tax

Adoption of ICTs is considered to advance the adoption of e-tax payment systems (Turner & Apelt, 2004). There is a wealth of research on business and consumer users’ acceptance of new technology, but very little work has been done on acceptance of e-Government services (Wang, 2002; Fu et al., 2004). The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), developed by Davis (1989), is one of the most influential research models for studying new IS user acceptance and can be profitably applied to e-Government scenarios, as well (Fu et al., 2004). TAM is an intention-based model derived from the Theory of Reasoned Action, in which intention resides in the influence of ease of use and usefulness perceptions regarding a technology (Davis, 1989), whereas the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) incorporates subjective norms and perceived behavioral control to predict behavior intention to use systems (Fu et al., 2006). TAM and TPB have converged in recent explanations of technology acceptance (e.g., Venkatesh & Davis, 2000; Venkatesh et al., 2003)

Users usually are willing to cope with some difficulties for a system that would provide critical needed functionality (Davis, 1989), but this willingness is predicated on an individual’s self-perceived capability to overcome such difficulties (Bandura, 1986). Hence, in predicting e-Government usage, while TAM has been efficient and useful in organizational contexts, there are limitations when applying it beyond one organization or a group involving a variety of challenging user task environments (Subramanian, 1994); this would be consistent with a nation-wide e-Tax system. For this reason, examination of the familiar TAM/TPB scenario in an e-Government context is useful, as the model clearly needs to be validated for such an unusual context (e.g., Paul & John, 2003).

## Research model constructs

In considering the combination of TAM and TPB in explaining e-Government adoption, Fu et al. (2004) outlined several factors that might act to restrict or encourage e-Government usage. In addition to the normal array of TAM and TPB constructs, relating to technology awareness and attitudes, there can also be issues of security, privacy and safety. Hence, one model construct that would supplement standard acceptance theory variables would relate to user risk.

Perceived risk (PR) As people engage in online transactions, concerns of inherent risk have been observed for many users (Hoffman et al., 1999), not the least of which would be accountants dealing with client’s sensitive financial and tax information. The actual and objective level of experienced risk presented by a proposed e-Government system might be difficult to measure, so the notion of perceived risk (PR) is defined as the taxpayers’ perceptions of uncertainty and expectations of adverse results arising from system usage (Fu et al., 2006). These risk perceptions are likely to influence attitude towards e-Tax system adoption processes, to the extent that if there were elevated concerns for e-Government system security, CPAs might would hesitate to use online tax facilities.

TAM and TPB constructs in e-Tax models While TAM is a parsimonious model, consisting of the interplay of usefulness and ease of use as they impact behavioral intentions, TPB is composed of numerous variables. TBP employs Subjective Norm (SN) and Perceived Behavioral Control (PBC) as direct determinants of Behavioral Intention (BI) of technology users as regards some technology-facilitated behavior (Fu et al., 2006).

The PBC construct, itself, is composed of three items: Self Efficacy (SE), Resource Facilitating Conditions (RFC)

and Technology Facilitating Conditions (TFC), and the underlying theory of planned behavior specifies that BI is determined by the interplay of SN and RFC. We define each element of these two theoretical perspectives in operational terms, below.

Perceived Ease of Use Issues (PEU). The adoption of electronic taxation websites and software developed and provided by the Turkish Ministry of Finance is highly dependent on user perceptions of ease of use as regards e-Tax systems. Aside from electronic payment and filing systems, the website for the Ministry of Finance is an important factor that Turkish CPAs evaluate in the process of attempting to file tax returns for their clients online. The degree to which the Turkish Ministry of Finance has succeeded in developing an easy-to-se site will play a determining role in the intentions that accountants have to engage in e-Tax activities.

Perceived Usefulness Issues (PU). An important factor underlying accountants’ assessments of usefulness for a proposed e-Tax system resides in the cost-saving opportunities provided by the new system. Another usefulness aspect would be the time savings provided by the system. The degree to which accountants perceive that the Ministry’s electronic resources save time and money should define their perceptions of how useful such resources are.

Compatibility (C). Compatibility is defined as the degree to which a target technology fits the potential users’ previous work experience, work practice and needs (Fu et al., 2006), and this quality serves as an antecedent of PEU and PU. The degree to which the Ministry’s e-Tax system resources are compatible with current accountant practice should be an important determinant of technology adoption decisions of these CPAs who are potentially considering electronic filing systems.

Self Efficacy (SE). The ability of a technology user to master a new technology under consideration for adoption is pivotal in the acceptance process (Venkatesh et al., 2003). This self-perceived ability to use a technological innovation, known as SE, plays an important role in understanding individual adopters’ e-Government technology adoption decisions (Wang, 2002), just as it has been demonstrated to do in organizational and consumer contexts. Self-efficacy is similar to perceived ease of use in terms of judgments of ‘how well one can execute courses of action required to deal with prospective situations’ (Davis, 1989), but is contextually distinct in as much as it defines the ability to actually use the technology.

Subjective Norm (SN). SN regards how an adopter’s peers consider the adoption target and the adoption decision (Azjen, 1988). SN has been found to serve as a strong determinant of behavioral intentions regarding adoption scenarios in a wide range of social settings (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975), and we consider that an accountant’s friends’ and colleagues’ opinions of e-Government applications might well be influential in the decision to adopt an e-Tax system.

Perceived Behavior Control (PBC). TPB incorporates SN and PBC as direct determinants of BI (Fu et al., 2006). PBC is an individual’s perceptions of the presence or absence of resources or opportunities that are necessary to perform an action. This construct is decomposed into two other constructs characterized by the internal notion of individual SE and external constraints of Triandis (1977) notion of facilitating conditions leading to behavioral intentions – in this case, the focal behavior of technology adoption. In any event, greater degrees of SE support greater perceived abilities to perform the target technology behavior. We expect more tax system edeclaration usage behavioral intentions from users who feel more capable of using such a system. Facilitating conditions consist of two sub-dimensions, RFC and TFC, and we construe this as the presence or absence of the requisite personal computer technology used for interaction with e-Government systems over networks. CPAs not possessed of computing equipment would likely unwilling to use or even incapable of using electronic tax declarations or payment systems, as a matter of course.

## The research model

The research model we test is drawn from the work of Fu et al. (2006). As our literature review demonstrates, the work of Fu and colleagues (Fu et al., 2004, 2006), as well as several compatriots (Chang et al., 2005; Hung et al., 2006) have established Taiwan as a stand-out exemplar among nations for the empirical study of e-Tax adoptions process. The wide variety of studies on Western government programs have largely been exploratory and often qualitative studies. We see great value in applying the e-Government models applied in Asia to circumstances in emerging transformational government scenarios in European contexts. Hence, the constructs we have developed and presented here are combined in an empirical format initially demonstrated in Taiwan by Fu and his colleagues. ## Research design and method

The research design calls for testing a group of theoretically related cause-and-effect hypotheses developed as a single interrelated theoretical network, in which case the optimal approach for assessing the overall theoretical construction would be structural equation modeling (e.g., Bagozzi and Phillips, 1982). This is in keeping with the method chosen for the study that informs our inquiry here (e.g., Fu et al., 2006), which was covariance-based structural equation modeling. In this method, a specific theoretically suggested model, such as the one we derive from technology acceptance theory, is specified as a single simultaneous equation model in which the hypotheses are tested simultaneously as paths demonstrating the inter-relationships of constructs explained in a proposed theoretical network. To the extent that the model fits well, the theory being explicated is considered to be correctly specified. Hence, the theoretical model specified in Figure 1 is tested with structural equation modeling in order to comprehensively assess the theoretical network represented by our group of hypotheses.

To ensure content validity for the constructs in our model, items selected for operationalizing the constructs are adopted from the prior studies in which they were originally validated for measurement. BI, PU and PEU are taken from Davis (1989), which is the primary source for scales used on most acceptance theory work. PBC constructs, including SE, SN, RFC and TFC are drawn from Taylor & Todd (1995), who extended the concepts of planned behavior in the context of technology acceptance. Compatibility (C) derives from Fu et al. (2006), who have extended technology acceptance contexts to e-Government research, while PR is taken from Cases (2002). The constructs are all measured with 5-point Likert scales anchored between Strongly Agree (1) and Strongly Disagree (5). The dependent and independent variables were aggregated with the method defined by Tavakolian (1989), in which responses for each scale set are summed and divided by number of items in the scale, forming a basic mean score for each scale.

Internal consistency for the measures is generally within acceptable ranges. Values of Cronbach’s alpha greater than 0.70 are usually sought in social science research (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994). Yet, based on the literature, while reliability over 0.70 is recommended and reliability of over 0.80 is desired, values of 0.50 or greater can often be considered sufficient for exploratory research (Hatcher, 1994). As can be seen in Table 1, values of alpha for each scale exceed the acceptable minimum (e.g., Hatcher, 1994), and typically reach the recommended levels of between 0.70 and 0.080.

## Survey design

The survey sought to collect two types of data. The first part of the survey included demographic questions, including technology usage levels, Internet experience and type of Internet connection available at home and work. Respondents were also asked about their views of tax laws and regulations, as well as technical questions on tax management perspectives such as typical problems faced when using e-declaration systems and costs saved when using e-declaration system. The second part of the survey included the scales for the theoretical constructs related to our hypothesized model.

## Data collection procedure

An Internet site was developed for purposes of hosting the survey at a prominent Turkish research university. Professional Turkish accounting associations across the nation were contacted by email, telephone or fax, and were invited to participate in the web-based survey. Turkish CPA associations were also requested to inform their members by posting the links to survey website on their association sites, in addition to notifying their membership via email. Lastly, an announcement was made in support of the survey on the Turkish CPAs website. As a result of this recruitment procedure, 352 usable responses were collected for analysis.

## Results

## Descriptive analysis of Turkish CPAs

According to Turkish Law, accounting professionals are characterized by three major certifications: Certified General Accountant (SM), Certified Public Accountant (SMMM) and Sworn-in Certified Public Accountant (YMM). The basic course of an accountant’s career begins with SM, with experience they evolve to SMMM, and YMM is the top certification level offered to professionals. As Turkish accountants advance within their certification scheme, they are able to advise larger firms and have more authority in terms of approval power over tax returns. However, in our study, SM and SMMM-level accountants are the most frequently encountered, and they are the professionals that generally serve the average (and hence must numerous) Turkish tax payer. For that reason, our data include only responses from the most common accountancy professionals in Turkey, namely SM and SMMM-level professionals.

As part of the descriptive section of our survey, we analyzed basic demographic characteristics of our respondents. We asked about their technology use, as well as their preferred tax payment method (traditional vs online). In Table 2, the basic characteristics of our accounting professional respondents are presented, along with a basic chi-square analysis to assess representativeness of various demographic categories across professional certification levels.

Of the respondents, 36.4% identified themselves as Certified General Accountants (SM) and the remaining 63.6% respondents identified themselves as Certified Public Accountants (SMMM). Average age of the respondents is 42 years, with a maximum value of 76 and minimum value of 22; in general, the majority of the respondents could be considered middle-aged CPAs. In terms of gender, the vast majority of respondents are male, but there is not a significant difference in the distribution of gender across certification level. The respondents are generally well-educated, and SMMMs demonstrate notably greater degrees of higher education than SMs, which is in accordance with Turkish law for SMMM certification. The respondents appear to characteristically have quite a bit of computer experience, with over 80% of professionals at both levels of certification reporting over 10 years’ computer use and practically the entire sample reporting up to 3 years’ computer experience.

Internet experience among both groups of professionals is lower than computer experience; about a third of SM certified professionals reported at least 7 years online experience, while about half of the SMMM professionals reported at least 7 years’ experience. This notwithstanding, nearly all respondents at each certification level currently report using the Internet on a daily basis.

The most important descriptive question in our demographic section would be with regard to the preferred tax payment method the professional use for their client returns. At present, roughly a third of each certification level express a clear preference for the online payment method (28.9% and 42.4%, respectively, for SMs and SMMMs). Half of each certification level expressed a preference for making payments at the bank, and only a few of each certification level indicated a preference for making payments at the Tax Authority. This being the case, even in light of emerging methods for streamlining the bank payment method on the part of the Ministry of Finance, it appears that Turkish accountants are still not largely interested in online solutions. The apparent possibility of an e-Government ‘digital divide’ (Dalziel, 2004) only tends to reinforce the necessity of learning what factors might motivate Turkish tax professionals to engage in transformational government e-Services in their tax practice.

The model was fitted in LISREL 8.51. ( Joreskog & Sorbom, 1999), and results are discussed below. As can be seen, the TBP constructs (SN, RFC and TFC) as well as the ancillary construct of perceived risk are largely non-significant in the model (hence, disconfirming H4, H5, H6 and H7, respectively), while the TAM constructs are all significant. Of all TPB constructs, only SE (describing the accountant’s perceptions of capability to use the technology) provided a significant structural path, in confirmation of H3. The implications of this outcome are useful for guiding subsequent research considering transformational interventions in Turkish e-Government processes.

In short, we found strong empirical support for the extended TAM (modeled with the compatibility antecedent). Yet, only self-efficacy among the planned behavior constructs had any discernable effect. One interpretation of this would be (particularly in view of the descriptive results for computer and Internet use in the context of preferences for traditional tax filing methods) that Turkish tax professionals have more or less accepted the technological bases for e-Tax, that being computer and Internet systems, but have not, as yet, formed specific plans for their use in emerging transformational government applications, such as e-Tax filing systems. Another interpretation may be that governmental transformation in Turkey is nascent, and that one of the key challenges to be addressed in moving the process forward entails a significant focus on the treasury function, through the process of encouraging a group of professionals inclined to use the emergent system to actually use it and use it well. Transformation can only be considered preliminary until this important function is fully automated, as intended.

Whether it is a matter of longstanding habits being hard to break (recall the large degree of professional reporting current practices of traditional filing), or whether there are larger issues of adaptation and implementation of the requisite technologies remains to be seen. The aspects of planned behavior and its components of perceived behavioral control and social norms (e.g., Taylor & Todd, 1995; Pavlou & Fygenson, 2006) are extensions of the theory from which technology acceptance evolved, and frequently are considered to supplement it, even though the acceptance model is frequently found to be more predictive (Mathieson, 1991). Hence, the intention to make use of transformational e-Tax systems on the part of Turkish professionals appears to be mediated by the perceived ability to do so along with the social support for the adoption process. This is likely to be problematic, particularly in the case of the influence of social norms in the process, as it is clear that the norms for tax preparation in Turkey among the bureaucracy favor inefficient and sometimes fraudulent practices, as seen in the prevalent practices of bribery and favoritism (Yılmaz & Kılavuz, 2002). In addition to rendering inappropriate behaviors on the part of tax professionals difficult, if not impossible, in the implementation of the automated system, it is also envisioned that tax evasion and tax exile problems will be greatly reduced in the coming transformation of the tax system, once fully achieved (Tecım, 2008).

For this reason, the need to provide normative support and sufficient training to instill appropriate levels of user self-efficacy with the technology will be critical mediators of the achievement of the implementation of the Turkish e-Tax system.

In either case, it would appear that two notable circumstances exists in Turkey: the government has implemented a nation-spanning electronic system to automate the taxation system, and Turkish tax professionals seem more than adequately inclined to use such systems. In view of the result that intention and planned behavior are disjoint in regard to the adoption process, it may well be that the next step in moving toward transformation of the treasury function in Turkish government might be the development of training programs to increase perceptions of behavioral control, along with incentives and rewards for implementing the new e-Tax system by tax professionals on behalf of their clients. Turkish accountants already accept the technology underlying the transformation that the government wishes to achieve; it is a matter of helping them plan for its use that remains to be dealt with.

## Implications

The current study tries to assess and understand empirically Turkish CPA acceptance and usage plans and intentions for a newly implemented transformational government application. It is not sufficient for governments to simply design and offer transformational systems; the underlying technologies must be understood and accepted among users for transformational processes to adhere (e.g., Kolsaker & Lee-Kelley, 2007). As part of such transformations, both electronic networks and treasury operations are essential components of governmental transformations (Hood, 2006). In our study, we can readily demonstrate not only the provision of a robust electronic system supporting treasury operations in Turkey, but also a prevailing acceptance of the underlying computer and Internet technologies among tax professionals responsible for potentially using such transformational e-Government systems.

We cannot demonstrate ongoing plans on the part of users to make immediate use of such systems, which suggests that the governmental transformation process still requires additional planning and work in Turkey. This is entirely in line with Kolsaker & Lee-Kelley’s (2007) ‘reach vs grasp’ characterization of the evolution of transformational government processes. While such transformational programs are showing widespread acceptance and use in more advanced nations, such as Taiwan (e.g., Fu et al., 2006), it must be remembered that these nations lead the world in e-Government systems implementation and utilization, as well as in technological development. Turkey is a special case, demonstrating an interesting combination of technological sophistication in terms of systems and infrastructure deployment, in concert with more modest adoption of systems at the individual citizen level (e.g., Stafford et al., 2006)

Deeper understanding and assessment of accounting professionals, who are the major facilitating players in the transformational Turkish tax management system, would provide valuable opportunities for e-Government initiatives in this nation. Moreover, as of this writing, the e-Tax payment system in Turkey is completely voluntary and the target users of the system have not yet been provided by the government with tangible incentives for system use. At the present point, only the innovative nature of individual practitioners will lead to e-Tax system use. It may be necessary for the transformational government initiative in Turkey to adopt compliance measures through the rule of law, as has been the case in other nations’ technology diffusion initiatives (e.g., Arendson et al., 2008).

## Synthesis with prior research

This study sought to contribute to the e-Government and transformational government literatures by demonstrating key constructs and theoretical considerations of citizen technology acceptance in the context of e-Tax system adoption and use by tax professionals in an emerging economy. The degree to which technologyenabled government transformations can take place will be highly dependent on the success with which critical transformational technologies can be put into use by the parties to such transformations, and a key research perspective in the study of transformational government, beyond that of the performance of the government, itself, is that of the citizen and his/her reaction to transformation (Irani et al., 2007). This study makes that contribution; we investigate aspects of citizen acceptance of key transformational processes in order to better inform the process of promoting important transformations to the populace.

One of the emerging best practices of transformational government initiatives is e-Tax (Gieber et al., 2008), and the importance of understanding both the effectiveness and the public desirability of e-Tax innovations in e-Government lies in the likelihood of close public scrutiny of revenue-producing and revenue-linked e-Government systems, on the basis that innovative new transformational systems are funded out of the public purse and will, as such, be subjected to significant public oversight (EU, 2003; Irani et al., 2008).

Technology acceptance is a robust theory for understanding why users in organizational contexts make use of technological innovations (e.g., Davis, 1989; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000; Venkatesh et al., 2003), yet the theoretical perspective has not been investigated closely in the context of acceptance of e-Government services (Wang, 2002; Fu et al., 2004). Our findings here provide clarity as to how technology acceptance theories pertain to transformational e-Government initiatives, and this is a particularly important contribution to the emerging literature on transformational government in view of the emerging trend toward legal mandates of transformational initiatives such as e-Tax by governments. As noted in the literature on the topic, it is far preferable to find ways to encourage voluntary citizen adoption of transformational processes such as e-Taxation (e.g., West, 2004) as compared to the increasingly prevalent practice of governmental enforcement (e.g., Arendson et al., 2008). Hence, our contribution on factors enhancing user adoption of e-Tax systems is particularly useful for purposes of informing governmental transformations, particularly as governments seek to find ways to attract, enhance and nurture stakeholder relationships as part of encouraging, rather than mandating, transformational e-Government services adoption (Tan & Pan, 2003; Navarra & Cornford, 2004).

Indeed, it is far more useful to examine user adoption characteristics in the determination of e-Government transformations than it is to simply benchmark the performance of one nation against another, without critically examining the user implementation and adoption processes involved in a particular national initiative (e.g., Sharma, 2004; Irani et al., 2007). Moreover, studies of governmental use of ICT in support of transformational operations are increasingly relevant and informative in the e-Government literature (e.g., Barnes & Vidgen, 2004; EU, 2006).

Although acceptance theory studies of business uses of technological transformations typically highlight the value of planned behavior constructs (e.g., Taylor & Todd, 1995) as part of the greater theoretical network in which the well-understood usefulness and ease of use constructs interrelate (e.g., Davis, 1989; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000), our results here indicate that much of the planned behavior variables do not contribute much to tax professionals’ intentions to make use of transformational systems in the emerging nation context of our study. In this case, simply put, the compatibility of the proposed system with existing professional practice, and the degree to which the professional feels capable of using the transformational innovation tend to be the primary antecedents and moderators of the expected effects of usefulness and ease of use of an innovation in stimulating adoption and use. This streamlined version of acceptance theory in the context of e-Government scenarios is demonstrated in our revised theoretical model, shown in Figure 3, and represents the critical role that technology compatibility and perceived capabilities of using an innovation play in the operation of the well-understood acceptance theory constructs.

This provides an important contribution in the context of the general body of acceptance theory research, which has focused extensively on business sector innovations. It appears that some of the theories that prove useful for business implementations of transformational technologies may not be as pertinent in government sector implementations. This will permit more judicious application of acceptance and planned behavior theories in e-Government contexts in future research.

As for the findings of this study and their contribution to the ongoing work of understanding e-Government and the technological transformations that take place in the process of implementing it, to determine that technology acceptance theories have use, even if limited to a specific subset of the standard variables typically specified in acceptance models, is very useful. The constructs of technology acceptance are robust and reliable (e.g., Venkatesh et al., 2003), and the effective demonstration of the operation of the expected Ease or
