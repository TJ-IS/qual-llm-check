## a b s t r a c t

This paper explores the impacts of essential elements of management on IT application maturity (ITAM). A game theoretical model is established to show that a <sup>fi</sup>rm's IT application maturity should match with its essential elements of management. In order to further validate the analytical result, a data set with 137 <sup>fi</sup>rms was collected via a survey instrument. Using reliability and factor analysis on the dataset, a four-dimension measurement construct of IT application maturity is proposed together with a three-dimension measurement construct of essential elements of management. Empirical analysis is then conducted to address research questions on the impacts of essential elements of management on IT application maturity. It is found that while a <sup>fi</sup>rm's strategic cooperation capability has no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on IT application maturity, its internal coordination capability positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences all four dimensions of IT application maturity. For the <sup>fi</sup>rm's execution capability, although it shows no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on the overall IT application maturity, it does positively affect the technology and man–machine synergy dimensions of IT application maturity. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study offer practical guidance for organizations to better align their IT application maturity with essential elements of management.

## 1. Introduction

The globalization and advancement of technologies, especially information technologies, have drastically changed internal and external environments for business organizations. Business competitions are becoming more intense; product life cycles are getting shorter; and consumers are more demanding on both the quality and diversity of products and services. In order to deal with these new challenges in both internal and external business environments, organizations must gain competitive advantage through business process streamlining. The implementation of these business processes is often dependent on the deployment and utilization of information technology. It would be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, if not impossible, for organizations to survive and grow in this information age without the support of information technology.

Information technology application (IT application) effectively re<sup>fl</sup>ects how information technology is utilized. It typically embraces three phases: planning and development, implementation, and maintenance. Each of these phases is critical to the success of IT application. For the implementation and maintenance phases, there exists a rich body of research topics, for example, IT management, IT capability, IT competence, IT application maturity, and so on [58]. Our paper continues to focusing on the topic of IT application maturity (ITAM) which stems from the concept of software capability maturity model (CMM) [27]. Since IT application is often viewed as a process of utilizing information technology, ITAM is therefore de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the maturity of this process. Based on a comprehensive literature review, Xiao [57] proposes a six-dimension framework to measure ITAM, which is composed of technology, data, man–machine synergy, business operation, functional management, and strategic support. Technology refers to IT infrastructure and software systems of IT application. Data includes basic operational data, business and transaction data, and re<sup>fi</sup>ned data for supporting decision making. Business operation and functional management represent how IT application supports business operations and functional management respectively. Strategic support measures the support from IT applications to business strategy. Man–machine synergy dimension is associated with the friendly user interface, human–computer interaction, visualization, and arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence capability in IT application.

Intuitively, when an organization has its ITAM matching with its management practice, a more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient utilization of IT application can be achieved. This consequently leads to production cost reduction, operation process optimization, and eventually a high return on investment of IT application. Different organizations, even within the same industry, might differ in their ITAMs. In this study, we focus on investigating the factors that may cause such differences. It can be of great value to identify these factors in order to help organization to improve the process of information technology utilization, that is, enhancing its IT application maturity. In fact, a vast body of literature has already been dedicated to studying elements directly related to organization's technical capabilities in IT application [37]. However, few studies can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as investigating IT application maturity from the perspective of management practice.

Joyce et al. [28] introduce eight essential elements of management, namely, strategy, execution, culture, organizational structure, talent, leadership, innovation, and mergers and partnerships. It is further validated that there exists a direct relationship among these eight essential elements of management and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. In this study, we continue to adopt the concept of essential elements of management to represent management practice in <sup>fi</sup>rms. We are interested in investigating whether essential elements of management would play vital roles in affecting organization's IT application maturity.

Existing studies on IT application maturity, however, have focused on either the theory of innovation diffusion [4] or evaluation models of IT application maturity [59]. In this paper, we explicitly investigate the impacts of essential elements of management on IT application maturity in order to reveal this important relationship. Both an analytical model and empirical validations are provided. Our speci<sup>fi</sup>c research questions are:

• Do essential elements of management have an overall positive effect on IT application maturity?

• If so, which essential elements of management have positive effects on IT application maturity?

• Further, how do essential elements of management impact various dimensions of IT application maturity?

In this paper, 137 <sup>fi</sup>rm-level data samples were collected from companies in China. These data are valuable and rare in the research <sup>fi</sup>eld of IT application. To the best of our knowledge, our study represents one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst analytical and empirical studies that explore the mechanism between IT application maturity and essential elements of management from a perspective of companies in China.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides a review of related literature. In Section 3, a game theoretical model is proposed to establish a strategic match between essential elements of management and IT application maturity. In Section 4, data collection and analysis procedures are explained in detail. In Section 5, several empirical models are proposed, tested, and discussed. Finally, we conclude and offer directions for future research in Section 6.

## 2. Literature review

The rapid development of information technology (IT) brings numerous changes to business practices. Extant literature in the past decades has devoted to validating the return of IT investment [15,23,25,40,41]. Sambamurthy and Subrannani [49] study how information technology affects business performance and how such effects are quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed. Others have discussed the conditions required for IT investments to yield bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to business [9,37,46]. Mata et al. [36] argue that IT adoption has to match with organizational structure and it must be integrated with business process to create sustainable competitive advantage. Mooney et al. [39] believes that the return of IT investment should be implemented by business process automation, information processing, and organizational changes to improve overall operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Trice and Treacy [53] <sup>fi</sup>nd that IT application is an important mediatory to link IT investment with business performance. Lucas [34] proposes that the effectiveness of IT investment is determined by IT design, IT application, and other variables that affect business performance.

Delone et al. [14] conduct a comprehensive review of 180 papers related to successful information systems (IS), and provided six dimensions to measure the success of IS, namely, system quality, information quality, system application, user satisfaction, personal influence, and organizational factors. This model has been widely adopted and modi<sup>fi</sup>ed in later research [47,50]. Among these research, Sabherwal et al. [48] combine this model with the theory of IS adoption, and categorize success factors of IS into two groups, namely, user-related properties and environmental factors.

In the research area of IT application design and development, there exists a representative model – strategy alignment model (SAM) – which emphasizes strategic match between management practice and IT application [22,51]. In the research area of IT application implementation, a majority of studies use the concept of critical success factors (CSF), which include organizational culture, management foundation, operational environment, management support, accurate user requirement analysis, and so on [54]. Peppard [44] studies how <sup>fi</sup>ve key factors – strategic planning, process design, system development resources, system development and monitor, implementation and maintenance – affect IT applications. Kalakota and Robinson [29] propose ten key factors for establishing a successful E-commerce system. Umble et al. [54] conduct case studies to summarize ten main reasons for the failure of ERP (Enterprise Resources Planning) systems. Martinsons et al. [35] propose a balanced scorecard application for information systems, which measures and evaluates IS activities from the following perspectives: business value, user orientation, internal process, and future readiness. Bloodgood and Salisbury [3] identify how information technology can best be used during different types of strategic change in organizations. Banker and Kauffman [2] conduct a comprehensive review of studies in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information systems research over the past <sup>fi</sup>fty years and conclude that future research should focus more on enterprise IT application management as well as on how to utilize IT to enhance management practices. Melville et al. [38] conducted a recent study to analyze the role of the competitive environment in moderating the productive impact of information technology. Its focus is on the industry level concentration and dynamism.

In 1987, Carnegie Mellon University Software Institute (SEI), commissioned by the U.S. Department of Defense, established a software process maturity model called Capability Maturity Model (CMM), which categorizes software process maturity into <sup>fi</sup>ve levels, namely, ad hoc, repeatable, defined, managed, and optimized [10]. Harmon [20] proposes a process maturity model or Business Process Maturity Model (BPMM) to categorize business process maturity into similar <sup>fi</sup>ve levels. Hammer [19] then proposes a process and enterprise maturity model or Process and Enterprise Maturity Model (PEMM). All these models emphasize on process and quality management. Based on these models, Xiao and Xie [58] propose a model with <sup>fi</sup>ve levels of IT application maturity, which are, from low to high, basic level, partial integration level, complete integration level, enhanced coordination level, and strategy driving level. Intuitively, a high level of IT application maturity can lead to the success of information systems and, consequently, the ef<sup>fi</sup>cient use of IT application as well as improved business performance. In this paper, we start with this six-dimension measurement framework of IT application maturity from Xiao [57] and modify the framework based on data analysis. Details of the framework are presented in Section 4.

Porter and Millar [45] propose that there are three approaches for business management practice to gain competitive advantage, namely, changing the industry structure, improving internal operations, and expanding new products and services. Joyce et al. [28] point out, in a ten-year longitudinal analysis, that there exists a direct relationship among eight essential elements of management and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. These essential elements are strategy, execution, culture, organizational structure, talent, leadership, innovation, and mergers and partnerships. In this study, we continue to use this concept of essential elements of management to represent management practice in <sup>fi</sup>rms. Instead of directly using the eight dimensions, we propose a threedimension measurement construct for essential elements of management based on other literature. Our focus lies in investigating the impacts of essential elements of management on IT application maturity.

## 3. Game theoretical model

Prior literature indicates that IT application maturity has a positive impact on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance [57]. However, there also exists the cost of investing in information technology application, which includes acquisition cost of hardware and software, cost of system analysis, design, and implementation, etc. In general, a higher IT application maturity implies a higher degree of complexity in IT application, a higher risk of application deployment failure, and a higher chance of seeking help from third-party consulting services. All of these lead eventually to a higher implementation cost with more resource consumption such as longer project duration and more labor hours. Therefore, in order to provide a guideline for a company to choose an appropriate level of IT application maturity, it is crucial to study its strategic matching of ITAM with essential elements of management (EEM).

In this section, we develop a game theoretical model to analyze a <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision of selecting its ITAM in a competitive market. We assume that the market structure is that of monopolistic competition, and this model can be applied to other market structures by changing the number of <sup>fi</sup>rms, n. In fact, the majority of the companies in our empirical dataset do have monopoly strength to a certain extent in the industry. These <sup>fi</sup>rms are neither price followers nor makers; therefore, our assumption of this market mechanism is reasonable.

## 3.1. Assumptions

Without loss of generality, we consider n identical <sup>fi</sup>rms participating in the production of a non-differentiable commodity. We assume that these <sup>fi</sup>rms face a linear inverse demand function of $p { = } a { - } Q$ , where $p$ is the price; a is the product market capacity; $\textstyle Q = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } q _ { i }$ is the total production output where q is the output from <sup>fi</sup>rm i [52].

The unit product cost for <sup>fi</sup>rm i is denoted as $c _ { i } = f ( m ) - r L _ { i } .$ The <sup>fi</sup>rst term f(m) is the unit cost when a <sup>fi</sup>rm does not use any IT applications. The parameter m represents the set of essential elements of management which drives the production cost. We also assume $f ( m ) { < } a$ to assure a reasonable cost range. The variable L measures ITAM of <sup>fi</sup>rm i. The parameter r is the rate at which the unit product cost decreases with ITAM. It can also be interpreted as the rate of return on IT application.

The cost of achieving and maintaining ITAM for <sup>fi</sup>rm i is denoted as $C _ { i } { = } p _ { 0 } L _ { i } ^ { 2 } .$ In this equation, $p _ { 0 }$ is the cost coef<sup>fi</sup>cient, and we assume that $p _ { 0 } { > } 2 r ^ { 2 }$ . In other words, the cost coef<sup>fi</sup>cient is greater than twice of the square of rate of return of ITAM. With the increase of ITAM, corresponding business process needs to be redesigned. Therefore, the success of IT application requires not only investment in implementation of individual modules in IT application, but also a tremendous amount of investment in integration of all modules to represent the redesigned business processes. For a large-scale management information system (MIS), workload and investment can increase exponentially with the increase of ITAM as well as the increase of the number of modules in the system. For example, Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) system is business process based. To implement ERP, all modules in the system have to be integrated together in order to eliminate “information isolated island” and allow information sharing across the organization. Therefore, the integration cost is much higher with a higher ITAM, which is the underlying reason for us to use a quadratic form to denote the cost of achieving and maintaining ITAM.

## 3.2. Equilibrium strategy

Base on the above assumptions, we propose a two-stage game. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, <sup>fi</sup>rms choose their respective ITAM, $L _ { i \cdot }$ In the second stage, they participate in a Cournot competition to choose their respective outputs, q . We show that there exists a unique pure Nash equilibrium for this game. The equilibrium con<sup>fi</sup>rms our intuition that optimal ITAM is determined by a <sup>fi</sup>rm's essential elements of management. In other words, a <sup>fi</sup>rm should choose an appropriate ITAM to match with its essential elements of management.

We assume that this is a non-cooperative game [17] where all <sup>fi</sup>rms make their decisions simultaneously in both stages. The existence and uniqueness of Nash equilibrium is important for the game because the equilibrium needs to be a stable business strategy for all <sup>fi</sup>rms [21]. The standard description of this two-stage game with complete information is as follows:

1. There are n identical participating <sup>fi</sup>rms in the market;

2. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, <sup>fi</sup>rms choose their own ITAM $L _ { i } ;$ at the second stage, they choose their output $q _ { i } , i = 1 , 2 , . . . , n ;$

3. We assume $\boldsymbol { L } _ { i } { \in } [ 0 , \overline { { \boldsymbol { L } } } ]$ and $q _ { i } \in [ 0 , \overline { { q } } ]$ for all i, where $L _ { i }$ and $q _ { i }$ are differentiable; L and q are the corresponding upper bounds. These upper bounds are necessary to imply that each <sup>fi</sup>rm's ITAM and output levels are constrained by other factors such as capital resources and production capabilities;

4. Parameters, n, a, m, $p _ { 0 } ,$ and r, are all constants and exogenous to the model;

5. Participating <sup>fi</sup>rms have complete information in the game; and

6. Pro<sup>fi</sup>t function is: $\pi _ { i } = ( p - c _ { i } ) q _ { i } - C _ { i } ,$ , where $c _ { i } = f ( m ) - r L _ { i } ,$ $C _ { i } { = } p _ { 0 } L _ { i } ^ { 2 } .$

Therefore, the pro<sup>fi</sup>t function of <sup>fi</sup>rm i is:

$$
\pi_ {i} = \bigg (a - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} q _ {i} - f (m) + r L _ {i} \bigg) q _ {i} - p _ {0} L _ {i} ^ {2}.
$$

We solve the game using backward induction.

During the second stage of the game, given the outputs of other <sup>fi</sup>rms, $q _ { j } ( j \neq i )$ , <sup>fi</sup>rm i chooses its output, q , to maximize its pro<sup>fi</sup>t, $\pi _ { i \cdot }$ The pro<sup>fi</sup>t function is concave in $q _ { i \cdot }$ Taking the partial derivative of pro<sup>fi</sup>t function with respect to $q _ { i } ,$ , and solving for $\partial \pi _ { i } / \partial q _ { i } = 0 ,$ , we have:

$$
q _ {i} = (a - f (m)) - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} q _ {i} + r L _ {i}.
$$

Summing the above expression over all the <sup>fi</sup>rms, we have:

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} q _ {i} = \frac {n (a - f (m)) + r \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} L _ {i}}{n + 1}.
$$

This yields <sup>fi</sup>rm i's output level:

$$
q _ {i} = \frac {a - f (m)}{n + 1} - \frac {r \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} L _ {i}}{n + 1} + r L _ {i}.
$$

Now given the optimal output $q _ { i }$ in the second stage, we can work backward to the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage to <sup>fi</sup>nd <sup>fi</sup>rm i's choice of its ITAM, $L _ { i \cdot }$ Substituting the optimal output level, we can rewrite pro<sup>fi</sup>t function of <sup>fi</sup>rm i as:

$$
\pi_ {i} = \left(\frac {a - f (m)}{n + 1} - \frac {r \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} L _ {i}}{n + 1} + r L _ {i}\right) ^ {2} - p _ {0} L _ {i} ^ {2}.
$$

First let us check the concavity of pro<sup>fi</sup>t function in $L _ { i \cdot }$ The second order condition is:

$$
\frac {\partial^ {2} \pi_ {i}}{\partial L _ {i} ^ {2}} = 2 \bigg (\bigg (\frac {n r}{n + 1} \bigg) ^ {2} - p _ {0} \bigg).
$$

We know that since $p _ { 0 } { > } 2 r ^ { 2 } ,$ hence $\partial ^ { 2 } \pi _ { i } / \partial L _ { i } ^ { 2 } < 2 ( ( n r / ( n + 1 ) ) ^ { 2 } -$ $2 r ^ { 2 } ) < 0 .$ Therefore the pro<sup>fi</sup>t function of <sup>fi</sup>rm i is strictly concave in its ITAM. According to the theory of Nash equilibrium, if each participant's decision space is a non-empty and compact concave set, and the pro<sup>fi</sup>t function is continuous and quasi-concave, we can infer that this game has at least one pure strategy Nash equilibrium.

We need to further prove the uniqueness of pure Nash strategy in our game. Using the approaches in Cachon and Netessine $[ 5 ] ,$ we de<sup>fi</sup>ne $L _ { - i } = ( L _ { 1 } , . . . , L _ { i - 1 } , L _ { i + 1 } , . . . , L _ { n } ) .$ . Further, $L _ { i } = R ( L _ { - i } )$ is the best response function of <sup>fi</sup>rm i. Denote H as the Hessian matrix of π with respect to $L = ( L _ { 1 } , L _ { 2 } , . . . , L _ { n } )$ . Now we just need to verify that inequality $\sum _ { j = 1 , j \neq i } ^ { n } \left| \frac { \partial ^ { 2 } \Pi _ { i } } { \partial L _ { i } \partial L _ { j } } \right| { < } \frac { \partial ^ { 2 } \Pi _ { i } } { \partial L _ { i } ^ { 2 } }$ holds for all i. It is easy to see that:

$$
\frac {\partial^ {2} \pi_ {i}}{\partial L _ {i} \partial L _ {j}} = - \frac {2 n r ^ {2}}{(n + 1) ^ {2}}.
$$

Therefore, we have:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \sum_ {j = 1, j \neq i} ^ {n} \left| \frac {\partial^ {2} \pi_ {i}}{\partial L _ {i} \partial L _ {j}} \right| - \left| \frac {\partial^ {2} \pi_ {i}}{\partial L _ {i} ^ {2}} \right| = \frac {2 (n - 1) n r ^ {2}}{(n + 1) ^ {2}} - 2 \bigg (p _ {0} - \bigg (\frac {n r}{n + 1} \bigg) ^ {2} \bigg) \\ = 2 \bigg (\frac {(2 n ^ {2} - n) r ^ {2}}{(n + 1) ^ {2}} - p _ {0} \bigg) <   0. \end{array}
$$

The inequality above holds, since $p _ { 0 } { > } 2 r ^ { 2 }$ . Therefore, the game has a unique pure strategy Nash equilibrium.

Now we solve the equilibrium strategy using the <sup>fi</sup>rst order condition:

$$
\frac {\partial \pi_ {i}}{\partial L _ {i}} = 2 \left(\frac {a - f (m)}{n + 1} - \frac {r \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} L _ {i}}{n + 1} + r L _ {i}\right) \frac {n r}{n + 1} - 2 p _ {0} L _ {i} = 0.
$$

Summing over i, we have:

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} L _ {i} = \frac {n ^ {2} r (a - f (m))}{(n + 1) ^ {2} p _ {0} - n r ^ {2}}.
$$

Because all <sup>fi</sup>rms are identical, the optimal ITAM of <sup>fi</sup>rm i is simply:

$$
L _ {i} ^ {*} = \frac {n r (a - f (m))}{(n + 1) ^ {2} p _ {0} - n r ^ {2}}.
$$

It is obvious now that there is a functional relationship between IT application maturity and essential elements of management.

## 3.3. Discussion

We have shown the existence and uniqueness of pure Nash equilibrium in the game of n identical <sup>fi</sup>rms, and analytically found the optimal ITAM for a <sup>fi</sup>rm. This unique pure Nash equilibrium is a consistent and stable outcome of the game. In this section, we further discuss the characteristics of the equilibrium result with regard to essential elements of management.

We begin with a discussion of unit product cost function f(m). We use m to represent essential elements of management. In general, product cost here driven by essential elements of management can include market transaction cost, internal coordination cost, and manufacturing cost [42,56]. Market transaction cost would be low when a <sup>fi</sup>rm has excellent strategic cooperation capability. Internal coordination cost would be low when a <sup>fi</sup>rm has strong execution power [11]. Undoubtedly, manufacturing cost would also be low when a <sup>fi</sup>rm has a strong synthesizing capability from essential elements of management. Therefore, we assume that $f ^ { \prime } ( m ) { < } 0$

Based on the unique pure equilibrium strategy of ITAM, it is easy to verify that:

$$
\frac {\partial L (m)}{\partial m} = - \frac {n r}{(n + 1) ^ {2} p _ {0} - n r ^ {2}} \frac {\partial f (m)}{\partial m} > 0.
$$

This implies that the optimal ITAM is an increasing function of essential elements of management. A high ITAM should be selected in the presence of a high EEM.

Essential elements of management can increase the investment return of IT application not only through effectively reducing product cost and increasing output, but also through other practices such as preventing potential new entry into the market, eliminating existing competitors, forecasting market demand accurately, retaining existing customers and capturing potential leads, taking advantage of partnership to increase demands, and so on. More importantly, a high EEM can induce a high ITAM by designing effective organization structure, cultivating outstanding organization culture, implementing retention strategy and incentive mechanism on talented employees, and thus reducing implementation and maintenance cost of IT application.

The implementation and maintenance cost of an IT application or an information system, especially a system requiring a high ITAM (e.g. ERP), is usually high for organizations. Under certain circumstances, implementation and maintenance cost of a system can be several times higher than acquisition cost of hardware and software [26]. Therefore, <sup>fi</sup>rms with a low EEM constraint might be better off by limiting their choice to a low ITAM. Although these <sup>fi</sup>rms can still acquire and try to implement IT applications requiring high ITAM, due to the low EEM, it might be impossible to realize the high ITAM and even more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to justify the enormous cost associated with IT application. On the other hand, <sup>fi</sup>rms with a high EEM, such as good management foundation and excellent organization culture, can usually reduce their product cost through a high ITAM, which can integrate both internal and external resources and achieve enhanced coordination across all business functions. If these <sup>fi</sup>rms choose a low ITAM, they would not be able to achieve integration and enhanced coordination in business processes and, consequently, the IT application can play only a limited role in reducing production, transaction, and coordination cost. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1. Essential elements of management have a positive effect on IT application maturity.

## 4. Data

Based on the above theoretical analysis, we are interested in observing and further investigating the impact of EEM on ITAM in real world companies. We conduct an empirical study to validate theoretical results and test Hypothesis 1.

It is obvious that IT application maturity and essential elements of management cannot be measured or observed directly. We have designed a survey instrument with experts' opinions and tested it with a pilot study. We modi<sup>fi</sup>ed the initial design after reliability and validity tests to generate a formal questionnaire. The formal questionnaire was then distributed to our target subjects. Through distributing a large number of questionnaires in a formal survey, we collected 137 valid samples. Reliability and validity analyses were again conducted before using data in our empirical models. In Section 4.1, we describe the details of measurement constructs of variables. Sections 4.2 and 4.3 provide the details about the corresponding data collection and analysis.

## 4.1. Measurement constructs

We need to study two key variables to test our hypothesis: IT application maturity and essential elements of management. Because these two variables cannot be observed directly, we <sup>fi</sup>rst adopt an existing framework for measuring ITAM in the literature [57], and then we propose a framework for measuring EEM.

## 4.1.1. IT application maturity

Based on a comprehensive literature review, Xiao [57] has proposed a framework to measure IT application maturity. A detailed description of the framework of construct of IT application maturity is as follows. ITAM can be measured with six dimensions: technology, data, man–machine synergy, business operation, functional management, and strategic support. Technology refers to IT infrastructure and software systems of IT application. Data includes basic operational data, business and transaction data, and re<sup>fi</sup>ned data for supporting decision making. Business operation and functional management represent how IT application supports business operations and functional management respectively. Strategic support measures the support from IT applications to business strategy. Man–machine synergy dimension is associated with the friendly user interface, human–computer interaction, visualization, and arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence capability in IT application. Table 1 lists these dimensions and further breaks them down into item variables. In fact, these items are directly measured through our survey instrument using Likert type scales [33].

## 4.1.2. Essential elements of management

Essential elements of management have crucial impacts on product cost, which includes market transactional cost, internal coordination cost, and production cost [28,42,56]. According to these three cost types, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne essential elements of management with three dimensions: strategic cooperation capability, internal coordination capability, and execution capability. Strategic cooperation capability mainly affects market transaction cost, such as the costs of preparing contracts and searching for suppliers and customers. Internal coordinated capability mainly affects internal coordination cost, which includes the costs of communication among employees, information and knowledge transformation, and coordination among different functional departments. Execution capability mainly affects production cost, such as the cost of manufacturing and delivering products and services. Our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of essential elements of management is similar to that used in the empirical study of Hitt [24]. It is also consistent with the approach in Wade and Hulland [55] which categorizes IT capability into external capability, internal capability, and integration capability.

Based on our game theoretical analysis of the relationship between essential elements of management and IT application maturity, we argue that a high value of any of the three dimensions of essential elements of management – strategic cooperation capability, internal coordination capability, or execution capability – should support a high IT application maturity. Therefore, we re<sup>fi</sup>ne our Hypothesis 1 with three dimensions:

Hypothesis 1a. Strategic cooperation capability has a positive effect on IT application maturity.

Hypothesis 1b. Internal coordination capability has a positive effect on IT application maturity.

## 4.3. Data analysis

## 4.3.1. Reliability analysis

Reliability is the extent to which an experiment or any measuring procedure yields the same result on repeated trials. For researchers, the three types of reliability are: stability reliability, internal consistency, and inter-rater reliability. Internal consistency is the most commonly used reliability and often calculated by Cronbach's Alpha ranging from 0 to 1. The higher the Cronbach's Alpha, the higher is the reliability of the test [12]. A rule of thumb is to have a Cronbach's Alpha above 0.6 [8,43]. Another statistics for internal consistency is Corrected Item-Total Correlation (CITC). If the CITC of an item is more than 0.5, and Cronbach's Alpha of the corresponding scale is more than 0.6, the reliability of measuring the scale through that item is acceptable. If the CITC of an item is less than 0.5, this item should be excluded until the above rules of thumb are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed [1,6,32]. Table 3 lists the results of Cronbach's Alpha for all dimension variables and CITCs for all items using SPSS 12. It is obvious that we need to exclude strategic support (y ) (Cronbach's Alpha is 0.478 and CITCs are 0.314) and comprehensiveness of technology $\left( y _ { 1 1 } \right)$ (CITC is 0.483).

## 4.3.2. Factor analysis

After reliability analysis on the data, factor analysis is further conducted to handle the potential high correlations among dimensions of ITAM and EEM. Factor analysis can also validate our measurement constructs [30].

Here is the procedure of our factor analysis. First, we test whether items are appropriate for factor analysis. If the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy is more than 0.5, and the covariance matrix of items is not an identity matrix (Bartlett test), items are suitable for factor analysis [27]. Second, if total variance explained by factors exceeds 60%, factors are acceptable [16,31]. Third, with regard to factor loading, we want a “simple factor structure,” i.e., items need to have a main loading greater than 0.50 and no crossloadings greater than 0.40, or the main loading factor is 0.40 and no cross-loadings greater than 0.20. If the criteria are not satis<sup>fi</sup>ed, we will exclude the item from our sample [13].

In the following section, we apply factor analysis based on the above criteria to examine the validity of our measurement constructs. We use the data reduction tool in SPSS 12 for our analysis, and in the tool, we used principal component analysis as extraction method and varimax with Kaiser normalization as rotation method.

## 5. Empirical models

We start with analyzing the overall impact of essential elements of management on IT application maturity. Because the functional relationship between IT application maturity and essential elements of management is not de<sup>fi</sup>ned, we use the Box–Cox transformation to estimate the functional form with the data [7]. The Box–Cox transformation of variable Z is:

$$
Z ^ {(\lambda)} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \left(Z ^ {\lambda} - 1\right) / \lambda , & \text { if } \lambda \neq 0; \\ \ln (Z), & \text { if } \lambda = 0. \end{array} \right.
$$

Note that this formulation yields a linear model when $\lambda = 1$ . We <sup>fi</sup>rst estimate the model in the following form:

$$
\frac {y _ {i} ^ {\lambda} - 1}{\lambda} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \frac {x _ {i} ^ {\lambda} - 1}{\lambda} + \varepsilon_ {i}.
$$

Here $y _ { i }$ is $I T$ application maturity of <sup>fi</sup>rm $i ; x _ { i }$ represents essential elements of management of <sup>fi</sup>rm $i . \beta _ { 0 }$ and $\beta _ { 1 }$ are the constant term and coef<sup>fi</sup>cient respectively. $\varepsilon _ { i }$ is the error term for <sup>fi</sup>rm i.

We assume that the transformation parameter, λ, is the same for each variable, and the error term is an identical and independent random variable. Because nonlinear least square (NLS) estimator, which can be obtained through minimizing sum of squares of residuals, neglects Jacobi transformation, it might not be a consistent estimator. Hence, we use the maximum likelihood estimation method in order to obtain more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and consistent results [18]. We use the following function as the probability density function of the error term ε:

$$
f (\varepsilon) = \left(2 \pi \sigma^ {2}\right) ^ {- n / 2} \exp \left(- \varepsilon^ {\prime} \varepsilon / 2 \sigma^ {2}\right).
$$

The joint density function of $y$ is:

$$
f (y) = \left(2 \pi \sigma^ {2}\right) ^ {- n / 2} \exp \left(- \left(y ^ {(\lambda)} - \beta_ {0} I - \beta_ {1} x ^ {(\lambda)}\right) ^ {\prime} \left(y ^ {(\lambda)} - \beta_ {0} I - \beta_ {1} x ^ {(\lambda)}\right) / 2 \sigma^ {2}\right) \left| \frac {\partial \varepsilon^ {\prime}}{\partial y} \right|.
$$

## 6. Conclusions and discussion

In the last decade, almost all the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant organizational changes are derived from the application of information technology. IT application is not only complex but also costly. Some <sup>fi</sup>rms have obtained remarkable achievement and competitive advantage through IT applications. However, for some other <sup>fi</sup>rms, they have invested heavily in IT application but barely achieved the expected performance improvements. Therefore, it is very important for managers to determine the appropriate application maturity and enhance existing IT application maturity.

This paper studies the impacts of essential elements of management on IT application maturity using both analytical and empirical models. Based on a two-stage non-cooperative game model, it is found that essential elements of management have a positive effect on IT application maturity. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is further supported by an empirical study based on 137 <sup>fi</sup>rm-level data. According to the results, a <sup>fi</sup>rm should make decisions on its IT application maturity based on its own essential elements of management. Only by matching IT application maturity with its management practices, can a <sup>fi</sup>rm fully utilize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of IT applications and, consequently, achieve a better business performance. Without the right level of essential elements of management, a <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot achieve the desired IT application maturity. Further, investments on sophisticated IT applications may turn out to be a waste of resources.

Overall, essential elements of management have positive effects on IT application maturity. We further categorize essential elements of management into three dimensions: strategic cooperation capability, internal coordination capability, and execution capability. Our empirical analysis shows that strategic cooperation capability does not have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive effect on any dimensions of IT application maturity in this study. The reason for this <sup>fi</sup>nding might be sample speci<sup>fi</sup>c since the majority of our <sup>fi</sup>rm samples are state-owned <sup>fi</sup>rms in China. We have eliminated the strategic support dimension in IT application maturity during factor analysis. This may result in the <sup>fi</sup>nal IT application maturity measurement not containing the information on how IT application supports business strategies. Internal coordination capability and execution capability have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positive effects on various dimensions of IT application maturity: internal coordination capability has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive effects on all four dimensions of IT application maturity, while execution capability has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive effects on technology and man–machine synergy dimensions of IT application maturity.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study offer practical guidance for <sup>fi</sup>rms to purposefully construct management strategies so as to improve their IT application maturity levels. From the discussions above, we can infer that improving IT application maturity through essential elements of management can be realized by enhancing <sup>fi</sup>rm internal coordination capability, such as establishing excellent organizational culture, training and attracting talents, rewarding management practices effectively, and so on. Excellent culture can advocate employees to embed IT application into daily business operations. Strategy on talent retention can be designed carefully so as to promote training programs on the planning, implementation, and maintenance of IT application. Appropriate incentive mechanisms can encourage high-level managers to pay more attention to the implementation and maintenance of IT application as well as to enhance their effort on maintaining good relationship with employees. It can also be observed that improving IT application maturity through essential elements of management can be achieved partially by enhancing <sup>fi</sup>rm execution capability, such as continuously improving product offering, simplifying decision making structures, encouraging cooperation, and so on. These approaches can lead to more effective technology utilization and enhance man–machine synergy.

There are a few directions for future research on IT application maturity. First, we assume that factors not considered in the model but presenting in <sup>fi</sup>rm's business and decision making processes are constant. In reality, those factors can interact with essential elements of management in our model simultaneously and indirectly impact IT application maturity. Second, we have not studied the impact of IT application maturity on essential elements of management, which might lead to endogenous problems in the model. Third, most of our data samples are from the Pearl River Delta area of China, which might pose a geographical limitation in interpreting the results. This study, however, serves as a good starting point for possible generalization. Our game theoretical model has no constraints on the geographical location of <sup>fi</sup>rms. Any market structure similar to our model setup can use the equilibrium strategy derived. Our empirical approach provides a useful guideline on conducting similar studies in other countries. This may create another research stream on the similarities or differences among <sup>fi</sup>rms from different countries with regard to the relationship of IT application maturity and essential elements of management. Finally, we have a small sample with <sup>fi</sup>rms having IT application implementations; a larger sample size can certainly improve the validity of our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings.
