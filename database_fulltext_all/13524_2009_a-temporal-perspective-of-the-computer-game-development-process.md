# A temporal perspective of the computer game development process

Patrick Stacey\* & Joe Nandhakumar<sup>†</sup>

Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, UK, \*email: patrick.stacey@wbs.ac.uk and <sup>†</sup>email: joe.nandhakumar@wbs.ac.uk

Abstract. This paper offers an insight into the games software development process from a time perspective by drawing on an in-depth study in a games development organization. The wider market for computer games now exceeds the annual global revenues of cinema. We have, however, only a limited scholarly understanding of how games studios produce games. Games projects require particular attention because their context is unique. Drawing on a case study, the paper offers a theoretical conceptualization of the development process of creative software, such as games software. We found that the process, as constituted by the interactions of developers, oscillates between two modes of practice: routin ized and improvised, which sediment and flux the working rhythms in the context. This paper argues that while we may predeterminately lay down the broad stages of creative software development, the activities that constitute each stage, and the transition criteria from one to the next, may be left to the actors in the moment, to the temporality of the situation as it emerges. If all development activities are predefined, as advocated in various process models, this may leave little room for opportunity and the creative fruits that flow from opportunity, such as enhanced features, aesthetics and learning.

Keywords: computer game, software development process, temporal structure

## INTRODUCTION

The computer games industry has made particularly extensive inroads into our social and economic lives of late. For example, the annual global revenues of computer games currently outstrip that of cinema (Gapper, 2004). Despite the growing social and economic importance of computer games, it is rare to find research in the information systems (IS) field that focuses on them, and more particularly, the process of computer game development. By process we mean the continuum of activities from conception to product delivery. There are only a few process-oriented studies of game development, including Scacchi (2004), Holmström (2001) and Baba & Tschang (2001). There are also games industry conferences (e.g. the Game Developer Conference) and publications (e.g. Game Developer Magazine) that discuss game development methods. However, these materials have not provoked the IS community in general, and so games development process research remains scant. Current research areas in computer games only consider specific activities in the process, such as game design. This stream of research discusses a variety of design techniques, like scenarios, body storming, paper prototyping, rapid prototyping (Clanton, 1998; Bjork et al., 2002; Cheok et al., 2002; Johnson & Wiles, 2003; Kaneva & Sugiyama, 1998; Rouse, 1999), simulation (Kaneva & Sugiyama, 1998), cuisinart (Rouse, 1999) and play environments such as mixed reality (Cheok et al., 2002). A second stream of research, artificial intelligence, tries to improve the intelligence of the computer opponent to enrich single-player gaming (Schaeffer & Jaap van den Herik, 2002). A third stream is not concerned with development, but rather with user/player behaviour issues, such as obsession (Rehak, 2003), aggression (Williams & Clippinger, 2002), social acceptance (Hsu & Lu, 2004), and real-life mimicry (Nutt & Railton, 2003).

Although there are only a few studies regarding the computer game development process in the field of IS, there are many concerning other software development processes. For example, Wood-Harper et al. (1985) expand the scope of existing academic-led methodologies, Baskerville et al. (2003) evaluate emerging industry-led methodologies, Curtis et al. (1988) conceptualize the behavioural process and contextual influence, Nandhakumar & Jones (1997) conceptualize user–developer relations, and Brooks (1995) discusses IS development project management. Although games development perhaps has something in common with these process types and issues, we still have a limited understanding of them in the distinctive context of game development.

Computer games, including those played on PCs, consoles, and hand-held devices like mobile phones, are a distinctive context for software development because they are designed to be compelling and sensory (Baba & Tschang, 2001; Swartout & van Lent, 2003); to provide an entertainment experience (Baba & Tschang, 2001). This experience may also be described as non-utilitarian, inherently pleasurable, exciting and enjoyable (Calder & Staw, 1975).

Generally, computer games are not produced in accordance with the specific needs of a specific player/user – there are no user requirements as such (Baba & Tschang, 2001). Thus the relationship between developer and player-user, at least in the pre-release stages of a new game, is weak. A game project’s requirements instead reside with a visionary designer (Baba & Tschang, 2001) who interacts with the ‘fuzzy’ trends and tastes of popular culture, i.e. ‘what’s cool’. Furthermore, game designers do not necessarily follow a linear development chronology. Instead they may develop in inspirational ‘bursts’ as they are influenced by and influence popular culture. In this sense developers craft computer games; the games take shape in an emergent fashion. And yet, as in any working context, game developers still have deadlines to meet, which produces a tension between their creative latitude and project control. In the UK, game developers have a mixed track record when it comes to honouring deadlines (Stacey & Nandhakumar, 2005). Therefore, such organisations need to find ways to release and rein in their creative talent so that the resultant product is not only of high entertainment value, but also is delivered in a timely fashion.

In order to gain better insights into the games development process, this research investigates one games development organization in depth. The main aims of this paper are (1) to elucidate the process of and practices involved in developing creative software such as computer games; (2) drawing on temporal structures as a sensitizing device, to examine how the mode of practice varies in games development organisations; and, (3) what are the implications for management of such practices, such that the game is not only entertaining but is delivered in a timely fashion.

The rest of paper proceeds as follows: we review the essential perspectives on time; describe the research setting and approach; provide a description and analysis of the case; conceptualise the game development process, and then discuss the implications for theory and practice of creative software development

## TEMPORAL PERSPECTIVE OF SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT

There is always a temporal element to any process. Because our focus is on the process and practices of software development, we use a ‘temporal lens’ (Ancona et al., 2001) to gain a better understanding of them. Ancona et al. (2001) claim that using a ‘temporal lens’ in studying organizations provides a useful perspective on processes and practices and also offers a way of conceptualizing their trajectories over time, various cycles that they align with, and their place in the history of events on the continuum of time. Ancona et al. (2001) therefore state that issues such as the timing of events, flow, pace, cycles, rhythms and temporal orientation are some of the main focuses of research using a temporal lens. Time has also been an important dimension in the study of software development (e.g. Nandhakumar, 2002). With the increasing pressure to meet software quality demand and timely delivery, there have been a few studies focusing on time pressures in software development (e.g. Austin, 2001).

Whereas temporal research in the management field has received increasing attention in recent years, Ancona et al. (2001) claim that research using a temporal perspective is in the experimentation phase and that there is little integration among researchers, especially across disciplines. Although there is no dominant paradigm for time research, Ancona et al. (2001, p. 646) argue, ‘a temporal lens could serve as an important framework for explaining and understanding organizational behaviour’.

Time can be objectively portrayed and interpreted based on measured clock time and can also reflect the subjective experience of each individual (Ancona et al., 2001). Much of the software development literature adopts a linear-objective view of time. With regard to the linearity-objective view, Grazia (1972) sees the straight-line perspective of time as being born out of Christian society in terms of the pathway from sin to salvation. Intrinsic to ‘line’ is sequence and linearity; notions evident in threshold process models such as the Capability Maturity Model and stage-wise structured development methodologies (Boehm, 1988), although, to varying degrees, the cycle and line coexist in the latter. Time is seen as unitized, homogenous (an hour in the morning is equivalent to an hour in the evening), measurable (Starkey, 1989) and therefore predictable (Paulk et al., 1993). The linear perspective ultimately structures the planning experience, giving the impression that work and time may be decomposed and then recomposed in order to derive project milestones.

The antithesis is that time is subjective, i.e. it is personally constructed (Orlikowski & Yates, 2002), heterogeneous (Starkey, 1989), non-linear, and non-sequential. Individuals exercise autonomy to self-determine their time patterns: everyday examples may include freelancers and the self-employed. Autonomy does not necessarily connote capability, however; one may have the opportunity to self-determine one’s time patterns but operationalizing that opportunity is not a corollary. Therefore, we can separate the ‘opportunity’ to self-determine time (Jacques, 1982) from its realization, i.e. separate the intention to do something from the act (Giddens, 1993).

A third view then, is that time is experienced intersubjectively through a process of temporal structuring (Giddens, 1984; Nandhakumar, 2002; Orlikowski & Yates, 2002). This view is thought to constitute a bridge between the objective and subjective ways of thinking about time (Orlikowski & Yates, 2002). From this perspective, time is considered as being constitutive of all forms of social activities (Giddens, 1987). Time in the day-to-day experience in a particular context is therefore constituted in repetition; activities recurring in temporal locations in the form of routines, phases and project deadlines. These recurrent practices help to produce and reproduce ‘temporal structures’ (Orlikowski & Yates, 2002). Temporal structures or rhythms are therefore individuals’ everyday practices that have been institutionalized through the reproduction of such practices. Under different conditions individuals reinforce or change their temporal structures, as well as introduce new ones. Individuals have the potential for reinforcing and altering temporal structures, although they are not always aware of it (Giddens, 1984). Thus, temporal structures serve to constrain or shape the actors’ activities and at the same time enable or be shaped by human social action. The concept of temporal structures is thus closely related to Giddens’ Theory of Structuration (1984), which builds on the notion of durée of day-to-day life and the routinization that leads to the recreation of the structural properties of a social system. Structuration theory, however, includes a more elaborate theorization, both of the human agent and of the organization of the setting of interaction.

In our analysis we draw on temporal structures as a sensitizing device (Walsham, 1993) to structure the interpretation of our data. This means that we seek to elicit and examine the various temporal rhythms at different levels of context that are found in the case-study organization. Temporal structures at each of the levels of context are considered not as independent, but as points on a continuum that stretches from the moment-by-moment daily experience of individuals to the lifetime of institutions. In drawing attention to the situated and routinized nature of software development practices, our perspective of time moves away from an objective treatment of time that is typical of much software development research, to an understanding of its role in the constitution of social organizations of game development.

## RESEARCH SETTING AND APPROACH

We conducted an in-depth study of a computer games studio in Singapore, a country which has little in the way of natural resources and therefore relies on foreign investment into industries such as manufacturing to buttress its economy. Time is of the essence in this agile economy, especially in the wake of economic and social crises such the Asian Financial Crisis and SARS. Although the computer games industry is a mere fledaling. it has been identified as one of the new ‘engines’ of economic growth by the government, who speak of developing games ‘factories’, ‘highways’ and ‘digiports’ (Lim, 2004). These metaphors are reminiscent of Singapore’s traditionally manufacturing-led economy. In such industrial metaphors there appears to be an implied desire to ‘ramp-up’ game development to the point where games are rolling off a conveyor belt somewhere; a Fordist notion of time at a national level.

The research approach adopted in this study is interpretive (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Walsham, 1993), because we attempt to study phenomena from the perspective of the people involved and to understand the meanings that individuals assign to them. In our study, this involved the collection of detailed, qualitative data on the game development process through intensive engagement with games developers in their organizational contexts. In this paper we focus on one studio, Computer Games Studio (CGS – a pseudonym) as an example.

CGS is a recognized games company with no more than 20 staff, although this headcount fluctuates somewhat as a result of turnover. Even though the company has its main operations in Singapore, it has developed numerous games in association with studios in France and Italy, and has distributed them throughout Europe. It is further recognized by Singapore’s Infocomm Development Authority (IDA) as one of the leading local studios.

## DATA COLLECTION AND ANALYSIS

From January 2004 to July 2005, we made several visits to CGS. We conducted 20 interviews with members of the core development team at CGS: Table 1 exhibits the nature of the interviews and the position of the interviewees. The data collection process was as follows: (1) we began by interviewing the team as a whole so as to become better acquainted, introduce our research project to them, and make initial observations of the team dynamics; (2) we asked each member of the team to prepare a flow diagram of their work practices, which they were asked to bring along to the first interview – this would then provide a starting point for the discussion regarding their work practices; (3) unstructured, structured and semi-structured interviews were then conducted with all of the members between 2004 and 2005; each interview lasted at least 1 hour and was sometimes followed by a meal and informal discussion that provided further useful data; and (4) a summary of the data collected was periodically presented to the managing director for him to validate and provide us with feedback. Detailed notes were kept on the interviews and the observations we made of their work practices during each visit. Company documents and flowcharts (as delineated in the ‘CGS game development process’ section, further in the text) were also analyzed to gain more insight into the practices observed and to verify interview notes. By adopting an interpretive approach to collecting and analysing the interview data, we attempted to understand the phenomenon (the game development process) through the meanings that team members assigned to that process, rooted in data incidents like jargon, symbols and metaphors the interviewee used.

To help organise the data and its analysis, the interview transcripts were imported into nVivo – a computer-aided qualitative data analysis software. nVivo is simply a tool for organizing and structuring the data: the analysis is ultimately done by the researchers, not the tool. Hence to fully digest the data, a microanalysis of words, phrases and lines was carried out. The micro-interpretations this produced were stored as ‘databites’ in each rich text document so they could be conveniently revisited later to inform the bottom-up conceptual development.

This data analysis was free flowing and stimulated a number of interpretations; an important principle in interpretive research (cf. Walsham, 1993). This process produced text per document which was of almost equal length to each transcript, i.e. it was extensive. Next came the low-level coding of the text. The ‘tags’ were based on actual words, concepts in the text that were coded in nVivo.

During the analysis we tried to stay close to the text so as to gradually conceptualise, to prevent us from being seduced into making premature leaps towards higher-order concepts predicated on our own minds. Altogether, the lowest-level analysis produced 306 ‘free nodes or ‘open codes’. We then structured these so as to facilitate the discovery of patterns in the data. To this end we used the concept of temporal structures as a sensitizing device; the free nodes were categorized as actions, intended actions, intended outcomes and unintended outcomes. Links were then established between nodes across these categories to form a ‘chain of evidence’, e.g. actions and their (un)intended outcomes. As patterns and themes emerged, we began to think of them in more abstract terms (cf. Miles & Huberman, 1994) and develop our conceptualization of the game development process as the fluxing (i.e. unfreezing) and sedimentation (i.e. freezing) of various temporal structures.

## CASE DESCRIPTION

CGS has a management hierarchy with four layers of managers: senior management, middlemanagement, project leaders and executives (the lowest rank of employee in Singapore’s working cultural context). The working environment at the premises gave a sense of homefrom-home: there was a meeting room with antique Indonesian furniture which doubled as the managing director’s office, an office for the more technical members of the development team who seemed subdued, although their walls seemed to reflect their female and Arthurian fantasies, an office for the artists who had various traditional games like table football set up, and a laboratory full of computers for rendering purposes.

## CGS’s game development process

Our first visit to CGS comprised a meeting with the organizational actors listed in Table 2. During that meeting, we asked the group to describe their development process for their most recent games project. This game was conceived in-house as a multiplayer first-person shooter for the PC platform, with ports to the mobile phone platform too. The A6 engine was used as a development platform to avoid coding from scratch

Led by the managing director, we documented the process in our field notes as:

Alf described how the process began with the conceptualization of the game. He trawled the internet for the look and feel before putting down his ideas into a design document (DD); we were shown an example of this. The DD was then circulated throughout the company for comments. The document was circulated and modified until a ‘stable’ concept was reached, i.e. all developers were happy with it. Then Richard translated the DD into software modules and assigned them to the various engineers. Once each engineer had done their respective coding and unit testing they integrated their modules and the game as a whole was assembled. Integration testing and fixing followed. Once they had eliminated the major integration bugs, the system or game as a whole was tested, i.e. black-box testing.

During subsequent visits, we asked the interviewees to prepare flow diagrams of their development practices for their most recent games project; an approach based on Radice et al.’s (1999: p. 100) programming process study. The interviewees produced illustrations o varying detail and content, which we present in Figures 1 through 4. They drew these on paper and we have tried to dutifully reproduce and present them here for the purposes of building a thick description (Geertz, 1973). Furthermore, these figures depict the, at times, subjective nature of the process. The intern produced the richest flow diagram (Figure 1). Her perspective was an essentially chronological one, i.e. it depicted a chronology of stages or processes. The only people mentioned were artists. The project manager’s (Figure 3) and managing director’s (Figure 2) perspectives were also chronological, although the managing director did mention the producer’s liaison with the programming, art and sound teams. Interestingly, the lead programmer (Figure 5) and CG programmer (Figure 4) diagrams did not depict a chronology of linear stages or processes but of interactions between people.

These perspectives formed the basis for further discussions with each participant during which we sought to ‘unpack’ and explore the meanings of their terms, such as ‘testing’. This terminological ‘unpacking’ also helped us understand the intentions, actions and outcomes that lay behind them. On a practical level, the exercise helped the interviewee to focus and bring to mind their working context.

## Documentation and tools

We found that two documents were produced before the game went into production, which were referred to as the design document (DD) and technical document (TD). The design document took the games designer months to produce, relying on a confluence between inspiration, market trends and demands on the games designer’s time. The technical document or specifications were based on the content of the design document and took the lead programmer a few weeks to compose. The implementability of game features were discussed and negotiated with the games designer. Once the specification was agreed upon, the technical team set to work with the aid of development tools installed on office PCs, such as Jcreator (an Integrated Development Environment for Java programming), Wireless ToolKit, a mobile phone screen emulator (provided by the respective manufacturer to test Java games).

## Play-test feedback loop

Throughout the project’s lifetime, the game was continuously play-tested in order to (a) evaluate its aesthetic appeal, and, (b) to ‘witch hunt’ (John’s expression) bugs. Determining the aesthetic appeal of the game involved a process akin to a hermeneutic circle – playing the parts (or modules), then judging how they interacted as a whole. Play-testing was conducted until the point when a (subjective) product evaluation could be made, i.e. an overall opinion was formed.

![](/api/attachments/2SSHN4V9/fulltext/images/cbdd7216e9eb00f21c4549b92c74152cbe0767c4fe28bf3234d7e68fb914a876.jpg)  
Figure 2. Game designer/managing director’s flow diagram.

This led to changes in the way the game was conceptualised, designed and coded. Play-testing appeared to be characterised by a rapid feedback between the broad phases of (re-)conceptualise, (re)design, (re)code and (re-)play-test (Figure 6). This is similar to Zimmerman’s (2003) description of iterative design. According to the developers, upon playtesting, the game project was thrown back into a state of re-conceptualization, redesign or recoding before being play-tested again, when key decisions on how to advance the project were made. The development cycle usually began in a linear fashion, starting with conceptualization and following through to design, code and test. Upon play-testing however, if the game needed much rework, it would go back ‘to the drawing board’ to be re-conceptualized. If, however, the concept was deemed sound but changes to the technical and/or aesthetic design were necessary, then the process would orientate to design and subsequently pick up the original linear process trail, i.e. flow through to code and play-test.

This feedback loop’s duration, frequency and reconfiguration were decided daily by the team members, depending on the circumstances facing them; therefore the feedback loop’s temporal structure ‘fluxed’ or formed and reformed through the developers’ ongoing improvisations. For instance, as ‘bugs’ emerged during play-testing, they inspired new opportunities for innovative features. The CG programmer recounted one example to us: during play-testing, the lead programmer found a place in the game-world where his character could ‘camp’ without being shot. Instead of seeing this as a ‘bug’ however, it inspired the team to create an additional enemy that uniquely had the range to reach that part of the ‘world’. Whether such a ‘feature’ would be added to the game was ultimately the game designer’s decision. However, because of business commitments (he was also the managing director) with government agencies and shareholders, he was largely unavailable to make such decisions. This dependency led to waiting and stalled development. Consequently, the developers began taking the initiative to make their own design change decisions.

Another dependency that was made known to us by one of the engineers was with respect to the artists who were perceived as ‘bottlenecks’, ‘artists could not work to the deadlines’, we were told, and when they tried to work to deadlines the standard of their artwork fell. The CG programmer, who worked closely with the artists thought the engineers should learn graphics tools such as Adobe Photoshop so they could at least produce their own test graphics. It was thought that this would reduce the engineers’ dependency on the artists. However, the lead programmer had already begun finding his way round Photoshop on top of his other duties; according to the managing director, Richard was a games designer, project manager, quality assurance engineer, programmer and overseer of interns (different from Richard’s account). Artists were perceived not just as ‘bottlenecks’ but as generally being highly ‘mobile’. According to Richard a string of artists had joined and resigned from CGS, perhaps because they did not have ‘the right knowledge-level or understanding’ he mused. By this he meant a grasp o terms like MIDP (Mobile Information Device Profile). Consequently many freelance artists had to be recruited to fill the ‘voids’ left by resigning artists. This compensatory strategy was problematized by the managing director ‘brokering’ between the freelancers and the core development team, leading to miscommunication and either the over or underproduction of artwork. This mobility of staff was made known to the researchers with respect to interns too. Interns were usually attached for only 3 months. There was some reliance on them to do the ‘tedious’ programming tasks which were executed with increasing productivity towards the end of their attachment. Learning curves were revisited and productivity fell each time one intern arrived and another left. Recruiting interns from overseas was a further bone of contention. They had one from Germany, but his inability to articulate his problems to the rest of the team was blamed for the project’s delay.

## ANALYSIS

The case provides a rich description of games development at CGS. In terms of the temporal lens, the everyday practices may be seen as a flux of interwoven temporal structures emanating from game development practices. For example, play-testing was characterised by rapid feedback between the activities of (re-)conceive, (re)design, (re)code, and (re-)play-test (Figure 6), and was subject to daily reconfiguration by the team members depending on the circumstances facing them. These development activities were contiguously and temporally located with no real sense of chronology. They appeared to be emergent, unplanned, occurring ‘in the moment’. The emerging circumstances of the process were partly produced by the actors themselves, through their evaluation of the aesthetics of the game, i.e. the gameplay, graphics, sound and narrative. We may think of aesthetics as surrogates for the intended and unintended outcomes of the actors’ development activities; the intended outcomes pertaining to those (standard) features which were planned (e.g. game lobby, high score, game objective), the unintended ones being unplanned, such as new enemies, as described in the case (play-testing). Thus, the intended and unintended outcomes of the developers’ activities fluxed and sedimented their temporal structures, which in turn served to constrain, inspire and enable their ongoing creative activities.

The temporal rhythm of the development activities occurred in a situation characterised by circumstances which were to some degree produced by the actors. It occurred to us that these self-produced circumstances were influenced by the inner-life (Ciborra, 2002) of the actors, which also relates to their constant state of reflexive monitoring (Giddens, 1984) of their situation. This may be evidenced in the manner in which the games aesthetics and features were (subjectively) evaluated and acted upon during play-testing. For example:

I guess our artist was a little more China than we expected . . . as in, his modelling is perfect, its nice, but the first few textures he came out with were really China-like in his design. He had those waves, you know those anime kind of waves on the car, it looked like a Chinatown, especially from the back, which is the main way you will see the car anyway! (taken from an interview with John).

Then I heard a playful wailing from the engineers’ office; a ‘someone falling over the cliff’ kind of sound. ‘Sounds like someone dying’ I remarked to Alf. ‘They’re a crazy lot’, he said (taken from field notes while talking to Alf).

The passion and moodiness of the artists and programmers was further evidenced when thei temporal rhythm was interrupted by the researchers and in their vocal attitude towards ‘process’, ‘its really about gameplay, product as opposed to process’ (Mac). Thus, the inne life, the mood and emotion of the developer is an inseparable aspect of the game developmen process and its associated temporality. Inner life emanates temporal rhythms in terms of feeling, like doing something, doing it on the spur of the moment, extemporaneously; impromptu or improvised.

However, the rhythms of the game development situation were also subject to the spatia comings-and-goings of actors. As actors left the context permanently (by resigning) and/or temporarily (e.g. absence of the games designer), incumbents had to fill the gaps or roles they left behind, whether it was one of their ‘competencies’ or not, which they did through improvised make-do actions. For example,

During the midway stages of the project, he always found himself waiting for the game designer’s comments. I asked him whether he could recount to me an actual example of this. His example was taken from a game called Cambodia. With respect to the magical powers that a character has access to, originally the lead game designer only defined one spell. After coding this and then trying it out, he found it rather boring and unchallenging. He then modified the game of his own volition to have more types of spells (taken from field notes while talking to Richard).

This interrupted their routine and created a different experience of time, because each role (artist for example) comprised categories of time-demanding activities (concept art creation, wire framing) and in effect constituted a subculture.

Actors joining the context also influenced the incumbents’ temporal rhythms. For example, John had only just joined CGS from IBM, where he used to develop banking systems. He described the development culture at IBM as: (a) ‘the specs were very thick to prevent scope creep’, (b) the development process was very structured, (c) most of his time was occupied with writing documents. (d) it could take two weeks iust to change a single line of code. He had been used to a development environment where his work activities occurred in a predetermined chronological order. Through the introduction of a suite of documents (concept, design and technical) he intended to ‘free up’ time, as well as to enhance productivity and improve guidance for the interns. To mobilise his intentions, he constructed symbols that would appeal to management in the form of proposals. We interpret this as (unwittingly perhaps) trying to reconstruct the routine that he had been used to at IBM in CGS, and thereby helped to transform the temporal structures in the CGS context.

Outside the CGS context, the mobile phone manufacturers, the Singapore games industry, the international games industry and the marketplace all have their own temporal rhythms or schedules which impact the temporal structure inside the context to some degree. At the industry level, most manufacturers take weeks or months to provide the unlock tools that CGS needs in order to conduct system testing of mobile games. One particular manufacturer provides the tool almost instantly to CGS. Consequently most mobile games are produced for that manufacturer’s phone; the temporal rhythms of CGS and that manufacturer may be described as being in-sync. At the government level in Singapore. local studios are encouraged to align with a national cause and calendar, i.e. to make Singapore an attractive business destination through participation in ‘recognized’ national (Games eXchange Alliance, IGDA

Singapore Chapter) and international games events (Games Developers Conference, E3). Fortunately, there is some temporal synchronity as far as international games events are concerned; most studios and games bodies around the world try to showcase their games or capabilities at E3, which is to games what Cannes is to film. While this helped to reinforce the routine, such structures also provided opportunities for development of different temporality through improvisatory work practices. For example, one of the consequences of attending such events was that developers were spatially removed from their development contexts and this temporary mobility and absence in turn fed the make-doism of the team.

## DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

The paper’s case analysis illustrates the complex and messy nature of the temporal rhythms of games development. The nature of the emerging temporal rhythms might be seen as ranging from fluxing structures that are formed and re-formed, reflecting the improvisatory nature of game development process, to sedimenting temporal structures representing estab lished routines within the context.

Figure 7 depicts how various activities contributed to the flux (unfreezing) and sedimentation (freezing) of these temporal structures. For example, individuals in the team produced temporalities through improvisation, which arose mainly through play-testing. The exiting of staff from the context led to improvised make-do actions to fill the roles they left behind. The developer’s inner life or mood led to impromptu or improvised actions. The temporal structure created through these improvisatory practices in turn shaped the game development rhythms. Companies attempt to shape the temporalities of the team by forming intentions (i.e. planning) and acting upon them through the construction of symbols with such legitimating intended outcomes as ‘freeing up’ time. Managerial intentions were also reflected in the behaviour of the new project manager, who attempted to reconstruct the routine that he had experienced in traditional organizations. Within the national context of our study, companies and industries appear to conform to or act in accordance with government policies, i.e. their temporalities are shaped by those of governments. Within the national context of our study, the nation appears to be shaped by the temporality of that economy and marketplace.

As illustrated in Figure 7, game development may be conceptualised as a compound of fluxing and sedimenting temporal structures that emanate from the individual, team, company, industry, nation, inter-nation and marketplace levels. In this the formation of fluxing structures reflecting the improvisatory nature of the game development process and the sedimenting temporal structures representing established routines within the context. The nature of these temporal structures indicates that the process, as constituted by the interactions of the developers, oscillates between two poles of routinized and improvised modes of practice. When there is a deadline for a project the process tends to become routine rather than improvised. However developers resort to improvised make-do action when there is a need to overcome structural constraints. With this in mind, a development activity may be said to occur along a continuum of temporal enactment. As well as a development activity being either improvised or routinized, it may also be ‘chaordic’ (Highsmith, 2002) – a combination of chaos and order – or contrapuntal, i.e. a harmonious combination of both modes of working.

The way the different courses of action were taken in different situations in games development depended on the games developers’ constant state of reflexive monitoring (cf. Giddens, 1984) of their situation and on the omnipresent potential for change. Routine actions therefore helped to create the feeling of security in this volatile context that was convenient and comforting. However the game developers were not consciously aware of this monitoring or of the continuous opportunities for change.

Our theoretical insights into the mode of practices of games software development have several implications for both systems development research, and for the practice of software development and such creative processes in general. First, this research provides a conceptualization of the development process of creative software, such as computer games development, in that the developers oscillate between two poles of routinised and improvised modes of practice, while these activities contribute to the flux and sedimentation o temporal structures. For systems development research, this conceptualization may provide one way of examining the development process and the underlying contextual influences that shape the development practices. Further research could explore development processes in other settings and contexts and thereby could help to elaborate our conceptualization theoretically.

Second, for practitioners, our findings suggest that while managers may predeterminately lay down the broad stages of game development, the activities that constitute each stage, and the transition criteria from one to the next, may be left to the actors in the moment, to the temporality of the situation as it emerges. If a sub-process such as design or play-test is too defined, this may predetermine the temporality of the situation, leaving little room for opportunity and the creative fruits that opportunity may offer, e.g. enhanced functions, features, aesthetics and learning. We may think of this as macro-management (Highsmith, 2002).

Third, our findings also suggest that, in practice, the creative software work may not be amenable to much of plan-driven software development. Instead, the broad stages of development, such as conceptualisation, design, code and test, may be defined but the activities that constitute each stage, and the transition criteria from one to the next, may be left to the knowledgeable participants in the unfolding production situation.

Finally, the findings suggest striking a balance between control and autonomy, between planned activities and improvised ones, such that modes of work are contrapuntal. However, this may depend on the degree to which the game is intended to be innovative. Improvisation, venturing, and intuitive action may be the preferred course of action with regards to innovative games, i.e. when the project situation is less clear (Mintzberg & Westley, 2001). Hence projects which are sequels may be developed by closely following a more established routine from the initial production version. The degree of innovation sought may determine the balance between improvisation and routinization.

Creative software development work is becoming more prevalent in contemporary software development. For example, we find it in computer games, in entertainment and in leisure robots such as Sony’s Aibo (combines hardware and software work), in military scenario training, and in CGI movies like Toy Story. Our findings therefore may have applicability beyond just game development.

## CONCLUSIONS

In this paper we set out to elucidate the game development process from a temporal perspective and to examine the implications for managing digital entertainment software work and software development in general. From a temporal perspective, we offered the insight that the game development process may be conceptualized as a compound of fluxing and sedimenting temporal structures that emanate from the individual, team, company, industry, nation, internation and marketplace levels. The fluxing structures reflect the improvisatory nature of the game development process, whereas the sedimenting temporal structures represent established routines within and without the context. We presented some implications of this conceptualisation: (1) computer games developers oscillate between two poles of routinized and improvised modes of practice, while these activities contribute to the flux and sedimentation of temporal structures; (2) managers may predeterminately lay down the broad stages of game development, but leave the constituent activities, stages and transition criteria between them, to the actors in the moment; (3) hence, creative software work may not be amenable to plan-driven software development; (4) which suggests a contrapuntal mode of work as being more appropriate, i.e. one that strikes a balance between control and autonomy, between planned activities and improvised ones, although the degrees to which these two modes of working occur may depend on the degree of innovation sought in the game, i.e. how ‘clear’ the development goal is.

Given that creative software development work is becoming more prevalent, our findings may have applicability beyond just game development, such as animated movies and enter tainment robotics.
