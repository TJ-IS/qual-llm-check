## Abstract

Information security policy (ISP) plays a critical role in information systems security management. Past research using General Deterrence Theory (GDT) on employees’ compliance intention (CI) with ISP produced mixed results. We use survey data to investigate how other factors influence the relationship between sanction severity and employees’ CI. The results show that none of the investigated moderating variables interacts with sanction severity on employees’ ISP compliance intentions. However, the significant impact of sanction severity on employees’ ISP CI disappears when the investigated variables are included, and the impact of sanction severity is mediated by perceived efficacy and descriptive norm.

Keywords: General Deterrence Theory; Sanction Severity; Response Cost; Self-Efficacy; Descriptive Norm; Information Security Policy

## 1. Introduction

Information security (IS) has been a serious concern since the beginning of the Internet era. Recent high profile security breaches in highly respected U.S. companies, such as Target [1], Citi Group [2], JPMorgan and Chase [3], etc. have brought more attention to IS because of the high financial costs accrued from these incidents. Academia and industry have realized that IS cannot be achieved through security software and hardware alone [e.g., 4, 5]. Target’s security breach was a result of hackers using login credentials stolen from a partner through an email phishing campaign [1] and Citi Group’s data breach was a result of the theft of data by a contract worker who then sold it [2]. Employee negligence and insider breach are major threats to IS [6, 7]. Certain computing behaviors, such as inappropriate use of computers, network resources, passwords, and email, cannot be addressed by IS technology alone [4]. These types of employee behaviors can be regulated and addressed only by organizational information security policy (ISP), which is defined as “a statement of the roles and responsibilities of the employees to safeguard the information and technology resources of their organizations” [8, p. 526].

Past IS research has studied how to prevent employee negligence and insider breach by understanding what factors affect employees’ behaviors toward ISP compliance or non-compliance [e.g., 4, 8, 9, 10, 11]. These research-tested factors are based on criminological theory such as General Deterrence Theory (GDT) [e.g., 9, 10, 12, 13], Rational Choice Theory (RCT) [e.g., 8] and health belief models such as Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) [e.g., 11, 14, 15]. GDT was found to be the single most cited theory in IS literature [12, 16, 17]. Despite the sound theoretical foundation of prior ISP compliance research, early security research reported disparate results about the effects of formal

#

sanctions on employees’ ISP compliance/non-compliance behaviors. D’Arcy and Herath [17] contend that these mixed results in research that used GDT may be due to missing moderating variables. In addition to the reason of missing moderating variables, we propose that inconsistent statistical analysis procedure for analyzing and reporting mediating effect may be another reason for the mixed results. This study investigates whether the factors, perceived response cost (PRC), perceived descriptive norm (PDN), and perceived self-efficacy (PSEC) to comply with the ISP, interact with the GDT factor, perceived sanction severity (PSS), to influence employees’ ISP compliance intention (CI) in the studied context. Using a staged testing process, this study also investigates the true effect of PSS on employees’ ISP CI when other related factors, PRC, PDN, and PSEC, are controlled. In addition, we examine through which factors PSS may influence employees’ ISP CI by analyzing the mediating effect of the selected factors on the PSS-ISP CI relationship.

The results suggest that informal sanctions and capability may play a more important role in influencing employees’ ISP CI than formal sanctions and cost considerations as reported by other studies [e.g., 18, 19]; however, we provide a theory-based explanation to support our results. The results also suggest that the effect of formal sanctions on ISP compliance intention is mediated by informal sanctions and capability factors. The mediation effects indicate that formal sanction may still matter in ISP compliance, however, indirectly. This perspective opens a new avenue to study the value of formal sanctions in IS management and could shed some light on the inconsistent results of sanction-ISP compliance behavior research in IS. Although this study did not find a moderating effect of the investigated factors on the formal sanction-ISP compliance behavior relationship, we discuss the possible reasons for this from a new perspective: deterrability and offending propensity. As an implication for practice, we will discuss how practitioners can utilize our findings in developing their organization's security culture and security training programs, which are reported as having an important role in influencing employees' ISP compliance behavior [e.g., 8, 10].

Section 5 analyzes the data and presents the findings. Section 6 discusses the findings. Section 7 discusses the implications and limitations.

## 2. Literature review

Information security has drawn attention from academia and practitioners due to its importance in protecting organizational digital assets and the resulting high cost associated with security breach. It is generally agreed that employees’ behaviors play an important role in any IS management program. Employees’ acceptable behaviors toward information security are outlined by an organization’s ISP and attendant statements such as “the roles and responsibilities of the employees to safeguard the information and technology resources of their organizations” [8, p. 526]. Therefore, employees’ compliance with their organizations’ ISP is critical to IS management.

Much ISP compliance research in MIS uses GDT and PMT as theoretical foundations [e.g., 4, 8, 10, 11, 12, 13, 15, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. Both GDT and PMT are rooted in fear appeals, also known as threat appeals, fear-inducing communications, or fear-based messages [13]. Fear appeals “are persuasive messages designed to scare people by describing the terrible things that will happen to them if they do not do what the message recommends” [25, p. 329]. Although GDT is the single most cited theory in IS literature [16, 17], there is no formal definition of GDT [26]. The theory suggests that when a fear appeal in the form of law, regulation, or policy is presented to people, they will assess the benefits and risks of violating the rules outlined in the fear appeal. The risk assessments include how severe the penalties could be, how certain they are that they may be caught if they violate the rules, and how quickly the sanction may come. It is commonly accepted that GDT is a psychological theory that emphasizes the effect of perceptual sanction variables on deterring criminal or deviant behaviors [26, 27, 28, 29]. Therefore, in GDT-related research, the perceptual states of sanctions are measured.

#

GDT contends that certain, swift, and severe sanctions deter or prevent criminal or deviant behaviors [e.g., 13, 25, 26]. In the GDT literature, there is a distinction between formal and informal sanctions. Informal sanctions reflect “the extent of social influence from those who are important to potential offenders” [30, p. 638]. Informal sanctions can be established using methods based on socialor people-related strategies [20]. Informal sanctions include, but are not limited to, social desirability pressure and moral beliefs [12] and normative beliefs and peer behavior [4, 5, 11]. Formal sanctions in the IS policy compliance context refer to policies that are formally outlined in an organization’s IS policy and which “focus on specifications, evaluations, and reward/punishment” [20, p. 284]. Most GDT research includes measurement of PSS and PSC. These constructs usually measure formal sanction severity and certainty. Some studies have investigated the separate effect of PSS and PSC (additive formal sanction effect) [e.g., 4, 10, 11], whereas others did not separate these two factors (multiplicative formal sanction effect) [e.g., 8, 18, 31, 32].

Although the GDT has a sound foundation, the literature reported mixed results on the effectiveness of formal sanctions. For example, in criminology, economics, and related literature, studies support sanctions as effective deterrents for criminal or deviant behavior [e.g. 12, 13, 14, 33, 34]. However, other studies found no or opposite effects of sanctions on criminal or deviant behavior [e.g., 29, 35, 36, 37]. The inconsistent results were also reported in prior IS studies that used GDT as the theoretical foundation. For example, PSS has been found to have a direct negative impact on IS misuse intentions [10, 23, 38]; whereas PSC had no such impact (negative measure) [10, 39]. In other studies, PSC has been found to have a direct impact on ISP compliance (positive measure) [4, 30], but PSS had no such impact [30]. The theoretical proposals and empirical results in IS literature indicate that the true effect of formal sanctions on ISP compliance behavior is far from clear. As shown in Table 1, researchers of recent IS studies have taken into account the theoretical advances and empirical evidence from other disciplines indicating that informal sanctions diminish the effect of formal sanctions [e.g., 18, 19, 37]. Earlier IS studies have also incorporated informal sanctions into their research designs [e.g., 5, 12, 13, 20, 31, 42]. However, from our review of the studies, we found that how informal sanction affects the relationship between formal sanction and ISP CI does not gain sufficient attention from researchers.

Table 1 also shows that most research studied only demographic or organizational control variables. Other variables that have a theoretical relationship with formal sanctions and ISP CI were not studied in the way that they should have been to examine the changes of relationship between formal sanction and ISP CI with and without the other variables. For example, most studies only included informal sanction variables along with formal sanction variables in their final structural model test. They did not use a staged or hierarchical approach [43] to investigate and discuss how the informal sanction variables influence the relationship of the formal sanction-ISP compliance behavior. For example, control variable C may have two effects on the variables of interest, using X as independent and Y as dependent variable. First, control variable C may contaminate the relationship of X and Y if it influences the observed values of X or Y or both. Second, C could create a spurious relationship between X and Y if it is correlated with X or Y or both [43]. In the first case, researchers can perform a simple statistical regression analysis to remove the contamination. In the latter case, the two-staged test process should be used. First, obtain the coefficient of X from the base model that does not include C; second, obtain the coefficient of X from the full model that includes C; then, compare the coefficients to explain the relationship between X and Y [43]. This suggested test process was not employed in the prior studies that included control variables in their studies. Therefore, it is important to include theoretically related control variable in a study. It is equally important to provide evidence to support the inclusion of control variables and employ the right methodologies to test the hypotheses [43].

#

From Table 1, we can see that recent IS studies have not consistently investigated moderating variables on the relationship between formal sanction and ISP CI. D’Arcy and Herath [17] proposed that, in addition to methodological and theoretical issues, moderating variables were also one of the reasons for inconsistent findings in the security literature that used the deterrence theory. We use the deterrability theory to support the importance of investigating the moderating effect of other variables in understanding the true relationship between formal sanction and ISP compliance behavior. There is a paucity of studies in the IS literature that investigate the effect of moderating variables on the relationship of sanctions and ISP compliance. Li et al. [41] found no interaction effect of self-control or procedure justice with multiplicative formal sanction on ISP compliance intention. Harrington [44] found personal denial of responsibility moderated the inverse effect of codes of ethics on computer abuse judgment and intention. D’Arcy et al. [10] found that moral belief has different moderating effects for the two dimensions of formal sanctions: severity and certainty. In Li et al. [30], personal norm was found to moderate the PSS-CI relationship, but not the PSC-CI relationship. These studies indicate that the relationship between formal sanction and ISP CI is moderated by other factors although the two components of formal sanctions may not have the same directional effect on IS compliance in certain circumstances, which indicates that the two components need to be tested separately.

Final point we want to make from Table 1 is that very few studies have formally investigated if informal sanctions mediate the relationship of formal sanctions-ISP CI/IT misuse intentions [8, 12, 31, 40]. The literature review indicates no evidence of a clear understanding of the mechanism behind the influence of formal sanctions on ISP compliance, if such influence exists. Fewer studies formally theorized the relationship among formal sanctions, informal sanctions as mediating variables, and ISP CI as a dependent variable before examining, analyzing, and reporting the results [12, 31]. Prior IS research that formally tested the mediating effect of informal sanction variables used multiplicative formal sanctions; however, different informal sanction variables were tested [e.g., 12, 31, 40]. We believe that the mediating effect of theoretically related factors on the relationship of formal sanctions and ISP compliance behavior needs more attention in IS research as well as in practice. Because the two components of formal sanction may have directional effect on IS compliance [10, 30], it is appropriate to test the mediating variables on each components of formal sanction separately. Mediating influences can shed light on the mechanism of how an independent variable influences a dependent variable [45]. We contend that the inconsistent results of prior studies with regard to the relationship between formal sanctions and ISP compliance behavior may be due to the lack of understanding of the mechanism behind such relationship. Our study provides a new perspective to examine the inconsistent results of the role of formal sanctions in information security management programs.

Following the calls from D’Arcy and Herath [17] and Williams and Hawkins [27], this study investigates the moderating effects of several moderating variables on the relationship between PSS and employees’ ISP CI. We selected these moderating variables because they are variables from two different categories: informal sanctions (PDN and PRC) and capability (PSEC). As suggested by D’Arcy and Herath [17] and deterrability theory [29], these variables may affect the PSS-ISP CI relationship. This study also investigates the control effects of the same moderating variables because those variables are correlated with PSS and CI. We test how these informal sanction variables influence the strength of the relationship of PSS and ISP CI. By controlling the variables related to the ISP CI from different categories, this study tests the true effect of PSS on CI. Lastly, we investigate and discuss the mediating effect of the informal sanction variables.

## 3. Hypothesis development

## 3.1 Moderating variables

There are two streams of thought on why the results of GDT in empirical studies are mixed. One stream of thought posits that the inconsistent results may be due to the lack of investigating situational variables, which can affect the strength of a relationship, and therefore can serve as moderating

variables. Another stream of thought posits that the inconsistent results may be due to uncontrolled

variables [36, 46]. In this section, we develop the hypotheses based on the first stream of thought. In the

next section, we develop the hypotheses according to the second stream of thought.

Situational variables may include individual variables, such as moral belief and self-efficacy, and

contextual variables, such as employee position and work characteristics, that may conditionally affect

(moderate) the effect of formal sanctions on security compliance [17, 29]. D’Arcy and Herath [17]

provided sound arguments for why the relationship between individual variables like Self-Control,

Computer Self-Efficacy, and Moral Belief and sanctions and security behavior should be examined.

Deterrability theory [29, 35] also supports the proposal that moderating effects of situational factors

need to be investigated for the PSS-ISP CI relationship. Although theorists of criminology have argued

and empirical evidence has supported that “punishment threats are consequential only for a subgroup

of the general population” [35, p. 432], this theoretical advance has not been heeded properly by many

IS researchers. Jacobs [29] and Pogarsky [35] argued that deterrence and deterrability are two different

concepts, and the distinction between them is critical to understand criminality. Jacobs [29, p. 417]

defined deterrence as “the perceptual process by which would-be offenders calculate risks and rewards

prior to offending” and “deterrability refers to the offender’s capacity and/or willingness to perform this

calculation.” Factors, such as sanction severity and sanction certainty, from prior IS research are mainly

derived from the deterrence perspective that focuses on the calculation of risks and rewards; then, the

direct relationship between those factors and ISP CI was examined. However, according to the

deterrability theory, criminals can be divided into deterrable and undeterrable [29, 35]. Deterrability is

influenced by risk sensitivity as the precondition, which denotes “the situated concern for detection,

identification, and/or arrest” [29, p. 422]. Deterrability is described as situational, meaning that the

action of would-be offenders will be affected by many situational variables of the type of crime,

personal traits, and motives [29, 35]. Because of the individual differences of deterrability, the relationship of PSS and ISP CI may be different. It is necessary, then, to investigate situational factors that may influence the PSS-compliance intention relationship in IS research; otherwise, the conclusions may be unsound.

We can, thus, propose that computer self-efficacy may affect the relationship of PSS-ISP CI with the following rationale: deterrability is influenced by personal capability/willingness to assess risk [29]. Computer self-efficacy can be viewed as one of the personal capacity factors and, therefore, could influence whether a person is deterrable or undeterrable. For less deterrable individuals, a formal penalty may not have as much effect on their ISP compliance behavior in contrast to more deterrable individuals. Therefore, computer self-efficacy could influence the strength of the relationship of formal sanctions and compliance. Computer self-efficacy has been categorized into two groups: general computer self-efficacy, which measures “an individual’s overall confidence in his/her ability to use a computer” [17, p. 645], and task-specific computer self-efficacy, which measures “one’s confidence toward specific IS tasks or applications” [17, p. 645]. General computer self-efficacy was found to have a moderating effect on the sanction-to-IS misuse intention [10].

In this study, we investigate the effect on the PSS-to-ISP CI relationship of a task-specific computer self-efficacy: perceived self-efficacy to comply (PSEC) with the tasks outlined in the ISP. Some studies argued [17, 29] that it is reasonable to expect that sanctions will have less influence on committing a crime for users with higher computer self-efficacy because they feel that they can commit the crime without being caught. However, in the ISP compliance context, we argue that sanctions may have more influence on compliance for users with higher compliance self-efficacy because they may feel that they can easily perform the tasks outlined by the ISP. Therefore, we propose:

H<sub>1</sub>. Perceived Self-Efficacy to comply with ISP moderates the relationship between perceived sanction severity and ISP compliance intention.

$\mathsf { H } _ { 1 \mathsf { a } }$ . Perceived sanction severity has less influence on ISP compliance intention for employees with higher Perceived Self-Efficacy to comply with ISP.

$\mathsf { H } _ { 1 \mathsf { b } } .$ . Perceived sanction severity has more influence on ISP compliance intention for employees with lower Perceived Self-Efficacy to comply with ISP.

Moral belief has been theorized as one of the moderating variables on the sanction-to-crime relationship [17]. The deterrability theory [29, 35] supports this idea because moral beliefs can influence a person’s willingness to calculate the costs and benefits of committing a criminal/deviant behavior, therefore determining whether a person is deterrable or not. Moral belief has been found to have significant moderating effect on the sanction-to-IS misuse intention [10]. Perceived descriptive norm (PDN) is a conceptual variable that measures the extent to which one believes that others are performing the desired behavior [4, 47]. We argue that an employee’s PDN about complying with ISP has traits similar to moral beliefs. Moral belief measures one’s belief of what constitutes a social norm in a society [17]. PDN also measures one’s belief of what others in society may do. One may think that if most others take an action, then the action should be a social norm and therefore should be moral to do. We propose that as a moral belief, PDN may also moderate the relationship between sanctions and ISP compliance. When an employee feels that everyone else is complying with the ISP, the employee will refrain from violating the ISP, which makes the PSS irrelevant. Thus, we posit that an employee’s PDN about complying with ISP will moderate the sanction-to-ISP CI relationship:

H<sub>2</sub>. A perceived descriptive norm about others complying with ISP moderates the relationship between perceived sanction severity and ISP CI.

$\mathsf { H } _ { 2 \mathsf { a } } .$ . Perceived sanction severity has less influence on ISP CI for employees with a higher perceived descriptive norm about others complying with ISP.

$H _ { 2 \mathbf { b } } .$ . Perceived sanction severity has more influence on ISP CI for employees with a lower perceived descriptive norm about others complying with ISP.

#

From the deterrence perspective, the relationship between sanctions and criminal action must be linked through subjective perceptions of the costs and rewards of committing a criminal action [29, 48]. This study argues that perceived response cost (PRC) can be viewed as one of the factors evaluated by potential offenders when they assess the costs and gains of committing a crime. In the ISP compliance context, PRC refers to the extent of the expected cost in terms of time, effort, and convenience for complying with ISP. In the IS security context, several studies argued that if complying with the ISP does not cost much in terms of time, effort, and convenience, it will be easier for employees to comply. However, if compliance involves costs related to performing their primary tasks, employees may proceed with the tasks at hand and ignore the sanctions [8, 11, 49, 50]. It is reasonable to argue tasks at hand. They may sacrifice compliance for accomplishing those primary tasks [8, 50]. When the PRC of an employee is low, the employee may pay more attention to sanction severity and evaluate the cost of violating the ISP more rigorously without distractions from other cost elements, such as cost of holding off other tasks at hands. Therefore, we propose the third set of hypotheses as follows:

H<sub>3</sub>. Perceived response cost moderates the relationship between perceived sanction severity and ISP CI.

H<sub>3a</sub>. Perceived sanction severity has less influence on ISP CI for employees with a higher perceived response cost.

H . Perceived sanction severity has more influence on ISP CI for employees with a lower perceived response cost.

## 3.2 Control and mediating variables

Another stream of research about the inconsistent effect of sanctions on criminal or deviant behavior has been proposed and empirically tested in the criminology and sociology literature since the 1980s [36]. This line of thought proposes that the inconsistent results from the GDT studies are due to uncontrolled variables [36, 46]. Two primary means of controlling variables are used in empirical studies. One is control by experimental design and the other by using statistical control [51]. Statistical controls are variables that are measured in a study and included in the primary analyses [51]. Control variables are used to answer questions as to whether the relationship between two variables is real or due to an accidental connection with other associated variables (control variables) [52]. Control variables are used in studies to remove “nuisance” variance [53]. There are three different purposes in using control variables in research: (1) purification, to remove the measurement noise of the independent variable; (2) accounting for other meaningful variables, to investigate whether there are other variables that may account for the variance of the dependent variable; and (3) incremental prediction, to investigate the incremental improvement of the model prediction [43, 53, 54, 55, 56]. Because we theorize that PSEC, PRC, and PDN have theoretical connections with ISP compliance intention, the purpose of using control variables in this study is to find other meaningful variables that may account for employees’ ISP compliance intentions.

GDT research in criminology indicates that extralegal (informal) sanctions influence the effectiveness of legal (formal) sanctions [35]. The extralegal sanctions include social disapproval and self-disapproval [35]. Based on GDT research in criminology indicating that extralegal sanctions diminish the effect of formal legal sanctions [28, 44], GDT research in IS security literature introduced the informal sanction to account for meaningful variables other than formal sanctions for ISP compliance intention [e.g., 4, 10, 12, 13]. Findings began to indicate that variables from other aspects of personal and organizational characteristics may have better explanatory power to account for employees’ ISP CI than GDT variables [e.g., 4, 5, 8, 10, 11, 12, 13, 20, 31]. However, few studies in IS research reported on how the effect of formal sanctions has changed. In this study, we use the procedure recommended by Spector and Brannick [43] to test how informal sanctions influence the strength of the PSS and ISP CI relationship. Following the advances of GDT research in criminology and IS, we incorporate informal

sanction factors in our research model and used the staged test process to evaluate how the informal sanctions change the PSS-to-ISP CI relationship. Based on prior research [12, 17] and the deterrability theory we argue that PRC, PDN, and PSEC may influence the relationship of PSS-ISP compliance behavior; therefore, theoretically they are related to PSS and ISP compliance behavior and, thus, will propose the fourth set of hypotheses:

$\mathsf { H } _ { 4 } .$ Perceived self-efficacy will have a significantly positive effect on ISP CI.

$\mathsf { H } _ { 4 \mathsf { a } } .$ . Perceived self-efficacy will reduce the effect of perceived sanction severity on ISP CI.

$\mathsf { H } _ { 5 } .$ Perceived descriptive norm will have a significantly positive effect on ISP CI.

${ \sf H } _ { 5 a } .$ . Perceived descriptive norm will reduce the effect of perceived sanction severity on ISP CI.

H<sub>6</sub>. Perceived response cost will have a significantly negative effect on ISP CI.

$\mathsf { H } _ { 6 \mathsf { a } } .$ . Perceived response cost will reduce the effect of perceived sanction severity on ISP CI.

We believe that reporting the test results for models with and without control variables is the first step in investigating the true relationship between the variables of interest. If a theoretical relationship is plausible between the independent variable and the control variable, we must test the mediating effect of the control variable to show if the original effect of the independent variable is mediated by the mediating variable. This extra step was not taken in most contemporary IS research as shown in Table 1. One study [12] theorized the relationship between formal sanctions and moral belief based on Gibb’s [57] concept of “normative validation.” With the same base, we propose that PSS may influence PDN. Evidence in GDT research from criminology and IS shows that informal sanctions may diminish the effect of formal sanctions. Therefore, it is reasonable to believe that the PSS influence on ISP CI may be mediated by PDN. We also argue that PSS may influence PSEC and PRC. Higher PSS may make individuals feel that the required actions are complex and may require a high level of skills that they may lack. Furthermore, individuals perceive a higher cost in dealing with those actions they

perceive as complex. We contend that PSEC and PRC may have relationships with PSS. On the other hand, GDT and PMT research has provided evidence that support the direct and strong influence of PSEC and PRC on ISP CI and a diminishing effect of formal sanctions on ISP CI. Therefore, we propose: H<sub>7</sub>. Perceived self-efficacy will mediate the effect of perceived sanction severity on ISP CI H<sub>9</sub>. Perceived response cost will mediate the effect of perceived sanction severity on ISP CI. The research model of this study is depicted in Fig. 1.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1 Moderating effect of informal sanctions

There are theoretical bases for the moderating effect of contextual and individual factors on the relationship between formal sanctions and criminal/deviant actions based on the distinction between deterrence and deterrability [29, 35]. However, the moderating effect of the three variables used in this study, PSEC, a personal capability factor, and PRC and PDN, the informal sanction factors, were not supported in this study. One reason for the non-significant moderating effect may be that they are not applicable in the ISP compliance context, at least in the studied sample. This issue will be further addressed in the discussion on informal sanctions and their impact on ISP compliance behavior.

Another reason for the non-significant moderating effect may be that formal sanctions may not have a true direct impact in the ISP compliance context. The GDT theory was developed in the context of criminality of a higher consequence such as incarceration, loss of assets, job, etc. In most circumstances, however, violation of security policy, does not rise to the level that was originally stated in the GDT theory development. Therefore, formal sanctions may not be as serious concern to employees in the context of ISP compliance as it is in a criminal context. The results of this study only support, in this ISP compliance context, the direct effect of formal sanctions on ISP CI may be spurious. Because there is no actual direct PSS-to-ISP CI relationship, the moderating variables have nothing to moderate on in the studied context. Future study may need to include the measurements of the types of violation and formal sanction specified in ISP. With different violations, people may feel differently about sanction severity [37]. For example, employees may feel that the sanction severity may be higher if being caught of leaking confidential data as compared to choosing weak password. In other words, formal sanction severity may vary depending on the violation types. This indicates that ISP compliance research is still in its early stage, lacking its own comprehensive framework that is specifically suitable for the context of

information security management in organizations. We suggest that more efforts are needed in refining the theoretical foundation for information security management.

The third reason may be related to the characteristics of the participant’s environment. The data were collected from a higher education institution, a public organization, which has characteristics different from a private corporation. As an example, employees of public organizations are less is not a significant factor in influencing ISP compliance in this study. Employees of public organizations are more motivated by intrinsic factors such as responsibility and recognition [e.g., 70, 71] and working in a stable environment [69]. It would be premature to reject the moderating effect based on the results of this study. More studies using data from a broader range of organizations are needed to understand the moderating role of the variables studied in this research.

We also propose that, in addition to potential moderating effects, the unexplored mediating effect and un-included appropriate control variables in prior IS research may also be the reason the results are inconsistent. This proposal is supported by the test results of the control and mediating variables in this study and will be discussed in the next section.

6.2 Effect of other factors on the relationship of PSS and ISP CI

The IS security literature has argued that GDT can apply to ISP compliance [e.g., 10, 11]. The test of the baseline model does show that there is a significant impact of PSS on employees’ ISP CI. However, the other results from this study paint a different picture about the true effect of PSS on ISP CI. A growing number of studies in the ISP compliance literature [e.g., 5] argue, and empirical evidence supports, [5, 12, 13, 17] that informal sanctions, including social influence, personal beliefs, and capabilities, may have influence equal to that of formal sanctions on ISP CI. This study supports the argument and goes beyond to suggest that informal sanctions and personal capability may be enough or more important to ensure employees’ compliance with their organization’s ISP.

#

There are two theoretical traditions in criminology [72]. One emphasizes the effect of enduring

individual differences. The other, based on the rational choice theory, focuses on the effect of proximate

and situational factors such as opportunity, conditions, and the benefits and costs of offending. GDT and

PMT have their roots in the second tradition. However, criminological theorists have proposed that, to

better understand crime behavior, it is necessary to examine the constructs from both criminological

traditions [72, 73]. Most IS research follows the rational choice perspective and focuses on investigating

the effects of formal and informal sanctions and the cost and reward of crime/deviant behavior. As such,

this study fits well with the proposed dictum of the crime theory. Pogarsky [35] theorized that from the

enduring individual differences perspective there are three forms of responses to legal sanction threats:

acute conformist, deterrable, and incorrigible. Acute conformist and incorrigible are the two distinct

forms of insensitivity to legal sanctions. Acute conformist describes those people “for whom extralegal

influences ensure compliance with the law” [35, p. 433]. The incorrigibles are those for whom legal

sanctions are inconsequential [35]. The deterrables are those whose responsiveness to legal sanctions is

between the two extreme ends. Researchers [35, 74] emphasize that the offending propensity

determines if a person may become a potential offender or not. Building on prior research, Pogarsky

argued that extralegal influences cause the acute conformists to comply with the law, whereas the

incorrigibles do not comply with the law no matter the legal sanctions. Extralegal influences that make

acute conformists comply with the law include social disapproval, such as condemnation of peers, and

self-disapproval, such as moral beliefs. Some IS research has suggested that violating ISP may be

different from street crime [12]. We argue that in the employees’ ISP compliance context, it is unlikely

that employees are incorrigible because in a business environment it is hard to assume that many

employees would deliberately violate the policies. It is more likely that employees are more often acute

conformists when facing the question of complying or violating the ISP because they want to keep their

jobs. This may be more applicable in this study’s environment, which is a public university. Studies show that employees of public organizations are more motivated by responsibility, recognition, and a desire for a stable work environment [70, 71]; therefore, they tend to take the policies more seriously, are more often acute conformists, and therefore, more responsive to sanctions. Therefore, informal sanctions, in the form of extralegal influence, are enough to affect their compliance intentions in the ISP compliance context. The results from this and prior studies [e.g., 13, 39, 75] support this proposal. This may also help explain why the selected factors do not moderate the PSS-ISP CI relationship in the studied context because the employees in this situation act more like acute conformists than incorrigibles. Based on the offending propensity theory, one possible and promising moderating variable on the PSS-to-ISP CI relationship from the enduring individual differences perspective is individual deterrability. Future studies could investigate this with data gathered from a broader range of organizations.

We examined the PSEC effect from a different perspective other than PMT and GDT. In his criminology research, Pogarsky [35] and others [e.g., 74] argued that the factors from two dimensions, which are social-disapproval (e.g., PDN) and self-disapproval (e.g., moral belief), will ensure acute conformists comply with the law. However, the effect of PSEC on the relationships of PSS-to-ISP CI and PRC-to-ISP CI in this study may indicate that a third dimension, which is personal capability, may also have strong influence on complying with the law. Therefore, based on the findings from this study we propose that in a context where the subjects can be characterized more as acute conformists on the continuum of criminal proclivity [76], personal capability to comply with the law/regulation will influence the subjects’ intention to comply.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first studies to test and discuss how control variables change the PSS-ISP CI relationship by looking at the coefficient changes of PSS both in the baseline and full models. The results indicate significant changes of the PSS-ISP CI relationship in the two models. These results indicate that the significant direct PSS-to-CI relationship may be spurious in

#

the employees’ ISP compliance context. PDN is an informal sanction factor and PSEC with the ISP is a factor in the personal capability category. The effect of PDN on the PSS-to-ISP CI relationship supports the proposition that informal sanctions play a more important role than formal sanctions in this context. The effect of PSEC on the PSS-to-ISP CI relationship indicates that personal capability greatly influences employees’ compliance intentions. There is empirical evidence that suggest that PRC has a strong effect on ISP CI [e.g., 8, 11]. However, although PRC shows a significant direct effect on the perceived ISP CI in this study, after controlling for the effects of PSEC and PDN, that effect disappears (p = 0.19). These results suggest that the impact of PRC on ISP CI may also be spurious. The potential spurious relationship between PSS and ISP CI and between PRC and ISP CI may have implications on what information security management should focus on in practice, which is discussed in the implication for practice section below.

The ensuing question is, do formal sanctions matter in this context? Although some IS research shows that in certain situations formal sanctions may have no impact on organizational security efforts [10, 30, 39], the indirect effect of PSS on ISP CI indicates that formal sanctions may have some role in this context. Formal sanctions may not directly affect ISP CI; however, these could affect ISP CI through other mechanisms as reflected by the mediating effects of PDN and PSEC on the PSS-ISP CI relationship. We theorized that PSS may affect employees’ perception of how others in their organization comply with the ISP, which is captured by PDN since higher PSS may suggest to employees that violating the ISP is serious and other employees will follow it. By the same token, we also argued that PSS may affect employees’ perception of their ability to comply with the ISP. The mediating effects of PDN and PSEC suggest that PSS does have an impact on ISP CI, however, indirectly. Our findings support previous research [12, 13, 20] indicating that formal sanctions still matter in IS management, but need to be viewed from different perspectives.

#

In summary, our findings suggest that an employee’s choice of complying or not complying with the ISP is based mainly not on cost or formal penalty, but on informal sanctions and personal capability. The factors that may hinder employees’ compliance with their organizational ISP come from their capability to comply, as reflected by the significant impact of PSEC. Furthermore, the organization’s culture toward ISP compliance plays an important role in deterring employees’ intentions to violate the staged test results of the effect of PSS on ISP CI as well as the results of the mediating effects of PDN and PSEC support the proposition that the threat of formal sanction leads to higher ISP CI by employees [12, 13]. However, that effect is indirect through PSEC and PDN in the ISP CI context. These results suggest that formal sanctions matter in the studied context, but perceived collective organizational attitude toward ISP compliance, as reflected by PDN, and employees’ capability, as reflected by PSEC, plays a more important role.

Collectively these findings impact how an organization should design and enforce their policy as discussed in the implications for practice below.

## 7. Implications and limitations

## 7.1 Implication for research

This study has several implications for IS research. First, although the study findings did not support any moderating effect of the three proposed moderating variables on the PSS-to-ISP CI relationship, the results indicate a strong direct effect of those variables on ISP CI itself. The applicability of GDT in ISP compliance research needs to be further examined. GDT research in criminology [29] and IS [17] has theorized that the deterrence impact of formal sanctions is situational. For example, people’s deterrability may influence the strength of the direct relationship of PSS and ISP CI. Therefore, by applying the advancements of GDT in the ISP compliance context, other moderating variables may have an important role in the PSS-ISP CI relationship. However, the results from this study suggest that the

search for the true effect of formal sanctions and exact moderating factors is still just beginning in ISP compliance research.

Most prior IS research focuses on factors from one criminology tradition that is based on the rational choice theory. Because the consequences of the behaviors in the studied context, such as no jail rewards and costs, may be not applicable as moderating variables in the studied context. Individual deterrability factors, such as sensibility to risk and social responsiveness, from the second tradition of criminology may be a more reasonable moderating variable in this context, which needs to be tested in future studies.

Second, from the perspective of deterrability and enduring individual differences, we propose that because employees in the ISP compliance situation will act more like acute conformists, informal sanctions are sufficient to ensure that they comply with the law/regulation.

Third, the strong effect of PSEC on ISP CI suggests that other than informal sanction factors, such as social-disapproval and self-disapproval, and formal sanctions, employees’ capability to perform the tasks outlined in the ISP plays a critical role in influencing their compliance intentions in the studied context. Therefore, we propose that personal capability development should be the third dimension in a complete ISP compliance campaign. Because a security education, training, and awareness (SETA) program can help develop personal capability, findings of this study support the claim that a SETA program is an important part of information security management [8, 10, 77, 78]. Future studies should focus on the factors from all three dimensions: informal sanctions, formal sanctions, and enduring individual differences such as PSEC, sensibility to social and personal pressure, etc.

Fourth, from our review of current IS research, we found that although many studies have incorporated the theory advances in GDT research, the analysis procedures were not adequate for the stated purposes. Staged model tests, which were employed in very few reviewed IS studies [e.g., 12],

#

should be employed in more research to compare the strength change of the effect of formal sanctions. By reporting the impact of an independent variable in both baseline and full models, a better understanding about the impact of the independent variable can be revealed.

Furthermore, because the role of formal sanctions in the ISP compliance context is still unclear, studied in different environments. Although the results of the PSS effect on ISP CI with and without the control variables in this study suggest that the direct PSS-to-CI may be spurious, the mediating effect tests of this study indicate that PSS may have an indirect effect on ISP CI through other factors, such as PSEC and PDN. Therefore, formal sanctions may still matter, however indirectly, in the ISP compliance context as suggested by prior research [12, 13, 20]. The inconsistent conclusions from studies that questioned [e.g., 77, 78, 79] or supported [e.g., 12, 13, 20] the role of formal sanctions in IS research suggest that the picture is still blurry. The results of direct and indirect effects of PSS on ISP CI from this study support the assertion that formal sanctions do matter, but maybe through other mechanisms.

Finally, although studies have begun to incorporate the advances in criminology in the IS research, the focus remains on the rational choice perspective with the attending formal and informal sanctions. For example, studies have investigated the role of informal sanctions and emphasized the importance of including factors from both PMT and GDT [13, 15], which have their roots in the rational choice perspective. We propose that more studies are needed in the IS context that incorporate central constructs from the enduring individual differences perspective. Those constructs may include deterrability and other enduring personal traits such as sensitivity to social pressure and emotional stability [72].

## 7.2 Implications for practice

Prior IS literature suggests that management should provide employees with an environment to learn organizational values and help them develop personal capability to comply with the ISP in order to increase ISP compliance [e.g., 5, 12, 20]. The findings of this study support that proposition. One of the reasons for introducing the mediating factors was to investigate where interventions should be placed to change the outcome of interest [80]. The results of this study suggest that although penalties may still matter, an information security management program should focus on informal sanctions and personal capability development. Therefore, when faced with the task of increasing ISP compliance in an organization, management should focus less on sanctions or cost-related factors, and more on building compliance culture and providing the means for employees to comply with the organization’s ISP.

Compliance culture could be built through various approaches such as showing the commitment of management through periodic communications on ISP matters; formal and informal rewards and recognition to those who comply, which helps to improve PDN; employee education via ISP seminars, training, and workshops etc. The significant findings of the direct effect of PSEC on ISP compliance in this study support a SETA program as critical to promote ISP compliance. Prior studies [e.g., 8, 10, 20, 77] show that training improves employees’ capability to perform tasks specified in the ISP. This study suggests that personal capability plays an important role in complying with ISP; therefore, efforts should be channeled toward training when organizations are faced with the task of improving compliance.

The significant direct effect of PDN reminds corporate security managers that building an ISP compliance culture may play a more important role in securing their organization’s information systems than trying to establish punishing rules. However, formal sanctions are still needed because of their indirect effect through PDN and PSEC in ISP compliance. This study’s findings further suggest that a corporate’s SETA program can be an effective way to improve employees’ ISP compliance intention and help secure the organization’s information systems. A SETA program can help create an organizational culture that values compliance with ISP, thereby reducing violations [8].

7.3 Limitations and future studies

The first limitation of this study is that the data were collected from one organization in a single industry (higher education). The characteristics of employees in the studied organization may not be typical; therefore, the generalizability of the results is questionable. More studies are needed that include data from different organizations and across industries to validate or refute the findings.

Secondly, this study has results different from prior studies in terms of the important relationship between PSS and CI. The data in this study were collected by surveying the employees’ CI with an ISP as in other studies [e.g., 4, 8, 11]; whereas other studies used the scenario-based method to collect employees’ intention to comply with/violate specific guidelines [e.g., 9, 10, 21]. The results from this study may not directly compare with the results from the scenario-based studies. More studies are needed to compare the model’s explanatory power of these two methods. One possible design to make the results comparable is as follows. In one study similar to this research, an ISP that includes common IT misuses/violations in a corporate context is presented to the research participants before they answer other questions so that they are aware of what they need to comply with; then in another study, the scenarios based on the same misuses/violations are studied.

The third limitation is the single source for all variables in the model, which can introduce common method bias. Although formal tests do not reveal the evidence of common method bias in the data, other experimental designs or controls may be needed to completely remove the bias. One possible approach may be through collecting the information about employees’ actual compliance behavior instead of their compliance intentions so that the value of the dependent variable is more objective.

The fourth limitation is that this study did not collect data about enduring individual differences, such as self-control, emotional stability, and sensitivity to sanctions. Future studies could measure these constructs to see if they moderate the formal sanction-ISP CI relationship.

Finally, this study does not consider culture. Research has shown that there are behavioral differences between people from collectivist and individualistic cultures [e.g., 81]. This study is based on data collected from the United States, which is considered an individualistic culture. More studies are needed not only in other industries of individualistic countries, but also in different industries of collectivist countries.

## Appendix A: Survey Instrument

## Employees’ ISP Compliance Intention:

1. I intended to comply with the information security policies of my organization in the last one year

2. I intended to protect information and technology resources according to the information security policies of my organization in the last one year

3. I intended to carry out my responsibilities prescribed in the information security policies of my organization when I used information and technology in the last one year

## Perceived Sanction Severity:

1. The organization disciplines employees who break information security policies

2. My organization terminates employees who repeatedly break information security policies

3. If I were caught violating organization information security policies, I would be severely punished

## Perceived Descriptive Norm:

1. I believe that other employees comply with the information security policies of my organization

2. It is likely that the majority of other employees comply with the information security policies of my organization to help protect organization's information systems

## Perceived Self-Efficacy to Compliance:

1. I have the necessary skills to comply with the information security policies of my organization

2. I have the necessary knowledge to comply with the information security policies of my organization

3. I can comply with the information security policies of my organization by myself

4. It is easy to comply with the information security policies of my organization

## Perceived Response Cost:

1. Adopting security technologies specified in the information security policies poses hindrance

2. Adopting security practices specified in the information security policies poses hindrance

3. Complying with the information security policies is time consuming for me

4. Complying with the information security policies is costly for me
