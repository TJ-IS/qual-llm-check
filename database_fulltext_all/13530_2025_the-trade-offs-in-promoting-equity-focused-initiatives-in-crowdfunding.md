# The Trade-Offs in Promoting Equity-Focused Initiatives in Crowdfunding

Lauren Rhue<sup>1</sup>

Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, USA, lrhue@umd.edu

## Abstract

Organizations interested in supporting matters of diversity and equity need guidance on whether to be transparent about their equity-focused initiatives and appeal to users’ sense of social justice. This paper explores how information transparency affects backer responses to equity-related initiatives. The educational crowdfunding platform DonorsChoose launched an “equity-focused” initiative to highlight the platform’s commitment to equity in education and encourage donations towards that cause. Using an observational analysis and an experimental study, I found evidence that information transparency, i.e., explicitly promoting equity-focused projects, dampens overall donations because any change in donations associated with the equity-focused initiatives is insufficient to offset the decrease in other donations. However, I found evidence that distant donors are more responsive to equity-focused recommendations, consistent with the hypothesis that a holistic mindset moderates the effectiveness of promoting equity-focused initiatives. This study provides insight into the tradeoffs associated with information transparency for equity-related initiatives and the complicated relationship between organizations and equity-related initiatives.

Keywords: Digital Platforms, Equity, Diversity, Inclusion, Field Experiment, Econometrics, Crowdfunding, Information Transparency

## 1 Introduction

Digital platforms have a complicated relationship with matters of diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI). On the one hand, digital platforms are a microcosm of society and thus reflect the same patterns of discrimination as the offline world (Cui et al., 2019; Edelman et al., 2017; Rhue, 2018; Rhue & Clark, 2022; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018, 2019). On the other hand, digital platforms can benefit traditionally marginalized communities by providing alternative options to more discriminatory offline options, such as crowdfunding providing more access to capital for entrepreneurs than venture capital funding (Mollick & Robb, 2016) or the gig economy absorbing unemployment shocks (Huang et al., 2020). Organizations encouraging or supporting DEI measures organizations need guidance on whether to be transparent and appeal directly to users’ sense of social justice. To explore the effect of explicitly highlighting DEI on a prosocial digital platform, this paper uses equity theory, which defines equity as resource allocation according to need (Cook & Hegtvedt, 1983).

My research explores this question through a preliminary observational analysis and an experimental study on the educational crowdfunding platform DonorsChoose. DonorsChoose enables backers to support classroom projects from US public schools. In the preliminary analysis, I examined how launching a new project descriptor related to equity affects donations. The equity-focused descriptor identifies projects from schools with high poverty rates in traditionally marginalized communities. The descriptor is not related to teaching social justice but instead to promoting equity in the US education system through supporting needs in those communities. Backers can filter projects based on this equity-focused descriptor, thereby reducing search costs to support schools in traditionally marginalized neighborhoods. While I expected that contributions to projects at equity-focused schools would increase after the attribute launch, the relative support for projects from equity-focused schools actually declined. In the experimental study, more than 200,000 previous donors received a marketing email with a link to a set of projects. Backers were randomly assigned to receive emails with either (1) a link to the default set of projects or (2) a link to only equity-focused projects. Backers were less likely to contribute to any project after receiving the equityfocused project set. One moderating factor was a holistic mindset, a cognitive perspective that views society as interconnected and complex (Zhou et al., 2021). A holistic mindset was reflected in the choice of backers who previously contributed to projects from distant schools, and those backers were more likely to respond to the equity-focused project descriptor.

This study makes several contributions. I highlight the complicated relationship between digital platforms and equity, both on and off the platform, providing nuance into the role of organizations in promoting equity. The heterogeneous nature of backer motivations lies at the center of platforms’ ability to fulfill the promise of increasing equity and reducing discrimination on the platform itself. The findings imply that platforms experience trade-offs in their use of equity-focused appeals. Information transparency—explicitly mentioning the equity needs of the community—may attract some backers and alienate others, and the net effect may lower overall contributions. My work provides evidence of a trade-off due to information transparency in equity-related issues. To mitigate this dilemma, this research suggests that platforms should either segment backers for targeting based on their prior behavior or that platforms should broaden their suggestions. This work further underscores the nuances of transparency for organizations seeking to further equity-related causes.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. The Theoretical Background section provides the theoretical background on peer-to-peer platforms and equity. The Hypotheses section motivates the hypotheses on equityrelated transparency and donor motivations. The Empirical Analysis section describes the observational and experimental studies. The Robustness Checks section examines the robustness of the results to alternative explanations, and the Discussion section concludes the paper.

## 2 Theoretical Background

This section introduces digital platforms, their inherent information asymmetry, and backer motivations. Next, I summarize the relevant literature on promoting equity on digital platforms and the role of digital platforms in promoting offline equity. Lastly, I describe the educational crowdfunding platform DonorsChoose as the context for this study.

## 2.1 Digital Platforms

Peer-to-peer digital platforms act as intermediaries for users who engage in transactions such as buying and selling (Hagiu & Wright, 2015). Peer-to-peer platforms exhibit variety in their types of transactions and their degree of specialization. Some peer-to-peer digital platforms are organized around collaborative consumption, e.g., the sharing economy, where the platform connects owners with excess capacity for their goods with short-term renters. Other platforms such as Lending Club or Prosper facilitate peer-to-peer borrowing by connecting individual investors with potential borrowers.

Participants engage and transact on digital platforms for a variety of reasons. First, participants often expect to benefit financially from the transaction and must vet the other participants and/or services. A defining concern across peer-to-peer platforms is information asymmetry, or the difficulty in assessing a user’s true motives (Radoynovska & King, 2019; Sundararajan, 2016; Wang et al., 2021a). Product and service descriptions can influence participants’ transaction choices and responses (Althoff et al., 2014; Gorbatai & Nelson, 2015; Mollick, 2014). Second, participants on platforms exhibit geographic preferences in their transactions, despite the seeming irrelevance of geography on digital platforms (Burtch et al., 2014; Grimson et al., 2020; Lin & Viswanathan, 2016). Backers may find that geographic proximity facilitates the vetting of products/services (Burtch et al., 2014; Grimson et al., 2020; Lin & Viswanathan, 2016) or may prefer to support others in their local community through their contributions (Agrawal et al., 2015). In addition, social relationships and interpersonal factors, such as the desire to build community, also shape the degree to which participants engage on digital platforms (Agrawal et al., 2015; Gleasure & Feller, 2016b). Lastly, backers may transact on digital platforms for many nonfinancial, reasons including purely altruistic or warm-glow motivations (Sutanto et al., 2021).

Crowdfunding is a specific type of digital platform that enables “individuals and groups … to fund their ventures by drawing on relatively small contributions from a relatively large number of individuals using the internet” (Mollick, 2014). The purpose of crowdfunding platforms ranges from broad causes, such as GoFundMe, to creative projects, such as Kickstarter, to fundraising for more specific causes like education, e.g., DonorsChoose. Even on rewards-based crowdfunding platforms like Kickstarter, backers may choose their specific projects for nonfinancial reasons such as community-building or social connections. These different platforms often connect backers through a shared set of values (Gleasure & Feller, 2016a).

Backers on donation-based crowdfunding platforms face similar choices and numerous nonmonetary factors affect potential donors’ choices about which charitable crowdfunding projects to support. Project characteristics such as the number of updates and target goal (Mollick, 2014), phrases and sentiment of the campaign descriptions (Mitra & Gilbert, 2014; Yuan et al., 2016; Althoff et al., 2014), and facial expressions and appearance in associated images (Kim & Park, 2017; Duarte et al., 2012; Rhue & Robert, 2018) have all been shown to influence success on charitable crowdfunding platforms. Because backers exhibit prosocial behavior through prior contributions, backers on donation-based crowdfunding platforms are expected to be more inclined to support equity-based initiatives than backers in other contexts.

## 2.2 Digital Platforms and Equity

Digital platforms such as Airbnb and Kickstarter are increasingly concerned with promoting equality and inclusion on their platforms (Cui et al., 2019; Murphy, 2016; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018). In addition to intraplatform dynamics, digital platforms may produce positive externalities and benefit marginalized communities in the offline world (Kane, 2018). This section discusses platforms’ efforts to ensure inclusion within their own ecosystem and how platforms can benefit traditionally marginalized people in the offline world.

## 2.2.1 Anti-Discrimination on Digital Platforms

Extensive studies have documented inequity and bias on digital platforms. Success outcomes, such as the amount of money raised and the probability of attracting backers in crowdfunding, are often lower for participants from traditionally marginalized groups (Cui et al., 2019; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018). For example, studies have shown that the skin color of the hand holding a product led to a 20% difference in the final price on the online auction platform eBay (Ayres et al., 2015) and also produced lower offers in the context of online classified ads (Doleac & Stein, 2013). Similarly, people from traditionally marginalized groups experience lower acceptance rates for offers on home-sharing platforms (Edelman et al., 2017), higher interest rates on peer-to-peer platforms (Pope & Sydnor, 2011), and higher wait times and more cancelations on ride-sharing platforms (Mejia & Parker, 2021).

In response to evidence of bias, digital platforms have implemented techniques to reduce and/or eliminate discrimination on their platforms. One technique is to increase automated decisions to reduce the scope of human bias (Edelman et al., 2017; Mayya et al., 2021; Murphy, 2016). Another technique is to reduce the information transparency related to the user’s identity, such as minimizing user pictures to deemphasize personal attributes (Murphy, 2016; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018). However, decreased information transparency is not necessarily successful at eliminating bias because discrimination may be postponed until later in the transaction process (Mejia & Parker, 2021). Plus, backers who seek to actively support traditionally marginalized communities may prefer heightened information transparency related to social identity in order to support people from those communities (Johnson et al., 2019; Rhue, 2018). Backers who support traditionally marginalized communities may belong to those communities or may have a more holistic, globally aware mindset (Winterich et al., 2009; Zhao & Zhou, 2022; Zhou et al., 2021). Thus, digital platforms may provide an avenue for interested backers to support marginalized communities (Greenberg & Mollick, 2017; Kane, 2018).

## 2.2.2 Equity Spillovers from Digital Platforms

This paper examines digital platforms through the lens of equity theory. Equity theory distinguishes between equality, i.e., equal resource allocation across all recipients, and equity, i.e., resource allocation according to need (Cook & Hegtvedt, 1983). Equity theory states that equity-focused allocations are necessary to progress towards “perfect social justice” to compensate for historical discrimination (Walster & Walster, 1975). Equality merely prevents disparities and resource gaps from widening but does not provide any restoration for systematically unequal power structures, such as the enforced segregation through racial covenants across the United States (Rothstein, 2017).

Even if discrimination occurs on their platforms, digital platforms may seek to increase equity in the offline world. Digital platforms may also be geared towards providing services and innovation in undeveloped or developing communities, depending on their design and implementation (Bonina et al., 2021). Digital platforms and their related ecosystems can facilitate coordination among multiple entities to address societal issues such as the alleviation of poverty (Jha et al., 2016).

Digital platforms may encourage participants to view the platform as a means to offset historical injustices and as a vehicle for equity. Some digital platforms have highlighted their role in distributing resources and addressing injustices in the offline world. For instance, Lyft and Uber both highlight how their service provides rides for communities that have traditionally been underserved by the taxi industry.<sup>1</sup> Indiegogo created a dedicated section of projects to actively highlight Black entrepreneurs and creators<sup>2</sup> and encourages backers to support those projects. Prosocial digital platforms may extend further, encouraging prosocial behavior through financial incentives (e.g., positive returns from microlending to small businesses in impoverished areas or rewards from project creators; Burtch et al., 2014; Hong et al., 2018). There is evidence that some backers similarly view crowdfunding as a mechanism to support marginalized communities. Backers wanting to boost gender representation in STEM may strategically use crowdfunding as a means to support female creators with STEM-related projects (Greenberg & Mollick, 2017).

Even in the absence of strategic backers who support traditionally marginalized communities, digital platforms can benefit those communities through available services. For instance, crowdfunding can democratize access to capital by lowering the barriers to fundraising (Cumming et al., 2021; Mollick & Robb, 2016). For instance, African American entrepreneurs receive only 1% of fundraising from traditional, offline venture capital, yet their success rates on crowdfunding platforms are significantly higher, between 8%-50%. However, African American entrepreneurs are still significantly less likely to be successful than other entrepreneurs on crowdfunding platforms (Rhue & Clark, 2022; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018, 2019). Easier access allows traditionally marginalized communities to better accumulate resources, even if the presence of discrimination remains on the platform itself. In another instance, gig economy platforms can potentially benefit traditionally marginalized communities by absorbing offline unemployment shocks and offering a novel alternative to lengthier spells of unemployment (Huang et al., 2020). Unemployment in the US is subject to systemic racial, ethnic, and gender disparities (Aaronson et al., 2021); therefore, alternative work options to dampen unemployment will particularly benefit those from traditionally marginalized communities. Indeed, gig economy platform workers are disproportionately Black and Latinx (Gelles-Watnick & Anderson, 2021), reflecting that reality.

One challenge for digital platforms that support equityrelated initiatives is that not all backers will inherently gravitate towards projects that improve equity. The aggregate decisions of individual backers can produce racial and gender disparities in outcomes on digital platforms (Greenberg & Mollick, 2017; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018), translating to a lower impact for traditionally marginalized communities and contradicting platforms’ desire to promote offline equity. Platforms can overcome this challenge by directing user resources towards particularly disadvantaged communities.

## 2.3 Context

This paper examines how digital platforms can attempt to steer backer choices towards equity in the context of donation-based educational crowdfunding. In the absence of national policies to address systemic inequalities, various organizations have emerged to redirect resources towards traditionally marginalized communities. One prominent donation-based educational crowdfunding platform is DonorsChoose, which was started in 2000 and enables US public school teachers to list classroom projects on the DonorsChoose platform and potential donors to decide which projects to support. DonorsChoose seeks to increase educational equity through donor contributions. Educational crowdfunding platforms offer an alternative route for public school teachers to acquire supplies and experiences for their students. Research has shown that a single crowdfunded project can reduce the achievement gap between lowincome and high-income students (Keppler et al., 2022).

Since donor preferences may not reflect the most pressing community needs, the collective impact of donations may yield suboptimal outcomes. For example, math and reading projects, arguably the cornerstones of education, are less likely to receive sufficient funding, as compared to other project types (Wolff & Carlson, 2021). Thus, donor preferences may conflict with socially optimal preferences and with platforms’ equity-related goals. To address this conundrum, platforms may seek to actively encourage backers towards equity-related projects. In educational crowdfunding, this translates, for example, to platforms recommending that donors contribute to schools in lowincome neighborhoods.

## 3 Hypotheses

## 3.1 Equity-Related Initiatives

Digital platforms may enact equity-focused initiatives that benefit marginalized communities to boost both inclusion on the platform and equity off the platform. For instance, digital platforms may explicitly highlight and promote equity-related projects. This equity-focused appeal is risky because it relies on affirming the presence of historical injustices. This color-conscious framework raises awareness about historical injustices and provides a useful language to describe structural inequalities (Ogbonnaya-Ogburu et al., 2020). However, this equityfocused lens is uncommon, particularly in the technology sector, because much of the technology sector prioritizes universalist and “color-blind” tendencies rather than employing language that engages with historical and structural inequalities (Ogbonnaya-Ogburu et al., 2020).

This reticence to engage with historical injustices is also reflected in the known “principle-implementation gap” (Bobo & Kluegel, 1993; Rabinowitz et al., 2009). Americans often support policies to promote social justice in principle but object to any policy implementation that explicitly targets specific populations (Bobo & Kluegel, 1993; Rabinowitz et al., 2009). Thus, organizations that enact information transparency around their equity goals (i.e., explicitly highlighting equity-focused efforts) may risk unintentionally dampening engagement and failing to increase equitable outcomes on platforms.

Participants on digital platforms have heterogeneous preferences. Those who are uninterested in matters of structural injustice may not respond to explicit calls for equity-focused action. Backers who are motivated by social relationships or an interest in the local community may not engage with equity-focused appeals. However, some backers will be receptive to calls for equityfocused actions. The overall effectiveness of transparent equity-focused initiatives, such as highlighting or recommending equity-focused projects, depends on the relative population of interested and uninterested backers. Given the prevalence of socially and geographically motivated backers, the overall effect of equity-focused initiatives is expected to lower overall contributions. Thus, I hypothesize:

H1a: Promoting equity-focused initiatives will increase contributions to equity-related recipients.

H1b: Promoting equity-focused initiatives will decrease contributions to other recipients.

H1c: Promoting equity-focused initiatives will decrease the probability of a contribution overall.

## 3.2 Holistic Mindset

Backers with nonfinancial motivations contribute due to some degree of altruism; however, some backers contribute due to pure altruism and other bakers contribute due to warm-glow altruism (Andreoni, 1990; Sutanto et al., 2021). The heterogeneity in backer motivations implies that some backers will value equity-focused appeals. Perhaps these backers already know about structural inequalities and want to alleviate structural issues due to their holistic mindset. I define a holistic mindset as a cognitive perspective that views society as interconnected and filled with complex causal relationships (Zhou et al., 2021). A holistic mindset includes openness towards others, open-mindedness, and cultural empathy, and is similar to a global mindset (Goxe & Belhoste, 2019; Gupta & Govindarajan, 2002). A backer with a holistic mindset may want to support people facing the greatest hardship no matter their circumstances or whether the backer receives a higher return because they view society as interconnected (Zhou et al., 2021). Thus, their pure altruistic tendencies will lead such backers to consider the entire marketplace in their choices and to derive utility from supporting projects with greater societal impact.

In the context of donation-based education, donors with a holistic mindset are concerned about social welfare across the entire US education system and want their donations to benefit the students with the highest need. I contrast the holistic mindset with other donor mindsets, such as a local mindset (donating to support schools in one’s local community) or a socially oriented mindset (donating to support friends/family in the education system). These mindsets are more connected to impure altruism in which donors want to feel good about supporting others (Sutanto et al., 2021), whether by helping a friend or supporting their community. A hallmark of donors with a holistic mindset is making donations to distant recipients. Prior studies have shown that backers are less likely to support distant individuals (Burtch et al., 2014; Lin & Viswanathan, 2016), even on prosocial platforms. By contrast, donors with a more holistic, global mindset are more likely to support distant organizations and display generosity towards distant others (Wang et al., 2021b; Zhao & Zhou, 2022). Furthermore, a holistic, global mindset correlates with higher-income donors (Zhao & Zhou, 2022) who have enough disposable income to donate outside their immediate community and social network. Because equity-focused recommendations ask backers to consider a holistic perspective and the broad historical context of the US education system rather than local or social communities, I would expect equity-focused initiatives to appeal to donors with a holistic mindset. I therefore hypothesize:

H2: The effect of promoting equity-focused initiatives will be moderated by a holistic mindset.

## 4.1 Equity-Focused Descriptor

In addition to these attributes, DonorsChoose uses a descriptor, equity-focused, to identify projects from schools in which the majority of students are (1) Black, Latinx, or Indigenous and (2) eligible for free or reduced-price lunch. An important objective for DonorsChoose is to support projects from equityfocused schools, thus progressing towards a more equitable outcome in which more students have the tools and experiences they need to receive a great education.

In the fall of 2021, DonorsChoose decided to make the equity-focused descriptor visible to donors. On September 28, 2021, DonorsChoose launched the descriptor “equity-focused” to highlight projects from schools that meet the equity-focused criteria and created a landing page to explain the definition of “equityfocused.” Figure 2 shows that the “equity-focused” landing page explains the structural inequalities in the

US education system and the platform’s commitment to help provide resources for traditionally marginalized communities. This equity-focused initiative specifically highlights the racial and socioeconomic disparities in the US educational system.

The other change was the ability to filter search results by the “equity-focused” descriptor. Figure 3 shows the search results page with the equity-focus criterion checked (top-left checkbox). The pop-out emphasizes that equity-focused schools are in low-income neighborhoods and primarily serve students of color.

The majority of projects on the platform are from equityfocused schools. On the day that the equity-focused descriptor launched, projects from equity-focused schools comprised 63.2% (17,220) of all projects (27,244), whereas projects from low-income schools and high-income schools comprised 15.6% (4,246) and 20.5% (5,590) of projects, respectively.

## 5 Empirical Analysis

The equity-focused descriptor served as the basis for both the observational and the experimental analyses. First, I compared donation patterns before and after the equity-focused descriptor launch. Next, I performed a more robust analysis to examine how equity-focused recommendations influence donor behavior using an A/B experimental design. The observational analysis and experimental analysis provide consistent results with respect to the hypotheses.

The equity-focused descriptor launched on September 28, 2021, and the anonymized donation data spanned a window of 14 days before the launch and 14 days after the launch (September 14 - October 12, 2021). This anonymized dataset included all the donations made on the website across all donor accounts. The dataset is aggregated at the donor-day level.

There are important limitations to this observational analysis. This analysis is susceptible to omitted variable bias, and the findings could be due to additional dynamics that were unobserved by the researcher. Furthermore, the donor population may exhibit systematic variation with the support for equity-focused projects. To address these concerns, I conducted a second, experimental study. Randomized experiments address the concerns of omitted variable bias and provide evidence of a causal relationship between treatment and outcome.

## 5.2 Experiment Analysis

To explicitly understand the causal effect of the equityfocused descriptor on donations, I ran a field experiment to test donor responses to asynchronous suggestions to support projects from equity-focused schools. In this experiment, donors received a marketing email with a call to action to support projects located near the zip code of the school that the donor most recently supported. This decision to suggest projects from donors’ recently supported locations aligned with the current practices. The marketing emails did not suggest specific projects because projects are removed from the platform once they receive the total amount of requested funds. Any recommendations for specific projects may be obsolete if the donor opens the email after those specific projects receive funding. Thus, the marketing emails promoted a set of projects and sent donors to a landing page of search results specific to that set of projects.

## 5.2.1 Experiment Design

The experiment contained two conditions. In the first condition, the donor email contained a link with all the relevant projects for that zip code (default condition). In the second condition, the donor email contained the projects for that zip code filtered to only display projects from equity-focused schools (EF condition). Aside from the landing page, the emails were identical in content. Table 3 illustrates the two email conditions for a donor named Sue whose most recently supported project was from a school in Rochester. Note that the content of the email is the same; the EF condition is only clear after the email recipient opens the email, clicks on the embedded link, and goes to the landing page with the set of projects on the DonorsChoose website. Upon clicking, Sue would be sent to a landing page with either (1) a default selection of Rochester projects or (2) an equity-focused selection of Rochester projects.

## 5.2.2 Randomization

I conducted a randomization check to confirm that the experimental intervention was indeed randomly distributed across donors. Table 5 shows that the randomization was successful. The donors in the EF condition were statistically indistinguishable from those in the default condition.

The random assignment of the conditions removed concerns related to endogeneity and the alternative explanations that confound observational analyses. Donors were unaware that their emails were part of an experiment, so there was no concern about observer bias.

Because the main body of the email was identical across conditions, only donors who clicked on the email received the treatment. Across the email conditions, neither the open rates (27.7% vs. 27.8%, p-value = 0.695) nor the click rates (4.6% vs. 4.7%, p-value = 0.169) were significantly different. Thus, this analysis dataset was restricted to donors who clicked on the emails and were exposed to the treatment. The analysis dataset had 9,257 donors across both conditions.

## 6.4 Politics

Political affiliations may factor into donors’ likelihood to donate to EF schools. To examine this explanation, I categorized donors’ states into “red” and “blue” using the electoral college votes in the 2020 US Presidential election. States were “red” if their electoral college votes went to the Republican candidate and “blue” if their electoral college votes went to the Democratic candidate. Using these definitions, I repeated the analysis with a split sample of red state and blue state donors. Table 11 shows the results.

The coefficient estimate on EF condition is significant and negative for seven out of eight models for blue state donors. For red state donors, the coefficient estimate on EF condition is significant for donations to lowerincome schools. The interaction between EF condition x Distance is significant and positive for blue state donors (Columns 2 and 4). Democrats typically have more concern about inequality (Horowitz et al., 2020), so this analysis is consistent with the supposition that equity-focused suggestions will resonate more with donors who have a holistic mindset and interest in structural issues.

## 6.5 Location Preference

Another potential explanation is that donors in the EF condition are exposed to different locations and thus donate to different locations than donors in the default condition. To explore this explanation, I examined the donation patterns based on the recommendation condition and report the results in Table 12. I estimated a model with the dependent variable as a binary of whether the donor donated to a school in the same zip code as the recommendation (Columns 1 and 2), the same city as the recommendation (Columns 3 and 4), their billing zip code (Columns 5 and 6), or their billing city (Columns 7 and 8). I found that the EF condition was not significant for any model except Column 7, which included the interaction between EF condition and distance. These models provide some evidence that the EF condition had limited effects on the location of the donation and instead mainly affected the school type.

## 7 Discussion

## 7.1 Summary

This study explores the effect of promoting equityfocused initiatives on an educational crowdfunding platform, DonorsChoose. In the initial observational analysis, I observed that promoting the equity-focused descriptor of schools leads to lower levels of donations to projects from those schools, relative to pre-launch levels. Next, I conducted an experiment. I emailed more than 200,000 donors and randomly assigned donors to receive emails with either a link to the default set of projects (default condition) or a link to a set of projects from equity-focused schools (EF condition). These findings suggest that donors who receive suggestions about projects matching the equity-focused descriptor are less likely to donate. I note that the overall decrease in donations is driven by the decline in donations to non-EF schools without a concomitant uptick in donations to schools matching the equity-focused descriptor. However, a holistic mindset appears to moderate the effectiveness of promoting equity-focused initiatives: donors who previously donated to geographically distant schools are more likely to respond to the equity-focused landing page. I examined multiple different alternative explanations, such as political affiliations and fewer equity-focused schools in a particular location, and determined that the findings are robust to these explanations. Collectively, this work explores how backer behavior changes in response to promoting equityfocused initiatives. I found evidence that the overall effect of promoting equity-focused initiatives dampens donations even though most backers support these projects, underscoring the potential trade-offs in explicitly highlighting the historical inequalities and overtly calling for donors to contribute to right past wrongs. This work emphasizes the nuanced trade-offs associated with information transparency regarding the platform’s equity goals.

This study builds upon prior research to address several gaps in the literature. Prior literature has considered firms seeking to engage in activism based on political ideology (Gupta & Briscoe, 2020). I explored the role of information transparency and how information transparency may attract some participants (e.g., those predisposed towards equity) but repel others (e.g., those predisposed towards social or geographic ties). The ambiguous nature of information transparency is consistent with previous studies on the role of transparency in promoting equity on platforms (Mejia & Parker, 2021). Information transparency motivated by good intentions can paradoxically hinder the fruition of those outcomes, suggesting the trade-offs in the use information transparency to promote equity initiatives. This research is particularly relevant to organizations that engage in corporate social responsibility (CSR) and aim to further equality through their initiatives (Gupta et al., 2019; Gupta & Briscoe, 2020).

I also theorize about why promoting equity initiatives may not improve equity-related outcomes in crowdfunding. Backers with kinship or geographic motivations are common on crowdfunding platforms (Agrawal et al., 2015; Burtch et al., 2014; Lin & Viswanathan, 2016), and these backers may not respond to platforms’ call to address inequity. Plus, many Americans feel uncomfortable with policies that benefit particular traditionally disadvantaged populations (Bobo & Kluegel, 1993) and prefer color-blind language (Ogbonnaya-Ogburu et al., 2020). Therefore, digital platforms that advertise equity-focused descriptors may reduce the nubmer of donors who are responsive to the message. Just as researchers note that users from marginalized communities experience more success with less transparency (Rhue & Clark, 2022; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018), organizations may experience more success in their CSR-related activities with lower transparency, particularly if these activities require broad support from the organizations’ constituents.

Despite the overall decrease in donations, I found that a subset of donors, those with a holistic mindset, are responsive to equity-focused initiatives. These donors are characterized by their willingness to donate to distant unknown beneficiaries, potentially due to a desire to broadly improve society through supporting the US education system and provide resources to distant classrooms in disadvantaged communities. While a single donation, or even an entire platform’s worth of donations, is insufficient to address the structural issues of education inequality in the US, holistically minded donors may find solace by directing their resources towards equityfocused initiatives.

These findings have multiple managerial implications. The potential of equity-focused recommendations to split donors implies that organizations should take a segmented approach towards equity-focused goals. Digital platforms and other organizations seeking to support equity-focused goals should target donors with an expected interest in structural reform, whether through previous explicit interest in equity-related objectives or through a willingness to support distant beneficiaries. Digital platforms can still adopt broad initiatives that benefit marginalized communities. For these broad equity-focused initiatives, digital platforms should consider promoting them without overtly mentioning equity by, for example, recommending projects in traditionally disadvantaged communities without explicitly referring to the composition of those communities. These less transparent recommendations may appeal to the broadest possible audience to attract the largest number of donations and provide more resources to marginalized communities.

## 7.2 Limitations and Future Work

There are several limitations to this work. First, I assumed heterogeneity in donor motivations but only collected donor behavior and inferred the motivations of individual donors. Future research could include a qualitative study to understand both donors’ motivations and donors’ responses to equity-related initiatives. Second, website donations include several different types of donors (e.g., such as new donors, returning donors, grant accounts, referred donors) and campaigns. There was limited information how many donors used the equity-focused descriptor immediately after the launch. The field experiment drove donors to explicitly consider projects matching that descriptor and was thus designed to address that issue. Third, I could not see the actual landing page for each donor in the experiment. Although I could see all the projects live on the website and the recommendation zip code and could thus control for the project inventory in any particular location, the dynamic nature of crowdfunding means that projects may become fully funded and removed from the website.

## 7.3 Conclusion

This study refines the understanding of organizational efforts to promote equity-related goals. Through preliminary observational analysis and a field experiment, I identified the causal effect of promoting equity-focused recommendations on donor behavior. Furthermore, I found a moderating effect of distant geographic interest on the donor’s receptiveness to equity-focused recommendations, suggesting a way in which organizations could segment their base to target more holistic and equity-minded recipients. My work investigates how highlighting equity creates trade-offs for digital platforms seeking to promote equity-related goals. This study contributes to a growing discourse on how organizations can advance equity-related initiatives.

## About the Author

Lauren Rhue, PhD, is an assistant professor of information systems at the Robert H. Smith School of Business at the University of Maryland. Her research explores the ethical, economic, and social implications of technology including projects on bias in artificial intelligence, the implications of using machine learning to increase equity, and disparities in crowdfunding. She has publications in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Health Policy and Technology, Journal of Management, and Social Networks, as well as law review articles in American Law Review and Journal of Science and Technology Law. Her research has been presented at numerous international conferences including the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS) and INFORMS. She received her doctorate in information systems from the New York University Stern School of Business and graduated from Stanford University with a BS in management science and engineering.
