# Understanding the influence of multiple levels of governments on the development of inter-organizational systems

Boriana Rukanova<sup>1</sup>, Eveline van Stijn<sup>1</sup>, Helle Z. Henriksen<sup>2</sup>, Ziv Baida<sup>3</sup> and Yao-Hua Tan<sup>1</sup>

## Abstract

In the arena of international trade, multiple levels of governments (ranging from national to supranational) play an important role in regulating and controlling cross-border trade activities. Recently, they have also become powerful players in influencing decisions about inter-organizational systems (IOS). The influences of these multiple levels of governments on IOS are of an enormous scale and impact on businesses and national economies. Understanding them is a prerequisite for informed actions. From a theoretical point of view we contribute with the MLxMC framework, a conceptual framework, which combines a processual, multi-level approach with the motors of change. We use the MLxMC framework to explicitly highlight the influences of multiple levels of governments on IOS developments that take place in the highly regulated environment of international trade. We demonstrate how the framework can be applied to analyze such developments, extending the existing IOS research with models that explicitly acknowledge the role of government. The framework makes use of multi-level analysis by taking the political and institutional aspects into account. As an analytical tool, the framework can support business practitioners as well as policy-makers in their strategic choices of which level to engage at and with whom to collaborate in order to influence the debate.

## Introduction

Globalization and technological developments have led to a boom in international trade in recent decades. International trade is ‘characterized not only by the physical movement of goods across national boundaries but [also] by voluminous paperwork that captures information pertinent to identification, delivery, and government control of transported goods (Teo et al., 1997, p. 144). Nowadays, the paper-based information exchange has been replaced to a large extent by electronic communication, resulting in a web of business and government information systems (IS). In Europe, numerous supranational IS, designed for the control of cross-border trade activities, are currently being introduced by the European Union (EU) under the header eCustoms.

The introduction of such eCustoms systems can be seen as interorganizational systems (IOS) phenomena. This process is influenced by multiple levels of government ranging from national to supranational. The implementation of these systems by governments and trading businesses is often made obligatory by law. Time and money have to be spent on the implementation and there are severe consequences when things go wrong. For example, the Integrated Cargo System introduced by the Australian government in 2005 led to a standstill of trade activities for weeks. Goods were not cleared and operators faced huge penalties for failing to deliver on time (Marshall, 2006). This example illustrates that governments are influencing decisions about IOS in the arena of international trade and that their interventions can be of enormous scale and impact. In order for businesses to be prepared and act strategically in this highly regulated international trade environment, it is crucial that they understand the influences that governments have on IOS developments.

The main question addressed in this paper is how can we understand the influences that multiple levels of government have on IOS developments that take place in the highly regulated environment of international trade? This question was inspired by our empirical work in the area of eCustoms, as well as the observation that in the context of our study, government influences on information systems (IS) could not be explained by the available IOS literature.

Most of the existing literature on IOS has a limited scope, it focuses on businesses-to-business (B2B) interactions, uses a factor approach (Gregor & Johnston, 2000; Johnston & Gregor, 2000) and neglects the role of government (Schooley & Horan, 2007). Although we identified a number of studies which challenge this narrow view and advocate multi-level, processual approaches (Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 1998a; Gregor & Johnston, 2000; Johnston & Gregor, 2000; Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 2001), they are limited in the way they define the various levels and the role of government. In some studies, government is positioned as part of the remote environment (Johnston & Gregor, 2000). In others, government is acknowledged as a key actor in the IOS arena (Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 2001) but its influences on IOS developments are either not made explicit or are considered with a limited focus at a national level (Teo et al., 1997), without taking other levels into account. Such an approach is inappropriate when we consider eCustoms, in which multiple levels of governments influence IS decisions.

The purpose of our work is to address these concerns and expand on earlier IOS research. We go beyond the limited focus on B2B interactions and we explicitly zoom-in on understanding the influences of government. More specifically, in this article we contribute with the ML-MC framework, a conceptual framework, which combines a processual, multi-level approach with the motors of change (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995). We use the MLxMC framework to explicitly highlight the influences that multiple levels of government have on IS developments in the highly regulated environment of international trade. We demonstrate how to apply the framework to analyze such developments. As such, this article is a response to the call made by Lyytinen & Damsgaard (2001) to extend existing IOS research with models that explicitly acknowledge the role of government and make use of multi-level analyses, by taking the socio-political and institutional aspects into account.

As our analysis demonstrates, IOS developments are shaped and reshaped by different political processes that take place at different levels of government. The impact and scale of such processes may have severe consequences for both businesses and national economies. Nevertheless, these processes are not necessarily predefined and can be influenced. There are numerous levels at which actions can be taken and influence exercised. Our ML-MC framework is a tool to understand these dynamics. As an analytical tool, it can provide business practitioners as well as policy-makers with support when they make their strategic choices as to which level to engage at and with whom to collaborate in order to become a part of the debate.

## Lagging research in public sector IOS

## The evolution of IOS literature over the past two decades

eCustoms is an IS-intensive practice that spans multiple organizations at business, government and supranational levels. Decisions made about IS are driven by different concerns and shaped by different forces. The eCustoms developments that we observe at present are clearly crossorganizational and share the characteristics of IOS.

Twenty years ago, when IOS was still a novelty, it was defined as ‘an automated IS shared by two or more companies’ (Cash & Konsynski, 1985, p. 134). This definition was later extended as follows:

An IOS is built around information technology, that is, around computer and communication technology that facilitates the creation, storage, transformation and transmission of information. An IOS differs from an internal distributed information system by allowing information to be sent across organizational boundaries. (Johnston & Vitale, 1988, p. 154)

These IOS characteristics can, to a large extent, be applied to eCustoms if it is considered to be an IS-intensive practice. However, the above definitions would then need to be extended to include the sharing of information between both business and government entities, rather than only among companies.

Earlier work on eCustoms shows that socio-political factors are at the heart of eCustoms developments (Fro¨-ler et al., 2007; Rukanova et al., 2007; Van Stijn & Bjørn-Andersen, 2007). In this respect, we concur with those studies, which call for an expansion of the IOS perspective to explicitly include the socio-political aspects. As Henriksen (2002) points out, the Johnston and Vitale definition of IOS is strongly focussed on the technical attributes of IOS. Other aspects which have to be included are socio-political traits that influence the collaboration across organizations (Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 1998a, b).

During our examination of current IOS literature, we identified several areas of concern, which lead us to believe that the IOS research carried out so far does not sufficiently explain the government influences on IS that we observe in eCustoms. We are concerned with (1) the approach; (2) the scope; and (3) the role of government and its interactions with businesses as observed in the existing IOS studies. These aspects are briefly reviewed below.

Most IOS studies follow a factor approach (Kurnia & Johnston, 2000), which assumes that outcomes of technology adoption by organizations are determined by a number of predicting variables (factors) identified at a particular time (Markus & Robey, 1988). Such studies normally consider individual organizational units to be a unit of analysis or, at best, pair-wise relationships. They are, therefore, unable to capture the context and dynamic interactions of individual organizations with trading partners and other industry organizations that occur in the course of IOS adoption (Kurnia & Johnston, 2000). To overcome this limitation, Kurnia & Johnston (2000) advocate the necessity of introducing a processual approach in order to examine the complex and dynamic nature of IOS. The processual approach is also advocated by Lyytinen & Damsgaard (2001), who argue that IOS researchers need to focus on process features (including histories) and apply varying time scales while drafting an account of what happened and why.

With respect to the scope of IOS research, Johnston & Gregor (2000) argue that most research on IOS deals with issues that arise when systems cross corporate boundaries or the difficulties of partnership. But it tends to focus on a limited scope of inter-organizational interactions, often pair-wise. To a large extent, the work has been ignoring the relationships between analysis at the level of an individual firm and analysis at a broader industry level (Gregor & Johnston, 2000). As a reaction to this, various authors argued that it is important to look beyond the micro-level interactions among individual organizations and focus on a multi-level analysis (Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 1998a; Gregor & Johnston, 2000; Johnston & Gregor, 2000; Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 2001). In the context of IOS adoption (focusing on Electronic data interchange (EDI) in particular), Damsgaard & Lyytinen (1998a) propose three levels of analysis: micro (a focus on individual and/or organizational units), meso (a focus on interactions between adopting organizations and external institutions), and macro (which recognizes regulatory regimes as focal points that can constrain or enable the diffusion process). Along similar lines, Johnston & Gregor (2000) and Gregor & Johnston (2000) define three levels of analysis: the individual industry unit (organizations that contribute to the operations of the industry); the industry group itself (consisting of the individual industry units plus the system of relations between them);

and the remote environment (those firms, organizations, institutions, and other factors on a larger scale than industry that affect organizations and the relationships between them). Although these levels allow for a more sophisticated analysis, they still do not make the interactions between businesses and multiple governments and regulatory regimes sufficiently explicit.

Historically, government has not been part of IOS research, which initially focussed on examining the use of IS to support B2B interactions among companies only (Cash & Konsynski, 1985). Although subsequent IOS studies do not usually make a distinction between whether the collaboration partner is another business unit or a governmental unit, it is rather the rule than the exception that the focus remains on B2B interactions. One aspect when involving government in IOS implementation is the shift in focus from the micro, that is the gains of competitive advantage among adopters (Johnston & Gregor, 2000), to a macro perspective where the value of IOS is for the benefit of society as a whole (Fountain, 2001). It is worth mentioning that we found a limited number of studies in which the interactions between government and businesses (G2B) have indeed been the focus of analysis. These include the study on the introduction of Singapore’s TradeNet (Teo et al., 1997) and the ‘e-export’ system in Denmark (Bjørn-Andersen et al., 2007). Nevertheless, these studies remain limited in their exploration into interactions between businesses and national government only.

In the previously discussed studies advocating multilevel analysis, government appears as a player, but only as part of the remote (Johnston & Gregor, 2000) or macro (Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 1998a, b) environments. As such, government remains peripheral in the analysis. Furthermore, according to Johnston & Gregor (2000) the role of the environment (of which government is part of) is seen as uni-directional, which means that the environment can cause changes at industry level and the individual industry units but not vice versa. This view is also supported by Andersen et al. (2007), who advocate that governments can set the agenda for IOS implementation by making systems obligatory by law. On the other hand, Gregor & Johnston (2000) advocate that a bi-directional interaction does exist. They argue that an industry as a whole, or a body representing the industry, could act so as to influence government regulations, and thus change the environment within which the industry operates. Although these studies refer to the interactions between business and government they do not provide further elaboration on how to analyze such interactions.

Despite the studies carried out, the issue of IOS in the public sector and its interaction with private businesses has received limited attention so far. More research on this particular focus is required, as argueded by Schooley & Horan (2007). This is in line with Lyytinen & Damsgaard (2001), who advocate the need to extend the current IOS research with models that explicitly acknowledge the role of government and make use of multi-level analysis by taking the political and institutional aspects into account.

## The MLxMC framework

Multi-level analysis and processual approach In order to understand the influence of government on IOS developments in the highly regulated environment of international trade, we adhere to the processual approach, as it appears to be more suitable in capturing the inherent complexity of the context. Indeed, the processual approach had already been advocated in the field of organizational studies and more particularly Pettigrew’s contextualist approach to change (Pettigrew, 1985, 1987, 1990; Pettigrew et al., 2001). Pettigrew argues that a study of change needs to place change in its historical, processual, and contextual setting.

Although the idea of multi-level analysis is also of great interest to us, the specific levels that we have identified in IOS literature (i.e. industry unit, industry group, and remote environment by Johnston & Gregor (2000); and micro, meso, macro by Damsgaard & Lyytinen (1998a, b)) limit our understanding of the influence of government on IS developments that we observe in eCustoms. We therefore make use of a more conceptual approach on levels and interactions (Pettigrew, 1990; Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 2001) and we populate specific levels, based on our findings from the empirical material. For analytical purposes, we find the concepts proposed by Pettigrew (1990) particularly useful, as they allow for a coherent way of connecting different levels and interactions with the notion of time. Pettigrew (1990) suggests that an analysis of a change process needs to draw on phenomena at vertical and horizontal levels and the interconnections between those levels over time. The vertical levels capture the interdependence between higher and lower levels of analysis. The horizontal levels refer to the interconnectedness among phenomena in historical, present, and future time. In our analysis, we refer to the vertical levels of Pettigrew as levels, to the horizontal levels as time and to the interconnections as interactions. Time here is used in a sense of relative time, which means that a change is preceded or succeeded by another change, rather than in terms of an absolute time.

In addition, we propose to use the concept of regulation to guide us during the identification of the different levels of government which play a role in influencing decisions about IS concerning cross-border trade activities. ‘Regulation is the intentional activity of attempting to control, order, or influence the behaviour of others’ (Parker et al., 2004, p. 2). Although this definition is quite general, it encapsulates a common understanding of regulation as a steering mechanism in society. In order to understand the influences of government on IOS, we need to understand the change processes that occur at each level and how such change processes influence each other as well as decisions about IS. Therefore, at each level we explore changes and interactions among them. Our theoretical approach is supported by visualizations as means of representation (Gregor, 2006). Van de Ven & Poole (1995, p. 520) base their model on two dimensions: mode of change and unit of change. The mode of change reflects the way change is understood as prescribed or constructed. The added value of using the motors of change In combination with the concept of levels introduced earlier (see Figure 1), the four motors provide us with a way to zoom in and make explicit the mechanisms that drive specific changes that occur at different levels. This provides us with an enriched conceptual framework to explore government influences on IS decisions.

Although there are many models that aim to describe change, we have chosen this framework for several reasons. First of all, a key strength of Van de Ven and Poole’s framework is that it is built on a thorough literature review of research on change. By making use of these four motors, we are therefore stepping on a solid conceptual base. Second, compared to frameworks on change found in the existing IS literature, this framework is more comprehensive, because it accommodates for different types of changes. In the available IS literature, we see research that predominantly advocates either the planned approach towards change (such as studies focusing on IS-enabled business process re-engineering and strategic IS planning) or view change as an emergent phenomenon (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Orlikowski, 1996; Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Sarker et al., 2006). Along similar lines, a review of IS literature by Nordheim & Pa¨iva¨rinta (2006) reveals studies that usually address only one of the four motors. Van de Ven & Poole (1995), on the other hand, provide a more comprehensive framework in which both the planned changes (e.g. such as those driven by the life-cycle motor, LM) and emergent changes (e.g. such as those driven by a dialectic motor, DM) are treated as equally important. We came across two earlier studies that utilize Van de Ven and Poole’s typology in the IS field (Cule & Robey, 2004; Nordheim & Pa¨iva¨rinta, 2006). Although these studies explore other contexts than eCustoms and international trade, they both find that the typology provides a useful perspective to investigate IS change processes. We therefore consider it to be a useful tool for shedding light on the role that different governments play in their attempts to influence IS changes in the area of international trade.

## Interpretive case methodology

## Methodological foundations

In this study, we build on the interpretative and contextualist tradition that is well established in IS research (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Walsham, 1993; Klein & Myers, 1999). Interpretive studies are ‘aimed at producing an understanding of the context of the IS, and the process whereby the IS influences and is influenced by the context’ (Walsham, 1993, pp. 4–5). In line with this, we do not propose a positivistic test of our conceptual framework, nor do we use our framework as a predictive model. In our study we make use of a single, in-depth case study, the use of which does not inhibit the achievement of generalizable results, as argued by Walsham (1995, 2006). In her taxonomy, Gregor (2006) made a distinction among five theory types in IS research, namely a theory type for (1) analysis; (2) explanation; (3) prediction; (4) explanation and prediction; and (5) design and action. Here, our goal is to analyze and explain the phenomena under consideration; this, in terms of Gregor (2006), refers to her theory types for (1) analysis and (2) explanation.

## Study focus: Dutch tax and customs administration (DTCA)

The study presented here is part of an EU-funded research project, which aims to propose innovative solutions for cross-border trade. Four European Living Labs provide the real-life settings in which the eCustoms solutions are developed. The Dutch Beer Living Lab (BeerLL) focuses on the export of excise goods and the key players are a large beer producer based in the Netherlands (BeerCo), two large technology providers, a Dutch university and the DTCA. DTCA is an example of a specific local government actor in the EU’s eCustoms developments.

DTCA is the focal organization of our analysis for two key reasons. First, the Netherlands, specifically through the port of Rotterdam, plays a crucial role in international trade in Europe. In order to maintain this role, DTCA is under constant pressure to change. Second, as a result of this pressure, DTCA has been a key initiator of eCustoms changes and innovations at the EU level as well. Because of its very high dependency on trade, the Dutch government feels the same urgency as the EU to improve competitiveness as well as control. As such, the case provides us with a rich context to study interactions between DTCA and businesses on the one hand and DTCA and supranational bodies like the EU on the other. It also helps us to examine how different levels of government influence and shape decisions about IOS.

## The field research

The bulk of the field research was conducted in 2006. However, we maintained close contact with the informants during the subsequent analysis stage as well. Through 2007 and 2008, we had ongoing follow-up meetings with experts to clarify aspects of the case and to obtain feedback on our interpretations of the material. Altogether, we attended 19 work meetings, five brainstorming sessions and we conducted 23 interviews with BeerLL participants as well as 22 interviews with external experts (see Appendix A for further details about the interviews). These figures do not include the informal interactions that we maintained with the informants, which during the intensive periods of the data collection were multiple times a week.

Access to the field was accomplished via the BeerLL. The BeerLL was a pilot project which, by using innovative technologies and redesigning customs procedures, aimed to develop a proof-of-concept of how it is possible to achieve significant trade simplification, while preserving the required levels of control and security (Tan et al., 2006). The BeerLL developed through several stages: analysis, redesign, pilot, and evaluation stage. The main sources of data concerning the analysis and redesign stage were brainstorming sessions, work meetings, and interviews with BeerLL participants. The brainstorming sessions and the meetings were hosted by the BeerLL partners on a rotating basis. We used the initial meetings to become acquainted with the domain, the procedures, the systems used, and the relevant EU legislation. The initial meetings were also meant for knowledge sharing among the involved participants. They included a combination of presentations concerning export procedures, the developments of DTCA and the EU in the area of export of excise goods as well as discussions on the available processes and systems at BeerCo. The brainstorming sessions were focused on generating ideas for the redesign of current procedures for the export of excise goods and how technology can facilitate that process. In addition to these brainstorming sessions, follow-up work meetings were organized with a sub-set of the BeerLL participants with the purpose of going into specific procedures and the related systems in-depth. Furthermore, extensive face-to-face interviews were held with each of the key participants in the BeerLL in order to get an in-depth understanding of the issues that were identified during the general meetings. Most of the interviews were held at the location of the interviewees and lasted 1.52 h on average. The majority of the meetings and interviews were digitally recorded and the files were archived for further analysis; detailed meeting notes were also taken. Due to the large number of recorded material. the interviews and meetings were only partially transcribed. The language for the interviews was mainly English. The meetings and brainstorming sessions were held in either Dutch or English, depending on the participants involved. The reports with the outcomes of these meetings were written in English, so that the results could be shared with the international project partners.

The following recurrent themes emerged from the BeerLL: fiscal facilitation, security, Authorized Economic Operator (AEO), fraud prevention and the introduction of the Excise Movement and Control System (EMCS) to counter fraud in the excise regime. We therefore collected further data about these developments. To this end, DTCA identified the relevant experts that could provide us with further information on these topics and we conducted follow-up interviews. In addition, we collected a rich pool of secondary data in the form of documents and reports. These encompassed documents and white papers from different institutions: EU policy documents, EU legal documents and white papers, documents from international organizations like the World Customs Organization, U.S.A. government documents concerning security certification, public and internal DTCA documents, information on past projects at DTCA, process models of the export procedures, and technical specifications of systems. These documents served as additional information to the recorded meetings and interviews in the process of data analysis.

## The role of the authors in the data collection and data analysis stage

The main investigator acted as observer during the BeerLL meetings and brainstorming sessions. She also conducted the interviews with the individual participants in the BeerLL, as well as the external experts from DTCA. Two of the other authors were actively involved in managing the BeerLL and took an active role in the redesign sessions. Though they were not directly involved in the data collection process in the BeerLL, the remaining two authors were involved in the collaborative effort of developing the conceptual framework as well as the analysis. During this stage they took a critical view when interpreting the findings.

## Method of data analysis

The data collection and analysis were conducted in an iterative manner (cf. Walsham, 2006). As discussed earlier, during the initial stages of the BeerLL we identified several high-level topics, which we decided to investigate further. These included: security and fiscal facilitation, AEO, fraud prevention and EMCS, as well as the respective IS systems. Although we obtained a rich pool of data related to these topics, we needed to add further structure in order to proceed with the analysis. To this end, we first developed extensive case descriptions and we used multiple sources of evidence to triangulate the findings. We compared the findings from the interviews and the meetings with the information that we had found in the documents. We also maintained close (in the intensive stages of the data collection up to several times per week) email, telephone, and face-to-face contact with key informants to verify our findings. A large part of the case descriptions was sent to DTCA experts and the data was verified in several iterative loops.

Although we are engaged in the dialogue between the empirics and the theories, we aimed to find further theories to help us capture the government influences on IS developments that we had observed. Not only did we observe a multiplicity of government levels involved in shaping IS decisions, but we also noticed that different types of change processes were taking place at different levels and motivated interactions among levels. During these iterations, our attention was drawn to the roots of our ML-MC conceptual framework, which is based on a multi-level, processual analysis and extended with the motors of change proposed by Van de Ven & Poole (1995). Subsequently, we used this framework as a synthesizing device to put our observations in perspective (cf. Klein & Myers, 1999). In our analysis, we sought to explore how the complex socio-political interactions among different levels influenced and shaped IOS developments.

Below, we first provide a brief introduction to the case data and then we present the steps followed during the data analysis.

Brief introduction to the three clusters analyzed in the case Our case findings are grouped in three clusters that cover the themes investigated during the study. In the description of each of the following clusters, the changes that are further discussed in the analysis section of this article are indexed with labels. For example (A1) means change 1 in Cluster A.

## Cluster A: shift in auditing and fiscal facilitation in the Netherlands

The container innovation (A1) and the subsequent opening of the European Container Terminal in Rotterdam led to a tremendous increase in the volume of shipments passing through the Netherlands. It provided opportunities for businesses to handle large volumes (A2) and to improve their process performance significantly. This, in turn, affected DTCA (A3). It was no longer possible for DTCA to rely only on the traditional physical inspections. Due to the large volumes of shipments, DTCA had to look for other mechanisms of control; the audit approach was proposed as an alternative. As a result, the combination of physical inspections and administrative audit emerged as the new approach and became institutionalized at DTCA. This shift in the method of controlling initiated subsequent changes on business (A4). It became possible for businesses to obtain licences and enjoy trade simplifications but it also became their responsibility to prove that they were in control of their operations. The large companies, in particular, had to rely extensively on IS which register supply chain information in order to provide this proof. To obtain the licences, companies therefore had to fulfil strict requirements concerning their IS systems and procedures. Once a licence is obtained, companies are audited periodically by DTCA to ensure that the compliance requirements are still met. One specific type of licence was the licence for periodic (monthly) declarations. It allowed companies to follow a simplified procedure and provide a consolidated declaration once per month, instead of per transaction. The consequence for DTCA was that enormous volumes of paper documents were received at the moment of audit (A5), because they did not have systems in place to handle electronic declarations. This in turn resulted in two initiatives for IS development at DTCA. One was an informal initiative led by an auditor: the auditor project. The other was an official DTCA project (the eDeclaration project) that required a great deal of human and financial resources. In the end, the eDeclaration project did not succeed and was halted. The system developed by the auditor was then announced as the one to be used temporarily at DTCA for handling monthly electronic declarations.

Cluster B: security and fiscal facilitation and the concept of AEO

We already discussed the change in auditing (B1 ¼ A3) that occurred at DTCA and the related IS requirements (see Cluster A). Sweden had developed a similar way of thinking about auditing. Due to the similarities, the two administrations engaged in cooperative efforts (B2). They conducted benchmarking studies and developed common grounds for the auditing and fiscal facilitation of companies. Despite the fact that the Netherlands and Sweden both use the audit approach, it has not been widely adopted in the EU. A series of changes that took place in the U.S.A. provided favourable grounds for the partial institutionalization of the audit approach developed by DTCA and Sweden in the EU.

After the 9/11 attacks (B3), countering the threat of terrorism rose high on the political agenda. In the U.S.A., a certification procedure called C-TPAT was introduced (B4) to deal with security threats in international trade. This initiative means that certified businesses are assigned ‘low risk’ status. A key benefit of this low risk status is that Customs makes fewer physical inspections on the goods shipped by certified businesses. The C-TPAT initiative also influenced the EU (B5). The EU started implementing similar measures under the label of AEO. These AEO developments created grounds for the partial institutionalization of the audit approach, developed by DTCA and Sweden, in the EU. The resulting AEO concept in the EU was considered to be more general compared to the C-TPAT. While C-TPAT only covers security aspects, AEO allows for both fiscal, as well as security facilitation. As a result, EU businesses that apply for AEO certification can enjoy a wider range of simplifications. However, in order to obtain the certificates they need to adjust their IS, information provisioning, and internal control procedures to meet the fiscal and/or security requirements depending on the degree of coverage they aim to achieve.

## Cluster C: fraud prevention and EMCS

To deal with large-scale excise goods fraud (C1), the European Commission has initiated the development of a new IS solution – the EMCS – to replace the paper-based document flow with an electronic one (C2). This system is part of larger EU strategic plans which are predefined, long-term and follow a particular path to the introduction of eCustoms in Europe. In the case of EMCS, all 27 member states must commence with the implementation of the EMCS system in their national administrations (C3) and all businesses involved with excise goods must develop interfaces with the national EMCS systems (C4). These systems are developed only for the purpose of reporting to the authorities and have no further commercial value.

In the collaborative innovation project, the BeerLL involving businesses and representatives from DTCA, an alternative concept is proposed (C5). The BeerLL concept builds upon the ideas of AEO (C6 ¼ B5) and the audit approach (C7 ¼ A3) but it goes further by proposing greater simplifications for companies than current legislation allows. The BeerLL concept suggests that AEO companies do not need to develop new systems such as the EMCS to report to the authorities. The authorities themselves can rely on the information available in a company’s commercial IS. Although technologically this concept can work, one major issue is that in order for this solution to be used in practice, it is necessary to make adjustments to EU legislation, to allow reliable traders to report to the authorities in an alternative way. Currently, the participants in the BeerLL innovation project are engaging in mobilizing a collective action involving powerful players from businesses, industry associations, and government to influence such legislative change (C8).

Steps followed during the data analysis The data analysis process can be summarized in the following steps:

## Step 1: identification of clusters of changes around the dominant topics

While exploring the topics mentioned earlier, we identified many changes and inter-relationships. We started with mapping these changes and interrelationships directly, without separating them first. This approach turned out to be very difficult, due to its inherent complexity. Therefore, we decided to identify smaller groups of related changes which we later labelled as clusters. Each cluster refers to a specific topic that we investigated during the case. We analyzed the clusters separately and then looked for relationships among different clusters. This enabled us to develop a step-wise approach to deal with the complexity. If a change appeared in multiple clusters, it was used as a strong indicator of relationships among clusters.

## Step 2: identification of changes in a specific cluster

As a second step, we aimed to understand what were important changes related to the topic under investigation. These changes formed part of the cluster addressing the topic. In order to map the changes into a cluster, we usually started with a change that was related to the topic and then we used the concepts of levels and time to identify related changes that appear before or after this change. A question that we asked ourselves was: is this change preceded or succeeded by other related changes? If so, at what level do these other changes occur? One may wonder how far back or forth in time one needs to go with such an analysis. This would depend on the specific situation. In some clusters, the time span was approximately 10 years. In other clusters we may see longer or shorter periods. However, in our analysis we were interested in relative time (i.e. we aimed to identify whether a change was preceded or succeeded by another change), rather than in absolute time of a specific change. The heuristics that we followed, therefore, were to look back in the past in order to identify a significant trigger event that initiated a series of changes. We then looked forward, as far as we could find evidence of related changes.

In the process of level identification, we used the interaction between business and government as a starting point, and the concept of regulation to guide us in the identification of multiple levels of government. This led to a progression consisting of national government in an economic zone, the economic zone itself, and other economic zones. We also identified levels which point to collaborations. The reason to consider collaboration as a separate level is that the collaborating bodies have established rules, which are applicable to the members collaborating, but differ in the form of the rules that are defined at other levels. Finally, we had a level for capturing all external events. An external event, in this context, is an event, which initiates a series of changes that are of interest for our analysis, but which we decide to treat as a black box. For example, in Cluster A we chose to treat the container innovation as a black box. We did the same for the 9/11 attacks in Cluster B and excise fraud in Cluster C. These events were identified as triggering eCustoms developments, but the events themselves were deemed out of scope for further analysis. As a result, we did not look further back in time for changes that brought about external events.

## The relationship among the clusters and summary of the findings concerning influences on IS

Some changes occurred in more than one cluster. As discussed in the methodology chapter, if a change occurred in two clusters it was considered to be an indication of a relationship among clusters. This approach helped us to accumulate all the levels that we had identified in different clusters. Furthermore, it enabled us to provide an overview of the different motors and interactions that take place and shape decisions about IS (see Figure 7). We use this comprehensive view in this section to reflect on the influence of government on IS (see also Table 7).

By examining the influences on IS, already identified in the various clusters, we can draw several conclusions. First of all, we see (in Clusters A and C) that government can influence IS developments at business level. This observation is in line with what we found in the IOS literature (Johnston & Gregor, 2000; Andersen et al., 2007), where government is viewed as an actor which influences businesses uni-directionally. Our analysis also enables us to shed further light on the power and the source of government’s influence in its interactions with businesses. In terms of power, government can exercise weak or strong influence on businesses as far as IS is concerned. An example of weak influence is to be found in Cluster A, where companies are not obliged by government to apply for licences and adjust their systems respectively; they can decide to do so on a voluntary basis. Examples of government exercising strong influence on businesses are found in Cluster C, where government makes the systems legally binding. In such cases, companies have less freedom to operate and they will be held liable in case of non-compliance. Government influences could also have different sources: the source can be rooted at the level of national government (Cluster A) or at the level of an economic zone (Cluster C).

Our analysis also illustrates (Cluster A), however, that changes at business level can initiate IS changes at government level. Unlike earlier IOS studies (Johnston & Gregor, 2000; Andersen et al., 2007) which argue that government affects businesses in a uni-directional manner, our findings demonstrate that this is not necessarily the case. Our study illustrates that changes at business level can initiate IS changes at government level and this suggests that the interaction is bi-directional rather than uni-directional. Our findings are in line with Gregor & Johnston (2000) who also explicitly acknowledge this bi-directional interaction. This is an important conclusion because it has implications for the reasoning behind action. While the uni-directional view suggests that government imposes systems and businesses will need to comply, the bi-directional view suggests that businesses can play a much more active role that is they can initiate changes and influence IS decisions at government level. ## Discussion and conclusions

The main question that we raised at the beginning of this article was how can we understand the influences that multiple levels of government have on IOS developments that take place in the highly regulated environment of international trade? This question was motivated by our empirical work in the area of eCustoms where we were confronted with an IOS phenomenon, which the available IOS literature was not able to explain.

To position more precisely what our study adds to the extant IOS research, let us make a brief recap of our earlier observations about the limitations of current IOS studies. Traditionally IOS research was focused on B2B interactions among companies (Cash & Konsynski, 1985), and not on interactions between government and business. With the development of IOS research we did find cases where interaction between business and government were put forward in the analysis (Teo et al., 1997; Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 1998b; Bjørn-Andersen et al., 2007). However, these studies remained limited to a national level only. With the growing scale of applicability of IOS in more complex domains there was a need to address this growing complexity. As a result, the multi-level analysis was introduced to the IOS field (Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 1998a; Gregor & Johnston, 2000; Johnston & Gregor, 2000; Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 2001). Although these multi-level studies allowed for a more sophisticated analysis, they remained limited in the way they examined the role of government and its interactions with businesses. Government and regulatory regimes were positioned only as part of the macro or the remote environment (Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 1998a, Gregor & Johnston, 2000) and their role was not further investigated. Again, Schooley & Horan (2007) pointed out that so far the issue of IOS in the public sector and its interaction with private businesses has received limited attention and there is a need for more research in this particular field. Along similar lines, Lyytinen & Damsgaard (2001) called for an extension of the existing research with models that explicitly acknowledge the role of government, making use of multi-level analysis by taking political and institutional aspects into account. Addressing this gap in the existing IOS research is precisely where our contribution lies.

The domain of international trade proved to be particularly suitable for addressing this gap and provided very interesting grounds for making the influences of multiple levels of government on IS explicit. What made international trade a very insightful domain to study is that it covers the interface between businesses and government, rather than only focusing on one of them. Furthermore, due to its global scale it embraces a complex arena, where multiple governments can have a say in how cross-border trade activities should be controlled and how IS should be shaped and used to support these processes.

Our MLxMC conceptual framework, combines a processual, multi-level approach with the motors of change (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995). It helps to make explicit the influences of multiple levels of government on IS developments that take place in the highly regulated environment of international trade. Although earlier IOS research also identified the need for a multilevels analysis (Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 1998a; Johnston & Gregor, 2000), the levels and interactions discussed remain too abstract and were not focused specifically on government. In our work, we zoom in and identify specific levels of governments and regulatory regimes and we examine specific interactions among these levels. The motors of change (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995) that we added to our analysis provided us with additional insights into the change processes that took place at different levels. This enabled us to gain a deeper understanding of the dynamic processes that shape multiple levels of government influences on IS. Of particular interest to us in our study were the dialectic motor, which signals opportunities for intervention and action, and the teleological motor, which indicates that different levels of governments (and businesses) can partner and play a role in shaping the outcomes of dialectic processes. Many earlier studies focused on specific levels (e.g. the traditional IOS studies focus only on the business level), or on a specific motor (for an overview of such studies see Nordheim & Pa¨iva¨rinta, 2006). Our analysis indicates that many eCustoms developments that we observed could only be explained by examining the multiplicity of levels and interactions between them and by taking into account the existence of different motors. This, therefore, reconfirms the added value to our combined theoretical framework and is in line with Lapointe & Rivard (2007), who also concluded that a multi-level view and the use of different models can explain paradoxes which cannot be explained when looking at a single level or using a single model only.

Socio-political aspects are important (Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 1998a, b; Henriksen, 2002), but unstudied where these take place between multiple levels of government in the complex context of the international arena. Socio-political processes such as interactions among national governments, between national governments and supranational governments of an economic zone, or among supranational governments of different economic zones are also very important for understanding IOS developments and deserve explicit treatment and attention.

From the point of view of practitioners, remaining passive observers in the arena of international trade, where governments play a key role in shaping decisions about IS, could be devastating. For example, the costs and extra requirements necessary for the introduction of numerous EU-wide systems for reporting to the authorities may be prohibitive for some businesses and they may be forced to withdraw from the international trade arena. National economies may be hit by disturbances in logistics processes and businesses may register huge losses as a result of the upheavals that could come with the introduction of eCustoms systems, as was the case in Australia (Marshall, 2006). It is therefore essential that businesses and national governments are able to understand the complex interactions that take place across multiple levels of government that shape decisions on IS, in order to be prepared and develop strategies for action. To enhance such understanding, the MLxMC framework that we provide in this article, as well as the illustration of how it can be applied, can serve as an analysis tool for practitioners. As our findings demonstrate, although IS decisions are shaped and reshaped by different levels of government, they are not necessarily predefined and can be influenced. There are numerous levels at which actions can be taken and influence exercised (especially when a dialectic motor is active) and practitioners can engage in such interactions to influence outcomes Engagement in such processes, however, may not be easy; it may demand many resources, may be time consuming and require smart partnering (both nationally and internationally). Nevertheless, bearing in mind the possible huge impacts of non-involvement in the debate may justify these efforts. Involvement in such a dialectic process can be seen as engaging in a relationship, which is potentially not only beneficial for businesses but also for government. As businesses are striving for efficiency gains and reduction of the administrative burden, they can propose innovative concepts, as is the case with the BeerLL in Cluster C. Such new concepts can offer increased efficiency and cost savings for government as well. We already see initiatives at EU level, such as The Action Program for reducing administrative burdens in the EU,<sup>1</sup> which foster such bottom-up innovations.

Our study has a number of limitations and opens opportunities for further research. First, in this article we explore one case in the domain of eCustoms only, with the focus on the Netherlands and the EU. Further insights can be gained from extending this study to incorporate cases from other countries or economic zones with different political systems. It would also be interesting to extend the study to include other highly regulated domains that are of an international character. Second, as any model, our MLxMC framework enables us to see certain aspects, and inevitably limits us to explore others. The framework that we developed is based on a limited set of constructs, focusing on levels, motors, and interactions. Further research may focus on expanding our framework by including additional constructs or by further zooming in on constructs that we have already identified. For example, when applying the motors of change we felt that some further operationalization of each motor can be useful for a better understanding of the changes driven by that motor. Especially interesting would be to zoom in further on the dialectic motor, which we found to be particularly illuminating in our study. This can be done, for example, by examining the processes of how different levels engage in shaping the synthesis. Earlier work in that direction (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2006), as well as initial application in the domain of eCustoms (Rukanova et al., 2007) is already available.
