# Be Together, Run More: Enhancing Group Participation in Fitness Technology

Zilong Liu,<sup>1</sup> Xuequn Wang,<sup>2</sup> Xin (Robert) Luo,<sup>3</sup> Xiaolong Song,<sup>4</sup> Na Liu,<sup>5</sup> Yuan Zhang<sup>6</sup>

## Abstract

Individuals are increasingly using novel fitness technologies, such as running applications (apps), to support their workouts. The literature has primarily focused on the use of fitness apps at the individual level (i.e., to improve individuals’ exercise levels) and few studies have investigated the role of fitness apps in facilitating group exercise. Consequently, there is a paucity of information on how to enhance the exercise participation of individuals using fitness apps through the use of groups (i.e., how to entice more individuals to engage in exercise). We selected a running app as the context and focused on a particular feature of this app called “Running Spot,” which facilitates members’ offline group engagement, a topic that has thus far received scant attention in the literature. Drawing on the perspective of psychological distance and relational cohesion theory, we propose that the Running Spot feature facilitating offline group engagement improved group participation in running. To advance this line of research, we utilized a panel dataset of 151 running groups from the running app platform over a period of 38 weeks. The aim was to empirically evaluate the effects of offline group engagement facilitation (e.g., Running Spot) using a combination of the difference-indifferences approach and the propensity score matching technique. Our findings suggest that Running Spot indeed promoted groups’ participation in running. Furthermore, the impact of Running Spot was magnified with smaller groups and groups that were moderately closely located to the designated running spots. Our study contributes to the growing body of knowledge on fitness technologies by revealing ways to support group participation and uncovering the complex impact of offline group engagement facilitation (e.g., Running Spot). Our study has important implications for fitness app developers in that it demonstrates that features facilitating offline group engagement should be prioritized to improve group participation in fitness activities.

Keywords: Fitness Technology, Running Group, Running Spot, Activeness, Offline Activities, Relational Cohesion Theory, Psychological Distance, Group Engagement

## 1 Introduction

With the rapid development of fitness technologies in recent years, we have witnessed a significant increase in the information technology (IT)-driven paradigm of individuals leveraging fitness technologies such as mobile applications (apps) to improve their workouts. Importantly, fitness technologies have been developed to support exercise by offering various features, such as goal setting, performance tracking, and support (James et al., 2019). This latest phenomenon is gaining momentum, with a recent report revealing that the number of fitness app users reached 87.4 million in 2020, representing a 39.39% increase since 2018 (Anurina, 2022). For example, AllTrails (a popular app that supports outdoor activities such as hiking and running) was downloaded over 320,000 times in the United States in September 2020 (Statista, 2020). The number of people using health and wellness apps in the United States is predicted to stay above 84 million in 2022 (Phaneuf, 2020). Moreover, sales of these apps reached \$2.1 billion in 2017 and are expected to surpass \$10 billion by 2026 (Osypenko, 2019).

The ultimate goal of fitness technologies is to support individuals’ exercise behaviors. However, despite the phenomenal growth of fitness innovations, a paucity of research has examined whether (and how) these technological tools are successful in supporting individuals’ exercise activities and improving their health. Surprisingly, the results in the growing body of information systems (IS) literature are contradictory. For example, whereas some research has revealed that app users engage in higher levels of exercise (Dallinga et al., 2015; Tu et al., 2019), a recent meta-analysis has discovered that activity-monitoring apps do not significantly increase the level of exercise (Romeo et al., 2019). Therefore, further research is warranted to elucidate how the functionalities of fitness technologies can support individuals’ exercise regimens.

One plausible explanation for the contradictory results in the literature is that routine exercise takes a significant amount of time and effort, and individuals are often prone to avoiding it, especially when they exercise alone (Mayo Clinic, 2019). However, individuals frequently participate in group exercise (Xie et al., 2020) to leverage the social support from others and thereby improve their exercise habits (Courneya & McAuley, 1995). Even though some types of exercise (such as running) can be accomplished individually, exercising with others can create positive social experiences (Xie et al., 2020). In such scenarios, individuals can participate in group engagement, defined as the process through which group members engage with each other around exercise tasks to develop and sustain a mutual focus of attention on exercise (Metiu & Rothbard, 2013). For example, individuals can encourage each other and exchange useful information when exercising together (Xie et al., 2020). As a result, exercise is often a grouprelated phenomenon, necessitating research examining the effects of fitness technologies at the group level.

However, while existing studies have examined how fitness technology promotes individuals’ exercise activities (Jin et al., 2022), few studies have explored the role of fitness technology in facilitating group exercise. We argue that such an investigation is critical for providing two unique and important insights. First, from a methodological perspective, groups have unique attributes, distinct from an aggregation of individual idiosyncrasies. Therefore, examining group-related phenomena by merely focusing on individuals cannot fully reflect what occurs at the group level (Fisher & Ellis 1990). Specifically, interactions among members are vital for group-related phenomena; hence, overlooking these interactions implies a failure to recognize the importance of “we-ness” in groups (Fishe & Ellis 1990). From this epistemological standpoint, we posit that since exercise is often conducted in groups, examining fitness apps from a group perspective will provide a unique perspective on their effectiveness and on the social aspects of exercise. Indeed, Tu et al. (2019) discovered that apps focusing on social activities perform better than apps focusing on fun, with respect to increasing individuals’ exercise levels. Therefore, instead of focusing solely on how fitness app features can improve individual participation in exercise, it is also important to examine how fitness app features can support group participation in exercise more effectively. The literature also suggests that aggregating members to examine group-related phenomena can produce inaccurate interpretations (Sarker & Valacich, 2010). Second, from a practical standpoint, examining exercise at the group level can reveal novel insights. Although some individuals might increase their exercise levels as a result of using fitness apps, others may remain inactive. For fitness app providers, it is more important to expand the number of active users than to increase the levels of exercise from active users. Thus, examining fitness apps at the group level will help uncover how fitness technologies can support more users in engaging in exercise by evaluating the role of social interactions in such a process.

To advance this line of research, and as a first step toward closing the previously mentioned research gap, we explored the impact of fitness apps on group exercise participation. In essence, the aim of our study was to investigate how features that encourage casual social interactions can support group members in engaging in exercise assisted by fitness technology. To achieve this objective, we integrated the perspective of psychological distance (Trope & Liberman, 2010) and relational cohesion theory (Lawler et al., 2000; Lawler & Yoon, 1996), focusing on running apps as the research context. Running apps were selected as the context for two reasons. First, running is a common form of exercise that requires minimal equipment (Hindley, 2020). Second, under pandemic conditions (such as COVID-19), people were subjected to stressors and governmental preventive measures (Prentice et al., 2020). As a result, they were more inclined to resort to low-impact workouts (such as running) to avoid going to gyms (Phaneuf, 2020).

From a methodological perspective, we employed a panel dataset from a prevalent Chinese running app and collected data from 151 running groups in Dalian over a period of 38 weeks to achieve scientific rigor and realism. In terms of market size, China is a leading country for running apps,<sup>1</sup> and we selected this specific running app because it is one of the most widely used in China.<sup>2</sup> The running app introduced a feature called “Running Spot” in November 2017.<sup>3</sup> We argue that the Running Spot feature can facilitate offline group engagement, and therefore conceptualize such a feature as offline group engagement facilitation (please refer to Section 2.3 for more detail). The introduction of Running Spot represents a quasi-experiment that divides running groups into two types of groups: those that run at the app-designated running spots and those thgat do not. Therefore, it is both interesting and important to investigate whether this app function improves group exercise. Thus, our specific research objective is to examine how offline group engagement facilitation (i.e., Running Spot) supports groups’ participation in running.

Further, from an epistemological perspective, our theory-driven empirical findings indicate that offline group engagement facilitation did promote group participation in running, and groups that ran at running spots designated by the feature demonstrated a higher rate of participation. Additionally, the effect of offline group engagement facilitation was strongest for small groups and groups moderately close to the spots. We thus make five important contributions to the evolving literature on fitness technology. First, our research is the first study to contextualize relational cohesion theory (Lawler et al., 2000; Lawler & Yoon, 1996) in the context of fitness technology-empowered running groups, thereby demonstrating its applicability to selforganized exercise groups conducting low interdependent tasks. Second, we integrate relational cohesion theory (Lawler et al., 2000; Lawler & Yoon, 1996) through the perspective of psychological distance (Trope & Liberman, 2010) to reveal how fitness technology features (i.e., Running Spot) that facilitate offline group engagement (i.e., casual social interactions) can encourage groups to participate in running. We posit that Running Spot reduced psychological distance and facilitated recurrent social interactions among members. Importantly, these interactions can increase participation in running. Our study adds to the body of knowledge by elucidating the theoretical mechanism underlying app features for encouraging casual social interactions. Third, our study contributes to the literature by clarifying the boundary conditions of relational cohesion theory. Specifically, we delineate the complexities of the positive impact of offline group engagement facilitation (i.e., Running Spot), focusing on group size and distance from designated running spots. Our empirical evidence suggests that the impact of offline group engagement facilitation is greater when groups are small and located moderately close to the designated running spots. Fourth, our study demonstrates how IT artifacts can specifically improve group fitness levels. Most previous studies have examined how IT influences exercise at the individual level (Jin et al., 2022). By comparison, our study contributes to the literature by providing further empirical evidence on how IT can facilitate exercise at the group level through examining the underlying idiosyncrasies of Running Spot. Further, by conceptualizing the Running Spot feature as offline group engagement facilitation, our study enriches the literature on group engagement by clarifying the crucial role of IT in facilitating this process. From a pragmatic perspective, mobile apps have a high churn rate, with 71% of users abandoning certain apps after only three months (Perro, 2018). Given the fierce rivalry between running apps (and fitness apps in general), our research may provide valuable guidance on how running apps can retain current users and support their workouts.

## 2 Literature Review and Research Context

## 2.1 Exercise and Fitness Technology

Running is a common form of exercise for improving overall health. Although individuals often run alone due to the low requirements for equipment (Hindley, 2020), research has shown that running can be viewed as a social activity (Shipway & Jones, 2007; Xie et al., 2020). Previous research has found that running in groups can help runners create a sense of belonging and generate positive social experiences in a variety of settings, including running clubs and self-organized running groups (e.g., Robinson et al., 2014; Xie et al., 2020). According to Hindley (2020), runners often view park running as a shared group activity that can create a sense of belonging through casual social interactions with other runners. Moreover, Xie et al. (2020) demonstrated that members of self-organized groups can provide social support by inspiring others, recognizing achievements, and exchanging running experiences. According to self-determination theory (SDT), individuals have a need for relatedness (i.e., the ability to build and sustain a sense of shared care with others) (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Individuals crave social and communal experiences to satisfy their need for relatedness, and activities such as social interactions before, during, and after running can offer such opportunities (Arai & Pedlar, 2003). As a result, runners place a high value on social and communal events.

Advances in IT have altered how people deliver care and manage health (Goh et al., 2021; Payton et al., 2011). Recent research has started to investigate how IT can help support exercise in general and running in particular (see Table 1). Some studies have focused on individuals’ adoption intentions. For example, Beldad and Hegner (2018) examined the intention to continue using fitness apps and discovered that perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and injunctive social norms can predict individuals’ intentions. In another study, Dam et al. (2018) revealed that internal competition orientations and gratifications, exercise self-efficacy, social utility motives, and attitudes can increase individuals’ willingness to use fitness apps. Other research has examined the use of fitness technologies. According to James et al. (2019a; 2019b), different types of motives are linked to different approaches to using fitness technology’s feature sets. Finally, previous studies have also assessed the outcomes of fitness apps. For example, Dallinga et al. (2015) found that app users increased their runningrelated physical activities, their perceived health and lifestyle, and their self-image, compared to non-app users. More recently, Tu et al. (2019) discovered that socially focused applications can improve individuals’ exercise performance and their intention to continue exercising with the app.

Despite the significant advances in furthering our understanding of fitness technology made by these studies, two significant epistemological gaps remain. First, few studies have examined exercise, in general, and running, in particular, at the group level. Because running can be a social activity, studying running at the group level will help us understand how it can be leveraged and supported. Second, few studies have assessed how fitness apps can facilitate group exercise by supporting group engagement among runners. Although Tu et al. (2019) found that apps with a social focus can help people exercise more effectively, it is unclear how and why specific features of fitness apps are more effective at facilitating group engagement among individuals.

To fill these two crucial research gaps, we selected a running app as the context and investigated how Running Spot influenced groups’ participation in running by facilitating offline group engagement. In the following sections, we first present the notion of group engagement and then describe our research context in more detail.

## 2.2 Group Engagement

Group engagement refers to the process through which members engage with each other around certain tasks to develop and sustain a mutual focus of attention (Metiu & Rothbard, 2013). In the context of group running, these types of tasks deal with members running activities such that members can engage with each other by providing support and helpful suggestions before, during, and after running (Arai & Pedlar, 2003; Xie et al., 2020). Group engagement is a complicated phenomenon per se, because it can be planned or casual, online or offline, and encompass numerous varieties.

First, regarding planned vs. casual group engagement, Sessions (2010) examined how offline gatherings (meetups) influence members’ relationship development in the context of an online community. Specifically, members go to prearranged locations to meet others according to predetermined schedules. In essence, such meetups signify planned group engagement. Members can also engage with each other casually. Group members, for instance, may regularly exercise in specific locations (e.g., large parks) (Hindley, 2020). In such a scenario, members may anticipate seeing others in these locations at particular times and forgo prearranged meetups.

Second, group engagement can transpire online or offline. In the context of online communities, members can interact with each other through both the community (online engagement) and meetups (offline meetings) (Sessions, 2010). In another relevant study, Metiu and Rothbard (2013) examined how members of project teams engage with each other offline or online during the course of software development. Their findings shed light on what individuals do when they perform the group’s focal work, as well as how they establish and sustain the mutual focus of attention needed for moving forward as a group on the task itself.

Third, there are different types of group engagement. For example, Macgowan and Newman (2005) conceptualized group engagement as a multidimensional construct including attendance (members attend group sessions), contributing (members actively participate in group activities), relating to words (members support leaders), relating with members (members interact with others), contracting (members support the norms of the group), working on one’s own problems (members spent efforts solving problems), and working on other members’ problems (members help others complete their goals).

## 2.3 Research Context

The chosen running app is one of the most prevalent fitness apps in China and shares a number of features with other running apps, such as the ability to set goals and track performance, as well as form and join running groups. Members often use the running app to learn about the running activities of other members or to arrange offline activities (such as picnics). On the other hand, this running app has underlying idiosyncrasies not available in other running apps. For instance, in November 2017, it debuted a new feature called “Running Spot” (see Figure 1), which designates running spots with the aim of inspiring more users to participate in group running activities. Running spots are typically identified based on users’ track record of previous running activities and are located in vast and popular running areas (e.g., large parks). Therefore, many users may have already run at these locations prior to the launch of Running Spot. The Running Spot feature allows group members to see where other members of their group are running, which was not possible before the feature was introduced. Group running and any subsequent social interactions are casual rather than organized events. As articulated below, we believe that novel app features such as this can facilitate offline group engagement.

First, in the context of fitness technology (e.g., running apps), group engagement can occur online or offline. For example, group members can use running apps to learn about others’ running activities and to exchange running experiences. These activities deal with online group engagement. Group members can also use running apps to organize offline activities and engage in social interactions. These activities focus on offline group engagement.

By using the Running Spot feature, members can easily recognize others running in the same spots and are thus more likely to engage in casual social interactions.<sup>4</sup> As a result, Running Spot can facilitate offline group engagement. For example, group members can motivate others during running activities and subsequently recognize their achievements (Xie et al., 2020). In these scenarios, members can receive emotional support and enjoy running within groups.

Second, group engagement is a multidimensional concept (Macgowan & Newman, 2005). In this study, we do not imply that Running Spot can facilitate all dimensions of group engagement. Instead, we focus on the aspect of relating to members, which deals with how Running Spot can facilitate casual interactions between members. According to Macgowan and Newman (2005), relating with members concerns members’ interrelationships, such as social interactions and encouragement. Relating with members does not necessarily require individuals to interact with every other member. In other words, group engagement (i.e., relating to members) can occur when a subset of members interacts with each other. This is also consistent with the scenario of Running Spot, where only a few members of a group be running at the same spot at a given time.

To summarize, we contend that since the Running Spot feature allows members to see where other members are running, this increases the likelihood of social interactions among members. Such social interactions are typically casual rather than planned. Therefore, we conceptualize Running Spot as offline group engagement facilitation, as the feature reflects the running app’s technological idiosyncrasies that facilitate offline group engagement. In the next section, we describe the theoretical underpinnings upon which our hypotheses are built.

## 3 Theory and Hypothesis Development

Our theoretical framework is firmly grounded using two well-established theoretical lenses: the perspective of psychological distance (Trope & Liberman, 2010) and relational cohesion theory (Lawler et al., 2000; Lawler & Yoon, 1996). By integrating these theoretical underpinnings, the epistemological progression of social interactions stemming from offline group engagement facilitation and cohesiveness with regard to commitment behaviors can be elucidated in a unified manner. The simplified research model is presented in Figure 2 (see the Appendix for the full framework). Essentially, the perspective of psychological distance can explain how offline group engagement facilitation (i.e., Running Spots) can influence the psychological distance between members and generate important outcomes (e.g., social interactions). Relational cohesion theory can then explain how these outcomes result in members’ participation in running through different cognitive processes. We describe our theoretical foundations in more depth in the following sections.

## 3.1 Psychosocial Distance

Psychological distance is defined as a “subjective experience that something is close [to] or far away from the self, here, and now” (Trope & Liberman 2010, p. 440). In other words, it represents how far individuals are away from a reference point with regard to objective distance (e.g., space, time, and/or social distance). Therefore, psychological distance is the product of objective distance (Wilson et al., 2013). Psychological distance has been widely applied to understand members’ perceptions toward others in distributed teams (Eisenberg & DiTomaso, 2021; Wilson et al., 2013). Therefore, it is conceptually appropriate to apply psychological distance in the context of running groups in which members may not be running at the same location at the same time.

The perspective of psychological distance is essential for understanding the effects of Running Spot. Specifically, Running Spot allows group members to engage with each other while running. Running Spot can thus change the physical and temporal distance (i.e., objective distance) between members. Members’ psychological distance from each other can then be influenced by changes in the physical distance between them (Trope & Liberman 2010). As outlined by Eisenberg and Ditomaso (2021), shortened psychological distance can generate important outcomes, such as informal communication. In the context of running groups, informal communication depicts members’ social interactions. Unlike planned meetups, these social interactions may be casual and can arise during or after running, such as by exchanging running experiences.

In summary, psychological distance can be employed to gain a further understanding of how offline group engagement facilitation (i.e., Running Spot) can foster social interactions among members. However, it does not account for the outcomes of social interactions. In the following section, we draw upon relational cohesion theory to further explain how social interactions can facilitate participation in running.

## 3.2 Relational Cohesion Theory

Relational cohesion theory (Lawler et al., 2000; Lawler & Yoon, 1996) was formulated to understand how individuals within a group interact and to delineate the repercussions of such interactions. One of its primary concepts is relational cohesion (i.e., a sense of coming together/belonging), and the literature has demonstrated that group cohesion is a crucial aspect of adherence to an exercise regime (Spink & Carron, 1992). More importantly, relational cohesion theory recognizes the vital role of social interactions (Lawler et al., 2000; Lawler & Yoon, 1996). This is consistent with the literature, which indicates that interactions among members are vital for group-related phenomena to occur (Fisher & Ellis 1990). Therefore, following the perspective of psychological distance, it is epistemologically appropriate to employ and further contextualize relational cohesion theory to examine running apps at the group level.

Relational cohesion theory expounds on how group commitment behavior is sustained. Here, commitment refers to the strength of ties between members and the group (Thye et al., 2002). There are different types of commitment behaviors, and we focus on collaborating with others to develop joint goods (Thye et al., 2002). In the context of group running, members need to collaborate to increase groups’ participation in running. Therefore, groups’ participation in running can be considered a joint good created collaboratively by group members.

According to relational cohesion theory, repeated social interactions between members can result in participating in running through two distinct processes. First, through the social bonding process, repeated social interactions can result in positive emotions (Thye et al., 2002). Emotion refers to a relatively shortlived positive or negative evaluation state (Damasio, 1999). Over time, individuals are likely to attribute social-interaction-derived positive emotions to the relationships themselves, and such positive emotions can thus create “relational cohesion.” In this sense, relational cohesion eventually leads to commitment behaviors such as participating in running.

Second, through the boundary-defining process, repeated social interactions can reduce uncertainty (Thye et al., 2002). Specifically, through repeated social interactions, members become more familiar with each other and perceive the activities of others to be more predictable. As a result of this process, a sense of distinctiveness develops. In other words, group members distinguish themselves from other groups, since members can now predict the behaviors of other members of the group. Uncertainty reduction can then support participation in running, which constitutes a specific type of commitment behavior.

In general, relational cohesion theory explains how an interaction-to-emotion process leads to individuals’ social commitment to groups (Lawler et al., 2000; Lawler & Yoon, 1996). The underlying assumption is that individuals desire exchanges (i.e., social interactions) to maximize their gains (Thye et al., 2014). This is consistent with SDT, which asserts that individuals have a need for relatedness (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Thus, relational cohesion theory links the exogenous structural conditions of interdependence with an endogenous affective process that ultimately leads to commitment (Thye et al., 2014, p. 105).

Although relational cohesion theory was originally developed to investigate dyadic structures (Lawler & Yoon, 1996), subsequent studies have extended the theory to larger groups and networks (Lawler et al., 2000; Willer, 1999) in various contexts, such as organizations (Yoon & Thye, 2002). Previous research has also used relational cohesion theory to examine online relationships in the contexts of social media (Huang et al., 2018) and online gaming (Liao et al., 2020). The underlying argument is that relational cohesion at the dyadic level can also occur in complete networks if the network (1) has a high proportion of equal-power relationships, and (2) allows members to interact with most other members (Thye et al., 2014). In relational cohesion theory, power does not refer to members’ abilities or status. Rather, it refers to how dependent they are on others to achieve the desired outcomes (Thye et al., 2002). For example, when Members A and B depend equally on each other to attain valued outcomes, their mutual dependency is higher (i.e., equal power). As a result of their relational cohesion, individuals perceive the network as a group.

## 3.3 Hypothesis Development

In this section, we develop our hypotheses regarding the effect of online group engagement facilitation (i.e., Running Spot). Embracing the perspective of psychological distance and relational cohesion theory, we argue that online group engagement facilitation (i.e., Running Spot) decreases physical and temporal distance, which can in turn reduce the psychological distance between members. Reduced psychological distance then facilitates offline group engagement (i.e., social interactions between members), ultimately increasing participation in running.

Further, we posit that such an effect can be moderated by group size and the distance between designated running spots and member locations. First, group size can influence the social distance between members, influencing their psychological distance. Second, physical distance, or the distance between running spots and members’ locations can also impact their psychological distance. Therefore, group size and distance between running spots and member locations can help clarify the boundary conditions of online group engagement facilitation (i.e., Running Spot) and further explain how it might influence psychological distance in other contexts. In the following section, we explain our hypotheses in greater detail.

## 3.3.1 Offline Group Engagement Facilitation (i.e., Running Spot) and Groups’ Participation in Running

Building on the previous discussions, the perspective of psychological distance (Trope & Liberman 2010) and relational cohesion theory (Lawler et al., 2000; Lawler & Yoon, 1996) can be useful in explaining the effects that offline group engagement facilitation (e.g., Running Spot) can have on groups’ participation in running. Running groups formed through running apps are self-organized, meaning they often lack strict hierarchical structures, and relationships within such groups often have equal power. As mentioned previously, power in relational cohesion theory does not refer to the abilities of members (e.g., stronger running capabilities) or their status (e.g., the team leader). Rather, it refers to whether members rely on each other to achieve desirable outcomes (i.e., participating in running) (Thye et al., 2002). In the context of running groups, Members A and B are equally reliant on each other to fulfill their goal of participating in running. <sup>5</sup> Therefore, their relative powers are equal, and this type of structural power can facilitate relational cohesion.

On the other hand, members of running groups may join via running apps and may not know each other. Thus, social interactions may not occur among members, thereby hindering relational cohesion. Although running apps allow members to organize offline activities, such activities are limited for three reasons. First, individuals may be hesitant to participate in offline activities when the majority of those present are strangers. Second, those who participate in offline activities may prefer to interact primarily with those who participate in offline activities, which is not beneficial to the entire running group (Sessions, 2010). Third, individuals must interact in a predetermined way or at a specific time.

Features such as Running Spot can further help facilitate offline group engagement. When members run at designated running spots, they can use running apps to see who else in the same group is running at the same spot at the same time. As discussed previously, the psychological distance between individuals can be influenced by objective distances, such as physical, temporal, or social distances (Trope et al., 2007). Therefore, group members can minimize their physical and temporal distance by running together at the same spots, reducing their psychological distance (Trope et al., 2007). Reduced psychological distance can subsequently promote social interactions among members (Eisenberg & DiTomaso, 2021), which can result in participating in running following two processes based on relational cohesion theory (Thye et al., 2002).

With regard to the social bonding process, members of a running group will be likely to develop a sense of closeness with other members through repeated social interactions (Eisenberg & DiTomaso, 2021). As a result, Running Spot can create shared places and times for running, and members are more likely to engage in repeated social interactions. Such interactions occur naturally as a result of shared running interests, and they can be open and candid (Xie et al., 2020). Members can also provide each other with varied types of support, such as encouragement, recognition of achievements, and the sharing of running experiences (Xie et al., 2020). According to relational cohesion theory (Lawler et al., 2000; Lawler & Yoon, 1996), such repeated social interactions among members can result in positive emotional and relational cohesion, which can result in social commitment. Accordingly, members can feel a sense of belonging to the running group (Hindley, 2020). In such cases, members are likely to remain in the group (Thye et al., 2014) and actively participate in running in the future. As a result, groups’ participation in running is expected to increase.

Further, through the boundary-defining process, members can also reduce uncertainty through repeated social interactions (Thye et al., 2002). Specifically, through repeated social interactions, members can become more familiar with others, rendering their activities more predictable. Such a process allows members of a running group to become distinct from other running groups. As a result, members are more likely to participate in running, especially with other members of the group. This process is also in line with social exchange theory, which suggests that reducing uncertainty is a pathway to generating commitment (Kollock, 1998). Moreover, repeated exchanges can lead to commitment due to uncertainty reduction because individuals tend to avoid uncertainty. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H1: Offline group engagement facilitation (i.e., Running Spot) increases group participation in running.

## 3.3.2 The Moderating Effect of Group Size

The effect of offline group engagement facilitation (i.e., Running Spot) can vary according to group size. When the size of a group increases, members are increasingly likely to have diverse backgrounds and to be dissimilar from each other, thereby increasing social distance. This increased social distance can enhance psychological distance (Trope et al., 2007), meaning that members are less likely to develop a sense of closeness with others (Eisenberg & DiTomaso, 2021). In such scenarios, members are less likely to engage in repeated social interactions, even when they see each other at designated running spots. Moreover, according to relational cohesion theory, relational cohesion is more likely to occur when members interact easily with the majority of other members (Thye et al., 2014). Following this logic, the positive effect of offline group engagement facilitation will probably be stronger in smaller groups because members in smaller groups are more likely to interact with most of the other members. Specifically, with the help of Running Spot, members can more easily identify most of the other members at designated running spots. With the reduced objective distance, members will have a reduced psychological distance between them (Wilson et al., 2013). Therefore, members are more likely to interact with most others in the group, leading to enhanced participation in running. By comparison, when there are many group members, It can be difficult for the majority of members to appear at designated running spots at the same time. In these situations, members might only interact with a subset of their peers, impeding the development of relational cohesion. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H2: The positive effect of offline group engagement facilitation (i.e., Running Spot) is stronger when the group size is smaller.

## 3.3.3 The Moderating Effect of Distance

Individuals are more likely to join a running group that is geographically located near where they live or work. The effect of offline group engagement facilitation depends on the distance between designated running spots and member locations for two reasons. First, long distances between running spots and member locations can increase the physical distance between group members. <sup>6</sup> This physical distance can increase psychological distance (Trope et al., 2007), resulting in a lower level of social interaction between members (Eisenberg & DiTomaso, 2021). Reduced social interactions can cause a decrease in closeness between members (i.e., members of groups using running spots) (Eisenberg & DiTomaso, 2021). As a result, members are less likely to engage in repeated social interactions with those running at the same spots. Second, due to the costs involved to travel to designated running spots, running groups in close proximity to a running spot are more susceptible to its effects compared to groups far away. Specifically, as the distance between running spots and member locations increases, it takes longer and costs more to reach running spots. Therefore, individuals may feel reluctant to travel to running spots far from their locations. Accordingly, we hypothesize the following:

H3: The positive effect of offline group engagement facilitation (i.e., Running Spot) is stronger when the distance between running spots and member locations is shorter.

## 4 Research Methods

## 4.1 Setting

The app from which we collected data is one of the most prevalent fitness apps in China, with more than 100 million users and more than 200 thousand running groups in hundreds of Chinese cities as of 2018. The app allows individuals to track their workouts and socialize with other runners by following them, finding runners nearby, and organizing (or joining) running groups. In a running group, members can contribute to their group by running longer distances. A leaderboard ranks running groups according to their total running distance. Similar to MeetUp.com, the app allows members to organize and promote offline events (e.g., parties and picnics) to strengthen social connections within the group. The app has technological idiosyncrasies that allow the tracking of members’ running activities. We found that members’ running activities were quite different by viewing their track records, indicating that members did not usually exercise together.

Our quasi-experiment started around November 2017 (the 15th week of data collection), when the app introduced a new feature called “Running Spot.” The intention of this feature is to incentivize users to participate in running. When runners use the app and run at a designated running spot, they can see whether running group members are running at the same spot. Accordingly, this feature divides running groups into those who run at certain spots and those who do not.

We selected this particular running app for our study for three main reasons. First, it is among the most popular fitness apps in China. Therefore, feature changes can be influential, and it is relevant to understand the implications of such changes. Second, the quasi-experiment occurred when the Running Spot feature was introduced, allowing us to examine whether Running Spot facilitated groups’ participation in running. Third, the app is a fitness app that also supports social interactions among group members. Thus, the platform enables users to investigate the offline activities of groups.

## 4.2 Data

Panel data were collected from running groups in a major metropolitan city (Dalian) in China. All running groups were from the same city to control for differences in weather conditions. Data were collected weekly at the group level. The data collection period lasted 38 weeks, beginning in August 2017 and ending in April 2018. Subsequently, the app significantly changed its approach to reporting certain data, such as group rankings. As a result, data collection was curtailed to ensure that the operationalization of the variables was consistent.

The Running Spot feature was introduced in the 15th week of our study and had an exogenous effect on group performance and offline activities. Specifically, the app designated four running spots in the city. We defined the pretreatment stage as the period from Week 1 (starting in August 2017) to Week 14 (before Running Spot was introduced), while the posttreatment stage as the period from Week 15 (i.e., the introduction of Running Spot) to Week 38 (ending in April 2018). In total, we targeteed 151 running groups that existed prior to August 2017 and tracked their activities.<sup>7</sup>

Following the introduction of Running Spot, each designated running spot had a list of running groups that ran at that spot during a specific week. Using these lists, we created our treatment (i.e., spot groups) and control groups (i.e., non-spot groups). Spot groups were identified as groups that ran at certain spots at least once, while non-spot groups were those that never ran at any spot (i.e., did not appear on the spot list). Of the 151 running groups, 76 were spot groups, and 75 were non-spot groups.

## 5.2.1 Assessing the Self-Selection Issue

Because the groups self-selected into spot groups versus non-spot groups, the different levels of participation in running after the introduction of Running Spot could have been due to differences between the two groups. Table 5 shows that the observed characteristics of the spot and non-spot groups before the introduction of Running Spot were statistically different.

Because of the pretreatment differences between the spot and non-spot groups, we employed a propensity score matching (PSM) method to reduce potential differences across spot and non-spot running groups by identifying control groups similar to the treatment groups with regard to their observed characteristics. We calculated the propensity score using logit regression with a set of observed characteristics as covariates that might influence groups’ participation in running at the designated spots.

These characteristics included tenure, group size, rank, activeness, number of pictures in offline activities, and distance to the nearest designated running spot before Week 15. We matched spot and non-spot groups using a one-to-one nearest neighbor matching method without replacement. As illustrated in Table 5, the PSM procedure produced a matched sample of 39 spot groups and 39 nonspot groups with similar observed pretreatment characteristics because the results of the t-test were not significant. Next, we reran the DID models on the matched samples without time-varying treatment (Model A3) and with (Model A4). Models A3 and A4 also clustered standard errors by group. ## 5.5 Dynamics of the Treatment Effects

It is reasonable to expect that running groups would learn about Running Spot over time. Moreover, the presence of other group members and social interactions at one designated running spot may have helped increase the effectiveness of that spot over time. To identify such effects, we examined the dynamics of the treatment effects. We generated a sequence of dummy variables $( A f t e r _ { i t } ^ { 1 } , A f t e r _ { i t } ^ { 2 } , A f t e r _ { i t } ^ { 3 }$ , and $A f t e r _ { i t } ^ { 4 } )$ that corresponded to six-week intervals in the post-treatment period. We conducted this check by including the interaction terms of the time dummies, as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & L o g A c t i v e n e s s _ {i t} = \alpha S p o t G r o u p _ {i} + \\ & \beta A f t e r _ {i t} + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {4} \delta_ {k} S p o t G r o u p _ {i} \times A f t e r _ {i t} ^ {k} + \\ & \Omega^ {\prime} X _ {i t} + \mu_ {i} + \tau_ {t} + e _ {i t} \end{array}\tag{A7}
$$

This specification allowed us to identify whether the effect that Running Spot had on activeness increased, decreased, or remained the same over time. The estimates presented in Table 7 (Model A7) represent the average treatment effects over the different posttreatment periods. The effect of the average treatment was significant over the short term, and it became more significant from the start of the second six-week period. However, the effect decreased significantly in the long term. In other words, when Running Spot was first introduced, the running groups needed time to learn about the feature. Hence, the Running Spot did not have a large early effect on the activeness of running groups. Subsequently, after some running groups had become familiar with Running Spot, they started to run at the designated spots, and activeness was enhanced. However, in the long term, groups running at the designated spots stabilized, and the enhancing effect decreased.

All the designated running spots were located in a core urban area. ## 6 Discussion

Fitness apps use various approaches to encourage individuals to exercise. Our study focused on the effect of offline group engagement facilitation (e.g., Running Spot) on groups’ participation in running, in the context of a running app. Embracing the perspective of psychological distance (Trope & Liberman, 2010) and relational cohesion theory (Lawler et al., 2000; Lawler & Yoon, 1996), we argue that Running Spot facilitates individuals’ engagement in repeated social interactions with other group members. Such repeated social interactions can then generate positive emotional/relational cohesion and uncertainty reduction, which will ultimately lead to participating in running. In our study, we used panel data from a quasiexperiment and conducted analyses using the DID and PSM approaches. Our results support the positive effect of offline group engagement facilitation. Specifically, the activeness of groups who ran at the designated running spots was higher than that of the groups who did not. This effect remained robust when the panel data were analyzed as a time-varying treatment and when the spot and non-spot groups were matched using the PSM approach.

## 6.1 Theoretical Implications

This study makes five important epistemological contributions to the emerging fitness technology literature, in general, and relational cohesion theory in particular. First, our study advances the literature by revealing how relational cohesion theory can be employed to improve team performance on tasks with relatively low interdependence. The notion of relational cohesion and relational cohesion theory in the context of organizational teams have been epitomized in the literature (Forsyth, 2021; Thye et al., 2014), and related research has shown that the effect of relational cohesion is stronger with high task interdependency (i.e., the degree of shared responsibility between members) (Thye et al., 2014). Diverging from prior studies, our research focuses on running as the research setting, where running groups are self-organized exercise teams. In this context, task interdependency is relatively low because members can accomplish running tasks independently of one another. By contextualizing relational cohesion theory in the context of running, our study discovers that relational cohesion can still play an important role in teams performing low-interdependence tasks.

Second, our study further extends relational cohesion theory by illuminating how IT can increase recurring interactions. Although relational cohesion theory claims that repeated interactions can increase relational cohesion, it does not explain how to increase repeated interactions (Thye et al., 2014). Our study addresses this lack by integrating the perspective of psychological distance with relational cohesion theory to elucidate how IT can facilitate repeated interactions. We contend that the technological idiosyncrasies of features such as Running Spot (conceptualized as offline group engagement facilitation) can reduce psychological distance among group members and hence facilitate social interactions (i.e., offline group engagement). Specifically, running together at app-designated running spots can reduce members’ temporal and spatial distance and ultimately their psychological distance (Trope et al., 2007), which can help members develop a sense of closeness (Eisenberg & DiTomaso, 2021). Based on relational cohesion theory, we argue that features like Running Spot can facilitate repeated social interactions among group members. Ultimately, this can support group participation in running through the social bonding process (via positive emotional and relational cohesion) and the boundary-defining process (via uncertainty reduction) (Lawler et al., 2000; Lawler & Yoon, 1996). Overall, our study revealed that offline group engagement facilitation (i.e., Running Spot) had a positive effect on group participation in running. Our study also contributes to the literature on fitness technology by explaining the mechanism through which offline group engagement facilitation (e.g., Running Spot) can enhance groups’ participation in running.

Although our study focuses on running groups, the results could also be extrapolated to groups in other research contexts. For example, Metiu and Rothbard (2013) found that online engagement may not be as effective as offline engagement in developing a reciprocal focus of attention on software development projects. This is consistent with our research, which demonstrates that an app that facilitates offline group engagement significantly improved group running participation. Further, one of our primary theoretical pillars, the perspective of psychological distance, has been extensively applied in virtual teams in organizations (Eisenberg and DiTomaso, 2021). Therefore, although our study was conducted in the context of running groups that were self-organized, its results have important ramifications for virtual teams in organizations. Specifically, we contend that when virtual team members’ psychological distance is reduced, they are more likely to engage with each other, which ultimately results in improved collaborations.

Third, our study contributes to the literature by describing the complex effects that offline group engagement facilitation can have on individuals’ participation in running. We also analyzed the moderating effects of group size and group proximity to designated running spots. First, we found that offline group engagement facilitation had a stronger effect on groups’ participation in running when the groups were smaller. Group members generally find it easier to interact with most other members in smaller groups. According to relational cohesion theory, relational cohesion is more likely to occur in such scenarios (Thye et al., 2014).

Next, the positive effect of offline group engagement facilitation was greater when the groups were moderately close (i.e., not too close and not too far) to designated running spots. It is possible that groups far away from designated running spots found it difficult to take advantage of them and that members in such groups perceived greater physical distance, resulting in greater psychological distance between members (Trope et al., 2007). Further, due to the cost of traveling to these spots, the Running Spot function likely did not improve running participation for groups geographically distant from the designated running spots. Regarding groups that were located very close to designated running spots, the small physical distance may have correlated with a small psychological distance, ultimately leading to a smaller effect of Running Spot for those groups.

Importantly, these empirical findings clarify our understanding of boundary conditions of relational cohesion theory. In essence, the literature has identified different types of factors influencing relational cohesion in the presence of interaction (Forsyth, 2021). By applying and further contextualizing relational cohesion theory to the context of self-organized running groups, our study resonates with the literature by highlighting the paramount role of group structure (i.e., group size) on the one hand, and enriches and extends the literature by distinguishing the substantial significance of contextual features (i.e., the distance from designated running spots) on the other.

Furthermore, the aforementioned discussions of group size and physical distance pertain to offline casual group engagement; we do not imply that they have similar effects on other types of offline or online group engagement. In regard to group size, we argue that members of smaller groups are more amenable to engaging with each other casually. Members of larger groups, on the other hand, may be more difficult to assemble offline. Therefore, they may be more likely to engage with each other online. Regarding physical distance, because it is more difficult for members to engage with each other and run together at far-away running spots, they may prearrange offline social activities (i.e., meetups) to conduct offline planned group engagement. In essence, it is plausible that pertinent factors (e.g., group size and physical distance) facilitate some types of group engagement while inhibiting others. We thus invite future researchers to further explore this issue.

We also examined the dynamic effects of the study’s treatment. Our results indicate that the positive effect of offline group engagement facilitation increased until reaching a maximum at 7-18 weeks after Running Spot was introduced. Subsequently, the positive effects subsided, although they were still significant. A possible explanation could be that after Running Spot was introduced, running groups needed time to learn about the feature and use it to support their social interactions with other members. Therefore, the effect of Running Spot increased with time. However, over time (e.g., 18 weeks after Running Spot was introduced), once members had become familiar with Running Spot, the feature exerted only a limited additional effect on groups’ participation in running.

Fourth, our study extends the literature on the effect of IT on fitness at the group level. While the literature has examined how IT influences exercise, these studies have only considered the individual level (Jin et al., 2022). Thus, there is a paucity of information about how IT can improve group fitness. We selected running as the research context and focused on the idiosyncrasies of the Running Spot feature. We discovered that although the activeness of the treatment and control groups did not differ significantly initially, the treatment groups had a higher level of activeness after Running Spot was introduced. In addition, for those groups moderately close to designated running spots, the activeness of treatment groups was also higher than that of control groups.

It is conceivable that the overall running patterns of many groups remained the same throughout our study and that many treatment groups had previously run at the spots before they were highlighted by Running Spot. In other words, members of the treatment groups may have been running at the designated spots both before and after the launch of the Running Spot feature for a number of reasons (e.g., habit, convenience). However, the treatment and control groups had similar levels of activeness before Running Spot was introduced. Conversely, treatment groups had a significantly higher level of activeness after the introduction of Running Spot, which allowed them to see where other group members were running. These results indicate that the running spots per se did not enhance groups’ participation in running and demonstrate that the idiosyncrasies of Running Spot (i.e., allowing group members to see where other members are running) indeed improved groups’ activeness. Overall, our study contributes to the literature by providing empirical evidence on how IT can facilitate exercise at the group level.

Finally, group engagement has been recognized as an important concept (Macgowan & Newman, 2005; Metiu & Rothbard, 2013). In essence, the literature has indicated that group engagement may occur both online and offline. For example, members of online communities can interact with others both online via communities and offline through meetups (Sessions, 2010). Similarly, team members can engage with others both offline and online while working on software development projects to develop a reciprocal focus of attention on these projects (Metiu and Rothbard, 2013). Although the existing literature has examined how online group engagement can be enhanced (Ray et al., 2014), there is a scarcity of research probing how to facilitate offline group engagement. To advance this strand of research, we conceptualized the Running Spot feature as offline group engagement facilitation and focused on the essential role of IT in facilitating offline group engagement in the context of exercise. Our study thus contributes to the literature on group engagement by clarifying how IT can facilitate offline group engagement. Further, by revealing how the technological idiosyncrasies of Running Spot enhanced groups’ running participation, our study sheds new light on how the use of IT (e.g., fitness technology) can influence individuals’ real-life behaviors. Since the treatment groups had a higher level of participation, members of these groups would have been more likely to continue utilizing the IT artifact (i.e., running app) and its inherent features to support their running. Therefore, our study can also inform how individuals’ real-life behaviors can influence their future IT usage. The marketing literature has demonstrated the value of omnichannel retailing (Verhoef et al., 2015); likewise, the IS literature can further investigate how IT can facilitate an omnichannel environment for individuals to engage with each other. For example, the focal running app in our study serves as an omnichannel environment where users can transition between online and offline interaction while engaging with other users both online via the running app and offline by running at designated running spots and participating in group meetups.

## 6.2 Practical Implications

Our study has important practical implications. For members of running groups, our findings emphasize the importance of facilitating repeated social interactions between group members, which can ultimately enhance their participation in running and improve their physical health (Xie et al., 2020). Therefore, members of running groups should take advantage of features such as Running Spot that support repeated social interactions between members, thereby increasing participation in running.

For providers of running apps, our study demonstrates that groups’ participation in running can be supported by social features, such as designated running spots. We also argue that running apps can facilitate offline group engagement among group members. Because the operation and survival of running apps depend on groups’ participation in running, running apps should implement additional features to facilitate offline group engagement. This can facilitate higher levels of repeated social interactions among members, improving participation in running. For example, providers could establish a ranking based on members’ activities at designated running spots. When several members run together, the running distance could be given more weight.

Our analyses also demonstrate that the introduction of Running Spot had a negative effect on offline meetups organized by running apps; thus providers should take measures to reduce this effect. For example, running apps could create virtual spaces in which groups can host online meetups. Running app providers could then collaborate with social media providers to integrate their apps. Then, when members, for example, engage in social activities after running together and share their activities on social media, these experiences could also be shared in the running apps’ virtual spaces for their groups.

## 6.3 Limitations and Opportunities for Future Studies

Our study has certain inevitable limitations. First, we only collected data from one running app. Although this app is popular in China, the results were not extrapolated to other running or fitness apps. Second, our groups were not randomly selected into spot and non-spot groups. Although we conducted PSM to alleviate the issue of selfselection, it remains a possibility that other hidden differences between spot and non-spot groups could have influenced participation in running and the use of Running Spot. Future studies are warranted to address this endogeneity issue by conducting randomized field experiments. Third, although we clarify the effects of Running Spot by integrating the perspective of psychological distance and relational cohesion theory, we did not collect group members’ psychological distance and social interactions. Future research is needed to collect these variables and fully examine the relationships proposed by relational cohesion theory. Future studies are also warranted to further validate these relationships in various contexts, such as software development. Finally, our study conceptualized Running Spot as offline group engagement facilitation, with an emphasis on casual interactions among members. It is plausible that Running Spot had different effects on other types of group engagement. Indeed, we found that Running Spot had negative effects on planned offline activities. We also acknowledge that additional features could facilitate different types of group engagement, and we encourage future studies to explore these differential effects further.

## 7 Conclusions

Because fitness apps have become very popular, practitioners are interested in how their features can support individuals’ exercise activities more effectively. Focusing on the context of running apps, our study examined the effect of offline group engagement facilitation (i.e., Running Spot) on groups’ participation in running. Our results indicate that Running Spot indeed increased groups’ participation in running. Our study also extends relational cohesion theory and reveals how groups participation in running can be impacted by app features like Running Spot. We suggest that running app providers implement additional features to further support repeated casual social interactions among their members.
