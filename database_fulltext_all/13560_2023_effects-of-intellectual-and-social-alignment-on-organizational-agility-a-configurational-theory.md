Hao Hu,<sup>1</sup> Nianxin Wang,<sup>2</sup> Huigang Liang<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Jiangsu University of Science and Technology, China, huhao\_just@163.com <sup>2</sup>Jiangsu University of Science and Technology, China, nianxin.wang@gmail.com <sup>3</sup>University of Memphis, USA, huigang.liang@gmail.com

## Abstract

Literature has shown that business-information technology (IT) alignment can exert both positive and negative influences on organizational agility, giving rise to the IT alignment-agility paradox. To better understand this paradox at a more granular level, we conceptualize the sensing and responding dimensions of organizational agility as two independent constructs and suggest a nonlinear analytical approach. Based on configurational and contextual perspectives, this study investigates how intellectual and social alignment and organizational and environmental elements combine into multiple configurations to affect sensing and responding capabilities. Fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) is used to analyze the survey data from 135 dyads of business and IT executives from the Chinese shipbuilding industry. Results show that different equifinal pathways can be used to achieve high sensing and responding capabilities, in which intellectual and social alignment play heterogeneous roles depending on the specific contexts. This study extends the ITenabled agility literature by deepening our understanding of the effects of multidimensional IT alignment on multidimensional organizational agility and providing new insights into the IT alignment-agility paradox.

Keywords: IT Alignment, Intellectual Alignment, Social Alignment, Organizational Agility, Sensing, Responding, Configuration, Fuzzy-Set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA)

## 1 Introduction

In today’s dynamic business environment characterized by competitors’ new strategic moves, customer preference changes, and the emergence of new technologies, firms have invested heavily in information technology (IT) resources to cultivate and improve their agility in sensing and responding to market opportunities and threats (Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Tallon et al., 2019). Agility, defined as a type of dynamic organizational capability that can quickly sense and respond to environmental changes and seize market opportunities in a timely manner (Sambamurthy et al., 2003), is a key competence for firms’ thriving and survival in hypercompetitive environments (Overby et al., 2006; Queiroz et al., 2018; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011).

Business-IT alignment (hereafter IT alignment) refers to the degree of fit and integration among business and IT strategies and structures (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993). Although information systems (IS) scholars (Chan et al., 2006; Preston & Karahanna, 2009) have shown that IT alignment can significantly enhance agility, promote adaptability to external changes, and enhance organizational performance (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), the tight alignment between IT and business strategies has also been found to impede organizational agility (Leonard-Barton, 1992; Liang et al., 2017).

To unravel this IT alignment-agility paradox (Zhou et al., 2018), one viable approach is to disaggregate the dimensions of IT alignment in order to assess its different impacts on agility. Drawing on the twodimensional view of IT alignment proposed by Reich and Benbasat (1996, 2000), Liang et al. (2017) found that intellectual alignment impedes agility through inertia, while social alignment facilitates agility through emergent coordination. Similarly, Zhou et al. (2018) argued that firms need to simultaneously pursue intellectual alignment and social alignment to achieve high agility, as either is insufficient or even detrimental to such achievement on its own. Another approach to reconciling this paradox is taking a contextual perspective, through which Zhou et al. (2017) found that in stable environments, intellectual alignment facilitates agility and social alignment has no significant effects on agility, while in dynamic environments, social alignment can enhance agility and intellectual alignment has no effect or a negative effect on agility.

Although these two approaches are helpful for understanding the alignment-agility paradox to some extent, little attention has been paid to the dimensions of agility and analytical approaches that we argue have the potential to further comprehend and manage this paradox at a more granular level. Park et al. (2017) posited the dimensions of agility can be differentially affected by business intelligence and communication technologies based on organizational and environmental contexts. If agility is treated as sensing and responding capabilities, different antecedents should thus be considered (Overby et al., 2006). Yet many studies on the IT alignment-agility relationship have viewed agility as a single aggregated construct (Liang et al., 2017; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011) without differentiating the agility dimensions. It is possible that IT alignment may impede one dimension of agility but enhance another (Leonard-Barton, 1992; Miller, 1992; Tallon, 2008) and thus cause mixed results.

The IT alignment-agility paradox can also be attributed to certain limitations of analytical approaches. Most research has presented the relationship between IT alignment and agility as linear and symmetrical:<sup>1</sup> i.e., the greater the IT alignment, the higher the agility, and vice versa (Bradley et al., 2012; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). But others have found that alignment between business and IT strategies is a nonlinear state (Sabherwal et al., 2001; Zhang et al.,

2021), making the true relationships between IT alignment and agility or other performance metrics asymmetrical or multidirectional. For example, scholars have found that a high level of IT alignment may not definitely lead to a high or low level of agility (Zhou et al., 2017) or organizational performance (Oh & Pinsonneault, 2007). In this regard, the linearity assumption cannot accurately reflect the nature of IT alignment and is also likely to mask its exact effect. A more comprehensive analytical approach that looks beyond simple linear effects is therefore necessary to address this limitation.

To fill these two research gaps, we seek to provide fresh theoretical and methodological insights into the complex relationships between IT alignment and agility. With the goal of offering a dimensional, configurational, and contextual lens to assess the effects of IT alignment, our research question is: How and under what conditions do the two dimensions of IT alignment influence the two dimensions of agility in a configurational manner?

The configurational theory approach (Fiss, 2011; Ragin, 2008) is used in this study to investigate how intellectual and social alignment and other contextual factors—including firm strategy, organizational size, and environmental dynamism—simultaneously combine into configurations to produce sensing and responding capabilities. As configurational theory focuses on combinations and synergies among multiple factors, it best applies to investigating the complex nonlinear and asymmetric relationships in this research (Maier et al., 2021; Misangyi et al., 2017). Specifically, we adopt fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) for survey data analysis (Fiss, 2011). Based on the configurational solutions, we further investigate the contingency effects of organizational and environmental elements on the IT alignment-agility relationship from a contextual perspective (Lee et al., 2015; Park et al., 2017; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), which also responds to the call for studies that explore boundary conditions for alignment that can enable or disable organizational agility (Tallon et al., 2019).

This study makes three key contributions to IS research. First, it extends the IT-enabled agility literature by examining the effects of IT alignment on the two dimensions of organizational agility. Prior studies on the alignment-agility relationship have mainly viewed agility as an aggregated construct and have not articulated or operationalized sensing and responding separately (Bradley et al., 2012; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). We could not find any other studies that empirically investigate how two types of IT alignment affect sensing and responding capabilities in different ways and contexts. This research not only enhances our understanding of IT alignment’s relationship with agility but can also guide firms in using IT alignment to effectively achieve the desired dimensions of agility.

Second, we adopt the configurational perspective and fsQCA approach to investigate the complex nonlinear relationship between IT alignment and organizational agility. Instead of investigating the independent linear net effects of IT alignment (Liang et al., 2017; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), we explore the combinatorial influences of intellectual and social alignment on sensing and responding capabilities in different contexts. Our findings suggest that intellectual and social alignment can produce sensing or responding in some configurations but may be irrelevant or even counterproductive in other configurations. This approach therefore confirms the nonlinear and asymmetric effects of IT alignment on agility, which complements and advances the traditional linear approaches in this research and provides new insights into the alignment-agility relationship.

Third, we extend the current alignment literature by explaining the alignment-agility paradox at a higher level of granularity. By distinguishing sensing and responding, we theoretically articulate and empirically demonstrate that intellectual and social alignment have different synergistic effects (complementary or substitution) and play multifaceted roles (enabling or inhibiting) in firms’ achievement of sensing capability in different contexts. In addition, we find that social alignment always improves responding capability, while intellectual alignment always impedes it or is irrelevant, with the one exception of a positive effect for large analyzers—versus prospectors and defenders (Miles & Snow, 1978)—in dynamic environments. By explicitly investigating the combinatorial impacts of intellectual and social alignment on sensing and responding capabilities, our study offers a better understanding of the alignment-agility paradox by showing the ways in which intellectual alignment and social alignment have heterogeneous effects on sensing and responding capabilities, and how they may only impede one dimension of agility while enhancing another, thus exhibiting various interactions according to specific contingencies.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we review the alignment-agility paradox, conceptualize the main constructs of the study, and explain our research model. In Section 3, we describe the details of our measurement development, data sample, research methodology, and fsQCA. In Section 4, we present the fsQCA results and interpret multiple configurations of sensing and responding capabilities in detail. With these findings as foundations, in Section 5, we propose theoretical propositions for the effects of intellectual and social alignment on sensing and responding capabilities from the contextual perspective. Finally, we discuss the theoretical and practical implications, as well as limitations and future research directions.

## 2 Theoretical Background and Research Model

## 2.1 Alignment-Agility Paradox

In the past three decades, IS scholars investigating whether IT alignment facilitates or impedes agility have proposed that both effects are exhibited (Bradley et al., 2012). This is known as the alignment-agility paradox (Zhou et al., 2018). Based on the knowledge and resource perspectives, Tallon and Pinsonneault (2011) systematically summarized the arguments that support both the positive and negative relationships between alignment and agility. From the perspective of knowledge, knowledge sharing between IT and business executives is an important factor in sensing environmental threats and opportunities and formulating responses (Preston & Karahanna, 2009; Reich & Benbasat, 1996). On the one hand, such knowledge exchange allows IT executives to detect critical changes in business domains and helps business executives to better understand the potential IT-enabled new business models. A close relationship between business and IT managers thus enables firms’ sensing and responding capabilities (Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011). On the other hand, knowledge sharing can foster myopia and motivate organizations to protect the status quo, which can potentially hurt their agility during sudden, frequent, or unexpected changes (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). Also, while shared knowledge between IT and business executives can build consensus, it can also create groupthink in decision-making, which can discourage the contributions of those who may be better able to assess the situation (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011).

From a resource standpoint, although firms with a high level of alignment have usually embedded IT resources into key business processes to facilitate their response to change (Tallon, 2008), this close alignment can also lead to resource commitments and rigidities that can impede agility (Liang et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2018). The alignment-induced exploitation of resources can foster IT capabilities and stimulate adaptation capability, whereas alignment-induced exploration of resources may motivate firms to create change by using resources in disruptive ways and enhancing their innovation capability. By deploying resources in such an ambidextrous manner, firms can not only create tight alignment but also enable agility (He & Wong, 2004). However, the literature has also shown that alignment-induced exploration and exploitation can result in competency traps that hurt responsiveness (Andriopoulos & Lewis, 2009; Gupta et al., 2006).

Recent studies have proposed two possible explanations for the IT alignment-agility paradox. One takes the dimensional view of IT alignment, which proposes that IT alignment can happen in both intellectual and social dimensions, and intellectual alignment and social alignment may play opposite roles in enabling agility. IS scholars have distinguished the effect of each dimension of IT alignment on organizational agility and found that intellectual alignment impedes agility by increasing organizational inertia, and social alignment enhances agility by enhancing emergent business-IT coordination (Liang et al., 2017). Also, the significant interaction effect of social alignment weakens the effect of intellectual alignment on inertia. Zhou et al. (2018) proposed that firms should pursue intellectual and social alignment at the same time to avoid declines in agility. These studies provide the dimensional and interaction insights associated with IT alignment needed to understand the alignment-agility paradox.

The second perspective to understand this paradox is to focus on the contextual effect in the alignmentagility relationship (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011; Zhou et al., 2017). Considering that IT alignment dimensions may have contrary effects on agility, researchers have investigated the boundary condition in this relationship. For example, Zhou et al. (2017) found that environmental dynamism has a negative moderating effect on the relationship between intellectual alignment and agility but a positive moderating effect on the relationship between social alignment and agility. They also adopted the configurational idea and suggested that in dynamic environments, intellectual alignment can enhance agility only when complemented by social alignment.

Collectively, as Figure 1 shows, the dimensions of IT alignment and the moderating effect in the alignmentagility relationship can both be scrutinized to possibly reconcile the alignment-agility paradox. However, a careful literature review in Appendix A has revealed that, as prior studies generally consider agility to be an aggregated construct and examine the effect of IT alignment on agility using a linear analytical approach, these two limitations suggest new ways to further manage the alignment-agility paradox.

First, in the IT alignment-agility literature, it has been found that IT alignment may have distinct effects on each dimension of agility (Leonard-Barton, 1992; Tallon, 2008). Also, as more fine-grained dimensions of agility can provide valuable theoretical and practical insights (Overby et al., 2006; Park et al., 2017), two key dimensions of sensing and responding should be treated differently. However, most studies in this field have considered agility as an aggregated construct (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011; Zhou et al., 2018) and drawn general conclusions. Thus, investigation of the alignment’s effects on sensing and responding capabilities may provide new insights into the alignment-agility paradox.

Second, while most studies have applied a linear and symmetrical approach (e.g., SEM) to examine IT alignment’s effects on agility, some scholars pointed out that IT alignment is a nonlinear state (Sabherwal et al., 2001; Saldanha et al., 2020), producing nonconstant and asymmetric impacts (Oh & Pinsonneault, 2007) that cannot be discerned by the traditional deductive correlational approach. Also, changing external business and technological environments and a variety of internal organizational contingencies (e.g., firm size and strategical orientation) make it more difficult to accurately predict the effects of IT alignment. Thus, in this light, an analytical approach that can fully capture the complex or even nonlinear and asymmetrical relationship between intellectual alignment, social alignment, and agility is required.

In addition, agility has been identified as a type of dynamic capability (Teece et al., 2016) as well as a complex and synthetical product affected by combined various factors such as IT competencies and organizational and environmental contingencies (Park et al., 2017). Although scholars have tried to apply a complex three-way interaction to investigate the effects of different dimensions of IT alignment under the boundary condition of environmental dynamism (Zhou et al., 2017), this approach becomes infeasible when considering more than three variables. The results might be too complicated to understand if we apply traditional analytical approaches, such as SEM and moderated regression. Therefore, collectively, a more comprehensive analytical approach to explore the complex interactions and nonlinear effects of different dimensions of IT alignment on different dimensions of agility in various contexts is warranted.

## 2.2 Dimensions of IT Alignment

The dimensional view of IT alignment has long been the focus of scholars. So far, two taxonomies in this context used extensively by scholars include the strategic alignment model of Henderson and Venkatraman (1993), which has six dimensions: business alignment, IT alignment, strategic alignment, operational alignment, and two cross-dimension alignments. The other model is Reich and Benbasat’s (1996, 2000) classification of intellectual and social alignment, which we use in this study for two reasons.

First, the latter two-dimensional alignment is theoretically concise and widely applied (Kearns & Lederer, 2003; Liang et al., 2017; Preston & Karahanna, 2009). In the process of creating alignment, the intellectual dimension concentrates on plan content, methodologies, and data, while the social dimension focuses on people, communication, and decisionmaking (Horovitz, 1984). This decomposition considers the value of intellectual-oriented strategy creation and actor-oriented informal interaction and knowledgesharing simultaneously. While achieving agility, human actors play a significant role in scanning and defining emergent threats and opportunities and decision-making (social alignment). Therefore, this classification that encompasses both strategy artifacts and human actors best suits our research contexts.

Second, the dimensional perspective helps to reconcile the conflicting findings regarding the relationship between IT alignment and agility in the literature (Liang et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2018). Notably, Liang et al.’s (2017) revelation that intellectual and social alignment play opposite roles in enabling agility suggests that intellectual-social decomposition is a promising direction and more research is needed to further explore its potential.

The original classification of Horovitz (1984) that divides the process of business strategy formulation and implementation into two key dimensions views the intellectual dimension as compassing special methodologies, techniques, and data used in this process, while the social dimension refers to the choice of actors, their degree of involvement, and methods of communication and decision-making. In applying this model (Horovitz, 1984), Reich and Benbasat (1996, 2000) described intellectual alignment as the state in which a high-quality set of interrelated IT and business strategies exists, while social alignment has been defined as the state in which business and IT executives within an organizational unit mutually understand and are jointly committed to each other’s mission, objectives, and plans.

As shown in Table B1 in the Appendix, the intellectual and social dimensions of IT alignment differ in focus, cause, and effect on organizational outcome (Horovitz, 1984; Reich & Benbasat, 2000). Intellectual alignment emphasizes the formal process involved in seeking the best way to formulate business and IT strategies, as well as the organizational arrangements that best suit the strategies. Social alignment involves the informal process of business and IT executives—their involvements, profile, and capability, as well as the CEO’s activating mode, which will heavily determine whether and how well the aligned business and IT strategies operate. Intellectual alignment implies that the methodologies for formulating and implementing business and IT strategies will determine the degree to which the set of IT and business missions, objectives, and strategies are internally consistent and externally valid. It affects the internal consistency and external effectiveness of the set of IT and business tasks, goals, and strategies. Social alignment describes the state in which business and IT executives understand and are committed to the organization and its IT mission, objectives, and plans (Reich & Benbasat, 2000), which helps them make better decisions and take better actions, preparing them for success. Social alignment also considers how factors such as choice of actor, timing, decision-making, and communication used in business and IT strategy formulation and implementation can lead to a shared understanding of the business and IT mission, objectives, and strategies of business and IT executives.

To enrich our understanding of the relationship between IT alignment and agility, different dimensions of IT alignment should be distinguished. Following Liang et al. (2017) and Zhou et al. (2018), we consider them simultaneously and further investigate how they affect different dimensions of organizational agility.

## 2.3 Dimensions of Organizational Agility

As detailed by Tallon et al. (2019), the definitions of organizational agility (aka enterprise agility, strategic agility) include the ability to detect and seize market opportunities, handle threats with speed and surprise (Sambamurthy et al., 2003), sense environmental change, and respond readily (Bradley et al., 2012). Although IS scholars have defined different characteristics and identified multiple dimensions of agility from different perspectives, a consistent theme is that agility reflects a symbiosis of sensing and responding capabilities (Nazir & Pinsonneault, 2012; Tallon et al., 2019). Others have also examined how IT plays different roles to enable varying dimensions of agility (Park et al., 2017; Roberts & Grover, 2012).

We conceptualize and operationalize these key and classic dimensions of sensing and responding as two independent constructs. Sensing capability means the ability of organizations to scan and define emergent threats and opportunities brought about by environmental changes, including competitors’ actions, consumer preference changes, and economic shifts. Responding capability denotes the ability of organizations to make timely, accurate, expeditious, and relevant decisions. With both sensing and responding, organizations can track turbulent markets, change business focus, redeploy business and IT resources, and eventually create and maintain competitive advantages (Teece, 2007). Specifically, we argue that sensing and responding capabilities are related to each other, forming a virtuous cycle. Organizations with a strong sensing capability tend to enhance responsiveness, and timely responses can benefit their performance, which further motivates them to enhance their sensing (Overby et al., 2006).

Yet firms with a strong sensing capability may also fail to respond to opportunities in a timely manner due to bureaucracy, lack of emergent coordination, or other barriers. Also, firms can have strong responsiveness but not perceive the right opportunities or threats. For instance, when there is a lack of effective communication between business and IT executives, information and knowledge cannot flow across the firm, which may hinder sensing capability (Liang et al., 2017). In addition, a large number of IT and business resources that are bundled together can also cause complacency in the focal firm regarding with its current strategic decisions and status quo, making it difficult to actively sense environmental change (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). Sensing and responding may thus have different enabling factors and cannot be equalized. For example, Overby et al. (2006) found that knowledge-oriented IT more directly supports sensing capability, while process-oriented IT more directly supports responding capability.

IT alignment has been widely considered a driver of agility (Bradley et al., 2012; Tallon et al., 2019). But given that agility has two key dimensions with different antecedents, the exact effects of IT alignment on each dimension of agility are unknown. It is possible that IT alignment has opposite effects on the two dimensions, leading to mixed results. The separation of sensing and responding allows us to investigate the more nuanced effects of IT alignment, which can provide new insights into the alignmentagility paradox in theory and also help firms efficiently strengthen a specific dimension of agility with fewer resources and efforts and eventually achieve agility in practice.

## 2.4 IT Alignment and Agility

Dove (2002) presented sensing capability as a form of knowledge management, representing the intellectual ability to find appropriate things to act on, while the response capability represents the physical ability to act. Overby et al. (2006) found that knowledgeoriented IT more directly supports sensing capability through discovering deep meanings, patterns, and data knowledge, while process-oriented IT more directly supports responding capability through facilitating process integration and visibility, and ensuring processes are modified and enhanced in a timely manner. Building upon these studies, we develop the underlying arguments on the relationship between alignment and sensing capability based on knowledge or intellectual perspectives, while the relationship between alignment and responding capability is built on the coordination and process perspectives.

## 2.4.1 Intellectual Alignment with Two Dimensions of Agility

The literature has shown that firms can enable sensing capability through data analysis and information processing (Park et al., 2017; Tallon et al., 2019). In this study, we posit that intellectual alignment can enhance organizational sensing capability in two primary ways. First, from an ambidexterity perspective, aligned business and IT strategies can motivate firms to exploit current resources and explore new opportunities, which can stimulate adaptation and innovation (Gupta et al., 2006; He & Wong, 2004). With these abilities, firms can not only obtain adequate knowledge about their current operating pattern but also create new knowledge of environmental changes that enable them to sense relevant changes actively and make sense of cues in the environment easily. Also, with high levels of intellectual alignment, IT and business activities allow firms to gather and share environmental information across units (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). By facilitating information flows, firms can promote their sensing capability.

The literature has also revealed, however, that intellectual alignment may hinder sensing capability in three key ways. First, IT alignment pursues internal fit that competes for resources against external fit with the environment (Liang et al., 2017). Miller (1992) proposed that firms with strong internal fit usually have weak external fit. This implies that once firms have established a high level of intellectual alignment, business and IT executives tend to pay more attention to internal events that protect alignment while losing sight of the long-term need for an external fit between strategy and changing environments. This view may foster tunnel vision and impede sensing capability (Liang et al., 2017). Second, intellectual alignment can enhance firm performance (Gerow et al., 2014) and often lead to early success, which may entice firms to believe and maintain their strategic focus even as the environment calls for rapid change, which can prevent the sensing of such changes outside their responsibilities (Tallon, 2007). Third, the short-term success of intellectual alignment may also result in competitive complacency, where firms become comfortable in their current strategies and ignore change signals (Ferrier et al., 1999; Overby et al., 2006).

Responding capability indicates organizations’ timely, accurate, expeditious, and relevant decisions. Prior research has found that facilitating and integrating processes can enhance the emergent coordination of firms, which can eventually improve their responding capability (Liang et al., 2017). Unlike the various effects of intellectual alignment on sensing capability, we argue that it will only impede organizational responding capability. Although Tallon (2008) contended that embedding IT into business processes can facilitate rapid responsiveness to change, other studies have suggested that tightly bundled resources would be difficult to mobilize in responding to environmental changes (Leonard-Barton, 1992).

As noted above, intellectual alignment can lead to short-term success or path dependence that may not only hurt firms’ sensing capability (Tallon, 2007) but also their responsiveness to external changes (Sabherwal et al., 2001). Intellectual alignment usually causes resource rigidity (e.g., fixed resource investment patterns) and routine rigidity (e.g., fixed resource processing models) (Liang et al., 2017). In this case, firms often find it difficult to respond to environmental change if their response requires modifying or updating existing resources and creating high costs when pursuing new strategies. As intellectual alignment is associated with formal and planned strategies, path dependence, and routinization (Zhou et al., 2017), such bureaucracy may also slow down the response process, causing firms to fail to act on emerging opportunities (Overby et al., 2006).

## 2.4.2 Social Alignment with Two Dimensions of Agility

Prior studies have confirmed that shared domain knowledge (SDK), shared understanding, and shared information between IT and business executives are important antecedents of IT alignment (Chan et al., 2006; Preston & Karahanna, 2009), which in turn can be further viewed as enabling factors of agility. We argue that social alignment can enhance both sensing and responding capabilities. First of all, it allows critical changes in the business domain to be detected by IT executives, as well as the IT’s potential of enabling new business strategies to be easily communicated to business executives (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). In this way, the close relationship among these executives will enhance firms’ sensing and responding capabilities (Fink & Neumann, 2007; Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011). Also, shared understanding between business and IT executives can facilitate their communication and commitment to their IT and business strategies, allowing them to sense internal and external changes easily and develop solutions on how best to respond to market opportunities or threats (Bradley et al., 2012).

Finally, social alignment-induced information sharing can help managers quickly develop common knowledge and collective sensemaking while allowing prompt strategic decision-making (Park et al., 2017; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010). For example, aligning IT with sales, marketing, and production can enable these functional departments to share information about customers needs for specific products, so that they can make full use of each other’s expertise and knowledge and build the ability to quickly sense and respond to customer and market emergencies. Social alignment can also suppress or eliminate misunderstandings across departments that might delay and impede agility due to language barriers, for example, where standardizing language will help reduce confusion (Preston & Karahanna, 2009). In this way, cross-department consensus and agreement can be achieved promptly to facilitate the subsequent decisionmaking process. Moreover, through facilitating emergent coordination, social alignment can particularly enable organizational responding capability (Liang et al., 2017).

Although examination of these positive effects is ubiquitous in the literature, some scholars have argued that social alignment may impede agility, especially for sensing. For instance, when knowledge sharing between business and IT executives fosters myopia, groupthink, or the status quo, the use of existing resources is favored. But less exploration of new opportunities can impede firms’ sensing of environmental changes (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). While consensus based on social alignment may help agility, the risk of missing out on new ideas or opportunities exists (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011).

## 2.4.3 Dimensional and Contextual Perspective

Based on the noted literature, we argue that new insights into the IT alignment-agility paradox can be gained when different dimensions of agility are treated discriminatively as two independent constructs and their respective relationships with IT alignment are explored. As agility has long been considered a multidimensional construct, prior studies have generally treated it as an aggregated ability and induced general conclusions (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). However, as intellectual and social alignment may have conflicting effects on both sensing and responding capabilities, it is important to study the effects of the two dimensions of IT alignment on the two dimensions of organizational agility.

Although intellectual alignment and social alignment have been shown to exhibit distinct effects on sensing and responding, we propose that the exact influence of the one dimension of alignment on different dimensions of agility is contingent on the other, as well as organizational and environmental contexts (Liang et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2017). We further submit that opportunities or threats caused by environmental changes may only be scanned and noticed by vigilant firms with high sensing. By scanning and processing this information, these firms can decide whether to formulate a response or maintain the status quo (Overby et al., 2006). In this sense, agility serves as a complex and synthetical outcome that is affected by the combination of various factors such as organizational and environmental contingencies (Park et al., 2017). Thus, from the contextual perspective, this study investigates how intellectual and social alignment combine into multiple bundles to affect sensing and responding capabilities under different organizational and environmental conditions by considering environmental dynamism, organizational size, and firm strategy as contextual factors.

## 2.5 Organizational and Environmental Factors

## 2.5.1 Environmental Dynamism

Environmental dynamism, defined as the instability and the extent of changes in a firm’s competitive environment (Dess & Beard, 1984; Simerly & Li, 2000), has been widely regarded as a significant contextual factor in exploring its roles in forming organizations’ agility and performance in the fields of strategic management and IS (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011; Yayla & Hu 2012). While firms in dynamic environments must be prepared to sense and respond to unpredicted threats or opportunities to remain competitive, the instability or changes in customer performance, rivals’ competitive actions, and innovation rates of new products and technology lead to an information deficit that reduces the firm’s ability to manage resources to create value (Sirmon et al., 2007). In addition, the literature has proposed that environmental volatility is a contributor to uncertainty and risk in decision-making (Dess & Beard, 1984; Child, 1972). Thus, we argue that the more dynamic the environment is, the more difficult it is for firms to scan business events, sense competitors’ actions, and make timely decisions.

To be agile in dynamic environments, firms should be able to continuously integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external resources and capabilities to handle environmental changes (Teece et al., 1997). Although a positive link between alignment and agility regardless of market volatility has been found (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), IT alignment (especially intellectual alignment) impedes the reconfiguring of core business activities (Francalanci & Galal, 1998; Markus et al., 2006) when the market environment is dynamic. Importantly, in highly dynamic environments, unpredictable actions of aggressive competitors, unanticipated changes in customer needs, and disruptive innovation of technologies or products can lead to urgent, unanticipated, and impactful shocks. In this case, it is insufficient or even impossible for firms to reconfigure their business and IT strategies for a quick response to environmental changes through formal planning based on intellectual alignment (Zhou et al., 2017). Social alignment can complement and ameliorate this deficient effect by forming emergent coordination and improvisational capabilities between business and IT executives (Liang et al., 2017). Improvisation and spontaneity in reconfiguring existing resources can help firms ameliorate the challenges of urgent, unpredictable, or novel environmental situations (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010).

In stable environments, outside uncertainties, such as changes in customer tastes and technological updates are minor effects (Zhou et al., 2017), the extent and direction of which may be forecasted or even led in advance. In this case, firms’ business and IT strategies could stay unchanged for a long time while intellectual alignment may continue functioning well to facilitate agility. For example, Zhou et al. (2017) empirically demonstrated that intellectual alignment has a positive impact on organizational agility in stable environments, while this positive effect is weaker when environmental dynamism is stronger. In contrast, social alignment can enhance agility by increasing real-time information exchange, informal communication, and personal interaction between business and IT executives (Liang et al., 2017). Considering that the effect of intellectual and social alignments on agility may depend on how dynamic the environment is, we take environmental dynamism as a contingency factor.

## 2.5.2 Organizational Size

The role of organizational size as a contextual factor has been examined in IT alignment research (Raymond & Bergeron, 2008) showing that business and IT strategies may be better aligned in large firms because their managers usually introduce formal processes and structures and have more organizational slack (Chan et al., 2006). In addition, Chan and Reich (2007) encouraged a focus on firm size and type to reveal the nature of alignment; size may have a positive effect on IT alignment in small and medium enterprises (Gutierrez et al., 2009). More recently, Shao (2019) called for studies to further examine whether industry and organizational size have contextual effects on firms’ IS-business alignment.

Organizational size also influences a firm’s behaviors (Ghobadian & Gallear, 1997), as large firms have more diverse communication channels and resources as well as supported business processes and improved IT infrastructure for sensing and responding to rapid environmental changes (Park et al., 2017). But firm size has also been negatively related to responding capability (Roberts & Grover, 2012), and large firms’ bureaucracy and formalized or standardize routines (Ghobadian & Gallear, 1997) may lead to organizational inertia or resource rigidity and eventually impede agility. Also, multiple levels of commanding and reporting mechanisms may reduce the efficiency of information dissemination and make firms fail to sense and respond to environmental changes in a timely manner. We thus include organizational size as a contextual factor in achieving sensing and responding capabilities through IT alignment.

## 2.5.3 Firm Strategy

Based on a competitive strategic scheme, Miles and Snow (1978) identified three types of business strategies, defender, prospector, and analyzer. Defender firms focus on protecting their market share by offering high-quality but low-price products or services, emphasizing operational efficiency improvement while rarely initiating the search for new opportunities outside their business domain. In contrast, prospectors usually operate in turbulent and fast-changing markets and continuously seek new opportunities. They value flexibility and innovativeness and tend to ignore formal control and operational efficiency. Analyzers usually pursue defender and prospector features simultaneously to maintain a stable market share while seeking new products or market opportunities (Chan et al., 2006; Yayla & Hu, 2012). Prior research has shown that the effects of IT alignment on business performance vary across these three different strategies. For example, IT alignment has been shown to enhance overall business success in prospectors and analyzers but not in defenders (Chan et al., 2006; Sabherwal & Chan, 2001), yet it can significantly impact different performance measures across all orientations (Yayla & Hu, 2012).

In this paper, we argue that firm strategy still plays a contextual role in the relationship between IT alignment and agility. We examine these contextual effects by using Miles and Snow’s (1978) typology of business strategies (e.g., defender, prospector, and analyzer) for two reasons. First, this typology is the most commonly used and the best understood (Zahra & Pearce, 1990) in business strategy research and is also widely adopted in the IT alignment literature (Chan et al., 2006; Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Yayla & Hu, 2012). Second, Liang et al.’s (2017) research also provides a theoretical basis for further assessing the effects of IT alignment on agility across these three business strategies, which suggests the potential value of this typology. For example, by definition, prospectors are more likely to sense external changes proactively and make corresponding strategic adjustments, while defenders may exhibit converse behaviors. Analyzers are not only likely to contain the same behavior patterns as prospectors or defenders but may also exhibit some new and distinct ones. Thus, to further enrich the understanding of our research findings, and for consistency with the previous study, we view firm strategy as a third contextual factor and choose Miles and Snow’s (1978) typology of business strategy.

## 2.6 Configurational Approach to Studying the Effects of IT Alignment on Agility

This study regards the two agility dimensions of sensing and responding as discrete outcomes that result from the combination of intellectual and social alignment under the different contingencies of environmental dynamism, organizational size, and firm strategy. Using the configurational theory approach, we investigate how IT alignment and organizational and environmental elements combine into multiple configurations in different ways to produce each dimension of agility. While most studies have viewed the effect of IT alignment on agility as linear and symmetrical, scholars have gradually recognized the nonlinear state of IT alignment (Sabherwal et al., 2001; Zhang et al., 2021) and have called for the use of nonlinear analytical approach to assess its impact (Oh & Pinsonneault, 2007). The linear approach in research cannot fully capture or accurately predict the consequences of IT alignment and may cause inconsistent results. For example, Misangyi and Acharya (2014) argued that the traditional approach of adopting deductive theory testing with correlation-based analyses can account for inconsistent corporate governance study findings, whereas a configurational approach can reconcile these via certain theoretical propositions.

In addition, as a type of organizational capability, agility is a complex and synthetical product that is affected by the combination of various factors such as IT resources as well as organizational and environmental characteristics (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). The complex relations between firm strategy, organizational size, and environmental dynamism, as well as the two different dimensions of IT alignment in enabling sensing and responding also suggest that agility can be better explained using a configuration of related elements. We therefore employ the configurational theory approach to investigate how intellectual alignment and social alignment and other contextual factors combine into multiple configurations to affect sensing and responding capabilities.

Figure 2 depicts the nomological network of our research. <sup>2</sup> This model illustrates the configuration paradigm used to build a configuration theory that explains the complex simultaneous interactions among intellectual alignment, social alignment, organizational size, firm strategy, and environmental dynamism. Qualitative comparative analysis (QCA), which we use in this study, was first proposed by Ragin in the 1980s to address the causal complexity phenomenon from the sociological perspective (Rihoux & Ragin, 2008). With set theory and Boolean calculation as cornerstones, QCA explores how the combinations of antecedent conditions cause observable changes or discontinuities in results (Fiss, 2007; Mikalef & Krogstie, 2020). Studies that use QCA have commonly viewed key interactional elements wherein outcomes are better explained by their simultaneous combinations rather than by any individual element in isolation (Park et al., 2020). We thus apply QCA to align with the configurational approach and choose fsQCA because it allows us to measure a set membership of a case as a continuous value between 0 and 1, rather than simply classifying cases into binary (csQCA) or three or four categories (mvQCA) (Rihoux & Ragin, 2008). This form of QCA can more fully capture the subtle effects of changes in antecedent conditions at different levels or degrees, thereby reflecting the diversity and uniqueness of the case (Fiss, 2011).

## 3 Data and Methodology

## 3.1 Construct Measurement

This study uses the survey method, which is most commonly employed to explore firm-level phenomena and behavior (Fiss, 2011; Mikalef & Krogstie, 2020). Appendix C summarizes the measures and sources of each construct. The measurement of intellectual and social alignment mainly follows Preston and Karahanna (2009) and Liang et al. (2017). Sensing and responding capabilities are measured using items from Overby et al. (2006), Roberts and Grover (2012), and Liang et al. (2017). Environmental dynamism is measured following Karimi et al. (2004). A sevenpoint Likert scale is used for all the measurement items. The natural logarithm of the number of full-time employees is used as a measure of organizational size. Finally, firm strategy is determined following Yayla and Hu (2012), which is presented as mutually exclusive options (i.e., prospector, analyzer, and defender) for respondents.

## 3.2 Data Collection and Validation

We collected survey data from the Chinese shipbuilding industry, which has experienced tremendous environmental and market changes over the years. Since 2009, China has become the world’s largest shipbuilding country (Jiang et al., 2013). Affected by the international financial crisis, the continual downturn in international transportation has imposed great pressure on the Chinese shipbuilding industry (Liang et al., 2017). Confronted with growing turbulent environmental changes, some shipyards have sought new developmental paths to respond to the financial crisis and pursue the high value-added ship market, which includes LNG ships, large container vessels, and ocean engineering equipment, while other companies are still following their original strategies and generally staying stuck in the status quo, which makes the context of this study particularly relevant for understanding organizational agility.

Further, it is noted that the Chinese shipbuilding industry not only includes shipyards that build entire ships, but also a lot of supporting firms that manufacture ship-related materials—such as coatings, power equipment, outfitting, interior outfitting, communication and navigation devices, deck machinery, electrical appliances, and research and design. Although these firms have also been affected by the financial crisis, their principal industries are different and thus their sense of the market or product changes also varies. These distinct levels of environmental dynamism perceived by firms then form diverse levels of agility. Furthermore, the Chinese shipbuilding industry has a long history of applying IT, such as enterprise-level IS such as SCM, CRM, cloud computing, and others to improve business operation efficiency or explore new business models based on changes in economics, markets, or customers. In the dynamic market environments characterized by the financial crisis, the different degrees of agility demonstrated by these firms using ITs also fit well with our research context.

In terms of the specific methods used herein, a matched-pair survey was used to collect data from business and IT executive dyads. Two different questionnaires were developed, which were filled out by business executives and IT executives. Based on the differences in domain knowledge and job responsibilities of these two types of executives, we invited business executives to measure sensing and responding capabilities, environmental dynamism, firm strategy, and organizational size, while intellectual alignment and social alignment were measured by IT executives. Of the 12,086 Chinese shipbuilding companies listed by the China Shipbuilding Industry Association, we randomly selected 1,000 for the questionnaire survey. We identified the CEO and CIO of each firm in the sample and collected data by sending separate emails to the CEO and CIO. Before administering the full-scale survey, we conducted a pilot survey to test the face and content validity of the questionnaire. We invited ten master’s students, five professors, and two information center managers from a major university in southeastern China to complete these items. Based on their comments and responses to the questionnaire, we further modified the double-barreled questions and resolved ambiguities. Sample company characteristics are shown in Appendix D.

We collected our data between October 2013 and March 2014.<sup>3</sup> After filtering, a total of 135 matched questionnaires entered the next stage of data analysis. The response rate was 13.5%, similar to other matched surveys in the IT alignment literature (Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). Following Armstrong and Overton (1977), we evaluated the nonresponse bias of our sample, and a t-test was conducted on the revenue and number of employees by comparing the earliest 34 respondents (25%) and the last 34 respondents. Results show no significant difference between the two groups of data. Thus, we conclude that nonresponse bias is not a serious concern for our study.

We made other efforts to validate the measurement of social alignment to eliminate biases. Besides the IT executives’ self-reports, we included a new item of shared domain knowledge (SDK) between business and IT executives for each organization as an indicator of social alignment. SDK was calculated by comparing business knowledge reported by IT executives and IT knowledge reported by business executives (Reich & Benbasat, 2000) (Appendix E). After conducting a principal component analysis, results show that SDK loads well (0.922) with other items as an indicator of social alignment. As SDK was measured by incorporating data from both business and IT executives, it was unlikely to be affected by common method bias, thus making social alignment measurement using only IT executives valid.

## 3.3 Measurement Evaluation

We used SPSS 20.0 and AMOS to analyze the scales reliability and validity. First, SPSS 20.0 was applied to conduct exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Results show that Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) is 0.817, greater than the recommended 0.8. The Bartlett value is 3207.510 (p < 0.001) and the cumulative variance contribution rate is 82.01%, indicating that the data is suitable for factor analysis. Second, we tested the reliability and validity of the model using Cronbach’s α coefficient and composite reliability (CR), which should be greater than 0.8 (Gefen et al., 2011; Gefen et al., 2000). CR is greater than 0.90 for all constructs, which indicates sufficient internal consistency. All Cronbach’s α values are greater than 0.8, indicating high reliability. Appendix F shows standardized factor loads, residual variance, Cronbach’s α, CR, and average variance extracted (AVE) values of each construct’s measurement items.

Convergent validity and discrimination validity were tested using AVE and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The AVE values of the variables are greater than 0.8, which indicates sufficient convergent validity (Segars, 1997). By applying AMOS, CFA results show that the goodness of fit of the model is acceptable (χ<sup>2</sup>/df = 1.697, RMSEA = 0.072, CFI = 0.965, NFI = 0.919), which means that the constructs have convergent validity (Hu & Bentler, 1999). We tested discriminant validity by comparing the absolute value of the correlation among constructs with the square root of each construct’s AVE value. The square root of each construct’s AVE is much greater than the construct’s correlations with any other construct, suggesting sufficient discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis for constructs are shown in Table 1. Further, each item’s factor loading is much higher than its crossloadings on other constructs, confirming discriminant validity (Hair et al., 1998) (Appendix G).

Finally, we tested common method variance (CMV) to address the problem that using the same measurement tool can cause false common variation among constructs. We adopted two methods to evaluate CMV. Following Podsakoff et al. (2003), we first applied Harmon’s one-factor test. After all items of the five theoretical constructs were entered into a principal component analysis, five factors were identified. The first factor of the unrotated solution explains only 29.28% of the total variance, which suggests that CMV is unlikely to be a concern. We then employed the unmeasured latent methods factor test (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Based on the confirmatory factor analysis model used, a latent method factor was added so that all measurement items loaded on the associated constructs along with this method latent factor. Evaluation results $( \chi ^ { 2 } / d f { = } 1 . 7 0 6 , \mathrm { R M S E A } = 0 . 0 7 3$ , CFI = 0.964, NFI = 0.919) show that compared with the CFA model, the change of $\chi ^ { 2 } / d f$ is not significant. After adding the common method factor, the model fit was not improved, suggesting there is no obvious CMV in the measurement.

## 3.4 Set-Theoretic Analysis with fsQCA

## 3.4.1 Calibration

It is necessary to use the process of calibration in assigning the antecedents and outcomes into setmembership scores when applying fsQCA (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). Calibration defines the extent to which a given case has membership in the set of, for example, a high level of sensing capability, wherein three anchors should be defined: full membership, full non-membership, and the crossover point, according to empirical and theoretical knowledge of the context and cases (Ragin, 2008). The case membership is a real number between 0 (indicating full non-membership) and 1 (full membership).

Except for organizational size and firm strategy, other constructs in this study were measured using a 7-point Likert scale. Following the calibration guideline for survey measurement (Fiss, 2011; Misangyi et al., 2017), we defined three anchors: a value of 6 for full membership, 2 for full non-membership, and 4 as the crossover point. Use of the qualitatively defined calibration scale can reflect reality more accurately (Park et al., 2017), and by setting the three thresholds, fsQCA transforms the values of constructs to a fuzzy score of 0-1. We calibrated the organizational size (normalized values) using the same method. The calibration anchors of the constructs are shown in Table 2. Finally, according to Mikalef et al. (2015), calibration of the firm strategy was done by constructing the three variables of prospector, defender, and analyzer, each representing the proportional strategic orientation with presence (1) or absence (0) as possible choices.

## 3.4.2 Truth Table Analysis

After calibration, we applied the truth table algorithm (Ragin, 2008) to identify combinations of elements that produce the outcome of interest (Park & Mithas, 2020). In Appendix H, we present truth tables for high sensing and responding. The truth table includes all logically possible combinations of elements, and each row corresponds to a combination. To avoid accidental errors caused by a single case, we set the minimum acceptable frequency of cases at two. In addition, according to Greckhamer (2016), we set the raw consistency threshold to 0.80 and the PRI consistency threshold to 0.65. Only combinations with at least two empirical instances and a raw consistency of at least 0.8 were considered, as well as a PRI consistency of at least 0.65 as reliably resulting in high sensing and responding. The sensing and responding columns show a value of 1 for these combinations, otherwise 0.

FsQCA can identify multiple equifinal configurations associated with an outcome (Fiss, 2007, 2011), implying that we can achieve the same outcome through different paths in different contexts. ## 4 Configurational Analysis Results

## 4.1 Necessary Conditions Analysis

It is important to examine the necessary conditions when conducting the fuzzy set truth table analysis to avoid its elimination by the parsimonious solution (Rihoux & Ragin, 2008), which could make our configuration results misleading. To identify these conditions, we drew fuzzy-set membership plots that depict the membership distribution of cases in terms of IT alignment and sensing and responding. If the value of set membership of an element is always equal to or higher than the value of set membership in the outcome, then this element is likely to be a necessary condition (Appendix I). Table I1 in the Appendix shows that the consistency score of each causal condition is less than 0.9, which means that no element serves as a necessary condition for sensing or responding. For intellectual alignment and social alignment in particular, the results of the set-membership plots also show similar conclusions (Figure I1). As the pattern is consistent with a necessary condition only when most cases appear below the diagonal (the blue dotted line) (Park et al., 2017), neither intellectual alignment nor social alignment passes this test. Following this analysis, we conducted a sufficient conditions analysis by incorporating all the antecedent conditions into fsQCA to explore their combined effects on organizational sensing and responding capabilities.

## 4.2 Sufficient Solutions for Agility

Truth table analysis was adopted to identify causal recipes that are sufficient for producing high sensing and responding (Ragin, 2008). Three types of solutions— complex, parsimonious, and intermediate—can be obtained through fsQCA. A complex solution includes configurations without any counterfactuals, a parsimonious solution includes configurations with both “easy” and “difficult” counterfactuals, and an intermediate solution includes configurations only with “easy” counterfactuals (Fiss, 2011). Elements that are part of both parsimonious and intermediate solutions are core conditions, which have a stronger relationship with the outcome. Elements that only appear in the intermediate solution but not in the parsimonious solution are peripheral conditions, which then have a weaker relationship with the outcome.

Following Ragin (2008), we graphically depict the results of the truth table analysis using the notation system. As shown in Table 3a and Table 3b, solutions are enumerated according to the strategic types that firms employ, resulting in three sets of solutions for achieving high sensing and responding. Large circles indicate core elements and small circles indicate peripheral elements. Full circles indicate the presence of a condition and crossed-out circles indicate its absence, which means that full circle elements are enablers for the outcome and crossed-out circle elements are inhibitors (Park et al., 2017). For example, the presence of INTE (dark circle) means full membership in a high level of intellectual alignment, and a high level of INTE has an enabling role. Its absence (×circle) means that full membership in a high level of intellectual alignment isn’t in configurations that result in the outcome, and a high degree of INTE plays an inhibiting role. By displaying the outcomes graphically, we can effectively interpret and compare the configurations to understand how these elements are combined systemically to produce the outcomes. We then explored the configurations in detail for each type of capability across firm strategies to obtain a deeper understanding of the role of IT alignment in firms achievement of sensing and responding capabilities.

## 4.2.1 Configurations for Achieving High Sensing Capability

Under the combinatorial impacts of intellectual alignment, social alignment, organizational size, firm strategy, and environmental dynamism, firms can adopt seven configurations to achieve high sensing capability (Table 3a). This suggests the importance of equifinality in the configuration perspective. Configuration S-P1 indicates the path to high sensing for firms following the prospector strategy. That is, in dynamic environments, small-sized organizations can achieve sensing capability through a high level of social alignment as the core element and a lack of high intellectual alignment as the peripheral element.

In the case of firms using the analyzer strategy, more diverse configurations are found in which social alignment is a core element that has a strong causal relationship with sensing capability, while intellectual alignment is a peripheral element that may be present and complement social alignment for firms to achieve sensing capability, or be absent according to different organizational and environmental contexts. In dynamic environments, just like configuration S-P1, social alignment is enough to enable small firms to achieve high sensing (S-A1) when intellectual alignment is absent. We also find that the neutral permutations within configurations S-A2a and S-A2b point to the existence of second-order equifinality as they share the same central conditions and only differ in their contributing conditions (Fiss, 2011). In stable environments, social alignment can effectively support large firms to achieve high sensing (S-A2a). But when environments are more dynamic, configuration S-A2b shows that high sensing capability is achieved in large firms when both social alignment (as a central condition) and intellectual alignment (as a contributing condition) are present. Thus, under this condition, intellectual alignment and social alignment tend to complement each other (Zhou et al., 2017) as their presence co-occurs (Misangyi & Acharya, 2014).

To validate this complementary effect, following Misangyi and Acharya (2014), we combined intellectual and social alignment via the “fuzzy and” operations in fsQCA 3.0, which equals the minimum value between them and thus can capture this effect, because when intellectual alignment and social alignment complement each other, both dimensions of IT alignment need to be present (Misangyi & Acharya, 2014). Results confirm that intellectual alignment and social alignment combine as complements to enhance larger firms’ sensing capability in dynamic environments.

Finally, for organizations following the defender strategy, three configurations are found that can lead to high sensing capability. The first solution (S-D1) shows that a high level of social alignment can support large firms to achieve this desired state in stable environments. The second (S-D2) indicates that intellectual alignment alone can help small firms to achieve sensing—they do not need a high level of social alignment and environmental dynamism is irrelevant. In turn, in the configuration S-D3, regardless of firm size, social alignment alone can enable firms in dynamic environments to achieve high sensing; a high level of intellectual alignment is not necessary. In this light, intellectual alignment and social alignment tend to substitute for one another as the presence of one is generally accompanied by the absence of the other. That is, either intellectual alignment or social alignment needs to be present in certain contexts for high sensing capability, which is congruent with the notion of substitution (Misangyi & Acharya, 2014). Similarly, we applied the “fuzzy or” operation on intellectual and social alignment, and the results equal the maximum value. This indicates that only the better score of the two matters, allowing us to capture the substitution effect. Result shows that in dynamic environments, two dimensions of IT alignment serve as substitutes that enable small defenders to achieve sensing.

In sum, for configurations of sensing capability, intellectual alignment and social alignment may play different roles under different contingencies. Overall, they may act as a core enabling element that has a strong positive causal relationship with sensing in some configurations but may also act as a peripheral or even counterproductive condition in others. Intellectual and social alignment also exhibit different synergistic effects involved in complementarity or substitution according to different contexts.

## 4.2.2 Configurations for Achieving High Responding Capability

We found eight configurations that are sufficient for firms to achieve high responding capability (Table 3b). Two configurations are available for prospectors in dynamic environments, where social alignment is the core element while intellectual alignment is absent (R-P1) or irrelevant (R-P2, especially for large firms). Four configurations can help analyzer firms formulate a timely response. The structures of the first three configurations are similar in alignment patterns to firms with the prospector strategy but differ in organizational and environmental contexts. Social alignment is the core enabling element and intellectual alignment is absent, especially for small firms (R-A1) or stable environments (R-A2), which means that in these configurations, a high degree of intellectual alignment will prevent firms from making a response promptly. Social alignment alone is also enough for smaller firms in stable environments to achieve high responding (R-A3). Interestingly, we also confirmed that intellectual alignment complements social alignment for large firms in dynamic environments (R-A4) in the achievement of responding capability, which is almost the same as configuration S-A2b. Finally, for defenders, R-D1 and R-D2 show that a high level of intellectual alignment should be absent when large firms rely on social alignment to achieve responding in dynamic environments. Furthermore, both R-D1 and R-D2 include intellectual and social alignment as core elements and could be regarded as similar first-order type (Fiss, 2011).

Collectively, for configurations of responding, social alignment always takes the core enabling role, while intellectual alignment always assumes the core inhibiting role or is irrelevant, except for their co-presence in R-A4. Compared with configurations of sensing, when intellectual alignment is absent, we found that it is always a peripheral condition for sensing while serving as a core condition for responding, which means that intellectual alignment has more negative and inhibiting effects on firms’ achievement of responding.

In addition, from the perspective of business strategy, we found that configurations of responding for prospectors and defenders usually apply to large firms or dynamic environments, while for analyzers, they almost apply to small firms or stable environments, which means that organizational size and environmental dynamism matter for making a timely response. Notably, large firms usually have more resources available to respond to changes (Chan et al., 2006). Dynamic environments seem to suit the nature of prospectors, who may respond to changes proactively. Also, defenders are more inclined to respond in dynamic environments because adjustments in their strategies or structures are not needed when the external environment is stable and is well suited for their business strategy or practices.

Nevertheless, while striving to maintain a stable market share, analyzers will also seek out new products or market opportunities (Yayla & Hu, 2012). When external environments are turbulent, however, these firms may be reluctant to pursue new opportunities that are uncertain (i.e., lack of intellectual or social alignment); maintaining the status quo may be an optimal choice. In stable environments where firms can predefine processes to handle events, analyzers may actively respond to these environments as soon as new opportunities are sensed. In addition, smaller firms appear to be more likely to work for high profits and survival than larger ones with more resources and capital to rely on.

## 4.3 Validation and Robustness of Solutions

The measures of consistency and coverage were used to validate the solutions. Overall solution consistency measures the degree to which all configurations together consistently result in an outcome. The overall solution consistency for sensing is 0.872 and for responding is 0.866, above the acceptable level of 0.75 (Fiss, 2011; Ragin, 2008). Raw coverage shows the proportion of cases that have membership in their respective path to the outcome and represents their empirical importance and effectiveness. The overall solution coverage for sensing is 0.626 and for responding is 0.625. That is, seven configurations for sensing explained 62.6% of the cases and eight configurations for responding explained 62.5%.

We also performed several sensitivity tests to check the robustness of our results. We first changed the anchors for the outcome and antecedent variables in the calibration process from (6,4,2) to (7,4,1) for full membership, crossover point, and full nonmembership. With this new calibration, we conducted new fsQCA analyses and found that configurations for sensing (noted in Table J1a) and responding (in Table J1b) show the same patterns and structures as configurations in Table 3a and 3b, except for the subtle changes in the consistency and coverage of each configuration as well as the overall solution consistency and coverage (Appendix J). We then conducted another analysis by grouping strategy according to prospector (n = 49), analyzer (n = 35), and defender $( n = 5 1 )$ ). After that, fsQCA was applied to each group, which finally led to three independent outcomes across firm strategies. Results show that the configurations in Table J2a and J2b are also almost the same as the results in Table 3a and 3b. In summary, these sensitivity analyses suggest that the configurations obtained by fsQCA are robust.

## 5.1 Intellectual Alignment Effect

Intellectual alignment focuses on the fit and integration of business strategy and IT strategy (Chan & Reich, 2007), which can enhance firms’ sensing capability. Bundling IT with other business resources allows firms to gather and share environmental knowledge proactively across business and IT units (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; Lavie & Rosenkopf, 2006; Zahra & George, 2002). This kind of knowledge sharing about environmental changes can help firms scan and define emergent threats and opportunities, thus enabling their sensing capability (Overby et al., 2006). Additionally, intellectual alignment motivates firms to exploit and explore their key resources (Gupta et al., 2006), which facilitates greater knowledge gains regarding their current business model and the creation of new knowledge about environmental changes. This, in turn, helps firms to easily achieve sensing capability.

Nevertheless, the effect of intellectual alignment on sensing may also exhibit negative characteristics. The literature has repeatedly shown that intellectual alignment can lead organizations to success and performance enhancement (Chan et al., 2006; Gerow et al., 2014), which can make firms overconfident in their current alignment and resource investment patterns (Zhou et al., 2018). This eventually results in the formation of myopic routine dependencies and reluctance to change the status quo (Tushman & O’Reilly, 1996). In this case, firms are more inclined to focus on what has already been implemented and ignore the signals of change and their related needs. Intellectual alignment could thus hurt firms’ sensing capability.

Although intellectual alignment plays contradicting roles in achieving sensing, it may have more definitively negative effects on responding. Responding is a range of decisions that firms make at the organizational level, and process-oriented IT more directly enables this capability (Overby et al., 2006). Intellectual alignment not only fosters behavior inertia and path dependence that undermine firms’ responsiveness to environmental changes (He & Wong, 2004; Liang et al., 2017; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), it is also formalized in firms through hierarchical control, structural design, and resource allocation that are likely to create rigidities that hinder the business operation process, further reducing the efficiency of responsiveness. Furthermore, as noted above, intellectual alignment often leads to early success, which entices firms to believe that they are on the right track and further align business and IT along the same trajectory, thus potentially creating a success trap that limits firms’ responsiveness to change (Leonard-Barton, 1992). However, from the contextual perspective, we further hold the opinion that intellectual alignment may have the potential value of enabling firms to achieve responding capability under special conditions, such as with the complement of social alignment (Zhou et al., 2017).

## 5.2 Social Alignment Effect

Social alignment can enhance shared understanding and shared information among business and IT managers, which can allow them to more quickly develop common ground and collective sensemaking (Park et al., 2017) and work out solutions for responding to market opportunities or threats in a timely manner. Social alignment-enabled knowledge sharing can also facilitate collaboration among executives (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), enhancing the detection of critical changes in business domains by IT managers and allowing IT’s potential to support new strategies or models to be easily communicated to business managers. The close relationship between IT and business executives can therefore improve firms’ ability to sense opportunities or changes both within and outside the firm (Liang et al., 2017; Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011).

Social alignment can also drive informal and emergent coordination actions and enable timely decisionmaking. Through increasing real-time information exchange and informal interaction between business and IT executives, social alignment can enhance their common understanding of market changes so they can cooperate to interpret events and devise solutions (Park et al., 2017). By helping business and IT managers make quick decisions and circumvent hierarchical decision-making mechanisms that are bureaucratic and slow, social alignment can facilitate the decisionmaking process and thus enhance responding.

## 5.3 Environmental Dynamism, Organizational Size, and Firm Strategy

It is often difficult for firms in dynamic environments to perceive unexpected changes effectively and to share this information across units to reach a consensus quickly. Bundling IT with critical business resources can enhance business and IT executives’ common awareness and knowledge about external changes and help them share knowledge to achieve agreement. Intellectual alignment can therefore enhance sensing, but different organizational sizes and firm strategies will impose further requirements.

Large firms tend to have more hierarchy, complexity, and interdependence (Park et al., 2017). Formal strategy arrangements offered by intellectual alignment alone may not sufficiently help them to sense the dynamic and complicated environments. Social alignment can enhance information exchange and knowledge sharing and enable firms to coordinate activities across different departments (Preston & Karahanna, 2009). This will help them overcome the complexities caused by size. Informal communication induced by social alignment can also help managers develop an agreed-upon interpretation quickly so that they can sense events promptly. In dynamic environments, social and intellectual alignment work together to support large-sized firms in achieving sensing capability.

These findings are also contingent on firm strategy. By definition, analyzers usually have multiple strategic cores. They maintain a stable domain of core products while seeking new markets and products. Compared with defenders, they need to invest more resources and energy to address innovation and efficiency (Chan et al., 2006). Besides the effect of social alignment that enables analyzers in dynamic environments to quickly interpret captured information and share knowledge across different departments to sense new opportunities, they also need the assistance of intellectual alignment to create important IT capabilities and facilitate learning about how to use existing IT or non-IT resources in new and innovative ways (He & Wong, 2004; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). Social alignment and intellectual alignment, when combined, will help large analyzers in dynamic settings achieve sensing capability where the potential value of intellectual alignment, in particular, has been exploited and confirmed (Zhou et al., 2017).

Defenders who emphasize operational efficiency and tend to sustain stable business and IT strategies, however, rarely make major adjustments. In dynamic settings, social alignment alone can thus enable defenders to achieve sensing and overcome complications caused by size, whereas a high level of intellectual alignment is not necessary. Surprisingly, we could not find configurations for prospectors under this condition. This may be due to prospectors continuous and proactive seeking of new products and market opportunities, making them more inclined to sense environmental changes, especially for larger firms with more resources and organizational slack (Chan et al., 2006). This implies that their achievement of sensing capability in dynamic environments may require less enabling of alignment.

Compared with large firms, small firms usually have fewer organizational levels and less interdependence, and fewer managers participate in information perceptions and sharing and decision-making. In this setting, social alignment-induced shared knowledge and understanding can help business and IT managers agree on the interpretation of unexpected environments. Social alignment can thus sufficiently support small firms to achieve sensing across all firm strategies. For defenders especially, given their more stable nature of highlighting organizational tendencies (Chan et al., 2006), intellectual alignment alone can also help them achieve sensing capability through establishing a well-planned IT platform to ensure timely and effective information transmission in dynamic environments. That is, for small defenders, these two dimensions of IT alignment substitute for each other to achieve sensing capability and they do not need to construct a high degree of intellectual and social alignment simultaneously. Based on these empirical findings and our theoretical logic, we posit the following two propositions for firms to achieve sensing capability in dynamic environments.

Proposition 1: In dynamic environments, intellectual alignment complements social alignment for large analyzers to achieve sensing capability, while for large defenders pursuing sensing capability, social alignment is enough, and they do not need a high level of intellectual alignment.

Proposition 2: In dynamic environments, small firms across all three business strategies can achieve sensing capability with only social alignment; for defenders especially, intellectual alignment can also substitute social alignment for high sensing capability.

In dynamic environments, intellectual and social alignment have significant distinct effects on responding capability. As social alignment can not only enhance shared understanding and knowledge among business and IT managers but also establish a close relationship between them, a firm’s ability to respond to environmental changes will be improved (Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011). Social alignment can also optimize the decision-making and responding process by providing informal interaction and enhancing emergent coordination (Liang et al., 2017). This suggests that social alignment plays a central role in firms’ achievement of responding capability. Our empirical findings support this argument.

In contrast, intellectual alignment may impede responding capability. If IT resources are embedded in business processes to maintain current strategies, closely aligned business and IT strategies may cause resource rigidity and path dependence (Liang et al., 2017), compelling firms to focus on a limited set of core business activities by restricting their strategic choices. Although changes in external environments may create opportunities, the alignment-induced exploitation of key existing organizational resources can create competency traps that reduce change and slow innovation (He & Wong, 2004; Henderson & Clark, 1990).

Our findings also show that when environments are dynamic, social alignment can effectively enable large firms to achieve responding capability across all strategies. The only exception is large analyzers in dynamic environments in which intellectual alignment complements social alignment to enhance timely responses. We apply the same logic to explain this configuration; the main difference between analyzers and the other two strategic types is that they generally employ a dual strategic core with stable and flexible components as well as a matrix organizational structure (Chan et al., 2006). Although social alignment can stimulate flexibility and innovation, it may also lead to disorder and inefficiency because a fast and stable foundation is also required for adjusting internal processes to take advantage of external opportunities (Zhou et al., 2018), especially for large firms with more complexity and interdependence. Intellectual alignment can thus promote and complement social alignment to help large analyzers respond quickly in dynamic settings by providing a formal and seamless platform (Nazir & Pinsonneault, 2012).

Overall, our findings indicate that in dynamic environments, social alignment is sufficient to achieve responding, whereas intellectual alignment effects may be overshadowed. Also, social alignment alone is sufficient for small firms to formulate timely responses across all three types of strategies through establishing informal interaction or face-to-face group meetings. We thus develop the following propositions.

Proposition 3: In dynamic environments, when large prospectors and defenders pursue responding capability, they must configure a high level of social alignment, whereas the extensive use of intellectual alignment is not necessary; for large analyzers, intellectual and social alignment play complementary roles in their achievement of responding capability.

Proposition 4: In dynamic environments, social alignment needs to be strengthened and intellectual alignment should be weakened for small firms to achieve responding capability.

In stable environments, most business events are welldefined and can be predicted in advance and firms have fewer uncertainties regarding potential challenges (Daft & Lengel, 1986). Also, the preferences and tastes of major customers remain largely unchanged, and the innovation rate of products or services and production technology is low in stable environments. In this case, environmental changes are usually subtle and difficult to detect. Social alignment is therefore needed to provide business and IT managers with mutual experiences and knowledge to sense and even predict environmental changes more efficiently and accurately. For large firms, social alignment can also help business and IT managers overcome complications associated with size so that they can develop consensus more easily and respond in a timely manner. Social alignment thus plays an essential role in firms’ achievement of sensing and responding capabilities.

Intellectual alignment may inhibit large firms’ achievement of sensing and responding in stable environments. Firms with a high level of intellectual alignment can often achieve short-term success, which promotes firms’ satisfaction with the status quo, reduces their market alertness, and leads them to neglect perceptions of external environments (Tallon, 2007) or underestimate the need for responsiveness. In stable environments, firms, especially large ones, may prefer to extract as much value as possible from their existing IT investments, given the high sunk costs (Gupta et al., 2006; Liang et al., 2017), and make as few changes as possible to their business strategies. Nevertheless, for small defenders, intellectual alignment can also replace the promoting effect of social alignment on sensing, as they usually have clear goals in terms of analyzing the market and optimizing current products. As intellectual alignment allows them to effectively enhance sensing by providing a well-formed platform to collect information and share knowledge, a high level of social alignment is not necessary in this case. But the achievement of a high level of sensing capability does not necessarily mean that defenders have to respond. In fact, our configurational solutions show that these defenders can achieve responding only in dynamic environments. However, small analyzers with multiple strategic plans are likely to respond for high performance. By establishing informal interaction and knowledge sharing between business and IT executives, social alignment can facilitate decision-making and thus support responding capability.

Finally, the fact that we could not empirically find any configuration for prospectors to achieve sensing or responding capabilities in stable environments is consistent with their dynamic nature (Chan et al., 2006); the more turbulent environment provides new opportunities and aids agility. We therefore offer the following propositions:

Proposition 5: In stable environments, social alignment alone is sufficient to enable large analyzers and defenders to achieve sensing and responding capabilities.

Proposition 6: In stable environments, small defenders can achieve sensing capability with intellectual alignment only, while small analyzers can achieve responding capability with social alignment and intellectual alignment is irrelevant.

Finally, by comparing the effects of the two dimensions of IT alignment on the two dimensions of agility from the configurational and contextual perspectives, we find that intellectual and social alignment may have different combinatorial (complementary or substitution) effects and play multifaceted roles (positive or negative) in achieving sensing and responding capabilities, as either present or absent and either core or peripheral conditions. Specifically, according to specific organizational size, environmental dynamism, and firm strategy, both intellectual and social alignment may be essential to achieve sensing capability in some contexts but may be irrelevant or even counterproductive in other conditions. While for responding, social alignment needs to be strengthened and intellectual alignment weakened, with the exception of large analyzers operating in dynamic environments, where intellectual and social alignment complement each other. We therefore posit the following proposition:

Proposition 7: Social alignment and intellectual alignment have heterogeneous effects on sensing vs. responding depending on specific contexts. Their effects in enhancing sensing capability are complex, showing different directions, strengths, or combinatory. In contrast, except for large analyzers in dynamic environments, their effects on responding capability are simple: social alignment is enabling while intellectual alignment is inhibiting or irrelevant.

These seven configurational propositions provide a clear understanding of the impact of IT alignment on agility. Through theorizing the complex and nonlinear effects of the two different dimensions of IT alignment on the two different dimensions of agility from the configurational and contextual perspective, this study not only clarifies the exact relationship between IT alignment and agility at a more granular level but also provides effective and accurate guidance for firms seeking to configure IT alignment to achieve a high level of sensing and responding capabilities.

## 6 Discussion

This study takes configurational and contextual perspectives to provide a more nuanced understanding of the alignment-agility relationship and the related paradox by investigating how IT alignment and organizational and environmental elements combine into configurations to affect agility. The sensing and responding dimensions of agility are viewed as two independent outcomes (Overby et al., 2006) and IT alignment is further divided into intellectual and social alignment (Reich & Benbasat, 1996, 2000). Then, by examining the combinatorial effects of intellectual and social alignment on sensing and responding capabilities in different contexts of organizational size, firm strategy, and environmental dynamism, fsQCA is used to identify multiple equifinal configurations associated with outcomes based on dyadic survey data from 135 pairs of business and IT executives. Finally, the links between IT alignment and agility are theorized, and the proposed concrete configurational recipes reveal how firms can apply intellectual alignment and social alignment to achieve sensing and responding capabilities according to specific contexts.

## 6.1 Theoretical Implications

This paper makes three significant theoretical contributions to the alignment and agility literature. First, it extends the IT-enabled agility literature in theoretically elaborating and empirically confirming that IT alignment has heterogeneous effects on sensing and responding capabilities. Prior scholars have acknowledged that agility includes two key dimensions of sensing and responding and have confirmed that different dimensions of agility have different antecedents and thus should be treated discriminatively (Overby et al., 2006). IT alignment may also exhibit different influences on each dimension of agility. However, few studies have conceptualized and operationalized these two dimensions as independent constructs and investigated their relationships with IT alignment (Bradley et al., 2012; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). By distinguishing sensing and responding, our results show that both intellectual alignment and social alignment have opposite (enabling and inhibiting) effects on sensing, while for responding, social alignment always improves it and intellectual alignment always impedes it or is irrelevant, with one exception—a positive effect for large analyzers in dynamic environments. This study goes one step further than Liang et al. (2017) by empirically showing that intellectual and social alignment have distinct effects on sensing and responding capabilities and by delineating a more detailed and granular relationship between IT alignment and agility.

Second, this study explicates the complex nonlinear and asymmetrical effects of IT alignment on agility from a configurational perspective. Instead of adopting a traditional deductive correlation-based approach that examines the linear net effects of IT alignment on agility (Liang et al., 2017; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), we contend that the configurational theory approach can provide refined building blocks for a thorough assessment of the complex and nonlinear impacts of IT alignment on organizational agility. By using fsQCA, we explore how intellectual and social alignment and organizational and environmental elements combine into multiple configurations to achieve sensing and responding capabilities. Our results indicate that IT alignment does not always positively or negatively affect agility, thus confirming its nonlinear effects. Specifically, IT alignment can produce sensing or responding in some configurations but may be irrelevant or even counterproductive in others. We then develop several general propositions on how intellectual alignment and social alignment act as enablers or inhibitors and work together as complements or substitutes effectively in firms’ achievement of sensing and responding capabilities from the contextual perspective. This study is an initial attempt to use a nonlinear analytical approach. Our findings also respond to calls for research on the contextual effects of IT alignment that enable or disable firms in achieving agility (Tallon et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2017).

Third, the study expands the IT alignment literature by endeavoring to better understand and manage the IT alignment-agility paradox. In line with prior research (Liang et al., 2017; Overby et al., 2006; Reich & Benbasat, 2000), we explain the choices of intellectual and social alignment and two dimensions of agility, as well as the contextual factors of organizational size, firm strategy, and environmental dynamism. Then, we empirically confirm that intellectual alignment complements social alignment for large analyzers in a dynamic environment to achieve sensing and responding (Zhou et al., 2017) while it can substitute for social alignment for small defenders so that they can achieve sensing capability regardless of the dynamics of the environment. Apart from this, social alignment always plays an enabling role while intellectual alignment always plays an inhibiting role in firms’ achievement of responding capability. As such, our study offers novel insights into the alignment-agility paradox, showing that IT alignment may impede only one dimension of agility while enhancing another. Although these conclusions are different from the proposed belief that intellectual alignment always positively affects agility (Bradley et al., 2012; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011) and that social alignment is an antecedent of intellectual alignment and its effects on organizational outcomes are fully mediated by intellectual alignment (Preston & Karahanna, 2009), these findings enrich the understanding of the different roles that intellectual alignment and social alignment play in firms’ achievement of two dimensions of agility and provide new insights into the IT alignment-agility paradox through offering a more explicit interpretation of the effects of intellectual and social alignment on sensing and responding from configurational and contextual perspectives.

## 6.2 Practical Implications

Our study offers three suggestions for practice. First, the heterogeneous roles of intellectual alignment in achieving sensing and responding capabilities suggest that firms should strategically implement this alignment. Although firms need to focus on aligning business and IT strategies, more flexible resource investments in intellectual alignment should be made according to different contexts. In dynamic environments, analyzers should not only invest in soft and intangible resources to enhance social relationships and facilitate shared understanding between business and IT managers but they should also commit resources to improving business-IT fit. For defenders in dynamic environments, as huge investments in intellectual alignment may not improve agility, they should build a high level of social alignment. However, in stable environments, both building a formal alignment between business and IT strategies and increasing real-time and informal information exchange can facilitate common knowledge and collective sensemaking. This means that in applying intellectual alignment to improve agility, not all types of firms will enjoy equal payoffs (Chan et al., 2006) and not all dimensions of agility will be equally enhanced. To take full advantage of intellectual alignment and avoid its negative effects in achieving agility, managers should carefully invest resources based on specific contexts.

Second, except for large analyzers working in dynamic environments, intellectual alignment always plays an inhibiting role while social alignment always plays a core enabling role in influencing responding. With high social alignment, business and IT executives can more easily arrive at a shared understanding and further coordinate the decision-making process, while intellectual alignment may impede information exchange and further restrict responding actions. To achieve high responding capability, firms should emphasize the establishment of social alignment and “decouple” intellectual alignment to decrease its inhibiting effect. But as analyzers in dynamic environments need to simultaneously address the demands of efficiency and innovation, managers should pursue intellectual and social alignment at the same time. This can ameliorate the challenges caused by dynamic environments and large organizational size.

Third, social alignment enables prospectors to achieve sensing or responding capabilities in dynamic environments only; as a result, prospectors should carefully assess the environment before applying IT alignment to achieve agility, although they may not need much IT alignment to obtain it. Finally, the multiple equifinal configurations of high sensing and responding capabilities imply that firms can choose a unique configuration to achieve agility depending on the context rather than following other successful cases. Managers have various options for sensing and responding that can take full advantage of the firm’s capabilities and resources and thus reduce the risk of failure.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Research Directions

This study has several limitations. First, our fsQCA results may show partial paths that produce high sensing or responding capabilities. However, more studies are needed to further test and complement our theoretical propositions. For example, we did not find a path to sensing for large prospectors in dynamic environments. Although we provide an alternative explanation that they may tend to seek new products or markets and sense external environmental changes proactively with less alignment enabling, more research is needed to make our theory more generalizable by exploring alignment-enabled solutions.

Second, there are many other potential contextual variables, such as firm history, firm culture, firm IT spending, CIO position, and systems of knowing, that may also have contextual effects on organizational agility. Future research could develop a more comprehensive framework to identify and elaborate on these elements to gain a deeper understanding of the IT alignment-agility relationship. Third, organizational agility is a complex construct with rich connotations. While we conceptualize agility as sensing and responding based on the classic general idea, more dimensions of agility, such as sensing, decisionmaking, and acting (Park et al., 2017), or sensing, seizing, and transforming/pivoting (Meier & Kock, 2021; Teece et al., 2016) could portray agility in more detail. Future research could find valuable insights by exploring the effects of alignment at each step.

Fourth, although intellectual alignment as a peripheral element can complement social alignment in some cases, its inhibiting effect on responding capability still predominates. We nevertheless stand by our various findings that intellectual alignment can facilitate rapid response to change (Tallon, 2008) in specific contexts. Given that our use of cross-sectional data is justified, a longitudinal design could further delineate the causal dynamics between intellectual alignment and agility.

Since its effects on responding capability may evolve over time, future research is needed to validate this conjecture.

Fifth, our paper only shows how organizations can achieve a high level of agility through IT alignment in certain contexts. To avoid the negative impact of IT alignment on agility, we also need to identify whether any configuration exists that leads to a low degree of agility because, in an asymmetric relationship, a high/low value of an antecedent condition indicates a specific value of an outcome without saying that the inverse value of that antecedent condition is also related to the value of the outcome condition (Maier et al., 2021). Future research on how IT alignment in different contexts might lead to organizations’ inability to achieve agility could enrich our understanding of alignment-agility theory and practice.

Finally, some factors that limit the general applicability of our solutions also guide our suggestions for future research. Since the survey data that we collected is from the Chinese shipbuilding industry, the generalizability of our findings is limited by particular cultural values and institutional pressures in China (Liang et al., 2007). Also, although the data is relevant and fits well with our research context, it is somewhat dated and may not reflect current changes in contemporary business environments. Future studies could be conducted in multiple industries and countries and more up-to-date data could be collected to confirm and complement our findings. In addition, similar to many other IS studies, we measured firm strategy using a simple classic typology (Miles & Snow, 1978). However, in current dynamic business settings, firms may apply hybrid or mixed business strategies (Chan et al., 2006) to meet complex and changing business requirements. Therefore, future research should better evaluate how these novel strategies relate to agility.

## 7 Conclusion

In today’s dynamic and digitized business environments, organizations are increasingly focusing on investing IT resources to achieve and maintain competitive advantage from the alignment between business and IT strategies. This allows them to be agile by sensing and responding to external market opportunities and threats (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). Yet there is also a concern about the real effect of such invested IT alignment on agility both in academia and industry, which gives rise to the IT alignment-agility paradox (Liang et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2018). Firms therefore need to better understand these complex and changing relationships according to specific contexts so that they can configure appropriate types of alignment to achieve agility (Zhou et al., 2017).

Using configurational and contextual perspectives, our research reveals that intellectual and social alignment may have different combinatorial effects (complementary or substitution) and play multifaceted (positive or negative) roles in firms’ achievement of sensing in specific contexts. For responding, however, social alignment always plays an enabling role while intellectual alignment always plays an inhibiting role or is irrelevant, with one exception—a positive effect for large analyzers in dynamic environments. As it is difficult to state definitively that IT alignment always enhances or inhibits agility, managers should correctly assess their organizational and environmental contexts and then decide whether and which dimensions of IT alignment should be configured or reconfigured to build agile organizations. We hope that our study will open up new paths for future research and advance IS theory to generate a more holistic and comprehensive understanding of the IT alignment-agility paradox.

## Appendix E: Shared Domain Knowledge

We follow Reich and Benbasat’s (2000) approach to calculating shared domain knowledge (SDK) between business and IT executives. As shown in Table E1, business knowledge of IT executives consists of two dimensions: their work experience in the shipping industry and experience as business managers. IT knowledge of business executives also includes two dimensions: experience in overseeing large IT projects and familiarity with new technology. The data for business knowledge was collected from IT executives, while the data for IT knowledge was collected from business executives. Each dimension of business knowledge can be rated as high, moderate, or low. Business knowledge is high if both of its dimensions are high, low if both dimensions are low, and moderate otherwise. IT knowledge of business executives is rated in the same way. After rating the levels of the business knowledge of IT executives and the IT knowledge of business executives, SDK can be calculated. If both business and IT executives have a high level of knowledge, SDK is high (coded as 1). If neither rating is high, SDK is low (coded as -1). If there are mixed levels of knowledge, SDK is moderated (coded as 0). SDK has strong correlations with all six items of social alignment.

## Appendix H: Truth Tables for High Sensing and Responding Capabilities

The column in the truth table for the outcome (i.e., sensing and responding) shows the extent to which each row consistently produces the outcome (Park and Mithas, 2020), that is, how likely the row (or configuration) is a consistent subset of the outcome. In fsQCA, there are two consistency measures: raw consistency and PRI (proportional reduction in inconsistency) consistency. In this paper, we mainly applied the rule suggested by Greckhamer (2016) to decide the cutoff for consistency. First, for a combination (or row) to reliably produce high agility, its raw consistency should be above 0.80 (Ragin, 2008). Second, to further make sure our results are valid, we consider PRI consistency, which can additionally eliminate the influence of cases that have simultaneous membership in both the outcome and its complement (i.e., y and \~y). Following Greckhamer (2016), we choose 0.65 as a cutoff for PRI consistency to guarantee the cases have membership in the outcome, rather than its complement (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). Taken together, we decide on a raw consistency of 0.80 and a PRI consistency of 0.65 as cutoffs for the high sensing and responding capabilities groups. We set a value of 1 in the sensing or responding columns for rows with raw consistency and PRI consistency higher than the cutoffs, where otherwise, it is set to 0.
