# Assessing ERP Post-Implementation Success at the Individual Level: Revisiting the Role of Service Quality

## Abstract

While previous studies have devoted great attention to success of ERP implementation, this study aims to investigate how the different qualities of an ERP system affect its post-implementation success from the user’s perspective. We refined DeLone and McLean’s IS success model to examine the relative importance of ERP system quality, information quality, and service quality on post-implementation success, with users’ satisfaction, users’ individual benefits, and a very critical yet seldom investigated variable, users’ extended use of ERP systems, as the outcome variables. Our research model was empirically examined with data from 151 ERP users. We found that service quality, in conjunction with system quality and information quality significantly affects ERP post-implementation success in terms of user satisfaction. More importantly, service quality was found to significantly interact with information and system qualities to promote an ERP system’s post-implementation success by increasing employees’ extended use. The results also support our argument that extended use is a rich measure that captures contextualized usage of an implemented ERP system. From a theoretical point of view, this study extended the IS success model by explicating the missing role of service quality as an enhancing force of information and system qualities, and demonstrating the importance of contextualizing usage behavior while determining a system’s success.

## 1. Introduction

Over the past few decades, organizations have made significant investments in Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems that enable them to synergize 4M resources (man, money, material, and machines), integrate business data throughout organizations, and support critical business functions including manufacturing, inventory management, human resources, sales, delivery, customer service, and finance (Davenport, 1998). An ERP system is generally considered an expensive investment with costs ranging from half a million to \$ 300 million, with an average cost of \$ 15 million (Rettig, 2007). Despite Consulting, 2010). A recent survey found that 57 percent of organizations suffered operation stoppages after ERP implementation (Panorama Consulting, 2008), and 67.5 percent failed to realize half of their projected benefits after implementation (Panorama Consulting, 2010). Companies often encounter great difficulties in using, maintaining, or enhancing ERP systems after implementation. These challenges may turn the costly investment into a post-implementation failure or even lead to a business disaster (Pan et al., 2011). Therefore, the ―ERP post-implementation,‖ phase also called the ―post go-live‖ stage, is viewed as being critical (Chou and Chang, 2008; Galy and Sauceda, 2014; Ha and Ahn, 2013; McGinnis and Huang, 2007; Zhu et al., 2010).

However, extant literature on ERP applications tends to focus on issues related to their adoption and implementation, with limited attention devoted to the post-implementation stage. For example, Esteves and Bohorquez’s (2007) review study indicates that the number of ERP publications geared towards the implementation phase is 47 percent, versus 15 percent on post-implementation usage. Other meta-analytic studies (e.g. Grabski et al., 2011; Moon, 2007) also report the paucity of research into ERP systems after implementation. Some scholars have acknowledged this gap and consider it a focus for the second wave of ERP research (Gattiker and Goodhue, 2005; McGinnis and Huang, 2007; Zhu et al., 2010).

Additionally, while the majority of research has investigated ERP success at the organization-level, focusing on consequences such as profits, costs, or market share, etc. (Pan et al., 2011; Zhu et al., 2010), few studies have concentrated on users’ perspectives. Assessing post-implementation success of ERP systems from the perspective of individual users is crucial because underachievement of the implemented ERP systems may be due in part to underutilization of the systems by the users (Hsieh and Wang, 2007; Jasperson et al., 2005). In line with this argument, research also suggests that positive impacts for the organization result from accumulated benefits that individuals obtain from their use of the ERP packages (e.g., Hwang and Xu, 2008; Ifinedo et al., 2010).

Thus, in this article, we extend current ERP literature by revisiting DeLone and McLean’s information systems success model (D&M IS success model; DeLone and McLean, 1992, 2003) and proposing a framework that explains the success of an ERP system at the post-implementation phase from individual users’ perspectives. Specifically, this study addresses two research issues that deserve further attention. The first gap is the need for understanding of users’ ―extended use‖ of an ERP system and its relationship with other components of the D&M IS success model while evaluating the success of the implemented ERP systems. Compared with other success constructs in the D&M model (e.g., system use, user satisfaction, and net benefits), extended use is a success construct that is often overlooked and its relationship with other success constructs is not well understood (Petter et al., 2008). Petter et al. (2008) conclude that the inadequate understanding of ―system use‖ seems to be largely caused by oversimplified or unspecified measures of this construct. While using an implemented ERP system, employees are required to use the system to perform their routine, making use/nonuse or frequency of use an insensitive indicator of individual impact. However, employees do have discretion or flexibility in deciding the extent of their system usage. Firms that intend to further realize the benefits promised by mandatory systems must shift their employees from simple and shallow use during initial adoption to ―extended use,‖ because full utilization of the system constitutes the basis for the system’s success (DeLone and McLean, 2003; Hsieh and Wang, 2007). Hence, apart from previous studies that focused on the simple dichotomous use decision, or amount of usage (e.g., frequency, time, etc.), this study turns to the notion of extended use, which refers to using a wider range of functionalities of the system for work productivity (Hsieh and Wang, 2007). In this way, we can better capture the important aspect of an ERP syste complex IS that permits employees to use the system at different levels of sophistication (Moore, 2002). By aligning the notion of extended use with the mandatory nature of ERP systems, because the system of investigation has important implications in explaining extended use (Hsieh and Wang, 2007), this study complements the literature on assessing an ERP system’s success after its implementation.

The second gap that needs to be addressed is the lack of understanding of the interrelationships among information quality (IQ), system quality (SQ), and service quality (ServQ) after ERP systems are implemented. In an effort to re-specify their original model, DeLone and McLean (2003) incorporate ServQ to complement the other quality dimensions, because evaluating success of an IS would be incomplete when services provided by IS personnel are not properly considered. Despite increasing attention to the effects of ServQ in IS research, our knowledge regarding its roles in facilitating success of an IS remains fragmented. Petter and McLean’s (2009) meta-analytical study reports that only few empirical tests of the D&M model examined ServQ and none of those studies found significant relationships between ServQ and other IS success constructs. Although researchers have urged the need to explicate the interactions among IS success constructs (DeLone and McLean, 1992, 2003; Petter et al., 2008; Sabhewal et al., 2006), existing investigations of the updated D&M model assumed the three types of quality do not affect each other. While the exceptional studies that examined the interrelationships among the three quality dimensions have proposed a mediation model (Ding and Straub, 2008; Xu, et al., 2013), the relationships of constructs within the D&M model can vary across contexts (DeLone and

#

McLean, 2003).

ERP systems are complex in nature and their deployment is typically in conjunction with continuous reengineering of business processes. Thus, despite of their initial acceptance of the systems, users’ utilization of such complex systems and realization of anticipated benefits at the post-implementation stage may rely even more on IS personnel support and services, such as user training and bridging communication between users and the vendor (Esteves and Bohorquez, 2007). Arguably, impacts of an implemented ERP system’s quality on use are, in part, a consequence of the interplay between ServQ of IS staff and the system’s IQ and SQ. Toward that end, this study is designed to contribute to IS literature by extending the D&M model and refining previous assumptions on the independency among IQ, SQ, and ServQ. Specifically, we shed light on the missing role of ServQ (i.e., its moderation on the influences of an adopted ERP system’s IQ and SQ in evaluating the system’s success). By highlighting the role of system’s IQ and SQ at the post-implementation stage, this study advances knowledge in the area of ERP post-implementation performance that has predominantly focused on services provided by vendors or external consultants (e.g., Ifinedo et al., 2010; Wang and Chen, 2006).

## 2. Literature Review and Theoretical Foundation

We first review existing studies focusing on ERP post-implementation performance. As summarized in the review table (see Appendix A), most prior studies examined ERP post-implementation performance at the firm level (i.e., the first nine articles in the Table). Typical outcome variables include firms’ profits, product quality, market value, productivity, process efficiency, shareholder return, and so on. Few of them have studied ERP post-implementation at the individual level. Notably, those studies tend to focus on one or two outcome variables, such as user satisfaction, user performance, or users’ intention to use

ERP systems. For example, Sykes et al. (2014) investigate how employees’ ERP post-implementation job performance is predicted by workflow and software advice. Through the lens of social network structure, Sasidharan et al. (2012) have found individual’s post-implementation performance to be a function of his/her in-degree and betweenness centralities. In other studies, researchers employed satisfaction (e.g., job satisfaction; satisfaction with the system) to measure individual-level post-implementation success (e.g., Morris and Venkatesh, 2010; Sedera and Tan, 2005). Lastly, others have considered individual’s use of the system as a proxy for the success of an implemented ERP and how such use associates with job design (Ke et al., 2012) and learning (Boudreau, 2003). There is no doubt that these studies have advanced our understanding of ERP post-implementation success at the individual level; however, that each of them focuses on only one or two ―success‖ constructs has resulted in fragmented knowledge regarding an ERP system’s post-implementation success.

Noting this literature gap, researchers (i.e., Gable et al., 2008; Sedera et al., 2004) argue the need to re-conceptualize IS success. Specifically, Gabel et al., (2008) model the success of ERP systems based on the assumptions that IS success is multi-dimensional and positive impacts of the IS are the ultimate outcomes sought by organizations. Their IS-impact model suggests that four dimensions, including IQ, SQ, individual impact, and organizational impact, can effectively define the system’s success. Despite their notable contributions in re-conceptualizing IS success, Gable et al.’s IS-impact model remains limited in explaining an implemented ERP system’s success at the individual level. First, their model did not consider ServQ, which has been proposed as an important factor when studying IS success (DeLone and McLean, 2003). Second, the IS-impact model, which intends to measure success at the organization level, has excluded success measures (i.e., extended use, satisfaction) that are critical to individual users who have adopted the ERP systems. Finally, relationships among the success measures of an implemented ERP system remain unknown.

DeLone and McLean’s (1992, 2003) IS success (D&M IS Success) model provides a useful lens through which we can understand ERP post-implementation success in a more integrative way. By reviewing over 100 measures used in 180 studies for definitions of IS success and their antecedents, DeLone and McLean (2003) developed an IS success model (see Figure 1) that systematically combines previously reported measures. The IS success model can be interpreted as follows: an IS system can be evaluated by three quality dimensions, IQ, SQ, and ServQ. In the context of ERP, IQ and SQ indicate the resources derived from the ERP system itself that impact users, which are technically-oriented, whereas ServQ could signal the resources controlled by the IS staff to affect users, which are human-oriented. These quality factors affect users’ use/intention to use and satisfaction. Through use or intention to use, certain net benefits will be achieved. The D&M IS Success model is the most widely cited and has made valuable contributions to our understanding of IS Success (Gable et al., 2008). In addition to many studies that have tested the D&M IS Success Model, Delone and Mclean (2004) validated their own model in the context of e-commerce.

Building on DeLone and McLean (2003) and Gable et al. (2008), we model ERP post-implementation success as multi-dimensional, with the aim to contribute to the literature in two aspects. First, this study examines the direct and indirect effects of ServQ on individual impact. A recent meta-analysis indicates

#

that ServQ has received scant research attention (Petter and McLean, 2009). Indeed, research on ERP systems has rarely examined human-based ServQ in the presence of IQ and SQ (e.g., Cao et al., 2013; Ha and Ahn, 2013; Zhu et al., 2010). Among the few studies that have examined human-based ServQ, their focus lies on service provided by the ERP system vendor or ERP consultants (Tasi et al., 2011; Wang and Chen, 2006). Taken together, these findings suggest much work is needed to learn how ServQ of internal IS personnel, together with IQ and SQ, determine the success of implemented ERP systems. Second, this research contextualizes measures of individual impact. DeLone and McLean (2003) noted that the selection of IS success dimensions and constructs should be contingent on the objectives and context of the empirical investigation. Given that it is employee’s use of an implemented mandatory system in which we are interested for this study, ―extended IS use‖ would more accurately reflect the success of the ERP system than constructs such as frequency of use. More importantly, we delineate the interrelationships between those success measures. We describe our model in more detail in the following section.

## 3. Research Model and Hypotheses

To address the two identified research gaps, we modified the D&M IS success model to elucidate how individual users assess the success of an implemented ERP system (Figure 2). The research model consists of six constructs that are categorized into two groups, one for the three types of quality and another for the impacts on users (i.e., user satisfaction, extended use, and individual benefits). By taking implemented ERP systems, the framework explicates how the three quality constructs independently, and through their interactions, affect user satisfaction and extended use. In addition, the framework posits that extended use and satisfaction will influence individual benefits.

## 3.1 Relationships among Measures of Individual-Level Impacts

## 3.1.1 Individual Benefits

Individual benefits refer to ―the effect of information on the behavior of the recipient‖ (DeLone and McLean, 1992) or ―the extent to which the IS has influenced users’ capabilities and effectiveness‖ (Gable, et al., 2008). While the major objective of a firm is to maximize profits, it is essential to recognize that profits are generated from productive employees. When the effect arising from an information system is high for an individual, it is likely that the impact for the organization to which the individual belongs will be high (Ifinedo et al., 2010). Organization will experience a positive outcome only when its constituting entities are positively impacted (Ifinedo et al., 2010). Therefore, this study investigates individual’s (i.e. employee’s) benefits rather than firms’ profits. The post-evaluation of an ERP system on an individual’s benefits is necessary since it not only justifies the investments but also provides insights about how to better manage employees’ behavior (Uwizeyemungu and Raymond, 2010). In this study, we define individual benefits as users’ perceptions of the importance and usefulness of the ERP system affecting their capabilities and effectiveness.

## 3.1.2 Extended Use

In the original D&M IS success model, system use refers to ―recipient consumption of the output of an information system‖ (DeLone and McLean, 1992), and use is often measured as times of visits to an IS and transaction execution (DeLone and McLean, 1992, 2003). However, many researchers suggested that use/nonuse, or amount of use (e.g. time, frequency) may not adequately explain IS Success in some contexts. For example, measuring the presence of system usage only makes sense for voluntary users (Gable, et al., 2008; Ifinedo et al., 2010; Seddon, 1997); in contrast to where using ERP is mandated in an organization since users have to use ERP systems to perform their routine tasks. Since no single measure of system use is applicable across contexts, a research call has been made for better conceptualizing and operationalizing this construct based on the study context (Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006). According to Burton-Jones and Straub (2006), definition of individual-level system use should specify ―an individual user’s employment of one or more features of a system to perform a task.‖ Toward that end, a meaningful measure of an ERP system’s use needs to consider characteristics of the system, the individual/user using the system, and the task performed with the system.

Based on this view, conceptualizing system use of the ERP system must recognize its being a rich, highly contextualized behavior. Given that system use typically involves system, users, and task, a rich measure of the system use should try to capture more or less of these elements (Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006). In this vein, we contend that extended use is an appropriate measure because it considers the elements of system context (i.e., breadth of use) and task context (i.e., variety of use in different tasks) in conceptualizing system use. For an implemented ERP system, although the use is mandated, a content valid and contextualized measure in this context must reflect the discretion of such usage and critical use behaviors that can contribute the system’s post-implementation success. Thus, extended use, defined as the extent to which employees are willing to use more functionalities of the system and apply the ERP system to execute more tasks, is consider an effective measure for assessing success of an ERP system (Bhattacherjee and Premkumar, 2004; Boudreau, 2003; DeLone and McLean, 2003; Park et al. 2007; Seddon, 1997).

Extended use is critical to post-implementation success of the ERP systems because most firms implementing an ERP rarely use their system to its fullest potential and realize the promised return on investment (Jasperson et al., 2005). Research found that, in the early stage of implementation, users often struggled to understand how to use ERP systems to support their jobs, and only used a small number of functions. This simple and shallow usage of ERP systems is frequently observed when users initially accept ERP systems (Hsieh and Wang, 2007). Over time, if users can find additional useful features and apply them to their jobs, they may consequently receive full benefits of the ERP system (Park et al., 2007). That is, by going beyond routine usage of the system, employees have the opportunity to exploit the richest potential assumed by the system to support their work, resulting in higher productivity and performance (Hsieh and Wang, 2007; Saga and Zmud, 1994). Accordingly, we hypothesize that

H1: Users’ extended use of ERP systems is positively associated with users’ individual benefits.

## 3.1.3 User Satisfaction

User satisfaction refers to ―recipient response to the use of the output of an information system,‖ or the degree to which users feel the IS meets their requirements (DeLone and McLean, 1992). Satisfaction is the consequence of users’ experiences during the stages of need arousal, information search, alternatives evaluation, purchase decision, and post-purchase behavior (Kotler, 1997). An ERP is a complicated information system since it involves almost all business processes, while users’ requirements for an ERP also cover all business processes, across vertical levels or horizontal sectors (Somers et al., 2003). Accordingly, the definition of user satisfaction in the present study is the extent to which users perceive a match between their requirements and ERP functionality. Evidence in the literature indicates that system use, though mandatory, is not sufficient to receive system benefits (Holsapple et al., 2005; Seddon, 1997). Instead, satisfied ERP employees are more likely to be productive, especially where the use of such systems is mandatory (Holsapple et al., 2005). Therefore, we hypothesize

H2: User satisfaction is positively associated with users’ individual benefits.

## 3.2 Qualities and Individual-Level Impacts

## 3.2.1 System Quality

System quality refers to measures of the information processing system itself (i.e., the quality of the performance of the IS from a technical perspective; DeLone and McLean, 1992; Gable, et al., 2008; Nelson et al., 2005). System quality is generally classified into (1) system-related dimensions and (2) task-related dimensions. System-related dimensions measure the characteristics that are unvaried across different uses and independent of task, context, or application, such as accessibility and reliability. Task-related dimensions measure the characteristics that depend on specific task and setting, such as flexibility, response time, and integration (Nelson, et al., 2005). If users experience a stable system, such as accessibility and reliability, and perceive the ERP system helps them better complete their jobs, they are more inclined to extend their use of the system’s functions and features. Furthermore, both the D&M IS success model (DeLone and McLean, 2003) and prior research (e.g. Chiu et al. 2007; Halawi et al. 2007; Seddon and Kiew, 1994) suggest a positive relationship between system quality and user satisfaction, and this relationship has been examined by many empirical studies. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H3a: Higher system quality is associated with higher level of extended use.

H3b: Higher system quality is associated with higher level of user satisfaction.

## 3.2.2 Information Quality

IQ refers to the measures of information system output (i.e., the quality of information the system produces, primarily in the form of reports or screens; DeLone and McLean, 1992; Gable, et al., 2008). IQ is generally classified into (1) contextual and (2) representational dimensions (Nelson, et al., 2005; Wang and Strong, 1996). The contextual dimension values the quality of information that ERP systems produce for users. The main measurement is the accuracy of information (whether information is accurate, updated, and consistent). Also included in this dimension is the degree to which the information is helpful, relevant, complete, and current. The representational dimension, on the other hand, reflects the degree to which information presentation is clear and interpretable; therefore, the key measurement is the format (Nelson, et al., 2005). It is reasonable to assume that, when users perceive that information is accurate, updated, consistent, relevant, complete, and the format is easy to understand, it would lead them to higher extended use and satisfaction levels. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

H4a: Higher information quality is associated with higher level of extended use.

H4b: Higher information quality is associated with higher level of user satisfaction.

## 3.2.3 Service Quality

ServQ refers to the overall support offered by the service provider (DeLone and McLean, 2003). Many scholars, such as Pitt et al. (1995), observed a drawback in that ―commonly‖ used measures of IS success focus on the systems rather than the services provided by IS departments or vendors. In response, DeLone and McLean (2003) included ServQ into the model and specifically indicated that, to measure the success of a single IT, information or system quality may be a very important factor, but for measuring the overall success of an information system, ServQ may become the most important factor. DeLone and McLean (2003) thus, adapted SERVQUAL; an instrument developed by Parasuraman et al. (1988) for assessing ServQ, into the IS domain, which includes the following five dimensions: tangibility, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy to measure ServQ. In this study, ServQ refers to the quality of services delivered by the IS department to users within the organization, evaluated based on the five dimensions listed above.

However, in a meta-analysis of 52 empirical studies based on or related to the D&M model, Petter and McLean (2009) found that only four of these studies tested the relationship between ServQ and use, while another three tested the relationship between ServQ and user satisfaction. More surprisingly, none of these studies empirically demonstrated significant relationships between ServQ and use or user satisfaction. In contrast, SQ and IQ consistently showed significant relationships with use and user satisfaction in many of these prior empirical studies. The meta-analysis shows that ServQ not only has received much less research attention, but also is not well understood for its relationship with other IS success constructs. Petter and McLean suggested a possible reason for these unexpected findings is lack of appropriate measures, and the ―use‖ construct needs to be carefully measured in different contexts. Toward this end, Hsieh et al. (2011) found that ―extended use,‖ in the context of using the customer relationship management (CRM) system, had a significant relationship with ServQ that was assessed by their customers. While services provided by IS personnel do not directly signify the quality of the ERP system itself, by having IS personnel provide good ServQ through courteous interactions with users (assurance), having users’ interests in mind and being understanding of their needs (empathy), and by solving users’ problem in a timely manner (responsiveness), users may feel encouraged to learn and try more of the available functions of the implemented system. The positive interactions with IS personnel may also promote more satisfactory experiences when using the mandatory system (Gorla et al. 2010; Wang and Chen, 2006). Based on this reasoning, we propose the following hypotheses.

H5a: Higher service quality is associated with higher extended use.

H5b: Higher service quality is associated with higher level of user satisfaction.

DeLone and McLean’s (2003) as well as other researchers (e.g., Seddon, 1997) strongly express the need to examine interrelationship among constructs in the D&M model; yet, research is generally silent on the interaction effects among the three quality dimensions. Among the few studies shedding light on this issue, Gorla et al., (2010) tested the relationship between SQ and IQ, while Wang and Chen (2006) examined the relationship between ServQ and SQ. Our study, thus, goes a step further to examine whether ServQ can enhance the impact of SQ and/or IQ in determining post-implementation success. In a mandated use situation, a system with high information and system qualities is essential but not sufficient for promoting extended use of more available functions. Support from IS personnel can contribute by increasing employees’ confidence in trying new functions. Thus, with equivalent levels of IQ and SQ, we predict that employees who experience high ServQ should feel more comfortable trying more ERP functions and feel more satisfied with the systems than those who received low ServQ. Accordingly, we hypothesize the following interaction effects:

H6a: Service quality enhances the relationship between system quality and extended use. H6b: Service quality enhances the relationship between system quality and user satisfaction. H7a: Service quality enhances the relationship between information quality and extended use H7b: Service quality enhances the relationship between information quality and user satisfaction.

## Control Variable: ERP Vendor

To determine whether the ERP vendor affects ERP post-implementation success, all ERP vendors investigated in this study were classified into two groups. The first consists of first-tier ERP vendors (i.e., the top players in the industry, such as SAP and Oracle) while the other group is comprised of medium-sized and smaller vendors.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1 Data Collection

To test our research model, a questionnaire was designed to collect data on each of the variables in the model. Each of the items on the questionnaire was reviewed for content validity by an expert panel comprised of faculty whose work focuses on ERP systems, as well as some practitioners and consultants from industry. The initial questionnaires were pilot tested on ten respondents randomly selected from the sample frame and, based on their responses, some items were revised for clarity. Q-sorting was also conducted to evaluate whether the measures could be categorized as per theoretical predictions (Straub et al. 2004).

Since our data is collected from single source, we took a number of steps suggested by Podsakoff et al. (2003) to reduce the possibility of common method bias, including appropriate instrument design and data collection procedures. First, we used multiple items for each construct and ensured the neutral wording of the items. Second, following the ―protecting respondent anonymity and reducing evaluation apprehension‖ strategy (Podsakoff et al., 2003), we assured respondents the anonymity of the responses and emphasized there were no right or wrong answers; each of which enabled them to answer questions as honestly as possible. Third, we used Podsakoff et al.’s (2003) ―separation of measurement‖ strategy and separated the measurement of predictors and criterion variables in the questionnaire. This diminishes the respondent’s ability and motivation to use his/her prior responses to answer subsequent questions<sup>1</sup>.

To collect data, the questionnaire along with a cover letter was sent by email or postal mail to ERP users in medium and large high-tech manufacturing companies in Taiwan. We choose the high-tech manufacturing industry because ERP systems are popularly adopted in this industry, and medium and large high-tech manufacturing companies are considered as the most experienced and mature firms in

#

using ERP systems (Mabert and Soni, 2003). The qualified respondents for the survey were ERP users (i.e., employees who have to use ERP systems to conduct their daily business tasks). In total, we collected 151 valid responses from 16 firms. The sample characteristics are shown in Table 1. We found that the respondents work in different departments in the high-tech manufacturing companies ranging from sales and marketing, technology, to administration. Up to 97 percent of our respondents use ERP systems at least once a week, 68 percent are frequent users who use ERP system every day, indicating our respondents have sufficient knowledge and experience to answer our questionnaire. In addition, about 81 percent of the respondents were non-managers while 19 percent were in managerial positions, and around 58 percent of our sample uses first-tier ERP vendors’ systems. Nearly 70 percent of our respondents have experience using an ERP system for more than two years, while the rest respondents have less than two years experience. It seems interesting to further investigate whether the novice ERP users may respond differently from experienced ERP users. We did some between-group tests regarding their response value to each variable used in our model. We used t-tests to compare the means, and further computed Kolmogorov-Smimov test statistics to examine the sample distribution of the two groups (Boes, et al., 1974). ## 4.2 Measures

All research constructs included in this study have multi-item scales derived from relevant literature. Each item in the survey employed a 5-point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree, 5=strongly agree), and a not applicable (NA) option was available for the respondents to choose. We now elaborate on our measures for the constructs, which are shown in Table 2 and Appendix B.

System Quality. SQ was measured with 8 items adapted from Gable et al. (2008). Following the suggestion of Nelson et al. (2005) to consider the dimensions of SQ using a spectrum ranging from system to task, we categorized the measuring items into system-related or task-related. Items for system-related quality assessed the characteristics of a system that are unvarying across different uses and independent of task, context, or application (e.g., ease of use, ease of learning system accuracy). Task-related quality comprised items measuring the characteristics that are best appraised in the context of a specific task and setting (e.g., task-related qualities of the system were modeled as reflective latent constructs, SQ was conceptualized as a second-order formative construct in this study. This conceptualization was used for two reasons. First, the causality directions move from the two dimensions to the overall assessment of SQ, rather than from the SQ to the two dimensions (Diamantopoulos and Siguaw, 2006). Second, the two dimensions as indicators of the higher-order construct, SQ, are not interchangeable (Jarvis, et al., 2003).

Information Quality. We measured IQ through a 5-item scale adapted from Gable et al., (2008), capturing two information system dimensions (i.e., contextual and representational) proposed by Nelson et al., (2005). Items for contextual dimension of information system assessed the degree to which the ERP system is helpful in performing particular tasks (e.g., relevance, availability), whereas items for representational dimension measured the extent to which information presentation effectively facilitates interpretations and understanding (e.g., format and conciseness). Based on the same reasoning for SQ, IQ is also modeled as a second-order formative construct composed the two dimensions, since changes in the contextual and representational quality of the system output will influence users’ perceptions of IQ of the ERP.

Service Quality. We refined prior ServQ measures (Parasuraman et al., 1988; Pitt et al., 1995) to represent the quality of the support that system users receive from the IS department and IT support staff. In line with the prior studies, a 22-item scale was used to capture the five dimensions of ServQ: tangibility, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy. Researchers suggest that ServQ should be a formative, higher order construct (e.g., Dabholkar, et al., 2000; Parasuraman, et al., 2005; Rossiter, 2002), because the dimensions generate the overall perception of ServQ. Thus, we model ServQ as a second-order formative construct formed by the five first-order dimensions, whereas the first-order dimensions are operationalized as reflective constructs.

Extended Use. To measure extended use, we adapted four items from Bhattacherjee (2001) and Hsieh and Wang (2007). The items were rephrased to correspond to assessing the extent to which the users intend to continue use and expand their use of the system, such as use more functions and use the system when performing different tasks. One item that was worded negatively to control for potential common-method bias assessed respondents’ discontinuance intention (i.e., recommend the firm to use an alternative information system).

User Satisfaction. Satisfaction with the ERP system was measured with a 4-item scale based on Wixom and Todd (2005) and Sedera and Tan (2005). The four items assessed the extent to which respondents feel satisfied with information received from the ERP system, their interaction with the system, service provided by the IS staff, and global satisfaction with the ERP system. We modeled user satisfaction as a formative construct here because overall satisfaction with an implemented ERP system is a composite of multiple measures; each measure captures different aspects of satisfaction in the occasion (Petter et al., 2007). In this instance, the combination of these measures defines an individual employee’s satisfaction with the system.

Individual Benefits. A 4-item scale developed by Gable et al. (2008) was adopted to measure individual benefits. According to Gable et al. (2008), individual benefits refer to the degree to which the focal system has affected the users’ capabilities and effectiveness on behalf of the organization. Specifically, the respondents were asked whether ERP systems influenced their learning, awareness/recall of job related information, decision effectiveness, and individual productivity. Following the same logic we used for modeling user satisfaction, we conceived individual benefits as a formative construct. As Jarvis et al. (2003) stated, changes in the formative measures cause changes in the underlying construct. Changes in each measure of individual benefits will cause changes in one’s perceived benefits of using the ERP system and, as a result, the measures of individual benefits are operationalized as formative.

## 5.1 Measurement model

We estimate the reliability, convergent and discriminant validity of each measurement scale to assess the measurement model. Construct reliability measures the stability of the scale based on an assessment of the internal consistency of the items measuring the construct. All the reflective constructs in our model shown in Table 2 have a composite reliability (CR) over the cutoff of 0.70, as suggested by Fornell and

Larcker (1981), implying high internal consistency. Convergent validity is verified through the t-statistic for each factor loading. As shown in Table 2, all factor loadings are greater than the typical cutoff value of 0.5 (Hair et al., 2010) and significant at the $p < . 0 0 1$ level. Cross-factor loadings are reported in Appendix D. Next, we performed tests to validate formative constructs contained in our research model. As for formative constructs, it is suggested that items of well-specified formative constructs should have significant weights (Cenfetelli and Bassellier, 2009; Petter et al., 2007). Multicollinearity among indicators should also be avoided, which can be checked by each construct’s variance inflation factor (VIF) that should be less than 3.33 (Diamantopoulos and Siguaw, 2006). Table 3 shows that the weights for indicators of ―user satisfaction‖ and ―individual benefits‖ are all significant $( p < . 0 0 1 )$ , together with the low VIF values indicating acceptable construct validity. In addition, our model includes three second-order formative constructs (i.e., SQ, IQ, and ServQ), each of which represents a broader contextual factor that covaries with several underlying first-order factors (Chin, 1998). Second-order constructs are modeled at a higher or more abstract level, and their use is common (Chin, 1998). As shown in Table 4, the weights of each dimension to its designated constructs are significant $( p < . 0 0 1 )$ , and the VIF values are low. Hence, a formative model seems to be suitable.

## 5.2 Results of Hypotheses Testing

We used SmartPLS 2.0 as the analytical software to evaluate the research model and hypotheses testing. The results of the structural model are shown in Figure 3. Our model offers adequate explanatory power with R-square values ranging from 41.4% to 63.2%. We provide the standardized path coefficient of each link suggested by our model, which allows for comparisons of the effects of different quality factors. First, we found that users’ extended use is significantly related to users’ individual benefits (ß=0.491, p<.001), which indicates that when individuals go beyond simple and routine use, but can extensively apply more ERP system features to support their work, their task performance could be significantly enhanced. H1 is supported. For a complex information system such as ERP, it is frequently observed that users may only use limited features or seldom apply task-related features to relevant operations (Davenport, 1998; Hsieh and Wang 2007). Therefore, our study provides a more sophisticated concept, extended use, which is a valuable perspective for achieving the full potential of a complex information system. Second, user satisfaction is also positively associated with individual benefits (ß=0.297, p<.001), and H2 is supported. The result empirically confirms previous studies’ observations that, though system use is mandatory, it is not sufficient to receive ERP benefits (Holsapple et al., 2005; Seddon, 1997). Instead, satisfied ERP employees are more likely to be productive, especially where the use of such systems is mandatory (Holsapple et al., 2005).

After we understand the importance of extended use and satisfaction, we go a step further to investigate the antecedents that may drive individuals’ extended use of ERP, and their satisfaction levels. Our results show that all the quality dimensions (system, information, and service) have a significant impact on user satisfaction, thus H3b, H4b, and H5b are supported by our data. On the other hand, extended use is significantly affected by SQ and IQ (ß=0.301, p<.001 and ß=0.258, p<.001, respectively), while ServQ shows only a marginal impact (ß=0.157, p<.1). H3a and H3b are supported, while H3c is not. However, we noted that ServQ has a dominant impact on users’ satisfaction (ß=0.507, p<.001).

H6 and H7 predict that ServQ will enhance the effects of IQ and SQ on user satisfaction and extended use. We found significant effects for ServQ in moderating the relationships between SQ and extended use (ß=0.310, p<.001) and the relationship between IQ and extended use (ß=0.235, p<.01), supporting H6a and H7a. In contrast to the predictions of H6b and H7b, ServQ is not a significant moderator for the effects of information and system qualities on satisfaction (ß=0.007, n.s. and ß=0.036, n.s., respectively). We have to note that ServQ’s main effect on satisfaction is already very significant (ß=0.507, p<.001). These results imply that the importance of ServQ is not only exerted directly on satisfaction, but also significantly increases the other two quality dimensions’ impacts on extended use, and eventually increase individual benefits. .

## 5.3 Alternative Model

To further understand how our proposed model could increase explanatory power in an ERP context, we tested the original D&M IS success model using our data. In other words, we replaced extended use with ―use,‖ which refers to users’ ERP system use frequency<sup>3</sup>, and removed the interaction effects triggered by ServQ. Figure 5 shows the results and we note three different findings. First, ServQ is not significantly associated with use, while its relationship with extended use is marginally significant. Second, though system and information qualities’ impacts on use and user satisfaction remain almost the same (in terms of magnitudes and significance), they only explain 3.8% of the variance of use, while our proposed model explains 41.3% of the variance of extended use. Third, while use is positively associated with individual benefits, in conjunction with user satisfaction, their explanatory power on individual benefits is only 31.2%, and a large portion of that is actually explained by satisfaction, while in our proposed model 48.6% of the variance in individual benefits is explained. The results obtained from the two models support our argument that ―extended use‖ is a rich and meaningful measure of system use for an implemented ERP system. Furthermore, the results also provide evidence for our proposed interaction effect of ServQ that can enhance system and information qualities’ impact on extended use, which is a much more valuable perspective for achieving the full potential benefits of ERP systems.

Lastly, as shown in Table 1, around 30 percent of our respondents have under two years’ experience using ERP, while the rests have more than two years’ experience. When conducting between group tests (i.e. and Kolmogorov-Smimov tests to examine sample distributions of the two groups), we found these inexperienced ERP users have significantly lower intentions for extended ERP use, and they have lower assurance from the service team. It seems that those inexperienced ERP users are still in their routinized use rather than extended use of ERP systems, and this finding confirms an earlier report that it takes 6\~18 months to routinely use ERP (Morris and Venkatesh 2010; Sykes et al. 2014). The results imply that it takes time to build trust and rapport between ERP users and the IS department (i.e., the assurance dimension) and if the IS department can build assurance and improve the perception of service quality for users, it might be possible for an individual to become an extended ERP user. As we understand the importance of extended use in achieving ERP post-implementation success, firms and IS departments should spend more efforts on triggering those inexperienced ERP users<sup>4</sup>.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusions

## 6.1 Implications for Research

This study, building on the D&M model, proposed and tested a model that explicates how ServQ, together with SQ and IQ, directly and interactively affect ERP post-implementation success from users’ perspectives. In addition, this study departs from extant studies that focused on simple use behavior, and goes a step further to investigate users’ extended use of the ERP systems. The results of this study offer important research implications for extending the D&M model and the assessment of a successful ERP system at its post-implementation stage.

This study contributes to the literature by bringing together the three quality dimensions of the D&M model and demonstrating that all of them have significant impacts on ERP post-implementation success. Despite that ServQ has been added to the updated D&M model since 2003, most ERP studies still focus on the rational aspects of user decision making (i.e., good ERP SQ leads to ERP success), failing to consider that satisfying the hedonic needs of ERP users with human-delivered service could engender extended use of a mandated system. Moreover, a recent meta-analysis on the D&M model (Petter et al., 2009) discovered the relationships between ServQ and other impact constructs within the model (i.e., intention to use, user satisfaction, use) were either not significant or not tested. This study not only replicates previous findings on the positive effects of IQ and SQ, but also shows ServQ to be a crucial determinant of user satisfaction. The confirmed relationship between ServQ and satisfaction is consistent with the argument maintained by marketing researchers that cognitive appraisal normally precedes emotional response (Bagozzi, 1992; Cronin and Taylor, 1992; Oliver, 1999). This finding also implies that while users need good system and information qualities to meet their utilitarian needs, their affective attitude (i.e., satisfaction) toward the ERP system is largely decided by human-delivered services of IS staff. Our findings complement previous studies on ERP systems by demonstrating that usage of the systems is bounded both rationally and emotionally.

With regards to the antecedents of extended use, we find significant effects of IQ and SQ while the influence of ServQ is relatively weak and only marginally significant (p<0.1). This result might stem from how ServQ is operationalized in this study. Unlike the studies that have conceptualized ServQ as one’s belief toward a specific system (e.g., Xu et al., 2013; Yang et al., 2005), which represents an object-based belief, ServQ in this study captures employees’ general assessment of IS staff’s services. Employees accumulate this holistic view of service quality throughout multiple encounters with IS staff on a variety of occasions related to use of different information technologies. Conceptualizing ServQ as a holistic belief is advantageous because it maps closely with the real-life scenarios in which employees interactions with IS staff are not restricted to the use of only one specific system. In this light, one’s assessment of ERP-based services is logically part of their general belief regarding the IS staff’s ServQ. Predicting object-based behaviors (i.e., extended use of the ERP system) with a general, holistic belief (ServQ of IS staff), however, could underestimate the real relationship between belief and behaviors. As Ajzen and Fishbein (2005) state, general belief and attitude is normally not a good basis for predicting object-based behavior. While the low correspondence between our measure of ServQ as a general view and the system-specific behavior (extended use of ERP system) justifies the weak effect of ServQ in this study, whether good ServQ in general is less or more influential than good ServQ for specific systems on extended use of the system remains unanswered and requires further investigation.

A key finding of this study is that ServQ can indirectly influence the success of an ERP system at the post-implementation phase; it fortifies the effects of IQ and SQ on extended use and, eventually, improves employees’ individual benefits. This attempt answers the call by DeLone and McLean (2003) to continuously explore the interrelationships among the constructs in the D&M model. To the best of our knowledge, very few studies (e.g., Ding & Straub, 2008; Xu et al., 2013) have empirically tested the interrelationships among IQ, SQ, and ServQ within the D&M model. These exceptional studies conceived ServQ as a mediator for the effects of IQ and SQ on users’ satisfaction or intention to use the system in the context of Web-based services. Such argument is reasonable because services are typically conducted by or through information technologies in the Web-based context, making IQ and SQ the prerequisites for delivering quality services. Thus, it is logical to theorize that IQ and SQ exert their effects on users by influencing SerQ. Nevertheless, this may not equitably apply to the case of using the ERP system in some respects. For example, employees often receive offline human-based services from the IS staff for use of the ERP system, rather than via technologies. Even if they interact with IS staff through IT-based interfaces, the ERP system is not the technology used by employees for communication. Also, employees may interact with IS staff on occasions not related the ERP system. Toward that end, it is unlikely that IQ and SQ of the ERP system exert their influences on extended use through the ServQ of IS staff. Alternatively, we found that ServQ could complement IQ and SQ in the condition when service is primarily delivered by IS staff and not by the system itself. Our investigation into the interaction effects of ServQ with IQ and SQ is a compelling extension of the updated D&M model that has been limited in speculating about the interrelationships among the quality dimensions. Although the moderating effect of ServQ on user satisfaction is not significant, contrary to our prediction, it is explicable when the strong direct effect of ServQ on user satisfaction is taken into consideration. In sum, this study is the first of which we are aware that examines both the main effects and moderating effects of ServQ. These results, together with previous findings on the mediation effects of ServQ, highlight the need to consider the context of use while defining interrelationship between the three qualities.

Our findings on the different effects of ―extended use‖ and ―use‖ in the D&M model contribute to IS research by answering the call for investigation of system use beyond simple and lean measures and the call for conceptualizing use based on research context (Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006; Hsieh & Wang,, 2007; Seddon, 1997). As Burton-Jones and Straub (2006) stated, system use is a complex activity that involves three elements: a user as the subject using the system, a system that is used by the user, and a task that constitutes a function performed by the user. Thus, it is essential to choose measures that are rich enough to capture the complex nature of system use in the specific context such that the roles of system use in determining a system’s success can be accurately estimated. In this vein, ―extended use‖ is a more appropriate measure of system use than a simple measure of use behavior (i.e., frequency of use) because the former assesses the users’ attitude toward continued use, the extent to which the ERP system is used and the variety of the use (i.e., in different tasks). That is, ―extended use‖ is a rich measure because it captures the complexity of intended use behaviors in the specific context. Our findings support this view by showing that ―extended use‖, compared with ―use‖ is a more powerful predictor of individual benefits, and can be explained by IQ, SQ, and ServQ to a much greater extent as an outcome variable. Such findings help explain why previous tests of D&M model have reported mixed results when system usage is investigated (Petter & McLean, 2009). Another important implication for research on the success of an ERP system is the need to carefully choose the measure of system use, which should be conceptualized based on the lifecycle of the system.

## 6.2 Implications for Practice

These findings offer useful implications for managers as well. First, our revised D&M model can be used

#

by managers who intend to realize the operational and strategic effectiveness of an implemented ERP system through strengthening employees’ individual benefits. The findings suggest that managers should be mindful of selecting metrics for monitoring employees’ use of an adopted ERP system, since system use is compulsory in such a context. Additionally, more management attention should be directed toward encouraging employees’ extended use of an ERP’s installed functionalities and applications of the system in a variety of work tasks. For example, ERP-related interventions, such as software and work process training, often stabilize at a point of being ―just enough‖ for employees to perform their assigned work tasks and activities (Jones et al., 2008), which are not designed to encourage further exploration of ERP functionality. For best results, managers should also devise interventions that are capable of elevating employees’ satisfaction because user satisfaction not only contributes to individual employees’ benefits of using the ERP system but is also the key to successful IS use in the long run (DeLone and McLean, 1992; 2003).

Second, ServQ of IS staff is vital to ensure the success of an adopted ERP system because it directly affects employees’ overall satisfaction and could exert an indirect effect by amplifying the positive influences of IQ and SQ with extended use. This suggests that managers need to closely monitor ServQ of internal IS staff, not only during delivery of ERP-related services but also in their regular service encounters with end-users. In order to help employees’ exploitation of the $\mathrm { E R P s }$ functionality, we recommend that managers continuously develop IS staff’s expertise in ERP applications so they can provide updated technical support and on-site education that end-users needed for use extension. Our findings also highlight the need to direct management efforts in developing an interpersonal oriented IS service team that can provide pleasant service experiences for the end-users.

## 6.3 Limitations

Our findings should be considered in light of a few limitations. First, our sample includes 151 respondents, of which 81 percent are staff and only 19 percent are managers. This is due to the fact that, in our data collection period, managers were more reluctant to join the study. However, mangers and staff may have different considerations on the weights of each quality factor since managers and staff use different functions of ERP systems (transaction vs. decision making), with a different use frequency (daily vs. monthly, etc.). Future studies may consider investigating whether differences exist between managers and non-managers so as to provide a more comprehensive evaluation. Second, this study assessed an overall evaluation of the IS staff’s services rather than their services focused on the ERP system. We call for future research to explore whether system-focused versus organization-wide services of IS staff would produce different results in terms of the direct effects and moderation effects on employees’ use of a mandated system, such as ERP. Third, our study focuses only on the manufacturing industry and different results may emerge when investigating other industries. For example, whether the role of ServQ may be strengthened in the service industry is unclear. Future studies could collect data from additional industries in order to understand the relative impact of the quality factors. Fourth, we assessed extended use and frequency of use to indicate system use of an adopted ERP system, which may be insufficient to capture all the essential elements of system use in the context. A promising avenue for future research is to follow the two-stage approach proposed by Burton-Jones and Straub (2006) to develop a measure based on a precise definition and conceptualization of system use in the specific context.

In conclusion, the current study contributes to the ERP literature since it focuses on the post-implementation phase while previous studies have devoted most attention to the implementation stage. Understanding ERP post-implementation success is crucial since ERPs are widely adopted, and most firms have gone beyond the go-live stage. As such, firms should shift their focus from implementation difficulties and challenges to post-implementation successes in order to receive the desired outcomes from their huge investments. The findings also extend ERP literature by viewing the success of a mandated system from the users’ perspective because users’ exploitative and explorative use of the ERP system is the basis leading to the success of the ERP system at the organization level. Finally, our scrutiny of the interaction effects of ServQ with IQ and SQ put forward a new research direction for improving the D&M model. While the firms usually rely on the vendor for implementation, in-house IS staff become essential to the end-users whenever there is an error to be corrected, optimization to be met, and upgrades or training to be provided (Esteves and Bohorquez, 2007). We hope this study encourages more research attention to explore the contextualization of system use and the relationships between ServQ and other quality dimensions of an IS.

## Appendix C. Common Method Factor Test

We followed Podsakoff et al. (2003) and Liang et al. (2007) and performed a common method factor test. Each construct was converted to a second-order construct, and each of its indicators was converted to a single indicator construct. To create the common method factor, we used indicators from all the constructs. Each single-indicator construct was modeled to be determined by (1) its second-order construct and (2) the method factor. The two squared path weights represented the variance explained by the substantive construct and common method, respectively. In Table A1, we found that for each indicator, the variance explained by its substantive construct was much greater than that explained by the common method factor, so there is less concern about common method bias in this study.
