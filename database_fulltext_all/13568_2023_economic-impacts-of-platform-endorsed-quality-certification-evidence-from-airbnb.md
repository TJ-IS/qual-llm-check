# ECONOMIC IMPACTS OF PLATFORM-ENDORSED QUALITY CERTIFICATION: EVIDENCE FROM AIRBNB<sup>1</sup>

Sanjeev Dewan The Paul Merage School of Business, University of California, Irvine Irvine, CA U.S.A. {sdewan@uci.edu}

Jooho Kim Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College, City University of New York New York, NY U.S.A. {jooho.kim@baruch.cuny.edu}

Tingting Nian The Paul Merage School of Business, University of California, Irvine Irvine, CA U.S.A. {tnian@uci.edu}

We contribute to the emerging literature on quality certification by digital platforms by studying the launch of the Airbnb Plus service, wherein the platform inspects properties and provides a badge that presumably signals the quality of the property and the reliability of the host. Our identification strategy relies on the fact that the Airbnb Plus service was launched in different cities at different times, and listings within the cities received the certification at different times. Using a staggered difference-in-differences estimation strategy in conjunction with suitable matching methods, we found that the Airbnb Plus certification increased the weekly booking rate of Plus listings by about 6.8% on average (direct effect). We also found some evidence that non-Plus listings saw a temporary decline in booking rate when one or more nearby properties received a Plus certification (externality effect). The net impact of the Airbnb Plus service on the platform itself was an annual increase in revenue of about \$37,500 for the average 2-kilometer zone in a U.S. city that included one or more Plus listings, as compared to matched zones without any Plus listings (local platform effect). We performed additional analyses, including a randomized experiment, to demonstrate the robustness of our findings. Overall, our results suggest that platform-endorsed quality certification has significant economic impacts—not just on the listings that receive the certification but on other listings on the platform as well as on the platform itself.

Keywords: Sharing economy, quality uncertainty, quality certification, externality

## Introduction

To combat information asymmetry, a number of sharing economy platforms have launched quality certifications to recognize the highest quality providers in their ecosystem (See Table 1 for details). Airbnb, the leading short-term rental platform, launched its Airbnb Plus service in 2018, which is different from certifications on other platforms in that the Plus badge signals the quality of the product (i.e., short-term rental property) in addition to the reliability of the provider (i.e., host). However, given the relatively low one-time inspection fee, and lack of capacity for ongoing quality monitoring, one might question whether the Airbnb Plus certification is a credible signal of quality or just “cheap talk.” We therefore address the following set of research questions. What is the impact of a Plus certification on: (1) the booking rate of the listings receiving this certification (direct effect), (2) other non-Plus listings nearby (externality effect), and (3) local platform revenues (local platform effect).

Considerable prior research has studied the theory and practice regarding quality assurance mechanisms in diverse industries (e.g., Dranove & Jin, 2010 for a survey) and specifically on digital platforms (e.g., Dewan & Hsu, 2004; Dimoka et al., 2012). However, relatively few studies have examined platform-endorsed quality certification, and most of them have examined online marketplaces (eBay, Taobao), rather than sharing economy platforms. A case in point is Elfenbein et al. (2015), who looked at the impact of the eBay Top Rated Seller (eTRS) badge in the U.K. marketplace, finding that the badge is associated with a higher probability of sale. Hui et al. (2016) studied the eTRS badge in conjunction with eBay buyer protection (eBP), and found that eTRS is associated with the exit of low-quality sellers while eBP mitigates seller uncertainty and reduces price premiums. In another related study, Hui et al. (2018) examined the impact of replacing the existing Powerseller quality certification with the more stringent eTRS program. We differentiate our study from those on online marketplaces (eBay, Taobao) since (1) the stakes of the transactions can be considerably higher, especially when it comes to staying in an unknown place with an unknown host, and (2) the products and services are highly unstandardized, making quality uncertainty an even more serious issue.

There are a couple of studies that have examined sharing economy platforms. For example, Reitveld et al. (2021) looked at the role of quality badges on Kiva, a leading social microlending site. They found that these badges had a positive impact on the performance of microfinance institutions (MFIs). The major difference from our work is that they focused on complementor performance rather than the performance of the direct market participants, as we do. In the setting of an online labor market, Barach et al. (2020) studied the matching of buyers (i.e., job openings) with freelance sellers. In a similar setting, Horton and Johari (2018) showed that buyer signaling of preferences for price and quality allows for the proper sorting of sellers to buyer type. Compared to these studies, our paper examines broader welfare implications of quality certification, going beyond direct effects (the performance of the certified entities) to study externality effects on noncertified entities and platform revenues and entry.

To address our research questions, we compiled daily panel data from Airbnb over the period of July-December 2018, which included around 50,000 listings in multiple cities around the world where Airbnb Plus service had been recently launched. Since the Airbnb Plus service was launched in different cities at different times and individual listings received the certification at different times, we were able to deploy a staggered difference-in-differences (DD hereafter) strategy to empirically measure the direct and externality economic impacts of the quality certification. To deal with potential treatment endogeneity (i.e., receiving a Plus certification), we used DD in conjunction with suitable matching methods, as we discuss in detail below.

To summarize our results, we found positive direct effects: receiving a Plus certification raised a typical listing’s booking rate by 6.8% on average. Through additional analysis using another third-party data set (AirDNA) we were able to conclusively demonstrate that the incremental bookings were not driven by any product changes made by the hosts in anticipation of the Plus certification. We also found some evidence of temporary externality effects in that non-Plus listings saw a small decline in booking rates when one or more nearby properties acquired a Plus certification. The net impact on local platform revenues was positive, to the tune of over \$37,500 per 2-kilometer zone on an annual basis. We also found evidence that the Plus program resulted in new net entry on the platform and found that the incremental host revenues were more evenly distributed at the margins, as measured by the Gini index. In sum, our results provide prima facie evidence that Plus certification is a credible signal of quality: it creates a separation in the revenue potential of listings with and without the certification and higher net revenues for the platform in areas where Plus listings are introduced. In the following section, we describe our data, followed by an overview of our empirical framework and identification strategy. We then present our results and conclude the paper by discussing the implications of our results.

## Data

We used a novel data set collected from Airbnb, which introduced Airbnb Plus in February 2018. According to Airbnb, Plus-certified listings need to satisfy several criteria: listings should maintain an average rating of 4.8, be thoughtfully designed, tastefully furnished, well-equipped with amenities, and immaculately maintained. Hosts apply for certification after paying a \$149 inspection fee. Once a listing passes the inspection, a Plus badge is added to the listing impression returned in search results. We compiled a data set of 49,850 listings in eight cities (Auckland, Austin, Cape Town, Mexico City, Montreal, Phoenix, Seattle, and Sydney) from July 2018 to December 2018, using a web crawler.

In order to enrich our empirical analysis, we additionally obtained a data set from AirDNA, a third-party data provider of short-term rental data. We procured detailed listing-level data for the city of Austin (one of the earliest cities to launch Airbnb Plus) with data spanning from January 2015 to December 2019. The AirDNA data set had additional timevarying variables, relative to our original Airbnb data set, such as response rate, a list of amenities for each listing, and whether a listing was instantly bookable or not. Also, the AirDNA data set had multiple property pictures for each listing which allowed us to control for image quality— estimated using a CNN-based deep learning, following Zhang et al. (2022)—in our CEM matching process (see Appendix for details). Lastly, data granularity was at the monthly level, with a relatively longer overall time span. We limited the use of AirDNA data to the period of January 2017 to March 2019, stopping just ahead of the start of exclusivity requirements in the Airbnb Plus program, which prohibited Airbnb Plus properties from being listed on other short-term rental platforms (starting in April-May 2019). We did this to ensure that our results on the impact Airbnb Plus primarily reflected quality signaling rather than exclusivity restrictions.

## Key Variables

Our unit of analysis is the listing-week, and we compiled the following set of variables. Our main dependent variable is booking rate. For a given listing on a given day, we first defined the daily booking ratio as the proportion of days in the following 30 days that the property was reported to be occupied. We then averaged these daily booking ratios in a given week to obtain the average weekly booking rate, which is our primary dependent variable.

With respect to listing characteristics, for each property, we collected its geolocation (latitude and longitude), zip code, the number of beds and baths, capacity, the textual description of the property, and the number of photos. Specifically, we observed whether a listing had a Plus certification in a given week or not, coding a time-varying dummy variable DD equal to 1 following the receipt of a Plus certification, and 0 otherwise. We constructed two proxies for the popularity of the neighborhood in which the Airbnb listing was located. First, we determined the number of other Airbnb listings within a 2-kilometer radius range of the focal listing. Popularity was also reflected in hotel room bookings, which we proxied by the number of hotel reviews posted on TripAdvisor.

Table 2 shows the summary statistics of selected variables in the data set. We see that Plus listings were much more likely to be Superhosts as compared to non-Plus listings (77% versus 37%); they received more reviews and had higher ratings (4.98 versus 4.85). Plus listings were also located in more popular neighborhoods as reflected in both the number of Airbnb listings and the number of hotel reviews within a 2-km radius range. Interestingly, the average daily price of Plus and non-Plus listings were almost exactly the same (around \$218), but the booking rates of Plus listings were substantially higher (64% versus 56%). As we discuss in the following section, we used matching methods to restrict the comparison of Plus listings with similar non-Plus listings, which is at the heart of our identification strategy.

## Empirical Framework and Identification Strategy

Our quasi-experimental empirical strategy exploits the fact that the Airbnb Plus service was introduced in different cities at different points in time, and within each city, listings received the quality certification at different points in time. A specific listing is defined as “treated” once it obtains an Airbnb Plus certification. The control group consists of listings that did not obtain Airbnb Plus certification in our observation window. Having access to observations of a large number of property listings over time, we were able to isolate a treatment group of listings that were non-Plus at the beginning of the observation window but become treated at some point—i.e., they received a Plus certification. To address the heterogeneity across properties, we used the difference-in-differences (DD) approach to “difference out” the pre-treatment trend associated with Plus properties.

## Difference-in-Differences Specifications

We used the following two-way fixed effects framework (for listing i in city j in time period t):

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & B o o k i n g \_ R a t e _ {i j t} = \alpha + \beta_ {1} D D _ {i t} + \\ & \beta_ {2} l n (N u m \_ R e v i e w s) _ {i t - 1} + \beta_ {3} (R a t i n g) _ {i t - 1} + \\ & \beta_ {4} (S u p e r h o s t) _ {i t} + \beta_ {5} (C a n c e l l a t i o n \_ P o l i c y) _ {i t} + \\ & \beta_ {6} l n (P r i c e) _ {i t - 1} + X _ {i t} + V _ {i} + T _ {j t} + \varepsilon_ {i j t} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

In the direct effects version of this model, each focal listing i in the treatment group received a Plus certification during the observation window, so that the indicator variable $D D _ { i t }$ is equal to 1 if listing i has a Plus listing in time period t, and is set to 0 otherwise. In the externality effects model, each focal listing i in the treatment group is a non-Plus listing, but in this case the indicator variable $D D _ { i t }$ keeps track of whether or not one or more of the neighboring listings (within a 2-km range) has received a Plus certification. Both the direct and externality models include a control group of listings, which we will describe later.

The coefficients $\beta _ { 1 }$ in both the direct and externality effects versions measure the average treatment effect. We used the lagged price $P r i c e _ { i t - 1 }$ as an instrument for the current period price (Villas-Boas & Winer, 1999). We included a listing fixed effect, $V _ { i }$ , to control for unobserved property-level timeinvariant factors. We included a city-time fixed effect, $T _ { j t } ,$ to capture all unobserved time-varying factors that affect all properties in a city temporally. Apart from these, the vector $X _ { i t }$ includes Capacity, Num\_Pictures, Deposit Fee, Cleaning Fee, and Response Rate (AirDNA data set only). Also, note that Num\_Reviews and Rating are lagged by one period to account for the fact changes in these variables take a little time to be reflected in bookings.<sup>2</sup>

We turn now to the construction of our control groups. As we saw in our discussion of descriptive statistics in the previous section, Plus listings are different from non-Plus listings in terms of a number of observable characteristics. Plus listings are also likely to be different in terms of a number of unobservable or intangible characteristics, such as style, design, range of amenities, cleanliness, etc. If the decision to apply for a Plus certification depends on these unobservable characteristics, then we may have an endogeneity problem. We dealt with this endogeneity problem through suitable matching methods. We used coarsened exact matching (CEM) on observable listing and neighborhood characteristics. To address potential endogeneity due to selection on unobservables, we used a novel propensity score matching (PSM) method that utilizes natural language processing of textual reviews and descriptions of listings, as discussed below.

In the direct effects model, for each focal listing in the treatment group, we first used coarsened exact matching (CEM) on the observable listing and neighborhood characteristics to find candidate listings for the control group (Iacus et al., 2012). In the AirDNA data set, we had multiple pictures for each listing, which we passed through a CNN architecture to predict the picture quality index (following Zhang et al., 2022), which we included as an additional CEM covariate (see Appendix for details). We included two proxies for neighborhood popularity: the number of Airbnb listings within a 2-km radius range of a focal listing and the number of hotel reviews posted on TripAdvisor within the same zone.

The CEM method described above can mitigate the selection on observables; however, it does not resolve potential endogeneity due to selection on unobservables. Researchers cannot directly observe intangible factors like attractive style, good design, well-equipped, well-maintained; however, these intangible attributes are likely to be reflected in textual reviews and listing descriptions. We mined textual reviews and descriptions from both Plus and non-Plus listings and fed them into a classification model, which predicted the likelihood, or propensity, of a property receiving a Plus certification. The control group was constructed by matching the propensity score of the treated focal listing with that of one or more of the CEM-matched non-Plus listings. The number of CEM-matched samples was 8,173 which consisted of 1,168 Plus and 7,005 non-Plus listings.

To unpack the propensity score matching steps in more detail, we started by scraping textual review and description data for the listings in our data set. We then preprocessed the data by going through multiple steps such as applying lower-case letters, removing punctuation and stop-words, and using lemmatization in order to convert the raw text to a suitable form to perform our analysis. We employed the tf-idf vectorizer to transform the preprocessed text into numerical feature vectors, which enabled us to analyze a set of features extracted from each listing’s textual information using a classification model. We adopted XGBoost, a recently developed decision tree-based algorithm based on a gradient boosting framework, as our classification algorithm. We additionally fine-tuned a few parameters by setting the max depth as 5 and the learning rate as 0.05 to control for the depth of the trees and ensure that the model learned the data slowly to avoid overfitting. The model yielded approximately 83% accuracy, 84% precision, 83% recall, and an F1-score of 83%. Using our trained model, we predicted the probabilities of Plus classification, which can be interpreted as propensity scores, for both Plus and non-Plus CEM matched pairs.

Next, we used the optimal matching package in R to match each Plus listing in the treatment group with one or more non-Plus listings in the matched CEM sample, on the basis of propensity scores, optimized to minimize the sum of absolute distance between the matched treatment and control units. In this way, we accounted for both observable characteristics and unobservable listing features embodied in textual reviews and descriptions that might play a role in the listing’s likelihood of obtaining an Airbnb Plus certification. We assume that the acquisition of the Airbnb Plus certification is orthogonal to any residual unobservables in the booking rate.

The construction of a matched control group for the externality effects model is along the same lines but with a few differences. The treatment group consisted of non-Plus listings that had one or more Plus listings in a 2 -km radius range. We first applied CEM on the basis of listing and neighborhood characteristics to compile an initial set of candidate control listings. We then narrowed that set using PSM to construct the final control group of listings, which did not have any Plus listings within a 2-km range of the focal listing—although one or more of them were classified as a Plus listing by the classification model. Note that we excluded focal listings in the control group if none of the listings in the 2-km range were predicted to be “Plus-like.”

## Parallel Trends Assumption

We check whether our models met the parallel trends assumption, which is a key condition for the DD model to be well-identified, we assign reverse negative integers to the pretreatment periods and positive integers to the post-treatment periods. For example, the normalized time periods are as follows: $( . . . , - 4 , - 3 , - 2 , - 1 , 0 , 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , . . . )$ where 0 is the time period when the treatment takes place for a listing. The specification for the relative time model is exactly the same as the one in Equation (1), except that the $\beta _ { 1 } D D _ { i t }$ term is replaced by $\scriptstyle \sum _ { t = - n } ^ { n } \lambda _ { t } Z _ { i t } .$ , where $Z _ { i t }$ is the interaction term of the treatment indicator and the (pre-treatment and posttreatment) time dummy variable at time t and $\lambda _ { t } ,$ is the corresponding coefficient. Here, we considered a 16-week time interval—eight weeks prior to the treatment period and eight weeks posterior (we also repeated the same analysis using AirDNA data to measure a longer term effect spanning 24 months). We used Period(-1) as the baseline period, the coefficient of which was normalized to zero. The externality model is similar, with the appropriate change in the interpretation of “treatment.” It is important to note that the coefficients for pre-treatment trends should not be statistically significant if the parallel assumption is to be satisfied.

## Results

We started with our baseline results for direct effects, followed by a number of additional analyses and robustness checks, followed by results for externality and local platform effects.

Starting with the direct effects, Table 3 shows the results for the Airbnb and AirDNA data sets. The coefficients of the key variable, DD, are positive and significant, indicating that receiving a Plus certification leads to a 6.8% to 6.9% increase in the book rate for the Airbnb and AirDNA data sets, respectively. To assess the economic significance of these estimates, we can calculate that the average increase in annual revenue associated with receiving a Plus certification amounts to \$3147.45 = 6.8% (direct effects coefficient) × \$218.64 (average price of a Plus listing pre-treatment) × 0.58 (average booking rate pre-treatment) × 365 (number of days in a year). This is a nontrivial incremental annual revenue for a Plus listing, suggesting that the Airbnb Plus certification is a credible signal of quality and not merely “cheap talk.”

As discussed in the previous section, the parallel trends assumption is verified using the relative time model, the results for which are presented in the first two columns of Table 4 (the result for the +/-12 months is omitted for the brevity of the paper but remains consistent). We see that all of the pre-treatment coefficients are insignificant, while virtually all of the posttreatment coefficients are positive and significant, which empirically establishes our key identifying assumption. The control variables all have the expected signs.

As a robustness check, we considered an alternative matching approach—look-ahead matching (LAM), as also used in Bapna et al. (2018). The rationale behind this approach is that those listings that were treated in an earlier period should be similar to those that became treated in a later period outside of the treatment window. The only difference between these two groups would be the timing of treatment. We defined a new treatment group as listings that became Plus in the first half of our time window, and the new control group as listings that did not receive Plus certification until sometime in the second half of our time window. We further applied CEM to ensure that the treatment and control groups were similar in terms of observable listing and neighborhood characteristics. Columns 3-4 in Table 4 report the estimation results using the LAM sample based on the relative time model. We found that the coefficients prior to the treatment time were not significant but those after the treatment time were positive and significant. These results are qualitatively similar to our baseline results of Columns 1-2, confirming the robustness of our matching methods.

## Robustness Checks

We conducted a number of additional robustness checks to enhance confidence in our analysis. Starting with the first column in Table 5, we implemented a placebo test wherein we generated random pseudo-treatment times for the Plus listings. The coefficient of the placebo DD variable is not statistically significant, lending added support to our causal claims. Another issue that we examined is the possibility of users booking in advance of their actual stay, in which case a property that was non-Plus at the time of booking may become Plus by the time of their stay. Due to these lags, the demand impact of a Plus certification may not fully manifest itself for a couple of weeks after certification. To address this concern, we substituted our dependent variables with the two-weekahead booking rates. The results are similar to the baseline results of Table 3.

Next, we examined the potentially confounding effects of product changes concomitant with Plus certification. If owners improved their properties or their description leading up to a certification, then our DD results would have confounded the effects of these product changes with the quality-signaling effects of the Plus certification. We were able to observe three types of product changes in our AirDNA and Insider Airbnb data sets: (1) an increase in the number of amenities, which we operationalized in terms of the Euclidean distance between the before and after vector of amenities provided in the unit; (2) improvements in the quality of property pictures, which we operationalized as the absolute difference in the main (i.e., cover) picture quality (estimated using a CNN deep learning approach à la Zhang et al. 2022); and (3) better property descriptions, operationalized as the difference between the entropy of the description before and after certification using anchored topic modeling (Gallagher et al., 2017).<sup>3</sup>

We also studied robustness to search rank effects because a purported benefit of a Plus certification is higher placement in search results (e.g., Ashworth, 2018). If the search rank effect is strong enough then it could potentially confound the effects of quality signaling, since both factors should affect booking rates in the same direction. Also, our research design relies on listing level rather than search session data, and it is therefore not suited to separate the two effects. To address this concern, however, we compared the rankings of Plus and non-Plus listings in search results for the eight cities in our data set. Specifically, we looked at the first 200 impressions in search results (about 10 pages on the desktop) in each of the eight cities and conducted a difference-of-means test of the Plus and non-

We found that in seven out of the eight cities in our data set, the differences in average search rank of Plus and non-Plus listings were not statistically significant in a one-tailed t-test. We found that the results (omitted in this draft) were essentially identical to the baseline results of Table 3. We can conclude that our results are robust to the search rank issue.

## Heterogeneous Treatment Effects

Extensive studies have investigated how price can enable firms to signal quality to imperfectly informed users (Milgrom & Roberts, 1986). If Airbnb Plus certification also serves as a signal of product quality, its impact should be smaller for properties with higher prices. To investigate this mechanism and the heterogeneous impacts across different types of properties, we partitioned our sample into properties based on the median price and then estimated the interaction effects in our original DD specification.

Our second partition is motivated by the observation that Airbnb tends to be more appealing to casual travelers, whereas hotels attract more business travelers who value hotel amenities, predictable and standardized quality, and convenience of location close to business or tourist centers (e.g., Zervas et al., 2017). In areas with a greater presence of hotels, we presume that Airbnb is less likely to be a popular option for prospective travelers. Therefore, properties receiving the Plus certification in these areas would not bring as much benefit as those in areas with a lower density of hotels. We segmented the properties according to the density of nearby hotels, proxied by the number of hotel reviews on TripAdvisor in a 2-km radius; we added an additional interaction term between DD and a dummy variable, indicating a property with above-median nearby hotel reviews, High\_hotel\_reviews.

Beyond price, Superhost status can serve as another quality signal to consumers. Superhost status is automatically granted by Airbnb if the property owner’s account meets certain criteria.<sup>4</sup> If a property earns Superhost status, a badge shows up in a prominent place next to the title of the property. In the third analysis, we split our sample into two subsamples. One subsample consisted of properties with below-median prices and without Superhost status; the other subsample consisted of properties with above-median prices and Superhost status. Given the presence of multiple quality signals, the latter group should have lower quality uncertainty and should thereby be less subject to the influence of Plus certification. The former group represents the group with the highest quality uncertainty; thus, Plus certification would matter most to those properties.

The results of the heterogeneous effects are presented in Table 6. The first column presents the results of the model with an interaction effect between the DD variable and the dummy variable identifying listings with an above-median price. We found that the coefficient on the interaction term, which captures the differential impact of Plus certification on the high-price properties compared to the low-price properties, is negative and statistically significant, in line with our intuition. In the second column, we interacted the DD variable with the dummy variable indicating that the property has above-median hotel reviews in its neighborhood. As seen from the table, the coefficient on DD × High\_hotel\_reviews is negative but not significant. In Column 3 (low price and non-Superhost) the DD variable is still positive and statistically significant; its magnitude is greater than what we saw in the baseline results in Table 3. The coefficient of the DD variable in Column 4 (high price and Superhost) is no longer significant, as discussed above. Overall, we conclude that the Plus certification indeed brings an outsized benefit for properties that lack quality signals but may not provide added value for properties with already strong quality signals.

## Externality Effects

We turn now to another dimension of the economic impact of quality certification, which is a potentially negative impact of a Plus property on the booking rates of neighboring non-Plus listings. We applied a DD estimation approach to examine the externality impact using the matched treatment and control samples described earlier. The last two columns of Table 4 provide the results from the relative time model applied to the externality samples derived from our Airbnb data set of eight cities. We first note that the pre-treatment terms are all insignificant, satisfying the parallel trends identifying assumption. Next, we see that the coefficients for Period (+1) and Period (+2) post-treatment are negative and significant. The other post-treatment terms have a negative sign but are not significant. We conclude that there is some evidence of a short-lived externality effect in the range of a 1.4%-1.9% reduction in booking rates one to two weeks following the arrival of at least one Plus listing nearby.

## Local Platform Effects

We now turn from examining impacts at the listing level to examining impacts at the platform level itself. We again adopted a DD specification that is a bit different from the models seen earlier. Specifically, we used a circle-level analysis where the goal was to determine whether the Plus certification of one or more listings in a 2-km circle resulted in higher or lower local (i.e., within the circle) net revenues for the Airbnb platform. This microlevel analysis is appropriate given that Plus listings only accounted for a small portion of the total listings in our sample, and competition is likely to be most intense for those listings in close geographic proximity to each other. The outcome variable is $l n ( A v g \_ R e v e n u e ) _ { i j t }$ , measuring the average weekly revenue for all the listings that were located within a 2-km radius range of the focal listing I in City j and Week t. The covariates used to explain this outcome variable are also aggregated to the circle-week level, resulting in the following specification.

$$
l n (A v g \_ R e v e n u e) _ {i j t} = \alpha + \beta_ {1} L P \_ D D _ {i t} + X _ {i t} + V _ {i} + T _ {j t} + \varepsilon_ {i j t}\tag{2}
$$

Here, $L P _ { - } D D _ { i t }$ is a dummy variable which equals 1 if there was at least one Plus listing within a 2-km radius range of Listing i (in City j) at time t, and 0 otherwise. $l n ( A v g \_ R e v e n u e ) _ { i j t }$ is the average revenue of all the listings that were located within a 2-km radius range of i at time t, and we denote $X _ { i t }$ as the set of control variables.

## Results from a Randomized Experiment

To supplement our quasi-experimental analysis above, we also conducted a randomized experiment to look at the issue of quality certification using a different lens. The matching methods in conjunction with our DD estimation are designed to deal with potential endogeneity and selection on both observables and unobservables. As with any quasiexperimental analysis based on archival data, however, we cannot be certain that the assignment and timing of the treatment are completely exogenous. To enhance the confidence in the qualitative nature of our baseline findings, we conducted a carefully designed randomized experiment to understand the causal impact of quality certification on booking propensity, while ensuring that the treatment was completely exogenous and the results are causal.

We built a mock Airbnb-like website, designed with the objective of highlighting quality certifications—Airbnb Plus and Superhost—while minimizing other listing heterogeneity. All properties were two-bedroom/one-bathroom units, located in downtown Austin. All the properties and related content were scraped from the actual Airbnb website. To eliminate the endogeneity of Plus certification and property changes in anticipation of the same, we only included non-Plus properties on Airbnb. We then deliberately assigned Plus certifications to the properties with the highest propensity scores estimated by running all property descriptions and reviews through our Plus-predicting classifier. Note that the Superhost badge is earned over time, based on published objective criteria, so it is not subject to the same endogeneity issues as Airbnb Plus. Accordingly, we used Superhost badges as already determined by Airbnb. The detailed design of the experiment is provided in the Appendix.

The results of the experiment are as follows: Table 8 shows the results of a probit regression at the user-impression level, where the dependent variable is whether a booking was made or not. There were 11,200 observations at the user-impression level. ## Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate the impact of a platform-endorsed quality certification system, Airbnb Plus, which allows properties to generate a quality signal beyond host and property reviews. The platform itself plays the role of inspector and certifier—with potential conflicts of interest that may detract from its effectiveness. This quality certification system is subject to both adverse selection (low-quality properties might pay the low inspection cost) and moral hazard problems (lack of long-term quality monitoring capacity); thus, it is an open question as to whether the quality certification is a credible signal of quality or just “cheap talk” (e.g., Barach et al., 2020). Even if the quality certification is credible, the positive demand effects for certified properties could be more than offset by the negative externality effects for noncertified listings nearby, meaning that the net revenue impacts for the platform is ambiguous.

Motivated by these open issues, we developed a causal empirical framework for estimating both direct and externality effects of Plus certification as well as local platform revenue effects. Our identification strategy relied on the differential timing of the launch of the Plus service across cities and the differential adoption of Plus certification across properties— in conjunction with suitable matching methods. We found that obtaining a Plus certification raised the booking rate by 6.8%, which translates to an additional revenue of over \$3100 for an average Plus-certified listing. On the other hand, a non-Plus listing in the neighborhood of a Plus listing saw a decline in booking rates of 1.4% - 1.9%, which translates to a reduction in annual revenues of about \$626 - \$849. Netting out the two effects, we did find a positive net revenue impact for the platform, to the tune of \$37,661 of additional revenue for 2- km zones containing one or more newly certified properties in U.S. cities. Accordingly, we found prima facie evidence that the Plus certification is a credible signal with durable economic impacts—at least for several months following certification. We also found evidence of net new entry in areas with Plus properties and found that the effect of the Plus program on host income is to make it slightly more evenly distributed at the margins.

Our findings have direct implications for property owners, consumers, and the platform itself. For consumers, Airbnb provides a solution for potential travelers with reservations regarding the potential quality of rental properties, which is helpful in light of the known review inflation problem (e.g., Zervas et al., 2021). The implications for property owners are rather evident. If the property is certified by Airbnb Plus, such quality certification will likely lead to increases in demand and revenue. As found in our paper, such positive influence is subject to a few moderating factors that lead to heterogeneous effects on demand, such as alternative quality cues such as price, Superhost status, and reviews. Property owners should weigh the marginal value of Plus certification against its cost, while deciding whether to spend efforts in acquiring Plus certification; our results provide guidance in this regard.

Turning to the platform, quality certification allows Airbnb to counteract the brand names and reputations of the traditional hotel industry. The “unraveling effect” theorized in the quality certification literature (e.g., Dranove & Jin, 2010) suggests that more and more properties would opt for certification, reducing the signaling value of the certification (Anderson et al., 1999). If this comes to fruition, then the platform would need to evaluate the trade-offs between the effectiveness of this quality signal and the net profit gain afforded by the Plus program. Our findings mainly pertain to a short- to mediumtime period after the launch of Plus certification. Entry effects in the long run are an open question and an interesting direction for further research. Our findings are directly applicable to other short-term rental platforms such as VRBO, because they essentially have the same structure and quality signaling mechanisms as Airbnb. The implications from our findings can be applied to such platforms, even if they haven’t yet implemented similar platform-endorsed certifications. Our findings can also inform other sharing economy platforms that have existing mechanisms for quality signaling, such as reviews, badges, and money-back guarantees (e.g., Uber, Lyft, Upwork, TaskRabbit, and so on). The viability of these platforms relies heavily on the trust built on those platforms (Ert & Fleischer, 2019; Fleischer et al., 2022; Ter Huurne et al., 2017), and these platforms all face similar problems in designing effective quality-assurance mechanisms when the existing quality signaling mechanisms are inadequate.

Our study is not without limitations. First, selection bias may unavoidably exist in our study due to certain unobservable factors that we failed to control for or the lack of available data. For example, it is possible that the acceptance rate by hosts is one of the factors determining eligibility for Plus, which also could have been considered in a matching process if we had used that variable.<sup>6</sup> However, we believe the chances of this are relatively low, given that we constructed comparable control groups using extensive matching methods, including natural language processing of qualitative review text. We also deployed a variety of robustness checks and a randomized online experiment to ensure the validity of our findings. Second, our analysis is based on a specific sharing economy platform, but our findings could be extended to other sharing economy platforms with the same peer-topeer (P2P) ecosystem whose existing signaling mechanisms cannot fully mitigate product- or provider-quality uncertainty. Finally, our analysis is geared toward short- to medium-term impacts over several months following certification; therefore, researchers should use caution in extending our results to the longer term. Overall, we believe that our research generates useful and robust empirical regularities that have important implications for both research and practice regarding quality assurance in the sharing economy.

## Details of Randomized Experiment Design

The study participants were 280 U.S. business graduate students and were told that they should book a property for a weekend visit to downtown Austin to catch up with an old high school classmate. Each user was shown a list of 40 properties, and they were randomly assigned to either the control group—which did not see any quality certifications—or to one of four treatment groups with varying rules for displaying the Airbnb Plus and Superhost certifications and screen ranks for the same. Specifically, the experimental gro ups were as follows: (1) Control: Users saw 40 listings without any Plus or Superhost badges. (2) Treatment 1: Users saw a total of 40 listings, and 7 out of the first 15 impressions had a Plus badge. None of the properties had a Superhost badge. (3) Treatment 2: Users saw a total of 40 listings, and 7 out of the first 15 impressions had a Superhost badge. None of the properties had a Plus badge. (4) Treatment 3: Users saw a total of 40 listings, and 7 out of the first 15 impressions had both a Superhost and a Plus badge. The remaining properties had no badge. (5) Treatment 4: Users saw a total of 40 listings, and 7 out of the last 15 impressions had a Plus badge. None of the properties had a Superhost badge. All participants were told to participate in a brief survey about their background (e.g., age, marital status, familiarity with short-term rental websites, etc.). The experiment was organized into three phases: (1) browsing phase, where participants were given 5 minutes to freely browse the website and get familiarized with the interface; (2) valuation phase, in which each participant was asked to provide their willingness-to-pay (WTP) for five random listings; and (3) booking phase, where each user had to book one of the 40 listings, so as to maximize their surplus, or the difference between their WTP and asking price. The experiment was gamified in order to provide the participants with the incentives to be thoughtful in their choices. Specifically, in Phase 2 the participants were offered a gift card for whoever’s stated WTP was closest to the average of all users valuing that property. In Phase 3, the participants were offered a gift card to whoever had the maximum surplus.

## The Construction of the Image Quality Measure

We adopted the CNN architecture of Zhang et al. (2022) and employed a transfer learning technique using VGG-16 model which was pretrained on the ImageNet data set (Simonyan & Zisserman, 2014). The rationale for applying transfer learning is that we maintained all the convolutional layers with their parameters that were learned from the ImageNet but replace d the last fully connected layer with our own classification algorithm to fine-tune our training model. Our final CNN architecture explicitly followed Zhang et al. (2022) in terms of transforming input images, setting convolution kernel size, padding, max pooling, and so on. We also retrieved the training data set from Zhang et al. (2022). For these steps, we used Google Colab (GPU enabled). We randomly split the data into training (80%) and validation (20% from the training set) sets to monitor the training and validation loss change over time in order to avoid overfitting. Using the remaining 20% of the data set, we tested the performance of our trained CNN and achieved 88% accuracy. We passed the property images obtained from AirDNA and Insider Airbnb through the trained model for the prediction stage. For each AirDNA image, we used its predicted class label to build the image quality index. Specifically, we summed up the values of the class labels and divided them by the number of pictures to come up with the average quality index for each property. Since our AirDNA data had more than one image for each property we leveraged this average quality index as one of our matching covariates in CEM. For each of the Insider Airbnb images, we assigned its predicted probability to measure the absolute difference of the probability when there was a change in the main cover picture over time.

## Implementation of Topic Modeling Approach

We employ anchored topic modeling (Anchored CorEx) to extract the hidden topics from the textual descriptions of the properties in our data set. It is effective in dealing with combining multiple granular topics into a more macrolevel topic and separating mixed overlapping themes into distinct sets of topics (Gallagher et al., 2017). After CorEx topic modeling generated the topics and corresponding keywords, it enabled us to choose keywords that can act as anchors to further search for more potential keywords that are more likely to be relevant to the given topics. Following the conventional approach, we decided that the optimal number of topics was 7 based on total correlation. Table A1 shows the detailed results. Based on the results, we figured out how each document (property description) was related to each set of topics. Then, we used the probabilities of those assigned topics and passed them through the entropy function. In this way, we computed the entropy index as a single value for each property. Since we could track the change of property descriptions over time, we derived the difference between the values of the entropy index if there was a change in the description before and after the Plus certification.
