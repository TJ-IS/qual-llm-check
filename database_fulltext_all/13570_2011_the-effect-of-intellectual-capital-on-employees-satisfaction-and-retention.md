# The effect of intellectual capital on employees’ satisfaction and retention

Mariolina Longo, Matteo Mura

Department of Management, University of Bologna, Via Terracini 28, 40131 Bologna, Italy

A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 10 December 2008 Received in revised form 22 March 2011 Accepted 16 June 2011 Available online 5 July 2011

Keywords: Intellectual capital Knowledge assets Employees’ job attitudes Survey Structural equations modelling

## A B S T R A C T

We examined the effect of intellectual capital on employees’ job satisfaction and retention, and also identified two human resource management practices that positively influence intellectual capital. Survey data were collected from 1117 employees of a large Italian company leader in the food product market. Structural equations modelling results showed that intellectual capital positively affects the job attitudes examined, although differences emerged between the three dimensions of intellectual capital. Specifically, human capital and relational capital did not directly affect employees’ job satisfaction and retention as we hypothesised; in fact, these relationships were fully mediated by structural capital. Also, our results identified two measures of human resource management practices (communication and alignment) that positively influenced intellectual capital and also contributed to the improvement of employees’ job satisfaction and retention. Our findings may help managers design and implement intervention programmes and policies for effective intellectual capital management.

## 1. Introduction

The past decade has been seen as a time of growing dematerialisation of strategic resources in firms. The globalisation of markets has forced firms to attempt to increase their competitive advantage via internally generated intangible assets which cannot be easily imitated by competitors. In this context, knowledge has been advocated as a key driver of sustained firm performance [3,19].

Intellectual capital (IC) has been defined as the knowledge that firms utilise for competitive advantage and it includes human capital (knowledge, skills, and abilities of individuals), structural capital (routines, processes, and databases), and relational capital (interactions among individuals and their networks of relationships) [23]. By taking an organisational perspective, scholars have suggested that IC drives firm performance, influences firm innovative capabilities, and positively affects firm capability and knowledge transfer [2,7,14,20]. Additionally, studies have identified specific human resource configurations and organisational structures as antecedents of IC [13,22]. Organisational-level metrics to assess IC have been proposed, together with the links among IC dimensions of human, structural, and relational capital [9,11,16].

IC is a resource that is embedded in the actions and capabilities of the individuals that operate in the company; it has therefore been suggested that researchers should explore IC from an individual-rather than an organisational-level perspective in order to gain more precise knowledge about IC-related phenomena [15,17]. Also, knowledge-based theorists have strongly underscored the need to address the process of knowledge-based value creation, and that this is typically rooted in individual action and interaction [6]. Therefore we decided to: (1) investigate the link between IC and firm outcomes; (2) refine existing IC metrics; and (3) investigate the factors that influence IC.

Thus we investigated the effect of IC on employees’ job satisfaction and retention and identify and empirically validate two human resource management (HR) practices and links them to IC improvement.

We surveyed all the employees of the 13 plants of a large Italian company in the food-product market. Overall, about 3400 questionnaires were distributed; with a response rate of 35%, the sample consisted of 1117 observations distributed uniformly across professional category, company area, and number of years that the employee worked in the company.

## 2. Definitions and research model

Our model was analysed in two ways:

(1) IC and employees’ job attitudes—we determined how IC affected job satisfaction and lowered turnover rate (commonly termed job attitudes [1]);

(2) HR practices and IC—we evaluated the effect that employees perception of two HR practices had on IC. The practices were identified by holding numerous focus groups with a panel of company managers.

## 2.1. IC and employees’ job attitudes

Although human, structural and relational capital are wellknown concepts in the IC and HR management literatures, their interactions have not been analysed in a quantitative study that adopted a single employee as the unit of analysis; therefore we first identified IC and HR measurement systems and noted that organisational learning literature stated that organisations do not create knowledge but that people do. Previous studies have proposed a broad view of human capital that reflect the overall skill, expertise, and knowledge levels of employees. We tool a narrower perspective and focused on individual-level constructs that allowed employees’ to learn and create knowledge and expertise that they could apply in their daily work. Specifically, we focus on two constructs: intrinsic work reflection (the ability of the employee to understand how his/her work fits into the overall company context and to separate the main aspects of the work from that part that is less important) practical application (the skill of the employee in autonomously resolving problems that may arise and the acquisition of practical knowledge about the task to be performed). Autonomy and skill variety represent employees’ abilities that have been found to positively affect job attitudes [12]. We therefore proposed:

## H1a. Human capital positively affects employees’ job attitudes.

Structural capital refers to knowledge that resides in and is utilised through databases, patents, routines, structures, systems, and processes. It consists of two dimensions: objectified (the shared knowledge in the organisation that requires the effective use of institutional mechanisms, such as databases, patents, process manuals, and IS) and collective knowledge (embedded in the form of social practice and residing in the tacit experience of the collective produced internally by individual employees and dwelling in an organisation’s norms, culture and processes) [10]. We therefore focused on this dimension of structural capital by adopting three constructs: the employee’s ability to work in a group (exchange and sharing of information among employees, the transfer of knowledge, and the development of innovative ideas), contribution (the understanding that the work carried out is a part of the whole organisational process and thus, effects the entire organization), and trust (which encourages collaboration between employees and the organisation and increases the sharing of knowledge and best practice).

Previous research has suggested that the sharing of noncodified knowledge among employees is positively related to employees’ job satisfaction and performance beliefs [5]. We therefore we proposed:

## H1b. Structural capital positively affects employees’ job attitudes.

Relational capital consists of the network of relations that the organisation, through its individual employees, creates internally; e.g., the collaboration between its employees, and suppliers, clients or other stakeholders. The resources generated and exchanged through this network constitute the relational capital of the organisation. Relationships between a employees and external stakeholders stimulate the creation, acquisition, and exploitation of knowledge, while intra-firm relationships are a source of knowledge development and exchange. The internal networks are the result of the relations that employees establish inside the organisation, at times overcoming the formal organisational structure. The external networks involve the perception that employees have about the expectations and needs of customers or suppliers, and their ability to satisfy the expectations.

Employees’ social networks have been found to be positively related to job performance [21]; also, relational capital of individuals has been assumed to enhance both career achievement and satisfaction [24]. We therefore hypothesized:

## H1c. Relational capital positively affects employees’ job attitudes.

Finally, some authors have suggested that the three IC dimensions influence one another, or that human capital is the principal element of IC that contributes to the development of the other two dimensions. Based on this we hypothesized:

H2. Human capital positively affects structural capital and relational capital.

## 2.2. HR practices and IC

To determine which of the firm’s HR practices makes a contribution to the creation of IC, we organised several focus groups with a panel of managers (see Appendix B for details). By making an in-depth analysis of the firm’s vision, mission, and human resource strategy we identified two HR practices of the company that could have a positive effect on IC and, consequently, on improving employees’ job attitude.

The first practice dealt with the communication from the CEO and top managers down to the employees and vice versa. The company had implemented instruments, such as organisational climate surveys and employee workshops, directed at stimulating bottom-up communication processes and thus identifying the professional and personal expectations of individual employees. Then the company introduced specific practices that could satisfy professional and personal expectations of employees. Two examples of practices that have been established include work targets that individual employees must reach and the introduction of ways to influence employee working conditions. In response to personal expectations, the company created a company nursery and developed flexible working hours for certain professional categories. We termed these communication practice and hypothesized:

H3. Communication practice positively affects IC.

which we divided into:

H3a. Communication practice positively affects human capital.

H3b. Communication practice positively affects structural capital.

## H3c. Communication practice positively affects relational capital.

The second practice focused on employee alignment and represented the employees’ perception of the company’s vision and strategic intent. This was characterised by a number of actions that company managers undertake in order to communicate company strategy to employees. They range from the annual company meeting, where the CEO states the company’s vision and strategic goals to all the employees to a company newspaper that highlights the milestones reached by the company distributed to all employees every quarter and posters depicting company strategy in the halls of the production plants.

Employees must be aware of and understand the strategy if they are to link their day-to-day operations successfully with the company goals [18]. Training and education practices that foster employees’ alignment can contribute to the human capital. Aligning employees to the company’s strategy empowers the employees and builds a collaborative organisational environment in which knowledge and information flow freely, thus stimulating the development of relational capital. We termed this practice alignment and hypothesized:

H4. Alignment practice positively affects IC. which we divided into:

H4a. Alignment practice positively affects human capital.

H4b. Alignment practice positively affects structural capital.

H4c. Alignment practice positively affects relational capital.

## 3. Methodology and sample

## 3.1. Measures development

The constructs were measured using scales mainly taken from the HR management literature. For communication practice and alignment practice, we developed, with the company managers, a survey using Likert-type scales that measured the employee’s perception of the company practices. The pilot questionnaire was administered to a small sample of managers, inside and outside the company and to academics who were considered experts in the IC field. They were asked to review the survey for content, clarity, and validity.

Based on this feedback, some redundant or ambiguous items were modified or eliminated. No new items were added. The final document included 11 five-point Likert scales (from 1 for completely disagree to 5 for completely agree). Appendix A shows the scales.

We also introduced four control variables for the employee as the unit of analysis: gender; job level (shop floor workers, office workers, middle-managers, executives); seniority within the company (more or less than seven years); and company area (operations, R&D, finance, marketing, human resources).

## 3.2. Sample

This research was performed in a leading Italian company in the agricultural food sector. With a total turnover in 2006 of about <sub>900M</sub>s<sub>, the company sells nationally through 13 production</sub> plants, has about 100 distribution centres, and employs about 2000 people.

Two surveys were conducted in 2005 and 2006. The questionnaire was sent to employees by enclosing it in with their pay slips. In 2005 a total of 1310 questionnaires were distributed and 460 were returned, a response rate of 35%. In 2006, 1928 questionnaires were distributed and 657 were returned for a rate of 34%. Thus 1117 questionnaires were collected and the data was uniformly split between the production plants and the job levels.

We analysed our model using PLS; it is more appropriate than LISREL for complex models with low theoretical information and when the measures are not well established. PLS can also effectively manage the high number of variables in the model and the low possible causal relationships between the constructs.

## 4.1. The measurement model

To assess construct reliability, we computed the composite reliability coefficients. All of them were greater than 0.80, proving reliability of all the scales (see Table 2).

In order to determine the convergent and discriminant validity of the constructs, it was necessary to show that: (a) indicators loaded much higher on their hypothesised factor than on all other factors and (b) the square root of each factor’s average variance extracted (AVE) was larger than its correlations with other factors. To satisfy the first criterion, we used PLS confirmatory factor analysis. All the items loaded well on their respective factors. For the second criterion, Table 3 shows that the square root of all the AVEs was larger than all other cross correlations both for second order factors (human capital, structural capital, relational capital and job attitudes) and first order factors. Jointly, these findings suggested adequate convergent and discriminant validity.

To overcome the concern of common method bias in the survey design, we first included some reverse-scored items in the principal constructs to reduce acquiescence problems. Subsequently, common method variance was assessed after the data were collected using Harman’s one-factor test (see Appendix B) where all the principal components are entered into a principal components factor analysis. Evidence of common method bias exists when a single factor emerges from the analysis or when one general factor accounts for the majority of the covariance in the interdependent and dependent variables. As each of the principal constructs explained approximately equal variance, the data did not indicate common method bias. Finally, the model was developed using 2005 data and appropriately cross-validated using the 2006 sample.

Although the communication practice significantly affected job attitudes, the communication practice positively and significantly affected human, structural, and relational capital, thus supporting H3a, H3b and H3c. ## 5. Discussion

In our study we examined the effect of IC on employees’ job attitudes. Our results suggest that IC positively affects these work behaviours, although differences emerge between the three IC dimensions. The direct influence of structural capital on employees’ satisfaction and retention reinforces the conclusions of prior studies: the sharing of non-codified knowledge among employees has a positive effect on job satisfaction. Also, there seems to be a direct link between job characteristics such as job autonomy, skill variety, and job satisfaction, and a direct relationship between social networks of employees and career satisfaction. Also, we found that human capital positively affected both structural and relational capital, confirming human capital as the core IC dimension.

The company we analysed in our study promoted top-down and bottom-up organisational communication. This practice positively

Attempts to align employees with the company’s vision and mission positively influenced human and relational capital by contributing to the development of the firm’s specific skills and abilities due to human capital. The presence of shared goals apparently promotes mutual understanding and cooperation [4,8].

Finally, our results showed that IC dimensions were clearly distinguishable and could be modelled as distinct second-order factors.

## 5.1. Implications for practice

By exploring the effect of IC on employees’ satisfaction and retention, our study increases our understanding of the microfoundations of knowledge-based value creation. Our finding that human, structural and relational capital influence job attitudes may provide managers with information necessary to design and implement effective organisational intervention programs that improve employees’ satisfaction and retention.

Also some HR practices positively influence IC. Top-down and bottom-up communication and employees’ alignment to the firm’s vision and mission positively influenced IC dimensions, therefore, managers should consider the use of such practices as a way to improve organisational knowledge.

## 6. Limitations and conclusion

Our study was cross-sectional and this precluded us from drawing causal inferences about the relationships among variables. Also the generalisability of our findings is doubtful’ although the sample is large, it is of a single company. Also, we adopted only perceptual measures for assessing IC, and this may limit the representativeness of the measurement tool.In conclusion, IC is a complex resource that could significantly contribute to company success. Understanding the factors that influence IC and of the ways that IC contributes to firm performance is still limited. Our study, by measuring IC from an individual-level perspective, and by assessing its influence on employees’ job satisfaction and retention, only represents an initial step.
