# The impact of Business Intelligence systems on stock return volatility

Eran Rubin <sup>a,</sup>\*, Amir Rubin <sup>b</sup>

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Business Intelligence Decision support Event study Information availability Volatility

## A B S T R A C T

Business Intelligence (BI) systems support decision-making and information-sharing in increasingly complex organizational environments. As investment in these systems is steadily increasing in a wide range of industries, it is important to understand their economic effect. Under BI, information can be accessed in a timelier manner, decisions become increasingly data driven, and reports become more informative. This, in turn, can lead to more consistent company behavior and performance. We therefore hypothesized that BI systems could help reduce the company’s stock return volatility. To test this hypothesis, we empirically analyzed a large sample of firms that had deployed a BI system. Our results indicated a significant reduction in stock return volatility subsequent to BI deployment. The reduction in volatility is of similar magnitude whether the BI system is implemented to serve upper or middle management, or the knowledge workforce. Overall, the results suggested that BI systems reduce the financial risk of an organization.

## 1. Introduction

Business Intelligence (BI) systems support decision-making in the increasingly complex environments in which organizations operate; they allow companies to aggregate and present data in different ways. Thus, as enterprise systems provide and consume greater volumes of operational data from multiple sources, companies using BI systems can exploit the new information to gain knowledge about the business domain. This knowledge is typically associated with customer needs, the competition, conditions in the industry, and general economic, technological, and cultural trends.

Naturally, the deployment of BI systems allows for more informed decision-making. Yet the economic value of these systems is still hard to determine using traditional payoff measures. It is rare that such systems pay for themselves by cost reductions alone, since savings are only a small portion of the payoffs associated with BI deployment. As a result, researchers and practitioners have called for better ways to evaluate BI systems’ economic effect to understand their role and better justify their procurement.

We assumed that when a firm deploys a BI system, the risk associated with holding its stock is likely to decrease, because such systems help generate comprehensive reports that improve access to corporate information and this benefits many different groups of users, from specialists in financial reporting to salespeople and board members; it also reduces the probability of underperformance or sudden extreme decisions due to late arrival of information. Also, BI systems facilitate data-based decision strategies, by shifting the basis for decision-making from intuition to data. Intuition-based decisions are often driven by the subconscious and emotions rather than a rational decision processes. Thus, BI promotes consistent decisions and reduces risk caused by inconsistencies in the decision-making process.

Using stock return volatility as our measure of firm risk, we found support for a moderating effect of BI systems following deployment. The reduction in volatility was significant and of similar magnitude regardless of whether the BI system was implemented to serve upper or middle management, or the knowledge workforce. The essence of our main result is shown in Fig. 1, which plots the squared errors of the regression specification $\boldsymbol { r } _ { t } = \boldsymbol { \alpha } _ { 0 } + \boldsymbol { \varepsilon } _ { t } ,$ where $r _ { t }$ is the return at time t, and $\varepsilon _ { t }$ is the error term at time t. This represents the mean volatility experienced by 129 firms during a period of 540 trading days around their BI deployment date. While different firms implemented BI at different times in the 1999–2007 period, a clear trend in volatility reduction subsequent to deployment is evident. Errors were averaged across firms each trading day.

While the properties of BI systems suggest their risk-reduction potential, other IT investments may also influence a firm’s risk. A paper by Dewan et al. [7] suggested that IT investments are generally associated with the exercising of a real option. They further argue that the opportunity cost of exercising these options should lead to an increase in the company’s hurdle rate and can be a significant contributor to the company’s risk premium. In other studies, e.g., Ref. [6], similar results occurred in e-commerce initiatives.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Business Intelligence

Broadly, the objective of BI systems is to improve the timeliness and quality of inputs to the decision process; they help companies store, find, and analyze the information they need to make better decisions.

BI systems are the natural evolution of DSS. March and Hevner [13] considered them to be composed of the DSS subsystems, as such subsystems correspond to the data warehouse in BI, and the model subsystem corresponds to the online analytical processing, knowledge discovery, and data-mining tools in BI.

BI systems are well suited to study the effects of information systems on company-related risk for two reasons. First, they are a type of enterprise system (ES) that function across different areas of the organization and are expected to have a wide effect across it. In contrast, implementation of other types of systems may not lead to enterprise-wide change.

Second, they are effective in examining volatility, while other ES do not limit their attention to decision-making and information flow. When other ES are employed, a business often undergoes dramatic changes, such as business process reengineering and organizational restructuring [12]. The effects of such improvements on volatility are ambiguous. Furthermore, other ES usually demand more significant investment and a higher level of commitment.

BI tools are being incorporated into an increasing number of businesses for use by financial managers, business executives, IT managers, and business analysts. The global BI market was worth about \$8.4 billion in 2008, and is forecasted to grow to \$13 billion by 2013 [3].

## 2.2. Event studies on IT

Since the suggestion of a ‘‘productivity paradox’’ was first introduced, numerous studies have proposed ways to study the relation between IT investment and outcomes. Different models have been developed to value IT investment and various productivity measures have been suggested (e.g., [5]).

One popular line of research examines the effects of IT investment on accounting performance measures such as administrative expenses, sales, number of employees, ROE, and ROA.

However, early studies resulted in mixed evidence. It has been noted that the relationship between IT and productivity is more complex than suggested [17].

In subsequent research, a new productivity paradox has emerged: rather than a lack of productivity gains, highly abnormal returns on investment were found. Thus, it appeared that companies have been underinvesting in IT.

Seeking alternative measures of IT value, a recent line of research built on the traditional event study methodology.<sup>1</sup> Based on the notion that IT value can be determined by analyzing stock returns following announcements of IT expenditures, this approach assumed that rational investors value both the tangible and intangible benefits of an investment and hence that the market’s reaction to an investment announcement captures the implications of system deployment.

Event studies have been gaining popularity in IS research in recent years. For instance, the event study approach has been used to measure the value associated with e-commerce initiatives, value creation in information-industry convergence, security breach announcements [10], and application service provider adoption [11]. Still, it is important to realize that an implicit assumption in traditional event studies is that the consequences of an event are effectively understood on investment announcement. However, IT deployment is a complex process that is affected by many ambiguous factors, including management support, affiliation, and system acceptance, and therefore this assumption may raise concerns. In contrast, event studies on volatility do not require that the market immediately understand the implications of BI systems. Rather, a lengthy event window may be defined, which allows the researcher to detect changes in volatility when the event date is not clearly defined, and it allows the market to consider the effects of a BI system over time.

Interestingly, while the effects of IT investments on stock prices have been intensively researched, studies on the effects of IT investments on stock return volatility have only recently begun to emerge. Dewan et al. explain that abnormal returns are associated with IT by incorporating IT risk measures. Dewan and Ren found that the risk-increasing properties of IT hold in the case of ecommerce initiatives. These studies highlight important effects of IT; however, the effects of other types of IT initiatives had still to be explored.

## 3. Theoretical foundations – Business Intelligence and volatility

BI systems improve information access through their reporting and forecasting properties. We argue that their functionalities reduce a firm’s stock return volatility.

## 3.1. Volatility

Volatility is generally defined as the standard deviation of an asset’s return. As the price of a stock changes over time, so does the variation in stock prices. It is therefore natural to think of a stock’s volatility as characterized by the uncertainty that surrounds its value. Consequently, price changes are expected when new information, such as a change in dividends or report of corporate events arrives at the marketplace. The literature has long claimed, however, that the market is much more volatile than one would expect from changes in value. In recent studies, researchers have used intraday data to identify announcement effects (e.g., [2]) and found that most information concerning an event was incorporated into prices within minutes. However, even these studies yield a low R-square, and perhaps more importantly, most variation in stock returns cannot be explained by firm-specific events. Thus, a host of other reasons have been suggested as factors contributing to volatility.

Basically, a company’s stock price varies for two reasons: first, in response to market-wide factors imposed by general financial news and industry-based events. Second, in response to individual company behavior as volatility is triggered by changes in investors’ confidence about the company’s future cash flows. In this context, consistent performance, be it good or bad, tends to decrease stock return volatility, while unexpected company-based news tends to increase stock return volatility. Stock return volatility is also affected by the accuracy of the investors’ information. The more accurate the indicators that investors receive from companies, the less surprised investors will be upon the arrival of information.

## 3.2. Executive management – strategic decisions and finance

Executive management uses BI to support strategic decisionmaking. Their actions vary in nature and are usually unstructured. Their decision process may differ, depending on the individual (decision-making skills), the problem (decision attributes and cues), and the context (decision environment). DSS normally change the values of different process variables, and this may significantly alter the decision process and outcome, making the systems difficult to analyze, and thus their analysis often produces mixed findings on their effect on decision outcomes. Regardless of the effect on decision outcome, however, with BI deployment, executive decisions are likely to become more consistent as BI systems drive managers’ attention to specific sets of data and business processes and their decision-making becomes increasingly data driven. For example, using interfaces such as digital dashboards, BI systems provide information on the current and past status of a firm’s key performance indicators.

It must be noted that although a BI system stabilizes executives’ decision-making process, it does not necessarily lead to improved decision quality. For example, although intuition may lead to erroneous decisions, intuitive unconscious processes are rapid, effortless, and can be quite effective [4]. Regardless of the decision outcomes, however, reducing reliance on intuition in decisionmaking should lead to reduced risk because of consistency in the decision process. When decisions are consistent, investors can better anticipate company behavior and thus surprises are less frequent and less pronounced.

Executive management decisions also relate to financial decision-making and reporting, where BI systems can generate informative reports such as financial statements. Indeed, often firms’ finance departments are leading the adoption of BI software. Thus, the enhanced internal reports generated by BI systems can facilitate the generation of more accurate external reports.

Prior to the appearance of advanced BI systems, many organizations lacked the systems and processes needed to obtain timely and accurate information about their organization’s performance. For instance, Myers and Durfee [15] found that only 48% of finance executives were satisfied with the speed and accuracy of plan forecasts during the fiscal year; in addition, only 40% were satisfied with the effort expended in creating an annual performance plan, and only 31% were satisfied with their ability to model and test the impact of proposed changes.

With the advent of BI systems, however, organizations can proactively manage their performance. For example, Myers and Knox [16] found that 80% of financial executives using BI indicated that they are supported by the right technology. In addition, by about a two-to-one margin, these users were more confident they could implement plans and monitor their success.

## 3.3. Middle management – structured decisions and monitoring

Decisions taken by middle management are more routine and less strategic; i.e., structured or semi-structured. Thus BI systems extend limited human capabilities by simplifying the deployment of decision-making strategies, supporting different representation methods, and allowing for more rapid information processing, thereby extending the ‘‘bounds of rationality’’.

Whatever the effect of BI systems on the quality of decisions, the incorporation of BI systems supports structured decisions and therefore stabilizes decision processes. In particular, by facilitating the execution of a decision task in a certain way, IT inevitably inhibits the task’s execution in some other way.

Either intentionally or unintentionally, the deployment of a DSS within an organization may foster specific decision strategies. The use of BI targeted toward middle management therefore implies the support of specific decision strategies, with decisions becoming more consistent.

## 3.4. Knowledge workers – information access

In addition to reducing risk by increasing decision consistency, BI systems support risk reduction by increasing access to internal company information. There is compelling evidence that the use of decision support tools in an organization improves information flow, communication, and operational problem solving. BI tools are steadily used not only as a high level corporate decision tool but also as a decision tool by people throughout the organization, providing quick access to new information. For example, BI systems enhance a company’s ability to react quickly to internal information by improving information visibility. BI tools also allow key information to be monitored through the use of intuitive indicators that are easy to understand. BI therefore supports the dissemination and use of information, enabling quick adjustment to a changing environment.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Volatility and event studies

To study an event’s effect on shareholder wealth $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , the value of a firm’s equity), a traditional event study methodology is typically used. These assume that the effects of an event will immediately be reflected in security prices; thus the impact of the event can be captured by changes in security prices surrounding the event date.

To measure the impact of an event, a researcher must be able to specify the event window in calendar time. If the announcement itself reveals all information of relevance to the market, the event window will be well defined and typically correspond to a few days following the announcement.

Since financial theory suggests that investors are compensated for the risk they bear in holding a security, an asset pricing model must be used to measure an abnormal return due to an event. To estimate the statistical significance of this return, a researcher needs to know the distributional properties of the returns, which may have changed by the event [1]. Thus, if the event has affected the return of the security, there is no theoretical reason to believe that it has not affected the variance of the security’s return also. This, in turn, makes it difficult to measure the significance of the coefficient, which implies that unless the event is well defined in time and strongly associated with ‘‘good’’ or ‘‘bad’’ news, traditional event studies may lack statistical power or produce ambiguous results. Thus, while the traditional event study approach may be appropriate for looking at the effect of a major event such as a merger announcement, it may be of limited value when studying the effects of BI, as such effects are likely to appear only over the long term and are expected to influence the variance of the return (i.e., risk) rather than the return itself. Fortunately, however, financial economics research has produced tools that allow us to test for the effects on the variance of the return by adjusting the traditional event study methodology to include changes in variance.

## 4.2. Measuring volatility

A firm’s stock return is the percentage change in the stock’s value over time. We examined stock return volatility: the standard deviation in returns. A firm’s return consists of: an expected or explained portion, and an unexpected, or error, portion. The larger the error of the return, the larger is its variance. Therefore, stock return volatility analysis amounts to analysis of the magnitude of return errors.

However, a simple analysis of errors will fail to account for their persistence. One of the more robust findings in empirical finance is that stock return volatility is serially correlated and highly predictable [14]. Serial correlation in return volatility means that the errors are not uniformly random, but come from different distributions over time; the wider the distribution, the larger the expected error. Accordingly, nearly all empirical work in finance accounts for serial correlation in the magnitude of the unexplained return.

While there are different volatility models, one of the most popular and successful error distribution models is the autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (ARCH) model, or its more general counterpart, generalized ARCH (GARCH). Because we also employ the GARCH model in our research, we briefly discuss the principles underlying it.

The least squares model assumes homoskedasticity; i.e., all error terms are equally distributed around zero at any given point. When the distribution of error terms is expected to vary across data points, the dataset is said have heteroskedasticity.

When trying to estimate both the return of a stock and its variance, the question of heteroskedasticity becomes an important estimation issue because of the persistence in the absolute error of a stocks’ return. As a result, the econometric challenge is to specify how the information should be used to estimate the variance of the return, conditional on past information.

A simple GARCH model involves the joint estimation of two equations:

$$
r _ {t} = \alpha_ {0} + \varepsilon_ {t}\tag{1}
$$

$$
\sigma_ {t} ^ {2} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \sigma_ {t - 1} ^ {2} + \beta_ {2} \varepsilon_ {t - 1} ^ {2},\tag{2}
$$

where Eq. (1) is the general return equation in which r is the return at time t and e is the error term at time t. In Eq. (2), $\boldsymbol { \sigma ^ { 2 } } _ { t }$ is the expected variance of the return at time t. Eq. (1) assumes a constant return process with noise, while Eq. (2) accounts for heteroskedasticity and specifies the expected variance of the return error, based on past information; it also estimates the variance of the day t return as a weighted average of a constant, the previous day’s variance estimate $( \sigma _ { \ t - 1 } ^ { 2 } )$ , and the previous day’s squared error $( \boldsymbol { \varepsilon } ^ { 2 } _ { t - 1 } )$ . By squaring the previous period’s error, we quantify the surprise effect. Inclusion of the previous day’s volatility allows for volatility persistence, while the previous day’s error in Eq. (2) is used to update the current volatility measure.

The GARCH analysis relies on time-series differences in the ‘‘purest’’ sense as it models the future volatility of a firm conditional on its current volatility – thus the GARCH model is a model of conditional volatility. When examining other factors influencing volatility, a modified model is commonly tested. For example, Donaldson and Kamstra [8] tested for volume effects on volatility using an augmented ARCH model.

Because of persistency in volatility, it is common to use either daily returns or intraday returns (for example, 5 min’ volatility) to detect factors that affect it. When analyzing longer frequencies (weekly or monthly), volatility persistence is significantly reduced and thus the power of statistical tests is also reduced (in some sense, longer-frequency volatility tests bear similarities to tests on returns, which also tend to be random and not persistent. See Ghysels et al. [9].)

## 4.3. Research design

We tested our hypothesis by analyzing volatility measures for companies that have undergone a BI initiative. Our sample was developed from press releases associated with the implementation of BI systems. Our procedure for collecting the sample was:

1) Using the FACTIVA news database, we limited our search to company events between January 2000 and December 2007 (as these systems started to emerge at the beginning of 2000).

2) Using FACTIVA, we searched for the term Business Intelligence

3) From the returned press releases, we obtain those associated with companies that had deployed BI systems and that were publicly traded on either the NYSE or NASDAQ.

The sample consisted of a total of 129 BI implementation events in the 2000–2007 period. All the deployments took place at the corporate level with the intent of providing enhanced organizational reports to support the organizational decision-making process. We checked that the deployments were not viewed as a failure by searching the web using the name of the deploying company, the term Business Intelligence, and any of the words failure, failed, or disappointing. Our search results did not yield any press release suggesting implementation failure.

Table 1 displays the descriptive statistics for our sample firms based on the values of the firm in the year in which it implemented a BI system, where market value is the value of the stock times the number of shares outstanding, price is the year-end share price of the firm’s common stock at year-end, dollar volume is the value of shares traded during December of the event year, common shareholders is the number of registered brokers holding the security, and leverage is long-term debt divided by the sum of long-term debt and market value of equity. In Table 1, the firms tend to be relatively large, with a median value of about \$2.9 billion. Out of 129 stocks, 93 were traded on the NYSE, while 36 were traded on NASDAQ. The 90% confidence interval for the price of these shares was \$6.99–110.36, with a median value of \$31.0. The shares are widely traded, with a median dollar volume of \$346 million per month. On average, the companies employed 37,300 people (median 17,000) and were held by an average of 85,660 investors (by street name, median 8650). The mean (median) R&D expense as a percentage of assets was 3% (1%). Overall, the firms seemed to be relatively profitable; only 16 had small negative incomes (before extraordinary expenses). Leverage levels varied across the sample, with a 90% confidence interval of 0.00–0.97.

## 5.1. Analysis of return errors

To analyze the effect of BI deployment on return volatility, we first examine the behavior of returns around deployment time. Fig. 1 shows the behavior of squared return errors pertaining to the regression (Eq. (1)) around the time of BI deployment.

By incorporating m<sup>2</sup> in equation (4), we controlled for market surprises that occurred on the same day (at time t) that are incremental to the information captured by the error $( \boldsymbol { \varepsilon } ^ { 2 } _ { t - 1 } )$ and volatility $( \sigma _ { \ t - 1 } ^ { 2 } ) ,$ , which are based on the time t-1 information set. In Eq. (4) we also include a dummy variable for each industry to control for any difference in variance that may result from industry effects.

The GARCH model allows us to extract the part of the squared error that can be predicted based on autocorrelation properties. Fig. 2 depicts this portion of the variance; it shows that when we exclude those parts of the error that cannot be explained with time-based correlations, the moderating effect of BI deployment is clearer. In particular, we see that following BI deployment, return shocks (large unexpected errors that are not serially correlated in returns) are significantly lower, leading to an overall reduction in volatility.

## 5.3. The enhanced GARCH model

A more robust way to test for BI deployment effects is to use an enhanced GARCH specification that allows volatility levels to differ between the pre- and post-BI implementation periods. We therefore examined the magnitude of the effect by employing an enhanced GARCH specification:

$$
r _ {t} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} m _ {t} + \alpha_ {2} D _ {t} + \alpha_ {3} I _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {t}\tag{5}
$$

$$
\sigma_ {t} ^ {2} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \sigma_ {t - 1} ^ {2} + \beta_ {2} \varepsilon_ {t - 1} ^ {2} + \beta_ {3} m _ {t} ^ {2} + \beta_ {4} D _ {t} + \beta_ {5} I _ {t} + \sum_ {t} \beta_ {5 + i} i n _ {i}\tag{6}
$$

In this we add the dummy variable $I _ { t } ,$ which is 1 during the implementation period and 0 otherwise. In addition, to evaluate differences in volatility between the implementation period and the post-implementation period, we add the dummy variable $D _ { t } ,$ which is 1 in the post-implementation period and 0 otherwise.

In this specification, the coefficients on the dummy variables a and $\alpha _ { 3 }$ in the return equation captured the effects of BI deployment on the return process, while the coefficients $\beta _ { 4 }$ and $\beta _ { 5 }$ in the variance equation captured the effects of BI deployment on volatility. Thus, this specification allowed us to test the long-term effects of BI deployment on both a firm’s return and its variance (Table 5).

These specification results revealed that a firm’s return was highly correlated with the market, but was not significantly correlated with a BI system being implemented or being in the post-implementation period. In the variance equation, the coefficients on the variance $( \beta _ { 1 } )$ and error $( \beta _ { 2 } )$ were both positive and highly significant, as was the coefficient on the market surprise effect $( \beta _ { 3 } ) .$ . Importantly, we found a strong effect of BI deployment on volatility reduction $( \beta _ { 4 }$ and $\beta _ { 5 } )$ . As might be expected, the rate of reduction in volatility was higher during the implementation period $( \beta _ { 5 } ) ,$ , and continued to occur after the system was active. This suggested that BI deployment had a substantial effect on volatility reduction.

## 6. Additional support: analyzing analysts’ forecasts

We claim that an additional consequential functionality of BI systems can have a moderating effect on stock return volatility; we suggest that they can enhance external reporting and thus improve investors’ accuracy in valuing the firm. If our claim is true, then with BI use in the firm, investors have fine metrics on the firm and hence their estimation error should reduce.

Arguably, the most important piece of information for investors is the firm’s profitability. If BI deployment improves the accuracy of external investors’ information, one would expect investors to be less surprised around earnings announcements as their priors are more accurate when BI systems are deployed. This implies that the effects of earnings announcements on stock prices should be smaller than they were prior to deployment. It is important to realize, however, that investors do not react to the earnings announcement, but rather to any surprise associated with the earnings announcement. Therefore, to analyze the effect of BI deployment, we needed to analyze whether analysts’ forecast errors were lower following BI deployment. To do so, we analyzed earnings forecasts that closely corresponded to BI deployment events in calendar time.

The results are shown in Table 7. In Panel A (for means), we compared the pre- and post-forecast error associated with a particular BI event (i.e., essentially comparing pair-wise forecast errors for the same firm). For this purpose, we employed the 70 deployment cases (out of 129) for which we had data on forecast errors both immediately before (within 90 days) and immediately after (within 90 days) BI deployment. In Panel B (for medians), we examined whether the forecast error was above or below the overall median across all observations (regardless of whether they were before or after BI deployment). This analysis allowed us to include all cases for which we had forecast error estimates either before or after BI deployment (or both). Here, the sample size increased to 89 firms, the 70 firms in Panel A and an additional 19 firms with only one observation. The results showed that at the 1–5% significance level, the analyst forecast error was lower following BI deployment.

## 7. Discussion and conclusions

BI systems improve operational monitoring, increase data sharing, and shift the decision-making process toward more datadriven decisions. The properties of BI systems suggest that they may reduce risk associated with the deploying firms. In empirical tests we found that the volatility of stock returns was significantly lower following BI implementation, and hence deployment of BI systems appeared to reduce the risks associated with holding a firm’s stock.

## 7.1. Summary

We integrated a set of econometric and statistical tools to examine the effect of BI deployment on stock return variance. Our approach can be used to analyze other questions associated with the impact of IT on firm risk. Thus, while researchers and managers focus largely on output in terms of its mean effect, our study was motivated by the fact that, in many business situations, the mean effect may be ambiguous and analyzing variance may be more useful.

We showed that changes in the volatility of stock returns can be assessed at different levels. First, difference of means tests can be conducted to examine whether two periods of time exhibit different levels of variance. However, while such an analysis provides insights into the possible effects of an event, it has limited statistical validity due to the autocorrelation in its observations.

One way to improve the analysis was to incorporate lag variables in regression analysis. This reveals the explanatory power of an event on variance. In our study, the incorporation of lagged errors indicated that approximately 8% of the squared error variance can be attributed to BI implementation. However, results should then be interpreted with caution as the inclusion of lagged variables in regression analysis does not control for autocorrelation as well as autoregressive volatility models can.

To assess the impact of an event on a heteroskedastic process, an autoregressive model that incorporates event variables needed to be devised; therefore we extended the GARCH model to incorporate BI implementation variables. ## 7.2. Managerial implications

The finding that stock return volatility is lower following BI deployment suggests an important benefit of improved information access. In particular, our results suggested that improved information access within a company leads to greater performance stability. Our analysis also provided evidence that comprehensive BI systems targeting users at a specific level of the organization have a greater impact on volatility reduction than systems targeting a combination of user types.

The finding that BI system deployment reduces volatility also has different implications for different stakeholders. Banks and other debt holders, for instance, are likely to be more interested in risk reduction than productivity growth. Similar to debt holders, managers and large individual shareholders who are not diversified and are highly invested in a company typically want to reduce the risk associated with the stock because it correlates strongly with the value of their wealth.

BI implementation reduces the magnitude of idiosyncratic variance, which is the source of some of the major conflicts in corporate finance. Lower volatilityalso enhances job securitybecause stable cash flows reduce turnover. Firms with BI systems may therefore attract more experienced managers and employees who care about the reputation and viability of the firm in the long run.

## 7.3. Limitations

First, our sample is limited to deployments mentioned in press releases. However, even if there was evidence of press release bias, our findings suggested a special effect of some BI systems that was not previously addressed.

Second, while we examine whether there was documented evidence of system failures following an implementation announcement, there was no guarantee that all failures were documented. On the other hand, to the extent that our sample includes some failed implementations, our results should be weaker than if the sample only included successful implementations. To this end, since we do not look at decision quality or performance improvements but rather at performance consistency, volatility is expected to decrease even if system implementation is only partially successful.

Third, an implicit assumption was that the implementation of a BI system does not trigger opportunistic behavior from managers that results in increased stock return volatility. For example, we assumed that mangers would not be involved in any criminal activity associated with providing false information simply because a BI system was implemented.

Finally, our analysis was based on the core functionality of BI systems – we do not posit that different methods for providing this functionality will have different effects on volatility. On the contrary, a company’s choice of BI system vendor and way to provide the functionality is probably related to specific user preferences and the decision environment.

In conclusion, our study analyzes the effects of BI systems on the market behavior of implementing firms’ stock. By examining the effect of BI systems on risk, our work improves our understanding of possible economic outcomes of IT. This has a number of implications for practitioners and academics alike.
