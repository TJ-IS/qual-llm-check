## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Culture Protection motivation theory Espoused cultural values Individualism–collectivism Uncertainty avoidance Developing nations

## A B S T R A C T

Evidence of cyber activities in developing nations has renewed discourse on users’ intention to protect information in the global economy. We present a context theorization in which espoused individualism–collectivism and uncertainty avoidance are posited as antecedents to an individual’s threat and coping appraisal toward protecting information. We tested our model with data from the United States and Ghana, countries that generally difer on cultural values. Individualism–collectivism and uncertainty avoidance significantly afect threat and coping appraisals with uncertainty avoidance demonstrating slightly stronger efect. Path estimate comparisons between Ghana and the United States show significant diferences for all of the structural paths.

## 1. Introduction

Large-scale attacks gaining unauthorized access to sensitive data, such as Oracle's payment systems [1]. state election databases [2], and Verizon’s customer information [3] have placed a spotlight on the seriousness of information security. One avenue for these attacks is through malicious software that infects users’ machines, providing an opportunity for hackers to conduct attacks on other targets or steal information from the target’s computer. Third party security breaches, commonly through malware and ransomware [4], are the top perceived security threat [4,5]. Recently, several technology service providers and newspapers have reported new forms of malware attacks. One such attack, the HummingBad malware, is designed to gain system-wide authorization access from the targeted device and generate false ad clicks, which create income for the attacker. The HummingBad malware has attacked as many as 10 million Android phones as of July 2016 [6]. Although these attacks originating from China have mostly targeted devices in Southeast Asia (including China, India, Philippines, and Indonesia), they have gradually propagated to the United States (U.S.), Europe, and Australia [7].

One countermeasure against third party security breaches is using antimalware software. Hence, antimalware software or “protective information technologies” is becoming imperative in the global networked society [8,9]. In the global networked community, security attacks against one computer can replicate across geographical boundaries. Because user behaviors (i.e., properly using antimalware software) influence security attacks, it is important to identify factors that influence these individual security behaviors.

Existing research explores security behaviors of individuals in de veloped countries, such as the U.S. [9–15], with some research being done in Asian [13,16–18] and European countries [19]. Like all other research domains in information systems (ISs), little has been done in information security in African communities, a developing region (e.g. [8,20,21],). The understanding of security behaviors in Africa is im portant for several reasons. First, Africa has experienced more growth in mobile and Internet access than any other region over the last decade [22] with a substantial rise in cybercrime in sub-Saharan Africa because of this increase in mobile Internet access [23]. Second, research sug gests that behavioral models difer across cultures [24,25]. Finally, researchers contend that the Western-oriented theoretical models may not be applicable to other cultures and that those models should be extended by including contextual variables such as culture [26–28]. The research question that this paper seeks to address is as follows: How do espoused cultural values influence individual threat and coping appraisals toward the use of protective information technologies?

Hofstede’s [29] cultural model has been found to influence behaviors [16,30]. The U.S. difers from countries in Africa in general and in sub-Saharan Africa in particular on all of the scales in Hofstede’s [29] cultural model [26]. We recognize that national culture is a macrolevel phenomenon, while the security behavior of interest in this research is an individual-level issue [24,25]. Scholars have argued that examining individual behaviors at a national or group level is an ecological fallacy [31]. Therefore, we study cultural values at the in dividual level of analysis (e.g. [16,24,32],) to enhance our under standing in behavioral information security research.

Through protection motivation theory (PMT), prior research sug gests that protective behaviors influence an individual’s ability to appraise threats and perform coping mechanisms. Although mounting evidence suggests that both threat and coping appraisals influence in dividuals’ security behavioral intentions, more integrative research is needed [8,9]. Some PMT-related studies have modeled culture as a moderating variable (e.g. [9,16],). However, some scholars suggest that culture can serve as an antecedent to coping and threat appraisals (e.g. [33,34],). The purpose of this paper is to broaden our understanding of the individual’s security behavioral intention by examining the influences by which cultural variables impact intention through threat and coping appraisal mechanisms.

Our research contributes to theory and practice. First, we extend the PMT model in understanding security behavior in a cross-cultural environment through the application of espoused cultural values. It is the first paper that has empirically validated espoused culture values as antecedents to threat appraisal and coping appraisal although scholars had earlier proposed that such relationships may exist. This form of theory contextualization “allows the efects of contextual variables to be explained by the underlying theoretical frameworks of general models” ([35], p. 116). We have also examined the mediating efects of the core PMT constructs on the relationship between cultural variables and the intention to use protective information technologies [35]. Second, the study contributes to understanding the information security behaviors of individuals in the rarely investigated continent of Africa [21]. Third, given that Ghana’s economy and democracy has been touted as an icon for other African nations, our research provides a roadmap from which further studies can be made to enhance the generalizability of the PMT model in Africa in general and sub-Saharan African nations in particular [35,36]. This is important because information and communication technology (ICT) has been recognized as a driver of growth for transforming the social, economic, political, and cultural conditions of developing nations [37–39]. Finally, in the cur rent highly networked society, studies on individual computer security behavior have implications to individuals, organizations, nations, and the world [20].

In the next section, we present a review of relevant studies on the topic. We then discuss our research framework and hypotheses. Following, we describe the research methodology, including data col lection from the diferent contexts. We then present our results, followed by discussion. Finally, we conclude the paper highlighting the theoretical and practical implications, limitations, and potential future research areas.

## 2. Theoretical background

Information security (InfoSec) research regularly focuses on both technical issues [40], and more recently on the individual user [13], with papers focusing on understanding individual behaviors drawing on theories such as organizational control [11], the theory of planned behavior [41], general deterrence theory [14,42], self-determination theory [43], and PMT [9,10,12,14,15,19,44,45]. The majority of these papers have drawn on PMT as a foundational theoretical underpinning to explain diferences in security practices.

## 2.1. Protection motivation theory

PMT, with origin in the field of social psychology, is regularly used in the IS literature $( \mathrm { e } . \mathrm { g } . , \ [ 9 , 1 0 , 1 2 , 1 4 , 1 5 , 1 9 , 4 4 - 4 7 ] )$ . PMT provides a theoretical explanation on how a person’s perception of a threat and his or her perceived ability to perform a coping behavior influence his or her performance of countermeasures to mitigate those threats.

PMT provides a theoretical understanding of the process by which individuals decide to perform a protective action. It first posits that as part of a cognitive mediating process an individual evaluates the information that they receive and decides what action (or inaction) they are going to perform. The resulting decision is to either continue performing the behavior as before or changing that behavior going forward [48]. The focus of empirical PMT research has been on the influence the cognitive mediating process has on an individual’s behavior. The cognitive mediating process is composed of a threat appraisal and a coping appraisal. Threat appraisal refers to how an individual perceives a given threat and includes perceived severity and perceived susceptibility of the threat [48]. Perceived susceptibility refers to the degree to which someone feels vulnerable to a particular threat, while perceived severity relates to how serious someone perceives a threat to be. The coping appraisal refers to an individual’s assessment of their ability to respond to a given threat and includes the individual’s confidence that performing a given behavior will reduce or mitigate a threat (response eficacy) and that the individual has the ability to perform the given behavior (self-eficacy). The coping appraisal often includes a response cost component, which captures the perceived cost of performing a given behavior [48]. From an information security context, one study illustrates that perceived severity, perceived susceptibility, response eficacy, and self-eficacy positively influenced a person’s intention to use antispyware software [45]. Lee and Larsen [9] also found that all five PMT variables significantly influence individuals’ behavior toward protective information technologies.

Seeking further improvement in theory formation, scholars have called on researchers to investigate the contextualization of well-established theories [16,49]. Researchers must seek the balance between generalizability and context-specificity for their theories. Although generalizability is a desirable feature in theory development, a thor ough understanding of the theory’s applicable boundary conditions is necessary as well. Sarker [50], while recognizing the importance of generalizability, argues for a deliberate selection of contextual variables that contribute to a theory’s applicability in diferent contexts. Like Chen and Zahedi [16], we subscribe to this recommendation. Specifically, Chen and Zahedi [16] used the integrated theory approach in Hong et al. [35] to examine the influence of poly-contextual variables as moderator variables including culture on individual security beha vior using PMT and threat avoidance theory (TTAT). This approach helps to explain inconsistencies in prior studies and improve explanatory power of extant theories. In our case, we selected applicable contextual variables given the theoretical diferences between Ghana and the U.S. and their potential explanatory power with regard to be haviors that protect information. To expand the understanding of user behaviors toward information security, we extend the PMT model by adding individual espoused cultural values as antecedents to the PMT constructs. This approach, which is termed the extended theory ap proach and recognized as the most common form of contextualization in the extant literature, “allows the efects of contextual variables to be explained by the underlying theoretical frameworks of generalized models… and directly accounts for the context distinguishing efects related to the phenomenon being studied” ([35], p. 116). Further, to “assure the efects of context-specific factors are mediated through the underlying theoretical framework” (p. 116), we explicitly examined mediating efects of the core PMT constructs on the relationship between the context specific constructs, i.e., culture, and the intention to use protective technologies.

## 2.2. Espoused cultural values

While a number of studies have employed a PMT-based model to explain security behaviors, little research has explored the antecedents to these factors. To address these issues, we draw on espoused cultural values to explain diferences in individuals’ assessments of threat and coping appraisals. Our justification for marrying the PMT and culture is that theoretical studies on stress and coping found culture as an antecedent to coping and threat appraisal [33,34]. Scholars recognize Hofstede’s cultural theory with (individualism–collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, power distance, and masculinity–femininity) dimensions as the most influential culture theory that has demonstrated strong empirical support in social-science research [51].

In particular, we suggest that individualism–collectivism and uncertainty avoidance afect an individual’s threat and coping appraisals. These were selected above other espoused cultural values for the fol lowing reasons. First, individualism–collectivism is found to be the key dimension among Hofstede’s cultural dimensions [52]. Individualism–collectivism, the dimension most commonly used in crosscultural research (e.g. [28,53,54],), was found to be highly correlated with both masculinity–femininity and power distance. However, individualism–collectivism has demonstrated a low correlation with uncertainty avoidance [55,56]. In a cross-cultural study that included participants from the U.S., India, North Korea, Singapore, and Australia, individualism–collectivism highly correlated with masculinity–- femininity (r = 0.922) and with power distance (r = -0.836); the correlation between individualism–collectivism and uncertainty avoidance (r = 0.155) was low [56]. In that study, only individualism-–collectivism and uncertainty avoidance were included in the research model.

Second, a separate study of managers from diferent ethnic groups in South Africa found that the groups difered mainly on the uncertainty avoidance dimension of Hofstede’s cultural model [55]. Similarly, Atsu et al. [57] did not find masculinity–femininity to be relevant in sub Saharan Africa. ## 2.3. Individualism–Collectivism

Individuals possessing collectivist attributes tend to place a greater emphasis on serving the interests of a larger group, such as extended family, race, nationality, or religion [29]. This in-group collectivism is shown through a profound sense of family beyond the customary nu clear family [58] with people protecting each other in exchange for unquestioned loyalty [17,59]. Conversely, individualist-oriented cultures are inclined to emphasize individual success, independence, and competition, with individual achievements and initiatives being encouraged [17]. Sharing typically does not occur beyond immediate families or close friends in individualist cultures. Sharing generally extends beyond the nuclear family in collectivist cultures, with collectivists seeking approval through group afiliation rather than in dividual identification [60].

The proclivity for sharing by collectivists may cause them to have a greater concern for protecting the assets of the group. This may be due, in part, to highly collectivist societies being more concerned with their loyalty to a group, which may cause an individual to emphasize the needs of the group rather than his or her own needs. Interest in building strong relationships and performing duties for group members is very important for such societies [8,61]. Also, collectivists are heavily influenced by the majority opinion of their group [28]. This could result in feeling responsible for the protection of assets that the group finds important. One prominent area of sharing in collectivist cultures is the sharing of information on technology [62]. Because an individual in a collectivist society trusts his or her group’s opinion, an individual that is consistently provided with similar information on a security threat is likely to perceive elevated threat susceptibility and severity. Others have argued that people with individualist tendencies feel uncomfortable when unexpected situations, such as a malware attack, occur, which urges them to cope with the situation. However, in the case of individualists, they tend to build skills in coping with negative and unexpected changes and are, therefore, less sensitive to the magnitude of the severity as they build skills to cope with security failure [63]. This results in individualists having low threat perception.

Research found women in collectivist societies demonstrate higher perceived susceptibility and perceived severity to threats than women in individualist societies [64]. Surachartkumtonkun et al. [65] found that people in collectivist societies were more likely to appraise system failure as a threat to their resources. In a risk assessment study, Bontempo et al. [66] found that respondents from collectivist cultures (Hong Kong and Taiwan) reported higher perceived risk severity than individuals from individualist cultures (the U.S. and the Netherlands). In a similar risk assessment study, which was conducted at the individual level instead of the national level, the results indicated that people with high collectivist orientations had higher risk severity perceptions than those with individualist orientations [63]. Taken together, we propose that elevated levels of espoused collectivism will lead individuals to perceive the severity of information security threats and the susceptibility to such threats to be greater.

## H1b. Collectivism is positively related to perceived susceptibility.

In collectivist societies, individuals build strong relationships with other individuals within their family and within a kinship system [67]. Through these strong relationships, individuals highly influence each other’s behaviors. One of the key ways collectivists may influence each other’s behavior is through increased knowledge sharing in collectivist communities [68–71]. Further, knowledge sharing has been shown to significantly increase perceptions of response eficacy [72,73]. When an individual evaluates the response eficacy of a behavior, the individual must process the information available about the response and determine whether the response is truly efective at mitigating the given threat. While collectivists must still conduct an appraisal of the response’s efectiveness individually, the information available to them regarding the response is filtered through the collective knowledge that has been shared among the individual’s group [69,70]. As individuals within a person’s group gain trust in the efectiveness of a response at mitigating a threat, this belief will be shared within the group [74], which can elevate an individual’s perception on their own eficacy in responding to such threat. Because of the increased knowledge sharing, and thus increased information vetting, that occurs within collectivist societies, we expect that collectivism will positively influence response eficacy. ## H1c. Collectivism is positively related to perceived response eficacy.

Collectivist societies are typically higher than individualist societies in general academic achievements [75,76], giving support to hypoth esis H1c. However, even given the increased ability, people from col lectivist societies generally possess a lower view of their ability to perform a task than those from individualist societies [77]. This discrepancy can be explained by the way in which collectivists view others in comparison with how individualists view others. People in collectivist societies, such as Ghana, tend to value interdependence, social hierarchies, and conformity to social norms. In individualist societies, such as the U.S., people tend to emphasize independence, achievement, and uniqueness [65]. Collectivists believe in “the importance of at tending to others, fitting in, and harmonious interdependence with them” ([78], p. 224). However, in individualist cultures, people try to distinguish themselves from others by expressing what makes them unique [78]. This suggests that collectivists would have a higher view of the ability of others than individualists would, as individualist would focus more on themselves. Individualist societies place more emphasis on autonomy such that people are encouraged to pursue their own goals and to be independent. Thus, individuals in individualist societies are expected to have a greater sense of control than those in collectivist societies [65]. A review of self-eficacy studies from cross cultural perspective identified several studies reporting lower self-efi cacy for collectivist societies than individualist societies [79–82]. In a more related internet security perceptions and behavior study, Chen and Zahedi [16] observed that while an individual in a collectivist society is generally modest in ascribing his or her self-eficacy, those in individualist cultures are optimistic when judging self-eficacy. Thus, in this study, we also expect collectivists to express lower self-eficacy than individualists.

## H1d. Collectivism is negatively related to perceived self-eficacy.

Collectivist societies value social order and control of feelings and emotions [83]. Further, collectivist individuals will sometimes engage in behaviors that benefit the group as a whole, instead of themselve individually, if a compelling argument can be made for collective in dividual behaviors efecting positive change for the larger group [84]. This value system results in more consideration of the efects that change will cause. In other words, when individuals need to change their behavior and they believe that doing so will influence the entire group, the cognitive cost of doing so will be greater for collectivist in dividuals. Research studies examining information security are uniquely positioned to explore this specific relationship. Threats to in formation security, such as malware, are propagated through individually unprotected machines. A widespread malware outbreak within a group could have been prevented through coordinated individual actions, such as each user ensuring that antimalware software is updated with the most recent malware signatures. If a collectivist perceives that performing an individual behavior, such as protecting his/her machine against an information security threat, is ultimately reliant on everyone in his/her group performing the behavior, he/she may perceive increased response cost, given the degree to which the behavior depends on group participation. Therefore, as consideration for changes in behavior increase, we expect the perceived response cost for these individuals to also increase.

H1e. Collectivism is positively related to perceived response cost.

## 2.4. Uncertainty avoidance

Uncertainty avoidance refers to one’s apprehension related to ambiguous or unknown situations [29]. Low levels of uncertainty avoidance indicate an acceptance of such situations and a high tolerance for risk [85]. Those with low uncertainty avoidance are generally more accepting of others as they will not feel threatened by individuals who project behaviors or opinions difering from their own [86]. High uncertainty avoidance leads to a more aggressive and emotional disposition, whereas a more contemplative and calm temperament is common with low uncertainty avoidance [87]. Individuals with high levels of uncertainty avoidance prefer utilizing laws and regulations to protect themselves from the ambiguity of human nature and unknown elements of the future. A highly structured environment is common for cultures that possess high uncertainty avoidance, as this ofers them a certain level of control over the future [87].

Because uncertainty avoidance indicates a highly risk-averse attitude, security is one of the most powerful motivators in individuals displaying high uncertainty avoidance [86]. Security ofers safeguards from unknown elements in the environment, adding some form of structure to uncertain situations. Previous research shows that in dividuals who espouse higher levels of uncertainty avoidance were more likely to limit the sharing of their personal information with others [88]. Further, individuals in societies exhibiting high uncertainty avoidance are expected to have a greater sense of control and more likely to appraise service failure as a threat than those in societies with low uncertainty avoidance [65]. These findings were confirmed in further research where higher levels of individual-level uncertainty avoidance led to greater information privacy concerns [17]. Privacy concerns are conceptually similar to threat severity perceptions regarding the protection of information. Thus, we hypothesize that those who exhibit elevated perceptions of uncertainty avoidance, and thus are more risk-averse, will have a natural inclination to perceiving threats as being more severe.

## H2a. Uncertainty avoidance is positively related to perceived severity.

Evaluating risk in a given situation requires an individual to determine the likelihood of an adverse event occurring. Regardless of tangible quantitative values applied to the likelihood of a threat, individuals who are more risk-averse express greater discomfort with such a situation because of elevated perceptions of susceptibility to the threat [63]. When it comes to risk, individuals from high uncertainty avoidance cultures are more likely to perceive risk in a given situation [51], and thus believe that they are more susceptible to a threat. Applying these findings to an InfoSec setting, an individual possessing high levels of uncertainty avoidance likely believes that they are more susceptible to threats to information security.

H2b. Uncertainty avoidance is positively related to perceived susceptibility.

People from cultures who demonstrate high levels of uncertainty avoidance generally feel anxious when faced with unfamiliar risks [29]. However, individuals who exhibit high uncertainty avoidance may also be more receptive to information regarding the efectiveness of a threatreducing response [89], which addresses their anxiety related to risk. Because these individuals are more risk-averse, they will be more inclined to believe a response will be efective and, therefore, reduce their risk, especially in cases where fear appeals are used for persuading their behaviors [90]. Based on prior literature in health-based fear appeals, individuals exhibiting high levels of uncertainty avoidance should be more receptive to messages pertaining to information security as well.

## H2c. Uncertainty avoidance is positively related to response eficacy.

Research has shown that some individuals who espoused high levels of uncertainty avoidance also had high levels of self-eficacy [91]. In particular, they found this relationship held for Americans but not for South Koreans, suggesting further exploration of this relationship is necessary. Other research shows that risk-averse individuals are more active in seeking ways to mitigate risk and, therefore, gaining taskbased skills in performing behaviors that mitigate risks [92,93]. Individuals espousing high uncertainty avoidance will habitually perform risk-mitigating behaviors more than those who espouse low uncertainty avoidance [94,95]. If risk-averse individuals continually perform such behaviors, they will gain confidence in their ability to do so [96,97]. This may translate to perceiving self-eficacy in mitigating the risk associated information security threats as well. In information security contexts, we expect those people who exhibit higher uncertainty avoidance to have higher self-eficacy.

## H2d. Uncertainty avoidance is positively related to self-eficacy.

People who demonstrate high levels of uncertainty avoidance feel anxious when faced with unfamiliar risks [29]. Prior research in information security contexts has demonstrated that individuals will perceive the response cost of performing protective behaviors to be lower given elevated perceptions of psychological ownership, which is the degree to which an individual perceives identity, control, and an emotional connection to a target of ownership [98]. InfoSec research has also shown that response cost perceptions are lower given elevated self-determination, which is the degree to which an individual internalizes the motivation to perform a behavior [43]. In each of these instances, the individual perceives the protection of their information to be of great importance and, as such, attaches a high psychological price to their information. The individual will weigh the psychological price of their information against the cost of performing the recommended response and will not perceive the cost of performing the behavior to be prohibitively high. This perception is formed regardless of the absolute cost of the recommended response. Uncertainty avoidance, which results in elevated levels of anxiety and risk aversion in individuals, may demonstrate a similar efect on response cost. Rather than attaching a high psychological price to the information at risk, individuals exhibiting high uncertainty avoidance will perceive a high psychological price on mitigating risk. Such individuals will likely perceive that the cost of performing the behavior, whether related to time, efort, or money, will not seem as high as it would be to individuals exhibiting low uncertainty avoidance. As high uncertainty avoidance individuals develop an understanding of what is necessary to perform a mitigation strategy to reduce the risk [51], they will want to engage in this strategy to lower their anxiety. Therefore, we expect individuals with higher levels of uncertainty avoidance to perceive the response cost of engaging in risk-mitigating behavior to be lower.

H2e. Uncertainty avoidance is negatively related to response cost.

## 2.5. Behavioral intention

InfoSec research regularly investigates people’s intention to perform security behaviors (e.g., [8–10,14,19,45]). An important user-driven behavior that protects an individual from security breaches is the use of antimalware software. As such, in this study, we explore the behavioral intention to use antispyware software. A number of studies provide support for the influence self-eficacy, response eficacy, and response costs have on intentions to perform security behaviors [9,15,44,45]. In particular, Lee and Larsen [9] studied executives’ intention to adopt antimalware software for their organizations. Similarly, Chen and Zahedi [16] found all the relationships between the PMT constructs and protective action significant. Therefore, we test the following hypotheses in our research.

H3. Perceived severity of threats is positively related to an individual’s behavioral intention to use antimalware software.

H4. Perceived susceptibility to threats is positively related to an individual’s behavioral intention to use antimalware software.

H5. Response eficacy is positively related to an individual’s behavioral intention to use antimalware software.

H6. Self-eficacy is positively related to an individual’s behavioral intention to use antimalware software.

H7. Response cost is negatively related to an individual’s behavioral intention to use antimalware software.

## 3. Methods

To answer our research question, we first identified individuals to study from diferent cultures. For this study, we selected the U.S. and Ghana because of their generalized diferences on cultural values along with Ghana’s unique status in the sub-Saharan African region resulting from its recent economic, democratic, and information technology advancement [99], and its ranking as a potential IT outsourcing destination [100].

## 3.1. Background information on Ghana

Ghana’s democratic process has been praised by international governments and institutions and heralded as an icon for other African nations south of the Sahara [36]. With the rapid growth in democracy and rule of law, successive governments have promised to use the nation’s resources to develop infrastructure, education, and health care, as well as prevent crime and reduce poverty. With discoveries of oil reserves, Ghana is poised to capitalize on the security of their democratic processes with new sources of income [101].

Although Ghana is a relatively small sub-Saharan African developing country, its economy is intricately woven into the global economy. Since the mid-1980s, various governments have been im plementing economic reforms and liberalization policies focused on building infrastructural capacity, increasing economic growth, and improving the well-being of its citizens. The economic reform and liberalization policies include free trade policies, removal of price controls, removal of government subsidies, institution of banking reforms, and divestiture of state owned enterprises [99]. One sector of the economy that has been benefited from these reforms is the telecommunications sector. This is in recognition that in the information age a major driving force for human development is the providence of investment in ICT [39]. Although ICT infrastructure in Ghana is generally poor, in the major metropolitan areas it is good [102]. Further, Ghana was ranked third and is one of the two African countries included in the list of potential future IT outsourcing locations, ahead of countries such as Korea, Malaysia, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Taiwan, and Thailand [100].

## 3.2. Cultural diferences between the United States and Ghana

Individuals in the U.S. and Ghana generally possess diferent levels of uncertainty avoidance and individualism–collectivism [26]. As such, both of these countries were included in this study with the intention of using individuals with diferent cultural values in our study to demonstrate the influence of cultural values on individuals’ InfoSec behaviors.

Regarding the cultural dimensions studied by Hofstede [29], individuals in the U.S. and Ghana are generally very diferent. One of the key diferences that exists between these populations is the orientation toward individualism or collectivism. Ghanaians usually possess a more collectivist outlook than the U.S., exhibiting especially strong ties to extended families, clans, and ethnic groups. They make clear distinc tions between those who belong to these in-groups and others who they consider outsiders [26]. Additionally, Ghanaians feel a substantial responsibility to share scant resources among those within the in-group [103]. Although urban areas of sub-Saharan Africa tend to have a more individualist orientation, the strong influence of African collectivism is still prevalent in these regions [104].

Another pertinent diference between the U.S. and Ghana is un certainty avoidance. Ghanaians typically have low tolerance for uncertainty, resulting in high perceptions uncertainty avoidance [57]. They are also generally more risk averse and likely to resist change. Further, people would largely desire to implement rules to reduce or avoid uncertainty [8,61]. As a heavily ritualistic country, Ghanaians adhere to numerous unwritten rules that have been passed on as tradition, contributing to generally high uncertainty avoidance [105]. Conversely, the U.S. is generally low in uncertainty avoidance [29].

In their study on cultural diferences in technology acceptance, Srite and Karahanna [24] recommended that the possible efects of cultural influence must be evaluated at the individual level, as an individual’s personal perceptions of cultural values may vary among other members of a particular group. It would be inappropriate to assume all people possess the same cultural value as generalized by the country they live in. For studies measuring behaviors of individuals, it is critical to measure individual-level cultural values as well. Straub et al. [25] state that afiliation in numerous other groups, which uphold their own sets of cultural values, may afect an individual’s views of a cultural norm. Other researchers have recognized this problem as an ecological fallacy and caution against relating country-wide cultural scores to individuals within the respective cultures [24]. Although we have utilized Hofstede’s cultural indices to provide a context for the diferences that may be present between the U.S. and Ghana, we recognize that individuallevel cultural diferences exist and, as such, propose that measuring cultural values at the individual level is appropriate for our study.

## 3.3. Instrumentation

To test the hypotheses developed, we used a survey methodology. When hypothesizing theoretical models that include the measurement of constructs, it is important to define and test the nature of the con structs in the model in addition to testing the proposed hypotheses. Models can be composed of any combination of reflective, formative, and multidimensional constructs [106]. Appendix A contains the constructs used in this study and where they were adapted from, all of which are reflective except for response cost. Prior to administering the survey instrument, it was necessary to first have the survey reviewed by experts in this context area to ensure that the adaptations used fit within the context proposed. Further, with collecting data in Ghana, it was necessary to have Ghanaians review the developed instrument. Initially, a panel of researchers from the U.S. reviewed the instrument to ensure the items were clear and measured what we thought the items were measuring. Following this, two individuals in Ghana reviewed the instrument. The response from these individuals was that the questionnaire was readable and understandable to an average Ghanaian and to the target sample in particular.

We administered a pretest to examine item loadings by exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Using SPSS, we conducted principal components EFA with a varimax rotation, showing proper loadings for all items. Following the pretest of the instrument, we collected pilot data using both an online data collection instrument and a paper collection strategy. Online surveys were used to collect data from the U.S. sample. Both paper and online surveys were used for Ghanaian participants. There were no significant diferences in responses obtained by the survey administration method, and online surveys were used for our subsequent data collection. Additionally, the pilot data indicated evidence of validity and reliability for our instrument items and preliminary hypotheses support.

Valid survey responses were gathered from 487 students, with 274 from two universities in the southeastern U.S. and 213 responses from Ghana. The same survey instrument was used in both countries, as English is the national language in Ghana. Therefore, the Ghanaian students readily understood the wording in the survey instrument. Males represented 61.4% of the participants, while 30.6% were Caucasian, 47.2% were African or African American and 13.8% were Hispanic. The remaining respondents reported as belonging to some other ethnicity. Also, 52.4% claimed the U.S. as their nationality and 42.9% claimed Ghana as their nationality, with the remaining respondents reported as South American, “other Asian,” or “other.”

Partial least squares (PLS) was used for data analysis. Researchers suggest that the use of PLS requires a sample size of 10 times either the number of structural paths to a particular construct in a model or 10 times the number of formative indicators to a particular construct [107]. The most items in a formative construct in the research model in this present study is three; however, there are 15 structural paths in the model. This suggested that a sample size of at least 150 was needed. However, we also conducted a power analysis to ensure a suficiently large sample size. A power analysis based on previous PMT research [48], which found a medium efect size of .15, an alpha of .05, and power of .80, suggests that 154 responses are necessary to ensure adequate power [108]. Our sample size from each country suficiently meets these recommendations.

The table shows that the composite reliabilities for the measures ranged from 0.790 to 0.915, far above the recommended 0.7 level [110]. Initially, there was some cross-loading. Some of the reflective items that had inadequate loading or had significant crossloadings were removed from the analysis. Because of their reflective nature, items capturing a reflective construct can be removed from data analysis without changing the meaning of the construct [111]. After analyzing these results and removing items, each item loaded on its own construct, and cross loadings were not significant across constructs. Following this, we conducted confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). PLS CFA is illustrated in Appendix B.

To examine discriminant validity, we used two criteria: (1) in dicators must have stronger loadings on their corresponding constructs than on other constructs, and (2) the square root of the AVE must be larger than the interconstruct correlations [112]. ## 4.3. Mediation testing

With the native PMT variables modeled as mediators in our research model, we conducted mediation testing to determine whether collectivism and uncertainty avoidance demonstrated indirect influence on behavioral intention. Based on Baron and Kenny [113] mediation testing recommendations, we conducted a series of Sobel tests to assess the significance of each of the indirect efects. Collectivism had a significant indirect efect on intention through self-eficacy, while uncertainty avoidance indirectly afected intention through threat severity, response eficacy, and self-eficacy. Finally, to determine whether significant mediated relationships were either partial or full, we tested the direct efects of collectivism and uncertainty avoidance on behavioral intention. Collectivism did not significantly influence intention, demonstrating that self-eficacy fully mediated the relationship between collectivism and intention. Uncertainty avoidance had a significant efect on intention similar to findings of a recent study [114], meaning that severity, response eficacy, and self-eficacy partially mediated the relationship between uncertainty avoidance and intention (Table 3).

U.S. respondents demonstrated significantly higher perceptions of severity and intention to perform secure behaviors. Diferences concerning self-eficacy were not significant. The research model utilized in this study empirically tested the role of individual cultural values in determining whether people utilized antispyware software. The pur pose of the study was to test whether adding espoused cultural values as antecedents in a PMT-based model provided enhanced understanding in individual’s security behavior. When cultural values are considered, a better understanding of what causes diferences in intention to perform secure behaviors is uncovered. The constructs that were added to extend PMT in a cultural setting provide a number of insights that help to determine whether people utilize antispyware software. First, the results demonstrate the relevance of the individualism-–collectivism and uncertainty avoidance dimensions of Hofstede’s [29] model in understanding security behavior. Further details reveal that hypotheses 1 (b, c, e), where collectivism acts as an antecedent, and 2 (a–e), where uncertainty avoidance serves as an antecedent to the PMT-based security model, were supported. In examining the path coeficient weights for the relationships derived from our cultural antecedents, uncertainty avoidance seems to have a stronger influence on each of the PMT-based constructs with the exception of perceived susceptibility. While individualism–collectivism has been recognized as the key dimension in Hofstede’s [29] model, here we note that uncertainty avoidance is probably the key cultural antecedent in this context. This supports prior observations from a South African study where the only cultural variable that influenced management behavior was uncertainty avoidance [55]. This may suggest that, in the African population, uncertainty avoidance is a better predictor of security behavior than individualism–collectivism. A plausible reason is that people in Africa in general and Ghana in particular are not comfortable dealing with uncertain situations, such as potential security attacks. In an efort to address the uncertainty associated with security threats, individuals perceive severity, susceptibility, response eficacy, self-eficacy, and response cost to be high. The results also demonstrate that performing a cognitive process involving threat and coping behavior in response to a potential security breach is something that can be influenced more by the uncertainty surrounding security attacks than by the individualism–collectivism construct, where the emphasis is togetherness and shared responsibilities. The sharing of responsibilities precludes individuals from making efective assessments of the severity of the threat and self-eficacy in responding to the threat. Although collectivists may feel that they are responsible for protecting personal assets of a member of a group to which they belong, they may not have the skills and resources to perform such functions. Similarly, a person in a collectivist society may not have the skills and knowledge to rank severity of the attack [77]. This may ex plain why individualism-–collectivism, although a significant influence, does not appear to be as strong a predictor as uncertainty avoidance. Further, contrary to prior work that indicates that collectivism has negative relationship with self-eficacy, we found that collectivism rather has a positive relationship with self-eficacy (e.g. [33],). This might be because of the high collectivism in the sub-Saharan African context as all prior related research used non-Asian cultures to represent collectivist societies. In addition, most of the studies in Klassen’s [33] re view did not actually measure collectivism but based their conclusion on conceivable cultural diferences. The few that did so used collecti vism as a moderator. Thus, our study indicates that the direction of relationship between collectivism and self-eficacy can be influenced by the context. In our context, although the self-eficacy value for the U.S. group was higher than the Ghanaian group, the diference was not statistically significant. Consistent with prior studies [33], we found that self-eficacy has a strong predictive power on subsequent behavior because it had stronger efect on intention than any other predictor.

In alignment with previous PMT research, behavioral intention was significantly influenced by perceived threat severity (H3), response eficacy (H5), self-eficacy (H6), and response cost (H7). However, threat susceptibility did not significantly influence intention in our findings. Ghanaians reported significantly higher perceptions of susceptibility than their U.S. counterparts, but neither mean score was particularly high (Ghana mean = 3.35; U.S. mean = 3.00). Our analysis suggests that although Ghanaians felt more susceptible to spyware threats, neither sample demonstrated that perceived spyware susceptibility was a significant driver toward intention to use antispyware. Prior research in PMT has shown difering results in support for the model based on the type of information security threat being used as the focal point of the study [13]. This phenomenon may also contribute to the lack of support for susceptibility demonstrated in our findings. For the most part, our findings further support PMT in the context of cross-cultural comparisons between the U.S. and sub-Saharan Africa for information security perceptions, extending the boundary conditions of PMT’s applicability.

The diferences between our samples also highlight some interesting findings. Nearly all of the latent constructs demonstrated significant diferences between the two countries. These diferences may highlight the role of cultural values in forming perceptions about InfoSec even further. The largest diferences occurred on perceptions of uncertainty avoidance, collectivism, and susceptibility, where Ghana scored sig nificantly higher for each. While the diferences based on uncertainty avoidance and collectivism may be expected, it is interesting that susceptibility is higher for Ghanaians. A possible reason is that people in the U.S., especially the student population, may be considered as risk taking and may not necessarily develop positive attitudes toward security behavior unless they feel that it is likely to occur. The high selfeficacy, low response eficacy, and high severity in a low uncertainty avoidance culture were responsible for the greater intention to use antimalware software. The findings that individuals in low uncertainty avoidance cultures have higher behavioral intention is confirmed in a recent study that explored influence of uncertainty avoidance on voluntary protective security actions [114].

Ghanaians’ elevated levels of uncertainty avoidance and collectivism certainly had an impact on each PMT construct, but the biggest diference in perceptions of PMT-related variables as a result of cultural values may occur on individuals’ perceptions of susceptibility to threat against information. As previously hypothesized, elevated perceptions of uncertainty avoidance and collectivism heighten an individual’s need for security and, therefore, attune an individual to an acute sense of threats posed toward himself or herself and those in his or her com munity or in-group. This may be the most critical diference between our samples.

## 5.1. Implications for theory

Our findings expand our understanding of the efects of culture on attitude toward security behavior. This study contributes to understanding the InfoSec behaviors of individuals in the rarely investigated continent of Africa [21] by expanding the PMT model and therefore enhancing its external generalization in understanding security behavior in a cross-cultural environment through the application of relevant cultural dimensions. The PMT-based security model held with the combined respondents from two diferent countries. An interesting finding is that both uncertainty avoidance and individualism–collectivism were found to be significant, although uncertainty avoidance demonstrated slightly stronger influence. The generalizability of a theoretical model across time, populations, and context is a first step in establishing the eficacy of the model. In particular, cross-cultural examination and testing a model in diferent countries is one of the main steps by which replication and generalizability of job satisfaction and theories, such as technology adoption and theory of planned behavior were achieved [115]. It is the first paper that has empirically validated espoused culture values as antecedents to threat appraisal and coping appraisal although scholars had earlier proposed that such relationships may exist. Thus, by examining the relationship between culture and PMT constructs in the same model, the understanding of the relations between these constructs is increased. In addition, understanding the antecedents to the PMT constructs deepens our understanding of the underpinning of the key predictors of PMT in relation to individual’s security behavior [115]. In doing so, we contribute to the IS field by developing a richer theoretical model around PMT just as it has been done with many theories, such as TAM and TBP, as well as a construct such as job satisfaction [35,115].

Also critical is the diference in threat susceptibility perceptions between our samples, where Ghanaians exhibited significantly higher perceptions of susceptibility. Further, given that Ghana’s economy and democracy have been touted as an icon for other African nations and top rated potential location for IT ofshoring, our study provides a roadmap from which further studies can be made to enhance the generalizability of the PMT model in Africa in general and sub-Saharan African nations in particular [36]. Finally, the results demonstrate that an individual’s cultural values are impacted by his or her national culture, which, in turn, influences his or her security behavior and that the use of individual espoused cultural values provides insights into understanding diferences in security behavior in developed and developing nations.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

Prior research suggested that the level of security concern may be regionally based [116]. Similar to these regionally based diferences in opinions, we observed that people in Ghana had higher perceptions of threat susceptibility and response eficacy than individuals in the U.S. However, we have noted that people in the U.S. possess higher perceptions of threat severity and are more likely to report an intention to perform secure behaviors than those in sub-Saharan Africa. Meanwhile, it has been reported that cybercrime is a rising concern in sub-Saharan Africa [23]. Thus, the potential of ICT to improve the development of the people in sub-Saharan Africa can be hindered if efforts are not made to address cyber threats in the region. Hence, the research results add to the literature that the regional bias of security behavior is of major concern that needs to be addressed. Thus, training individuals on security behaviors, such as the need to implement antispyware and the techniques for doing so, will be helpful. In addition, providing this type of software freely to individuals may equip them to enhance security behavior toward the use of protective information technologies. If people with high uncertainty and high collectivist societies are trained and made aware that the antimalware software is free and is easy to use, they may perform the security behavior that will help them protect their computer systems, reducing the chances that they would be attacked or their systems would be used by cyber criminals to commit crime [51]. However, Klassen [33] reports that in collectivist societies where individuals have tendencies to report low self-eficacy, in-groupbased training was more efective than individual-based training in ensuring subsequent positive impact. Hence, the findings in this paper dictate that group-based training on the use of antimalware are needed to improve the use of protective technologies such as antimalware software. Finally, we believe that in the current highly networked society, the discussions presented in this paper provide practical implications that can add to the discourse and solutions needed to address the global impact of cyber-crimes in the sub-Saharan Africa region [23]. Further, with growth in mobile applications and new forms of malware attacks, individuals, software vendors, and mobile application, developers need to understand security behavior so that efective mechanisms can be developed to reduce the threat that malware attacks pose to people living in these cultures.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

This research is not without limitations. One limitation of the research is social desirability bias. The survey involved self-reported tasks individuals say they perform. Individuals may say what they believe they are supposed to say, which does not accurately reflect what they actually do. To overcome this, researchers could create a way to monitor the actual security tasks a person performs. Further, this research captures data at one point in time, but InfoSec is an evolving

## 6. Conclusion

This study was conceived with the intention to examine how cul tural values afect security behaviors, specifically targeting an area of the world that has not been studied extensively. This study is one of the first to explore InfoSec in a sub-Saharan African context where significant relationships were demonstrated between known cultural dimensions and the threat and coping appraisals that inform an individual’s security behavior. It is also the first paper that has empirically validated espoused culture values as antecedents to threat appraisal and coping appraisal although scholars had earlier proposed that such relationships may exist. The data presented in this study indicate that adding individual cultural dimensions to a PMT-based research model is a worthwhile research opportunity to continue exploring. To fully understand further what is going on from a cultural standpoint, it will be necessary to collect more data from individuals in other countries where people have diferent cultural values. Cross-cul tural issues will continue to be an interesting research avenue for InfoSec scholars for the near future with a variety of potential implications for both practitioners and researchers. Notwithstanding the limitations of the current paper, it provides an initial roadmap from which theoretical contributions can be developed with respect to security practices and behaviors among cultures.
