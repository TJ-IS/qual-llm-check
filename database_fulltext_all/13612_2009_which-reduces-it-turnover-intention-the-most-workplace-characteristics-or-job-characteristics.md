# Which reduces IT turnover intention the most: Workplace characteristics or job characteristics?<sup>§</sup>

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Turnover Satisfaction Trust Fairness Security Information sharing Job characteristics

## A B S T R A C T

Studies have shown that positive perceived job characteristics, such as job significance and task autonomy, tend to decrease IT personnel turnover intention. In addition, employee perception of their workplace characteristics may affect turnover. Few studies have examined this. We tested whether workplace characteristics – structural fairness, trust in senior management, employee information sharing, and job security – affected turnover intention as much as did job characteristics. We found that workplace characteristics out-predicted job characteristics. However, this was true only for programmer/analysts. The reverse was true for technical support personnel. Practical implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Retaining IT employees is vital to an organization as they often hold tacit knowledge about how systems interface with key business processes. They tend to have specialized, hard-to-replace skills, which makes turnover expensive. Because the demand for skilled technical workers is strong, experienced IT workers can find new jobs, creating turnover.

Past IT retention research has focused on how employee job perceptions predicted their psychological feelings and thus affected turnover. For example, perceptions about one’s job was shown to influence job satisfaction which, in turn, lead to turnover intention. Some research (e.g., [11]) examined how job perceptions influenced work exhaustion and thereby affected turnover intention. Thatcher et al. [12] tested how job characteristics affected job satisfaction.

But, while the job can be a significant motivator, IT employees consider many other factors when deciding to leave a firm. Fig. 1 distinguishes between job characteristics and context characteristics within and outside the workplace.

We defined job characteristics as worker perceptions about the task: its nature and content. These included skill variety, autonomy, job feedback, etc. But IT professionals work in a context and the workplace characteristics affect worker perceptions of the organization in which they operate. These include perceptions of reward fairness, job security, and supervisor satisfaction. They refer to how the employee feels about the workplace. Outside the workplace characteristics, like work-family conflict [3], may also affect turnover.

Companies may create workplace conditions that fail to address basic employee needs, leading to employee turnover. We focused on four needs: structural (i.e., workplace) fairness, employee/team member information sharing, job security, and trust in senior management.

Studies have shown that both job and workplace characteristics influence turnover. For example, Igbaria and Siegel [8] differentiated between task- and organization-based rewards, suggesting that both job and workplace based variables should be considered. But IT managers need to know which is more important. Hence, our research question was:

Do workplace characteristics affect IT turnover intention as much as do job characteristics? The answer will suggest how much effort managers should apply to each.

To answer the question, we modeled job and workplace characteristics as second-order concepts created by combining formative perceptual variables (Fig. 2). The job characteristics concept was formed from the five Job Characteristics Model (JCM)

While some studies used them as separate variables, others combined them; e.g., Igbaria et al. [7] summed them and found that the sum was highly correlated with job satisfaction.

We believe that both job and workplace characteristics will affect turnover intention through job satisfaction and work exhaustion. Our model thus treated specific job and workplace factors as formative first-order constructs of the second-order job and workplace characteristics.

## 2. Research model development

2.1. Existing turnover intention factors

2.1.1. Job satisfaction and work exhaustion effects

We began by formulating hypotheses about job satisfaction and work exhaustion—constructs already known to affect turnover intention.

H1. Job satisfaction among IT workers will negatively influence turnover intention.

Fig. 2. Our research model.

Moore [11] found that work exhaustion (how much a worker felt emotionally drained) affected IT-related turnover perceptions.

H2. Work exhaustion among IT workers will positively influence turnover intention.

## 2.1.2. The effects of job characteristics

Ang and Slaughter [2] proposed that job design characteristics would impact IT professionals’ attitudes, behavior, and job performance. We felt that the five job characteristics form a second-order concept: Perceived job characteristics, representing the extent to which an employee feels favorable towards the way the job was designed. Autonomy evaluates the employee’s freedom, independence, and discretion in scheduling the work and how to carry it out. Job feedback is a measure of the extent to which the manager communicates how the employee performs the job. Skill variety defines how many skills are required by the employee. Job identity evaluates the extent to which workers believe the task is well-defined. Job significance determines how important the job appears to be. This set of attributes provides an overall perception of the job’s features.

H3. IT worker perceived (positive) job characteristics will positively influence job satisfaction.

Negative job characteristics will increase work exhaustion. Job feedback lets one know how to improve performance and this should decrease burnout. Skill variety should decrease work exhaustion by reducing repetition. Autonomy should reduce work exhaustion by allowing self-redirection of efforts. Seeing one’s work as part of a complete whole (task identity) makes the job more meaningful. Thus, each job characteristics could help reduce work exhaustion.

H4. IT worker perceived (positive) job characteristics will negatively influence work exhaustion.

Job characteristics may impact intention directly. But this impact may be fully mediated by job satisfaction and work exhaustion, because they summarize positive and negative affect: the influence of job characteristics’ will mostly be reflected in work exhaustion and job satisfaction.

H5. IT worker job satisfaction and work exhaustion will fully mediate the effects of perceived job characteristics on turnover intention.

## 2.1.3. The effects of workplace characteristics

We believe that four workplace characteristics are key to IT workers: job security, reward fairness, team information sharing, and trust in senior leadership. We felt that IT management could influence how employees felt about their work environment. For example, by growing the firm and maintaining a no-layoff policy, management provides a sense of job security. Our model placed job security as a first-order factor of the second-order workplace characteristics (see Fig. 2). Trust in senior managers results in worker satisfaction due to the belief that the company will be guided effectively. Structural fairness and information sharing among workers should also positively influence job satisfaction. Thus each of the four workplace components were expected to affect job satisfaction.

H6. IT worker perceived workplace characteristics will positively influence job satisfaction.

The more favorable the workplace, the less exhausted the employee. Knowing that one’s job is secure removes the stress. A worker who feels rewarded fairly can concentrate on the job instead of worrying about rewards. Knowing one has fellow workers who openly share information decreases the chances of burnout. The workplace characteristics concept represents the perception that the work setting is right—which should reduce work exhaustion. But if the work setting has negative aspects, overall perceptions are likely to increase work exhaustion.

H7. IT worker perceived workplace characteristics will negatively influence work exhaustion.

Thite [13] found that job insecurity predicted intentions to quit. Information sharing and trust in senior management are also likely to affect turnover intention. Hunter and Tan’s study found this to be true [6]. Workplace characteristics are broader than job characteristics—covering organizational issues that form a context around the job. Thus, job satisfaction and work exhaustion should only partially mediate workplace effects.

H8. IT worker job satisfaction and work exhaustion will partially mediate the negative effects of perceived workplace characteristics on turnover intention.

Based on the above mediation arguments, we believe that workplace characteristics will more strongly influence turnover intention than job characteristics; thus:

H9. IT worker perceived workplace characteristics will have a stronger total influence on turnover intention than will perceived job characteristics.

## 3. Method

Our study was conducted in an IT department of a large, publicly traded United States food products organization. Questionnaires were sent to programmer-analysts and systems support employees. 169 surveys were distributed and 127 surveys (75%) were returned; all were usable. Eighty-seven respondents were programmer/analysts and forty were systems support workers. Since we were not allowed to contact non-responders, we tested the mean differences between early and late responders and found no significant mean differences on any variable.

The job characteristics and job satisfaction measures (see Appendix) were adapted from Hackman [5]. Turnover intention and work exhaustion each used four measures from Moore [11]. Structural fairness items 1, 2, and 5 were new; items 3 and 4 were from Moore. The information sharing, job security, and trust items were new. A pilot study validity-tested the job and workplace characteristics items. Due to favorable results, all items were retained. Tenure (time in position) was included as a control variable, since it tends to affect turnover.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. The PLS measurement model

PLS (partial least squares) is often used to evaluate research models with modest sample sizes (<150); it is also used to test complex models with formative factors. We ran PLS and inspected the outer model loadings, eliminating five items with loadings less than 0.65. We next assessed the psychometric properties; examining the first-order factor psychometric properties, using the internal composite reliability (ICR) and the average variance extracted (AVE). An ICR of 0.7 is considered sufficient: the lowest shown in Table 1 was 0.88. A valid construct has an AVE greater than 0.50: the lowest was 0.67; thus the constructs were valid. Discriminant validity requires that the square roots of the AVEs for two latent variables must each be greater than their intercorrelations. The data passed this requirement.

We evaluated the second-order model in two ways. First, we calculated whether the second-order constructs were appropriately modeled as formative latent constructs by examining the correlations among the dimensions: they were statistically significant at $p < 0 . 0 5 ( r = 0 . 1 0 \ – 0 . 4 4 ) ,$ , except for autonomy, which related neither to job feedback nor to job significance $( r = 0 . 1 0$ for each). We decided to retain autonomy, since it formed a distinct aspect of job characteristics and since formative first-order factors are often modestly correlated. All correlations among the workplace characteristics factors were significant $( r = 0 . 3 4 \ – 0 . 6 5 )$ . The modest correlations among first-order factors suggested that we correctly modeled them as formative indicators.

We then assessed second-order factor validity. The magnitude of the path coefficients from the first-order factors to the secondorder factors suggests second-order construct convergent validity and unidimensionality. We found that each first-order factor significantly related $\left( p < 0 . 0 5 \right)$ to its respective second-order construct (see Fig. 3). The second-order factors correlated with each other at only $r = 0 . 5 5$ , indicating that they were discriminant. The second-order factors appeared to operate well within their proposed nomological network (Fig. 3).

## 4.2. Results—structural model

Fig. 3 path coefficients represent standardized betas while the $R ^ { 2 }$ amounts represent the variance explained. The structural model supported all but one hypothesis. Per H3 and H4, job characteristics predicts both job satisfaction $\left( p < 0 . 0 0 1 \right)$ and work exhaustion $\left( p < 0 . 0 5 \right)$ . Per H6 and H7, workplace characteristics predicts both job satisfaction $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and work exhaustion $\left( p < 0 . 0 1 \right)$ Job satisfaction predicts turnover intention $( p < 0 . 0 0 1$ [H1]). However, work exhaustion did not predict turnover intention (H2).

To test the mediation hypotheses, we used a variant of Baron and Kenny’s [4] three steps: (1) the independent variable (IV) must predict the mediator (M); (2) the IV must predict the dependent variable (DV); and (3) when entered, M must decrease the predictive strength of the IV-DV link. First, we ran a model directly from the IVs to the DV (turnover intention). Both job characteristics $( \beta = 0 . 2 0 ; ~ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and workplace characteristics $( \beta = 0 . 3 4 ;$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ significantly predicted turnover intention. Second, job and workplace characteristics predicted both of the mediators. Third, we added links from the mediators to turnover intention; job satisfaction and work exhaustion fully mediated the effects of job characteristics on turnover intention $( \beta = 0 . 0 2 ; \mathrm { n s } )$ , supporting

H5. They partially mediated the effects of workplace characteristics on turnover intention $( \beta = 0 . 2 2 ; p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , supporting H8. To test H9, we summed the direct effects and indirect effects (i.e., through the mediators) of job and workplace characteristics (Table 2). The total effects of workplace characteristics were larger (0.34–0.20), supporting H9.

## 5. Discussion

We performed this study to determine whether workplace factors affected IT turnover intention as much as did job characteristics. IT employees are often more loyal to their profession than they are to their firm, and we felt that this might manifest itself by a heightened concern for workplace characteristics. We found that both job and workplace characteristics had a significant effect on turnover. However, workplace characteristics had the greatest impact.

We also showed that characteristics of the workplace and job are clearly distinguishable and could be modeled as distinct second-order factors, not only as first-order factors. Our study also confirmed the importance of job satisfaction, but, at least for the organization studied, work exhaustion was not a major turnover factor.

## 5.1. Alternative model robustness tests

In order to assure the results were not merely a function of the selected variables, we ran two tests of model robustness. First, to be sure the results were not due to the unique qualities of the JCM variables, we ran a second model, in which we substituted role conflict, role ambiguity, perceived workload, and autonomy for the JCM factors. We positioned them as formative factors, as before. We found these job characteristics significantly predicted job satisfaction $( \beta = 0 . 3 1 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and work exhaustion $( \beta = - 0 . 3 5 ;$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Again, the effects of job characteristics were almost fully mediated $( \beta = - 0 . 0 9 ; \mathrm { n s } )$ . The results showed that for programmer/analysts, workplace characteristics was a much stronger predictor of turnover intention than was job characteristics (0.41 vs. 0.17). By contrast, for support workers, job characteristics had the stronger predictive effect (0.39 vs. 0.14). Since the results differed significantly by job type, putting all IT employees together may hide a group’s unique motivational needs. From this result, it appears that different IT subgroups may have very different reasons for leaving the company. Programmer/ analysts have domain knowledge tied specifically to the company. This makes them more valuable. Companies will often indoctrinate new programmer/analysts by placing them with end users to learn the business; thus preparing them to design and build systems better suited to user needs. Support personnel, on the other hand, may not be tied to their workplace through such business training.

## 5.3. Implications for practice

Managers need to be aware that workplace context characteristics affect turnover. Trust in senior management reflected the strongest on workplace context. IT management needs to nurture worker trust by having and projecting competence and concern and by enunciating policies and procedures that create a fair workplace. Workers should also feel that their jobs are reasonably secure. Managers should make sure that IT jobs have skill variety. IT workers also like jobs that have felt significance and provide feedback. Managers should design support workers’ jobs carefully and should manage workplace structures favorable to programmer/analysts.

## 5.4. Limitations

Our study was cross sectional, creating the possibility of ambiguity in causal direction. Further, the possibility of common method variance was present, since allthe constructswere measured using a single survey instrument. We tested for this using the Harman one-factor test, placing all the items for the 12 constructs into one factor analysis to see whether more than 50% could be explained by one factor. This analysis resulted in eleven factors with eigenvalues over 1.0, and only 30% of the variance explained by the first factor (80% explained by all factors with eigenvalue > 1.0). Thus, common methods variance is not a major factor.

Another limitation was that the results have limited generalizability, since the sample was collected from a single IT department. Nonetheless, the results did provide insight into reasons that IT employees exhibited intentions to leave. We must however caution that turnover intention does not always produce turnover, as they generally intercorrelate from r = 0.32 to 0.52.

## 6. Conclusion

We tested whether job or workplace characteristics formed a more important factor of turnover and its antecedents, job satisfaction and work exhaustion. In this study of one large corporate IT shop, we found workplace characteristics constituted the more important turnover factor. Job characteristics were also important, but their effects on turnover were fully mediated by job satisfaction and work exhaustion. This means that organizational workplace factors play a vital role in IT retention, especially for programmer/analysts. Since IT management can influence all four of the workplace factors that we studied, our research suggested management could reduce turnover by employing these workplace factors as managerial levers.
