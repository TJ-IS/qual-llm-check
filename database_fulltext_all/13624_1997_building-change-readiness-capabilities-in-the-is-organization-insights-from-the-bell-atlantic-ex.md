## Abstract

Change-readiness is the ability of an information systems (IS) organization to deliver strategic IT applications within short development cycle times by utilizing a highly skilled internal IS workforce. This paper examines two important questions: What are the design elements of a change-ready IS organization? How can transformations to such designs be effectively managed? Insights to these questions are generated through a case study of the conceptualization and implementation of an innovative organization design within a large IS unit at Bell Atlantic, a Regional Bell Operating Company. First, a rich description is provided of the components (strategy, structure, processes, people skills, reward systems) of a centers of excellence (CoE) design that has yielded measurable gains in IS performance. Then, an analysis of the two-year implementation of this CoE design is provided in terms of anticipated and unanticipated change actions, as well as a summary of “lessons learned.” The conclusion describes the design as a model worthy of consideration by other IS managers for developing change-readiness IT capabilities. Comparisons with other models for the IS organization are then drawn.

Keywords: Structure of the IS function, issues in organizing IS, IS staffing

ISRL Categories: EB, EG02, EG03

## Introduction

Turbulent business and IT environments are posing new imperatives for systems development activities: (1) to rapidly deliver IT applications that facilitate agile responses to changing markets and competitive rivalries and (2) to cultivate desired IT skills and effectively leverage them across an ongoing stream of systems projects. As the velocity of these imperatives rises and IS organizations reposition themselves as strategic business partners, their ability to build and sustain change-readiness IT capabilities will become crucial (Nolan and Croson 1995; Rockart et al. 1996; Ross et al. 1996). Change-readiness IT capabilities include a firm's ability to:

1. Enhance competitive agility by delivering IT-based products, services, and business applications within short development cycle times. Many of these IT-based applications are of the first-mover type that could surprise the competition and potentially alter industry practices (Goldman et al. 1995; Hofman and Rockart 1992; Meyer 1993; Ross et al. 1996).

2. Build a highly skilled, empowered, and energized IS workforce with an entrepreneurial orientation toward leveraging technological knowledge into business applications (Bowen et al. 1991; Keen 1991; Lawler 1996; Ross et al. 1996).

Organization designs are one of the key levers for building and sustaining such capabilities (Lawler 1996; Mata et al. 1995; Nadler et al. 1992). This paper examines two important questions:

\- What are the design elements of a change-ready IS organization?

\- How can transformations to such an IS organizational design be effectively managed?

Insights are drawn from an analysis of a successful effort to develop a change-readiness IT capability within a corporate information systems unit at Bell Atlantic (1996 revenues: \$13 B)—a Regional Bell Operating Company (RBOC) with a history of competing in the local voice and data market segments of the telecommunications industry.

The next section describes the forces compelling change-readiness IT capabilities and the vision of an IS organization to build these capabilities. The components of this innovative design are described using a prior published framework (Galbraith 1995; Lawler 1996). The key elements of the transformation process are then described, and the key initiatives are summarized along a timeline as well as in the form of “lessons learned.” The paper closes with a comparison of the centers of excellence model and other IS organization designs. The research methodology is described in the Appendix.

## The Bell Atlantic IS Organization

## The business transformation imperative

Traditionally, the telecommunications industry has operated as an oligopoly, with the markets and activities of the RBOCs governed by regulations. However, during the last few years, this industry has been in the throes of dramatic alterations to its competitive forces and a hypercompetitive environment has emerged. Landmark federal legislation deregulating the industry passed in February 1996, allowing competition between the RBOCs, long-distance carriers, and cable companies. This legislation is expected to fuel an industry consolidation and shakeout in which only a handful of major national and international providers are expected to survive. Bell Atlantic faces the prospect of a world where “market windows of opportunity” open and close very rapidly. Within this context, sustained success would require mastering the “sense-and-respond” business paradigm: anticipate customer needs through intimate relationships and satisfy customers through products and services generated from a platform of capabilities (D'Aveni 1994; Haeckel and Nolan 1993). In the words of CEO Raymond W. Smith,

The winners in this era of open markets will be those who are organized around the requirements of the market and ready to take advantage of emerging opportunities [excerpt from the 1995 Bell Atlantic Annual Report].

Since this emerging environment is a discontinuity from the past, Bell Atlantic faces a transformational imperative: move from a protected, regulated, and oligopolistic market environment to a hypercompetitive market environment, characterized by a focus upon speed, surprise, customer intimacy, and operational excellence (D'Aveni 1994; Treacy and

Wiersema 1995). By the end of 1993, in anticipation of this paradigm shift, CEO Smith had launched several significant initiatives, including a major cultural change and a new role for IT and the IS organization.

## IS transformation thrust

Historically, the IS organization at Bell Atlantic was designed to provide support for products, services, and operations of the company within its regulated markets. An internal survey of Bell Atlantic's senior management revealed that the IS organization was viewed as playing a "neutral" role. When Ralph Szygenda took over the reins of the IS organization as CIO in 1993, $^{2}$ he found an IS workforce whose expertise was concentrated in large mainframe applications. The prevailing capabilities were appropriate for stable, regulated, and protected business environments: long (two-plus years) development cycle times, a narrow technological talent base, and a low strategic focus in IT applications.

The hiring of a new CIO with responsibilities for IT and business reengineering signaled a fundamental change in the role of the IS organization: from a reactive, cost-control enabler to a proactive, strategic differentiator of the business. Faced with the corporate transformational business pressures and the new, emergent role of IT, the building of a change-ready IS organization became an important priority on Szygenda's agenda:

As a company we need to be agile, dynamic; we have to be able to move quick enough.... First, I had to have the best team possible. I needed people who had been there before—winners elsewhere. Second, we needed to create the environment for a rapid-moving company, with acquisitions, divestitures, and alliances overnight.

At the end of 1993, the IS organization was restructured (see Figure 1): five direct reports served as group information officers for the restructured lines of business (LOBs) and had systems development responsibilities for their business clients. Four transformational goals were identified:

1. Move from a legacy, mainframe application development environment, characterized by long development cycle times and excessive maintenance activities, to a more business responsive environment, characterized by short development cycle times, a philosophy of continuous product innovation, and delighted user clients.

2. Infuse strategic thinking within the IS organization to conceptualize and execute novel business applications that sustain the firm's competitive agility and customer satisfaction.

3. Build an IT talent base that provides a solid internal pool of skilled IS professionals, trained in current skills (client/server, object-oriented, networking), customer relationship management, and project management. Further, create a process for the rapid absorption of new skills and retirement of obsolete skills.

4. Build an IS workforce whose values change from an entitlement mentality (“the company is responsible for my career progression”) to an entrepreneurial mentality (“I am responsible for enhancing my skill and career opportunities”).

To help achieve the IS transformational objectives, Szygenda also deliberately encouraged his management to “experiment” with innovative organizational designs.

The most significant of these initiatives was a radical redesign of the Large Business and Information Systems (LB & IS) unit within corporate IS, implemented in July 1994, under its visionary, Charles E. Clark, vice president and IS-LB&IS CIO. This unit of about 250 employees is responsible for new applications development and maintenance for three LOBs located within three major geographic clusters. Prior to Clark's arrival at Bell Atlantic in January 1994, IS-LB&IS had done a fairly good job of staying ahead of the demand curve and reducing unit costs. However, its resources were primarily devoted to the support of legacy systems that would soon outlive their usefulness. In addition, the IS managers were responsible for a variety of activities, including applications development and maintenance, defining new projects and enhancements, managing customer relationships and personnel resources, and developing the careers of IS personnel. Generally, these managers' first priority would be applications development and maintenance, leaving them little time to focus on strategic thinking and customer relationships management, let alone the development of new skills for IS human resources. The existing type of managerial focus worked well in a structured and stable legacy environment, where the applications were at least five to 10 years old, and the work force was rewarded for subject matter expertise and consistent, repetitive performance. However, such an organization was not appropriate for the emerging role of IS as a strategic differentiator of the business. According to Clark, "to paraphrase a Chinese proverb, it was realized that if we didn't change our direction, we might end up 'where we were headed.'"

## The Change-Ready IS Organization Design at IS-LB&IS

The vision for a change-ready organization at IS-LB&IS was a skills-based “centers of excellence” (CoE) approach, with a deliberate design molded after a quest of more than two years by Clark as an IS manager in a different firm. The CoE organization design at IS-LB&IS created distinct roles and processes for conceptualizing strategic IT applications, rapidly delivering IT applications, and building the requisite IT skillbase.

In April 1994, Clark enlisted one of his current directors, Nancy Cavanaugh, who had experience and interest in organization and personnel development to serve in a key role as a CoE director responsible for implementing the model. Since the implementation of the CoE design in mid-1994, the IS-LB&IS unit has witnessed significant improvements on a variety of measures: customer satisfaction, projects completed on-time, and the resident IT talent base (see Table 1). Comments by senior client managers on formal IS project team evaluations provide additional qualitative support:

The products provided are high in quality, innovation, and responsiveness. And all at a "price tag" within best cost.

[The IS team is] always thinking about the impact on customers: internal and external.

IS met every deadline to date on the project and has exceeded expectations on the quality and thoroughness of the deliverables.

I have found a significant improvement over 1994—I am very pleased with the team's support of [the application] this year—job well done!!

One client manager provided this assessment of the CoE design impact on IT talent leadership:

Our IS team is more technically qualified than their equivalents at [an outside software contractor]. Their technical expertise has called the [outside software contractor] to accountability. Because our IS team is competent, the risks of aggressive timelines are minimized.

The CoE initiative was also publicly credited with the rapid delivery of an innovative application. The members of this project team received the highest corporate honor: a Bell Atlantic Spirit of Excellence Citation award. In the words of the project manager:

In the traditional management structure an employee must impress, through job performance or otherwise, his immediate supervisor in order to progress to jobs of more responsibility and visibility. The problem with this structure is that more often than not a person's supervisor becomes a barrier to and not a facilitator of career development. Rarely will a supervisor, after the initial hiring, examine a person's resume and ask the basic question of career development: Based upon this individual's skills, education, and performance, what is the best position for this individual? . . . From the manager's point of view, they are responsible for delivery to the client and the development of people that work for them is not a priority and, many times, ignored completely.

## Strategy: change-ready IT capabilities

According to the CEO, Ray Smith:

Bell Atlantic intends to compete by sharpening our market focus, reducing our cost structures, speeding up our response time, and offering the highest value products and services of any competitor in the business [from the April 1993 Investor's Reference Guide].

Therefore, IS-LB&IS initiated a change-readiness functional strategy with three thrusts:

1. Rapid development of "bite-sized" applications to dramatically reduce cycle times for applications delivery and facilitate speeding of products to markets. According to Szygenda, "We have to build systems in six months; that's driven by the business."

2. Greater strategic focus in IT applications in order to sharpen the market linkage and customer intimacy through IT.

3. IT talent leadership through building, learning, and renewal of valued IS skills. The IS-LB&IS unit aims to build an internal pool of highly skilled staff whose expertise can be rapidly and economically leveraged across different projects.

## Structure: groups and integrator roles

Structure is the manner in which people are grouped together, their roles and reporting relationships, and their task assignments. Structures should be designed to be flexible and learning-enabling, as well as effective in promoting efficiency and cost-effectiveness (Bahrami 1992; Nadler et al. 1992; Tushman and O'Reilly 1996).

The CoE design includes two types of structural overlays (see Figure 3): team-based groupings and integrator roles (Galbraith 1995; Mohrman et al. 1995). The three key team-based structures are examined in more detail here.

The IS-LB&IS management team, consisting of CIO Clark and his five directors, is the steering committee responsible for achieving the functional strategy of the IS-LB&IS unit and its ongoing conduct. It is also the planning team for the IS transformation and the decision body for interface issues involving other corporate IS units.

The skill centers (CoEs) are semi-permanent teams of technical specialists, or people trained in a specific IT skill. Each skill center is a “virtual homeroom” of IS personnel who share a high level of proficiency in the specific skillset. The initial 12 skill centers were defined using three overarching guidelines: (1) avoid skillsets that are too fine-grained, (2) keep the total number of members at or below 30, and (3) create skillsets needed for the future, even if there are no IS personnel to populate them currently. Grouping employees by skills was a major philosophical change: based on their current skillsets, all IS personnel below the rank of director selected a skill center to be initially assigned to and a CoE that they would like to belong to in the future. (For specific skillsets, see people skills below.)

Delivery engagement teams are temporary project teams created for each new approved development (or support) project. These teams are formed by drawing a delivery manager from the Delivery Management CoE and individuals from other CoEs based on the project's skill requirements. The ideal team is appropriately skilled and tightly focused on delivering the application on-time, within cost, and according to client expectations. The team is disbanded at the conclusion of a new development project. Most members are assigned to a different project, or a training and development stint; a small subset is assigned to an application support team to ensure knowledge transfer.

As shown in Figure 3, the CoE design also utilizes three types of integrator manager roles: skill center managers (CoE managers), managers responsible for business client relationships (account managers), and managers of the delivery engagement teams (delivery managers). These managers report to one of the five directors on the IS-LB&IS management team. Each one of these integrator roles is discussed below; a summary of their role responsibilities is provided in Table 3.

CoE managers (CMs) manage one or more skill centers and have three primary responsibilities: (1) assign CoE personnel to specific application projects, (2) coach, groom, and counsel CoE members to achieve high skill levels through training and development, performance appraisal, and career management advice, and (3) develop rules, tools, and processes for the CoE. The CM position is a clear signal of commitment to a separation of people development work from project management and delivery: CMs are exclusively responsible for development of the CoE human resources.

CMs also have the sole resourcing authority for all IS-LB&IS personnel below the level of director; based on skillsets requested by the delivery manager, they assign specific CoE members to the projects. In making sourcing decisions, the CM weighs an individual's needs for training and development against a project's needs for expertise. Since the project teams are temporary assignments, the CM is also the "permanent report" for an employee and provides a continuity to the counseling role.

Account managers (AMs) manage the accounts of one or more business unit by partnering with their business clients in anticipating strategic opportunities for IT applications and by serving as the client's primary point-of-contact for the corporate IS organization. According to the CFO of one LOB, "the AM is my window into IS. Now I have a clear face and clear interface into IS." Although the AMs have a formal reporting relationship only to IS-LB&IS, their partnerships with clients led them to "feel" a dotted-line reporting relationship with business management. They work without direct staff support.

The AM is responsible for initiating a “statement of work” with the business client, and using this document to monitor and communicate the project status to clients and IS directors on a regular basis (see processes below). The AM is also responsible for getting signoffs for each project phase, working out any client relationship problems encountered during the project, and capturing formal client feedback at the close of a project. Getting timely closure on the initial requirements phase of a project is a critical AM task.

The AMs also contribute biweekly “radar reports” to communicate the current and future needs of their clients to other AMs, CoE managers, and the IS-LB&IS management team. These reports are important for anticipating the human resource skillsets needed for future projects.

Delivery managers (DMs) manage projects for new applications or provide support for existing applications. Their responsibilities include: (1) identify skills needed for delivery engagement management teams based on the statement of work, (2) manage the team of IS resources assigned to the project, and (3) deliver a quality project on-time (within six months) and within budget. The DM's primary focus is upon the delivery of "bite-size" applications, while the AMs are accountable for customer relationships, and the CMs manage the IS human resources.

## Processes

Contemporary firms utilize three key processes for sustaining their strategic focus and performance (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1995): (1) an entrepreneurial process for sustaining creativity and entrepreneurship among front-line managers, (2) an integration process for building and integrating competencies across internal organizational boundaries, and (3) a renewal process for continual revitalization of ideas for driving the business forward. These processes nurture lateral relationships among organizational managers and collaborative problem-solving behaviors vital to the building of an organization's reservoir of knowledge and skills (Galbraith 1993; Lawler 1996; Leonard-Barton 1995). The IS-LB&IS change-ready design includes three new formal processes, one of each type, as described below.

Statement of work. AMs utilize an entrepreneurial process for conceptualizing value-creating applications for their LOBs. The process involves collaborating with business clients in order to identify innovative ways in which IT could facilitate new products and services, reengineered work processes, or reconfigured relationships with external customers and other stakeholders. As managers of customer relationships, AMs are not only heavily involved in the conceptualization of innovative IT applications, but also in defining business requirements, scheduling prototype evaluations, and planning roll-outs. The outcomes of this process are documented in a statement of work (SOW) which becomes a living guide for work by the delivery engagement teams. The SOW is a critical mechanism for linking the activities of the AM and DM roles, because the DMs are responsible for delivering an application according to the SOW developed by the AM.

Resource planning is an integration process for aligning customer relationship management, delivery management, and skills enhancement activities of the AMs, DMs, and CMs respectively. For example, AMs and DMs work interdependently to ensure client satisfaction with the delivery of an application; CMs and DMs work together to ensure the availability and appropriate use of skilled IS staff on projects; and AMs and CMs work together to cultivate skillsets consistent with the emerging stream of application requirements from business clients. According to one IS-LB&IS director, “Communication is the common thread to make it work; to get resources; to operate day-to-day.”

The resource planning process involves biweekly meetings of all of Clark's directors and all of the AMs and CMs; the DMs are represented by their directors. The purpose of the meetings is to monitor progress on current projects, availability of IS staff with appropriate skills, and the staffing needs of future projects based upon three inputs: project status reports (from each DM), "radar reports" on client projects in early planning stages (by each AM), and "resource movement" analyses and projections of future skillset demands (by each CM). The resource planning process is a critical integration mechanism for ensuring coordination of the different integrator roles in the CoE model.

CoE life cycle management. Each CoE has a life cycle—moving from birth (creation), to growth and expansion, and then to retirement (decline) and eventually phase out (disbandment). The pace at which a center travels through its life cycle depends upon demands for the skillset. The goal is an IS workforce always in a “ready state.”

CoE life cycle management is a kind of renewal process: identification of desired skills, creation and growth of a CoE through reskilling, and retirement of CoEs when the skillsets are no longer required for either current or future projects. The director of the Centers of Excellence at IS-LB&IS, Nancy Cavanaugh, is responsible for evaluating business demand for existing skill centers and currently unavailable skillsets;

information gathered from the resource planning process is used to anticipate the need for specific skillsets, including retiring existing skillsets and acquiring new ones.

## People skills: centers of excellence

Technical competencies or skills are the building blocks of desired organizational capabilities (Hamel and Prahalad 1994; Mohrman et al. 1995). The CoE design is a skills-based organizational approach emphasizing IT talent leadership. In the words of Clark,

If we truly believe knowledge currency is key, then it deserves 100% attention in its own right. This is the fundamental argument for advancing skill centers of excellence. Pooling resources by skills enables an organization to focus on advanced training, conceptualize new approaches, and hone future thinking in a business-driven manner.

In the spring of 1994, the LB&IS management team identified 12 IS competencies and vested each one of them within a specific skill center. Most competencies were for technology and methodology skillsets: mainframe software engineering, mainframe operations support, data modeling, data access, client-server software engineering, client-server support, ICASE, testing and quality assurance metrics, documentation training. Three additional competencies were also identified for the three new integrator roles: account management, delivery management, and CoE management. Some of these were existing skillsets (e.g., mainframe software engineering), whereas others were competencies that needed to be developed (e.g., client/server software engineering).

## Reward systems: multi-input performance appraisals

Today, there is a greater emphasis on encouraging individuals to manage their own careers in an environment that is less paternalistic, less entitlement-oriented, and more skill- and performance-oriented (Bowen et al. 1991;

Lawler 1996). Reward systems that attract, develop, and motivate the right individuals for the new organizational capabilities are key to the success of a new design (Gupta et al. 1992; Shuster and Zingheim 1992).

The IS-LB&IS management team felt strongly that an appraisal process that placed high value on customer satisfaction, as well as successful project teaming, was a critical component of the new design. The performance appraisal system implemented only six months into the new CoE design initiative differed from the standard corporate IS process in several ways. First, it included performance ratings from multiple IS bosses: the skill center manager (CM) and all project managers (DMs) during the prior 12-month period. Second, it included a process for capturing business client evaluations for each project on which the IS individual had worked. Third, the ratings from these three sources were weighted, with much higher values placed on client and DM assessments than those of the CoE coach (CM).

Since the corporate human resources department had been considering the merits of a multi-input appraisal process, the IS-LB&IS unit was able to design and implement this new system as a pilot for the organization. Clark's director for the CoE design (Cavanaugh) was given the responsibility for designing and implementing the new system. Like other corporate IS annual evaluations, the multi-input appraisals for the IS-LB&IS unit were intended to be used for annual raises, bonuses, and personnel development planning.

## Congruence among five components

Three criteria of "fit" have been suggested for evaluating the effectiveness of organization designs: (1) strategic fit, or whether the strategy is appropriate to the competitive environment, (2) strategies, and (3) internal organizational fit, or whether the architectural elements are congruent (Nadler and Tushman 1992). The CoE design appears to meet all three criteria. First, the change-readiness functional strategy (rapid development of "bite-sized" applications, strategic focus, and IT talent leadership) is in alignment with the business goals for competitive agility, customer intimacy, and operational excellence. Second, the structures, processes, people skills, and reward system components were designed to enable the change-readiness functional strategy. Third, these components are both internally consistent and mutually reinforcing. For example, skill centers build and nurture competencies required for the new integrator roles (e.g., account management). The resource planning process facilitates coordination across the new team-based groups and integrator roles. A new reward system motivates leveraging of IT skillsets and teamwork to meet changing client needs.

Nevertheless, the CoE design described above was the result of a multi-year transformation process that was not totally pre-planned and not without some unexpected pitfalls. In the next section, an analysis of the key learning elements of the transformation journey is presented.

## Managing the Transformation Journey

The CoE design involved a radical reconfiguration of structures, processes, people skills, and reward systems. Successful implementation was dependent on the buy-in of both business and IS personnel to not only the change-readiness goals, but also to the uncertainties associated with an as-yet-unproven design. Further, the transformation of the IS-LB&IS organization was initiated in anticipation of a new business environment where change-readiness IT capabilities would be imperative—i.e., no crisis was currently at hand. As a result, an additional challenge was to create a sense of felt-need and urgency to mobilize commitment of relevant stakeholders (Nadler and Tushman 1995).

Traditional models of organizational change (e.g., Lewin 1951) suggest that the change process should be planned for and deliberately managed. Based on a vision of the need for and nature of the change, managers should (1) sell the vision to key stakeholders, (2) implement the change, and then (3) institutionalize the new order (Beer et al. 1990; Goodman and Associates 1982; Nutt 1986; Srinivasan and Davis 1987). Changing the attitudes and values of organizational members affected by the change is a key part of this process.

However, recent researchers have argued that an alternative model that takes account of both the anticipated changes and the unanticipated reactions and adjustments is more reflective of good practice (Mintzberg 1987; Orlikowski and Hofman 1997). Anticipated actions are deliberate and preplanned. Unanticipated actions are innovations that arise as the organization better understands the complexity of a given organizational change and its consequences.

The two-year transformation journey embarked on by Clark and his management team reflected this improvisational approach from the start. Clark's intent was to not over-plan the CoE design implementation; only a few months were devoted to establishing the new structure and "selling it." However, Clark had also learned through experiences at another organization that a "shallow end approach" to entering the pool—i.e., an incremental approach—was not an effective implementation tactic: the CoE design was a radical change, and the "fit" among the different components of the design was one of its strengths. The challenges faced, and "lessons learned," categorized according to the three phases suggested by the traditional change models are discussed below. However, Table 4 also depicts these milestones on a timeline that distinguishes between preplanned actions and unanticipated innovations.

## Phase 1: establish the need for change and sell the vision

Radical and anticipatory organizational changes require the commitment of key stakeholders affected by the change (Goodman and

Dean 1982). The key stakeholders for this design change were not only the IS-LB&IS managers and professional staff and IS-LB&IS's business clients, but also the rest of the corporate IS organization.

The buy-in of the corporate IS organization was achieved in two ways. First, organization design experimentation was encouraged by Szygenda; this provided IS-LB&IS with the autonomy to innovate. Second, Clark's knowledge about CoE approaches and organizational roles, gained while working at another organization, were recognized as assets during his own hiring process. Soon after his arrival at Bell Atlantic, he presented the CoE design concept to the corporate IS management team. He argued that the IS-LB&IS unit was large enough in personnel and range of responsibilities to be a "microcosm" of an IS organization. Further, under the January 1994 restructuring of the corporate IS organization to align with the overall firm (see Figure 1), Clark had the authority for autonomously managing applications delivery for four LOBs within IS-LB&IS. In March 1994, he received corporate IS approval for moving forward with his vision of the CoE model and implementing it within IS-LB&IS.

## Use a "Jump-In" Approach

With top management support behind him, Clark took a "jump-in" approach: a new IS-LB&IS management team was formed and a CoE director (Cavanaugh) was selected to spearhead the implementation. Many of the new account manager, CoE manager, and delivery manager roles were filled with internal candidates. In July 1994, the new structure was presented to the rest of the IS-LB&IS staff, IS professionals were assigned to skill centers, and the IS-LB&IS organization anticipated working through the remaining issues as they arose. The IS-LB&IS management team acted as an oversight group, intervening whenever barriers impeded the pace or success of the transformation process. The "jump-in" approach helped create a sense of urgency and an environment of excitement and energy that was instrumental in mobilizing commitment to the CoE design.

## Don't Waiver From the Vision

Traditionally, project managers and IS development staff at Bell Atlantic worked for a business unit for long periods, so that their subject matter expertise and working relationships were highly valued. This was a “comfort factor” for the clients; and some business managers had come to expect “a lot of handholding.” The IS-LB&IS business clients were considerably skeptical about the change. Since all IS staff below the level of director were reassigned to specific CoEs, clients were anxious about losing “their” IS people. A common business response was: “Why are you taking my person away?”

The six-month time-box for applications delivery also turned out to be a major mindset change for both IS-LB&IS clients and developers. According to Clark,

both users and developers may initially regard the six-month time-box as being insensitive to need, and even Draconian in practice. But its ultimate payoff is enormous: in the PC world, you build systems for obsolescence—not ongoing maintenance.

Special efforts were needed to educate the IS staff and clients about the role of six-month delivery goals in facilitating responsiveness to the changing business climate and the need to modularize the requirements for larger projects to meet this time-box. Overall, despite some initial challenges, Clark and his management team persisted with their vision and continued to sell the transformation as a way to bring in business value by positioning IS as an enabler of change. Some “early wins” with bite-sized applications that delivered strategic value helped them overcome early concerns (see Table 5 for an illustration of some of these applications). A determination to not waiver from this overall vision during the initial period was a critical element of the transformation process.

## Phase 2: implementing the change

## Create and Nurture a Team-Based Collaborative Environment

Planned change theories also suggest that teamwork is critical for successful organizational transformations (Beer et al. 1990; Nutt 1986). Implementing an organizational design change that has no precedent requires a tolerance for flexibility and the relinquishing of a "command-and-control" management style for a "cultivation" style (Orlikowski and Hofman 1997). Recent organization design writings emphasize the concept of teamed management, whereby a group of senior managers collaboratively improvise organizational transformation processes (Lawler 1996; Mohrman et al. 1995).

A teamed management concept was not the traditional management style within the corporate IS organization at Bell Atlantic. Clark's belief in the value of teamed management and his demonstrated commitment to this concept were keys to making it work. The IS-LB&IS management team (Clark and his directors) was responsible for driving the implementation of the CoE design. While individual directors assumed responsibility for specific elements of the implementation, monthly meetings of the management team helped ensure that attention was given to the interdependencies among the different elements of the CoE design.

## Evolve Formal Coordination Mechanisms

The initial CoE design implemented relied upon monthly meetings of the IS-LB&IS management team alone to ensure alignment and integration among the AM, DM, and CM roles. However, a few months into the implementation, misalignment problems began to surface. For example, IS staff were not being freed up fast enough for other projects; a lot of effort (e-mails, phone calls, etc.) was being spent tracking the status of particular IS staff members; and the CMs were experiencing difficulties in anticipating the demand for current and future skills. The monthly meetings of the IS-LB&IS management team were not frequent enough to adequately address these misalignment problems. Further, resource planning was only one item on the monthly agenda of these meetings and did not receive the required level of attention. Overall, there was a realization that monthly IS-LB&IS management team meetings were not sufficient; the new design required additional mechanisms. As a result, a separate formal resource planning process was established. Initially, the directors attended biweekly meetings in order to focus on current and future skillset needs and people training. Formal vehicles for input to the process were developed (see the process description above). Subsequently, the AMs and CMs also began to participate in the process. This change also freed up time at the monthly IS-LB&IS management team meetings for other issues.

## Change Use of Outside Contractors

In the past, the Bell Atlantic IS organization had regularly contracted outside resources for their expertise with new technologies and used these resources to do project work—sometimes for extended time periods. But under the new CoE design, this practice meant lost opportunities for IS-LB&IS employees to develop new skillsets and to possibly move from a CoE devoted to mainframe support into a new technology or methodology CoE. Continuing this old practice therefore contributed to morale problems in a CoE environment emphasizing the reskilling of internal IS staff. Therefore, the IS-LB&IS management team began to revise their usage of outside contractors: consultants were hired to transfer best practices involving new technologies under short-term contracts, rather than to do long-term project work. Beginning in January 1995, reliance on outside contractors began to be intentionally reduced, and contractor engagements were used to accelerate internal diffusion of knowledge and growth of skillsets associated with change-readiness.

## Manage Employee Anxieties

About half of the IS-LB&IS workforce was trained in mainframe application skills. Under the new CoE design, some IS professionals who had been “heroes” under the old environment faced the prospect of their skills becoming obsolete in a client/server world. About 20% of the legacy staff wanted to remain working on legacy systems until these jobs disappeared. Interviews with some technical specialists yielded comments like these:

What you know suddenly doesn't count. . . . Your self-esteem is tied to your position, and you're told you're not important any more.

For some legacy folks, it's not too positive to learn from the scratch again. They can be upwardly mobile in an old area, but they become the low guy on the ladder to the new area.

Not everyone is comfortable in the CoE environment; some people are being pulled out of their comfort zone.

For others with legacy skills, the opposite problem began to surface about six months into the implementation: they were not being freed up for new training or other assignments as quickly as they wanted since their delivery managers were afraid that the old systems would "fall apart" if individuals with specialized subject matter expertise were moved to other assignments. As a result, a number of disgruntled employees were waiting their turn for training, while others who had already been trained in new technologies, such as PowerBuilder, were able to keep moving on to new assignments. Since the new development projects were concentrated in one geographic area, some remote staff also felt they were being overlooked.

In response to these problems, transition plans for specific individuals began to be developed as part of the transition planning for legacy applications beginning in the fall of 1995: these plans were developed by the CMs responsible for employees in the legacy skill CoEs, were reviewed with DMs of the legacy applications, and then communicated to the affected individuals. As IS staff began to be transitioned out of their legacy roles without the initial fears of their delivery managers being realized, the concept of customized transition planning for individuals took hold, and these morale problems subsided.

Toward the end of 1995, a less formal mechanism was also implemented to help control rumors and allay fears: brown-bag sessions with the CoE director at the two sites geographically removed from headquarters. The CoE director provided information “straight from the horse’s mouth,” heard individual concerns, offered personal advice/counseling in one-on-one discussions, and relayed relevant concerns to the respective CMs for follow-up.

Another continuing challenge has been the "bench-time" anxieties of the technical staff: some individuals waiting to be assigned to a new project felt that they were vulnerable targets for downsizing. While efforts were made to reduce bench-time through better coordination in the resource planning process, these perceptions of vulnerability remain an unintended impact of the CoE design in a hyper-competitive industry context.

Finally, the IS-LB&IS employees were not uniformly receptive to the transformation from an "entitlement" to an entrepreneurial mindset in which employees take more responsibility for their career development. According to Cavanaugh, "Some employees still expected a sports agent rather than a coach"—they expected a supervisor to create opportunities for them. A variety of human resources programs (newsletters, training, etc.) were developed in partnership with the human resources department to help the CMs in moving more reluctant IS-LB&IS employees toward an entrepreneurial set of values.

Overall, the IS-LB&IS management team learned that not all employees embrace change enthusiastically. According to Clark:

Converts must convert themselves, and some may never move. Not everyone is excited; you won't get everyone to change with you.... Also, people are at different points in their own life-cycles. You need to accommodate this, or some good people will bail out.

## Implement and Fine-Tune New Appraisal Systems

As described earlier, a new appraisal process based on assessments by multiple IS managers as well as business clients was considered an important component of the new CoE design. The concept of client evaluations of a project team was new at Bell Atlantic, as were the heavy weightings on non-IS input. Initial weightings were 40% client(s), 40% DM(s), and 20% CM. These were revised to 50% client(s), 40% DM(s), and 10% CM input in mid-1995 in order to promote customer-orientation among the IS staff.

However, problems began to surface. In particular, IS staff who received less-than-satisfactory team ratings from clients expressed tremendous anxiety relative to their job security and promotion prospects within not just IS-LB&IS, but also the IS organization as a whole. Rather than motivating all team members to be more customer-oriented, the unintended result for some IS staff was a perception of having little personal control over their own performance ratings. Two aspects of the process were the primary causes: (1) the business client ratings were at the project team level, not the individual employee level, and (2) since the client ratings were collected at year-end, there were concerns about unfair client biases due to recent events that might be unrelated to the project team being rated.

Several changes were therefore made to the new appraisal system. First, business client evaluations were collected at the end of each project, rather than via a semi-annual process; electronic data collection methods for client input were also eventually introduced. Second, effective January 1996, the IS-LB&IS management team made a major adjustment to the use of the client evaluations: client ratings began to be used only for determining annual bonuses. These changes were necessitated because of an alignment problem with the rewards system for the rest of the corporate IS organization. That is, performance ratings at Bell Atlantic were traditionally used not only to determine annual raises, but also to establish the “organizational worth” of an individual—including the identification of prospects for promotions as well as candidates for career counseling or layoffs. Given the increasingly turbulent business environment and the organization’s move away from an “entitlement” workforce, a reward system that differed from the rest of the IS organization—even though it was an official “pilot”—became too threatening for many of the IS-LB&IS personnel and forced a retrenchment on this issue.

An additional problem also occurred at the director level. Like most other IS organizations, Bell Atlantic had a well-established process for assessing and rewarding systems delivery activities. However, equally visible and salient performance indicators for the new coaching (CM) and account manager (AM) roles did not exist. As a result, directors in these new positions with no delivery responsibilities did not fare as well as those that did have systems delivery responsibilities in the corporate-level appraisal process at the end of the first year. This led to a second unexpected modification to the original design: some amount of systems delivery responsibilities were assigned to all directors to avoid penalization in the year-end corporate appraisal process for 1995. Since role differentiation is one of the fundamental elements of the CoE design, this modification blurred the differentiation of responsibilities. However, until an enterprise-level appreciation of comparable criteria for evaluating account management and resource management activities is established, this action was viewed as a necessary accommodation.

## Communicate Progress and Demonstrate Value With Performance Measures

Radical transformations take time; they are not overnight changes. Yet as the transformation unfolds, both internal and external stakeholders need "proof that it's working" (Beer et al. 1990). The IS-LB&IS management team learned the importance of instituting measures that captured progress toward the CoE design objectives. As reported in Table 1, several metrics were developed to demonstrate progress toward achieving the corporate IS goals, including: (1) customer satisfaction ratings, (2) on-time project delivery, and (3) IT talent base (number of contractors). However, some of these metrics were set up as part of the transformation process, so the data did not exist to provide a full before-and-after comparison. As a result, the IS-LB&IS managers were handicapped in their ability to demonstrate initial successes under the CoE design to external stakeholders. In particular, difficulties in measuring the value of the account manager role hampered efforts to educate business clients about allowing DMs and development staff to focus on delivery activities without significant distractions. The clients' perceptions and willingness to accept the role of the AMs as a point of contact for all of their needs continue to pose challenges to the transformation effort. On the other hand, the elusiveness of measures to adequately capture the value of the account manager role has also been reported by others (Iacono et al. 1995).

The IS-LB&IS management team also learned the importance of sharing internal statistics at their own meetings. As one IS director noted:

We started reporting [staff moves] at our meetings, and began to see probably 40 to 50% more personnel movement than we've ever seen before. It also provides an answer [to those] who say "this isn't working."

## Phase 3: institutionalize the change

By late 1995, the overall consensus was that the CoE implementation had enabled IS-LB&IS to achieve the corporate change-ready IS goals. Ralph Szygenda provided this assessment:

[Systems development] teams need to be focused on getting the job done, with high quality, and within the cost allowed. And you need someone to analyze the individuals and get them the right training and career path. Evolving people is the hardest part. . . . Charlie Clark has done that. IT projects are now on time, on budget, with a capable empowered work force.

By the time of the second annual performance appraisal process in late 1995, the CoE design had been institutionalized—from the perspective of both the IS-LB&IS unit and its business clients—and client support for the change-ready capabilities of the IS staff was reflected in the year-end ratings. However, in January 1996, the vice president of IS-LB&IS thought that it was important to reassess the CoE design pilot, given some of the challenges related to: (1) employee anxiety, (2) valuing account management and coaching activities on par with delivery activities, and (3) establishing the account manager as the point-of-contact for all applications project concerns. The result was a resounding endorsement of the CoE design by the IS-LB&IS management team, and an agreement to continue with its implementation.

## Implications for the IS Community

Change-readiness IT capabilities are expected to be an important issue on the agenda of CIOs today and in the future. IS organizations will be called upon to deliver high impact business applications that facilitate strategic differentiation of their firm relative to its rivals in intensely competitive, fast-paced environments. At the same time, the effectiveness of the IS organization will be measured by its ability to quickly acquire, renew, and deploy superior IS knowledge and skills in their ongoing development activities (Hofman and Rockart 1992; Merlyn and Parkinson 1994; Parker 1996). What insights does the IS-LB&IS experience with the CoE design offer to the IS community? It is the conclusion of this paper, based on the two-year organization design experiment, that the CoE design described here is indeed a model worthy of consideration by organizations seeking to build and maintain change-readiness IT capabilities. In support of this conclusion, the CoE design is evaluated below in comparison to other IS organization design models.

The CoE model is a project-based design (Lawler 1996) with roots in practice in consulting firms (e.g., McKinsey and Company, as described by Kiely 1997). First, the CoE model separates project delivery work from customer relationship management on the one hand, and skills or people development on the other. Second, the CoE design views skillsets of IS personnel as assets to be managed, with particular attention to knowledge currency and renewal (Conner and Prahalad 1996). It creates and maintains skill centers in response to customer demand and effectively leverages new skillsets across a portfolio of projects through rapid team formation and disassembly and a progressive retasking of IS specialists. Similar to the consultancy model, CoE managers serve as mentors who match project assignment opportunities with the training and development needs of their mentees.

Third, the CoE design highlights some of the key processes associated with project-based organizations. Most of the prior IS literature has focused on processes to align the IS function with the business, including strategic planning, service management, and chargeback systems (Zmud 1988). However, when skill centers, transient project teams, and account management are the fundamental concepts of IS organization design, new processes based on a consultancy model need to be developed.

The analysis in this paper focused on three processes: one anticipated but not yet fully developed (CoE life cycle management) and two formal processes not fully anticipated (statement of work, resource planning). As discussed earlier, the framework of entrepreneurial, integration, and renewal processes (Ghoshal and Bartlett 1995) appears to be useful for identifying the differing thrusts of these new processes. More problematic at Bell Atlantic was the attempt to redesign a corporate-wide performance appraisal process that would nurture the values of the CoE design (teamwork, client responsiveness, skill development), yet not be perceived as misaligned with the corporate IS organization as a whole.

How does this project-based design compare with earlier models of IS organization design, specifically the SBU-team and life-cycle design models? The life-cycle design sets up a structural organization based on skills utilized at different stages of the development life cycle: systems analysis, technical design, programming, and maintenance (Boar 1996; Zmud 1984). This approach also requires good project management skills and a standard methodology to ensure integration across the life cycle steps. The primary advantage here is that deep IT skills can be leveraged across a portfolio of systems development projects with a relatively small pool of skilled IS personnel. A major weakness is that IS staff have limited opportunities for developing deep business expertise.

In contrast, the SBU team approach sets up a structural organization in which IS personnel are assigned to specific business units, functional areas, or processes of the overall firm in order to develop a deep expertise in a particular business area and close working partnerships with their business clients. While facilitating a greater business responsiveness, the drawback of the SBU team model is that it inhibits a firm's ability to leverage IS skills and expertise across the enterprise. When IS skills are expensive, in short supply, and characterized by a high rate of obsolescence, the business-centric approach of the SBU team design creates deficiencies in IT talent leadership as well as the enterprise-wide leveraging of expertise and solutions.

The CoE design described here combines some of the advantages of both the life-cycle and the SBU team approaches (see Table 7). Similar to the SBU team, the CoE design addresses the need for IS staff to develop a deep business understanding as well as strong client relationships. However, in the CoE design, this role is played by an account manager. An underlying assumption of the CoE design, then, is that client perceptions of business responsiveness do not require intensive client access to and relationships with temporary project delivery team members. Since the IS-LB&IS unit previously operated under an SBU team model, in which systems project team members were typically assigned to a business unit for long time periods, the change to a CoE design appeared to some client groups to be a step backwards. In reality, the design established a single-point-of-contact for the client that is totally accountable for the customer relationship and enabled the project managers and IS development staff to focus on quality project deliverables.

Similar to the life-cycle design, the CoE design also pools IS staff. However, the pools here were primarily focused not on life cycle steps, but on new technologies to support rapid prototyping and new management methods. A life-cycle design approach may be more suitable for an IT skills-centric approach under a more stable environment. However, when IT skills are at a high rate of obsolescence and there is an organizational emphasis on rapid-prototyping tools, the CoE design should prove superior in facilitating IT talent leadership within the context of continual reskilling, in which today's new skillsets may become tomorrow's legacy skills. Similar to a consultancy model, the specific CoEs implemented by IS-LB&IS also foster strong project management skills and standard methodologies within and across skillsets.

Further, the CoE design model also avoids a disadvantage of typical implementations of the two more traditional IS organization designs: the bundling of responsibilities for project management and people management into a single managerial role. This leads to inadequate attention to the internal development of IT talent, as short-term delivery pressures typically take precedence over long-term people development. Similar to a consultancy, the project-based CoE design sets up a separate structure for mentoring and places responsibility for resource assignments in the hands of the mentors.

This case study suggests that a CoE design based on a project-based model prevalent in the consulting industry has considerable utility for IS organizations in large firms outside the consulting industry—especially in firms facing major industry changes with a long-tenured IS workforce relatively unskilled in newer technologies. However, in assessing the generalizability of this case study, additional factors that may have contributed to its successful implementation at Bell Atlantic need to be recognized. First, the CoE design was introduced by a new corporate IS management team and was spearheaded by a newly arrived IS executive who had prior experience with redesign initiatives. Nevertheless, special efforts to gain client buy-in were required, suggesting that the account manager role inherent to a consultancy/client relationship may be a "harder sell" when the consultancy and the client are part of the same organizational "family." Second, the CoE "fit" with its organizational context in several important ways. The teamed management approach fit Clark's collaborative management style, as well as the Bell Atlantic values (e.g., teamwork) established by the CEO several years earlier; the strategic focus of the new account manager role fit the new corporate IT role; the four LOBs client units had just recently been formed, which set up a management environment conducive to mutual IS/business adjustment; and the newly decentralized structure for corporate human resources placed the first-line facilitators for new CoE job descriptions and reward systems under the corporate IS umbrella. The timing of the CoE design implementation was also a potentially important factor: the transformation was couched as a radical redesign in anticipation of still uncharted, major industry changes. This meant that the organization had some slack resources as it waited for the deregulation landscape to unfold.

Other limitations to the potential relevance and applicability of this CoE design are acknowledged. First and foremost, the design described here was only implemented within an IS unit with applications development responsibilities; it was not rolled out to the entire Bell Atlantic IS organization. On the other hand, the metrics for the success of this transformation are all widely sought IS capabilities: rapid delivery of value-added business applications, superior customer satisfaction, and the ability to quickly assimilate IT skillsets (Hofman and Rockart 1992; Merlyn and Parkinson 1994; Parker 1996). The key challenge to implementing the CoE design within only a single systems development unit was the institutionalization of a new performance appraisal process that would reward behaviors associated with the change-readiness functional strategy, but not cause inequities for members of the IS-LB&IS unit.

Other factors that may limit the applicability of the CoE design model include the fact that the IS-LB&IS unit was of a moderate size (approximately 250 IS employees), and the rapid application capability for this organization was conceived in terms of a six-month cycle time. Project teams thus had a limited life, and IS specialists with highly valued skills typically would be available for multiple assignments within a given year. For IS organizations of a different size, and with large mission-critical projects that may include the reengineering of global work processes, this specific model may not be optimal. On the other hand, a six-month modular release schedule might yield some of the same benefits (Koch 1996).

This case study also provides valuable insights into the process for managing the transformation to a CoE design. In addition to the "lessons learned" (Table 5), a key insight is that radical organizational changes must be managed collectively in a teamed environment and not in a hierarchical, command-and-control mode. Radical changes cannot be totally pre-planned; after establishing the initial mission and mandate, management must place appropriate individuals in key positions and empower them to collaborate and innovate in managing the transformation process. The analysis of the transformation process provides support for the assertion by Orlikowski and Hofman that contemporary organizational transformations must be viewed from “improvisational” models.

Finally, the case analysis illustrates that managing employee and client anxieties are transformational challenges that should not be underestimated. While some improvisational tactics devised to manage employee anxieties have been discussed, further research is required on this topic. For example, the reduced use of contractors helped the IS-LB&IS unit improve morale. However, for other IS organization contexts, the variable (vs. fixed) costs associated with contract personnel may be a more important tradeoff.

Further, if the organization is moving from an SBU team model to a CoE design, its business clients may regard the change as a "pendulum swing" equivalent to a kind of "recentralization": IS project team members who were designated to develop and support applications specific to a business unit are now consolidated in skill centers and are assigned to work for multiple client groups. Despite there being no changes to the centralized IT governance model at Bell Atlantic, the change to a CoE design model led to greater client concerns than had been anticipated. This suggests that even large IS units under a federal governance model could face similar client concerns if they attempt to consolidate resources that were aligned with different business departments, although concerns about a recentralization to a corporate function would at least be avoided under a federal model. Exploring the advantages and disadvantages of a CoE design under a federal governance structure appears to be another fertile area for future organization design experimentation and research.

## Conclusion

The Centers of Excellence design at Bell Atlantic's IS-LB&IS unit appears to be a successful model for developing change-ready IT capabilities for systems development; it can be a means for transforming an IS organization from an operational backwater, focused solely on legacy systems, to a consultancy concerned with enabling IT-based strategic differentiation of the business. The IS-LB&IS experience demonstrates the feasibility and impacts of separating project delivery work from both customer relationship management and IS people development. It also demonstrates a design in which requisite skillsets can be leveraged across a portfolio of application projects through rapid team formation and disassembly, and assignments that stretch the capabilities of IS specialists. And finally, it demonstrates the usage of "virtual" skill centers to help build, maintain, and renew IS talent with technical and soft skillsets to meet current demands and anticipate future needs. The CoE design therefore appears to yield the key advantages of both of the other IS organization design models discussed here: deep business knowledge and strong customer relationships under the SBU team model and the ability to leverage IT expertise under the life-cycle model.

Bell Atlantic's implementation experiences have also yielded some valuable insights for IS practitioners and researchers. The two-year transformation journey was not without pitfalls; some of the anticipated and unanticipated management actions in the form of "lessons learned" have been highlighted. We hope that this case study will stimulate interest in alternative organization designs to meet the needs of the IS function in tomorrow's organizations, as well as enable other IS managers to more effectively navigate their own IS transformation journeys.

## Appendix

## Methodology

This case study is based on first-hand experiences of two senior Bell Atlantic managers leading the IS organizational transformation, a multi-phase data collection over a period of 14 months by two academics, and collaborative analyses of this case study by all four authors. More specifically, this research collaboration began in April 1995, when the first author, the vice president and CIO of Information Systems for the Large Business & Information Services unit (IS-LB&IS), participated in a telephone interview with one of the academic authors for a research study on IT coordination mechanisms in large, divisionalized firms. The second author, the director of Centers of Excellence within IS-LB&IS, subsequently made arrangements for both academic authors to conduct on-site interviews at three different Bell Atlantic sites between October 1995 and June 1996. A total of 27 Bell Atlantic employees were interviewed, including the CIO for the corporate IS organization, five direct reports to the corporate CIO, five directors reporting to the IS-LB&IS VP/CIO, other members of the IS-LB&IS unit, plus three business managers in the client organizations supported by IS-LB&IS. Numerous archival documents including annual reports, strategy documents of the IS organization, excerpts from key speeches given by the CEO and the CIO, statements of work descriptions for systems projects, job position descriptions, performance appraisal forms, and client feedback on completed projects were also available to the authors over this multi-year collaboration.
