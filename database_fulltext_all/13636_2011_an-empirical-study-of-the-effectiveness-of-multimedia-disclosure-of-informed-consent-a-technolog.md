Informed consent is a term used to describe communications between physicians and their patients; it gives rise to important ethical and legal implications. We explored how multimedia-mediated disclosure of informed consent prior to surgery influenced the learning process and learning outcomes of patients. A total of 107 samples were obtained from patients with degenerative knee arthritis. Our research indicated that multimedia-mediated disclosure outperformed other more traditional method in that it enhanced learning, interest, remembering, comprehension, and satisfaction. Also learning motivation and interest were positively and strongly correlated with perceived comprehension and satisfaction. Furthermore, learning interest was more influential in improving the process than was learning motivation.

## 1. Introduction

Informed consent involves a medical doctors’ action of ‘‘informing’’ a patient and obtaining ‘‘consent’’ from him or her prior to any medical treatment; it allows the patient to make medical decisions based on the material disclosed and knowledge of the results of prior treatments. The medical service industry has recently undergone drastic change due to its use of automated IS, resulting in gradual dissolution of the conventional physician– patient relationship. Furthermore, obtaining consensus and achieving mutual confidence about a medical treatment via physician–patient communication depends on transparent medical information and decision-making processes.

Because of the realization of the fact that human experiments could contravene the normally accepted notions of patient’s rights, the doctrine of informed consent was developed and has become an important part of the doctor–patient doctrine. Thus obtaining informed consent from patients is now a routine task for medical staff with ethical and legal implications.

Based on our experience as outpatients, we had decided that physicians were generally too busy to respond effectively to patient enquiries. For both physicians and patients, expressing and understanding risk in a treatment is a problem, particularly for patients undergoing surgery. Many patients who signed surgical consent forms did not understand their purpose and only about half were able to identify a primary risk associated with their operation. Such a lack of transparency in physician–patient communication can significantly threaten patient safety [18]. Lack of understanding may be due to the use of poor communication techniques, lack of time in consultation, patient anxiety or denial or a lack of reading comprehension. Consequently, improving communication and enhancing patient knowledge and comprehension of healthcare services is a major medical need.

Technology-mediated learning (TML) can enhance learner cognitive level [14], [20]. Using IT that supports disclosure of preoperative informed consent information may be effective. Furthermore, multimedia aids have been found to improve the informed consent process in clinical practice in America, the United Kingdom [15], and Switzerland [11]. Further, Flory and Emanuel [7], reviewed informed consent literature on MEDLINE published between 1966 and March 2004; they identified 12 trials with multimedia intervention among 30 research papers describing 42 trials. However, a lack of improvement in accuracy in the research was noted; primarily due to differences in population, education of respondents, disease status, and mental conditions. Furthermore, they suggested that more research should be initiated to confirm that multimedia presentation of consent information improved patient understanding. Alavi and Leidner [2] and Santhanam et al. [19] suggested that future work should examine the interaction among IT features, instructional strategies, and psychological learning processes in IS learning and training. Although multimedia informed consent has received considerable attention, few studies have utilized empirical research methods and most of them used very small samples.

We used field experiments involving large samples for an indepth examination of whether multimedia disclosure of preoperative informed consent information affected the psychological learning process and learning outcomes. We used the TML to explore and compare the effectiveness of conventional and multimedia disclosure of preoperative informed consent. Thus, we had the following aims: (1) to investigate differences in remembering, comprehension, and satisfaction between conventional and multimedia disclosure; (2) to explore how different disclosure methods influenced psychological learning processes; and (3) to examine how psychological learning processes influenced learning effectiveness.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Informed consent

Informed consent acknowledges the right of patients to participate voluntarily in healthcare [5]. It is typically assumed to require three basic elements during disclosure, physicians must provide:

(1) Relevant and comprehensible information to patients on all treatment aspects, including the risks, benefits, limitations, and alternatives [10].

(2) Sufficient capacity of information to patients so that they can understand and foresee what will occur.

(3) An environment which ensures that the Patient’s decision is voluntary and is uninfluenced by extraneous factors, threats, or controls.

Disclosure helps patients understand healthcare decisions and, thus, helps maintain a trust relationship between them and their physician. Disclosed content should cover treatments, expected effects, alternatives, benefits, limitations, risks, and consequences of rejection or delay. Extended discussion between physicians and patients may be required before patient consent is given.

Physicians are obliged to acquire patient consent before treatment. However, consent acquisition is not a single or independent event but a process. Consent can be expressed orally or in writing. However, it is effective only when patients express their willingness to take part in specific medical trials or undergo treatments, and cannot be simply based on their action.

## 2.2. Cognitive theory of multimedia learning

Multimedia integrates video, audio, and animation to provide users with rich information. It can be adapted to different learning styles. Furthermore, different communication channels can be provided to achieve maximum learning effectiveness.

Mayer [16] showed how the cognitive process varied according to message type (see Fig. 1). Graphic processing is the process by which visual information is received by the eye. It is then selected and enters the image area of working memory and is organized into a graphic model which is then integrated with prior knowledge. Audio processing is the process by which audio information is received by the ear, and is selected for entry into the aural area of working memory; the information is organized into the appropriate language model and integrated with prior knowledge. Word processing is the process by which textual information is received by the eye; this is selected and entered into the image area of working memory and organized into the appropriate language model, and finally integrated with prior knowledge.

Theories of multimedia learning agree that working memory contains two information-processing units and that different multimedia information presentations can enhance learning effectiveness. However, working memory has a limited capacity. If the design of multimedia learning systems helps learners to use working memory more effectively in selecting, organizing, and integrating information, better learning will be attained.

## 2.3. Technology mediated learning

The conceptual framework of TML developed by Alavi and Leidner pointed out that instructional strategy and IT affected the psychological processes of learners and thus influence2 learning outcomes (see Fig. 2). Notably, they described learning as a psychological state and, thus, the learning processes should be considered when evaluating learning outcomes. An instructional strategy is a method and model for presenting, organizing, and synthesizing instructional materials. Teaching approaches consist of methods for organizing material, while teaching methods are required to present it. Psychological learning processes are associated with the learners’ ability to process information, their motivation, and their interest.

TML assumes that learning outcomes are determined by interactions among IT, instructional strategy, and the psychological learning processes in specific contexts. When assessing learning outcomes, it is necessary to consider the effects of various combinations of instructional strategy and IT on learner psychological processes, including the learners’ ability to process information cognitively, their motivation, and their interest.

Both Santhanam et al. and Alavi and Leidner saw the psychological learning process as a mediator variable that is important. For instance, multimedia has been used to make learning playful, and hyperlinks have been used as multimedia tools to enable learners to control learning progress [17] and increase learning effectiveness.

## 3.2. Research hypotheses

Although previous studies addressed multimedia-mediated disclosure of informed consent associated with learning outcomes, results were controversial; however they demonstrated that different instructional strategies influenced the psychological learning processes of learners. Learner interest is the degree to which a learner is interested in content. Alonso-Tapia and Pardo [3] indicated that the instruction style significantly affected learning motivation. Thus, we hypothesized.

H1. Multimedia disclosure method for informed consent leads to higher learning motivation and learning interest.

H1a. Multimedia disclosure method for informed consent leads to higher learning motivation than the conventional method.

H1b. Multimedia disclosure method for informed consent leads to higher learning interest than the conventional method.

Multimedia-mediated instruction can increase teaching efficiency and quality (since such methods) can facilitate learner memorization and retain learner attention. Also, different instructional multimedia influenced learning outcomes differently.

With a focus on learning outcomes related to preoperative informed consent, we addressed both the cognitive and affective dimensions. In the cognitive dimension, information process theory emphasizes cognitive learning processes and, learning is considered to be a process of receiving, processing, remembering, and extracting information that is information initially stored in short-term memory. Individuals also establish associations between a stimulus and prior knowledge and store such associations in long-term memory. Therefore, we used remembering and comprehension as indicators. Mahoney et al. determined that multimedia technology effectively improves memory, particularly when teaching adults.

Additionally, in the affective dimensions, satisfaction (the degree to which learners feel pleased with the learning process and their expectations are satisfied) was considered to be an important motivator of learning used to verify whether demand was satisfied. Thus, we hypothesized:

H2. Multimedia disclosure method for informed consent leads to better remembering, comprehension, and satisfaction than the conventional method.

H2a. Multimedia disclosure method for informed consent leads to better remembering than the conventional method.

H2b. Multimedia disclosure method for informed consent leads to better comprehension than the conventional method.

H2c. Multimedia disclosure method for informed consent leads to higher satisfaction than the conventional method.

Psychological learning processes have been found to influence learning outcomes and many studies have demonstrated empirically that learning motivation influences learning outcomes. High learning interest leads to superior learning outcomes. Thus, we hypothesized.

H3. Learning motivation influences remembering, comprehension, and satisfaction.

H3a. Differences in learning motivation lead to different degrees of remembering.

H3b. Differences in learning motivation lead to different degrees of comprehension.

H3c. Differences in learning motivation lead to different degrees of satisfaction.

H4. Learning interest influences remembering, comprehension, and satisfaction.

H4a. Differences in learning interest lead to different degrees of remembering.

H4b. Differences in learning interest lead to different degrees of comprehension.

H4c. Differences in learning interest lead to different degrees of satisfaction.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Experimental design

We used a block sampling technique. The joint center at a regional teaching hospital in southern Taiwan was selected as the research site. The hospital has 948 beds and 38 outpatient divisions, with an average daily outpatient volume of about 3,000 patients. The joint center in the hospital integrates the orthopedic and rehabilitation divisions, general internal medicine, and geriatrics groups to provide patients with comprehensive medical treatment. They mainly live in central and southern Taiwan. Based on national health insurance statistics, approximately 7% of patients with osteoarthritis of the knee have undergone arthroscopic knee surgery at the hospital.

The research purposes and content of our research were presented to the hospital’s department of educational research and development and discussed with the hospital’s educational vicesuperintendent in order to obtain experimental consent forms from the hospital. The field experiment was conducted between March 2007 and May 2007. The multimedia method presented healthcare knowledge about arthroscopic surgery in degenerative arthritis of the knee using animation, video, audio, text, and pictures. The multimedia presentation was designed to aid physicians and nurse practitioners in the disclosure process rather than to replace them. The conventional method had used oral disclosure of informed consent information and forms handed out by the physicians or nurse practitioners.

## 4.2. Measurements

We used the measure developed by Yi and Davis [21] for assessing motivation; it used a five-point Likert’s scale ranging from 1 for strongly disagrees to 5 for strongly agrees. We also used the learning interest scale (the degree of subject willingness to participate in the preoperative informed consent process) developed by Alavi [1]; which also was scored on a five-point Likert’s scale.

The dependent variables we used were remembering (the degree to which subjects remembered preoperative informed consent information), comprehension (actual {a subject’s understanding of the preoperative and postoperative healthcare activities} and perceived {subject-perceived comprehension of informed consent information}) both measured by using the scale developed by Alavi, with a five-point Likert’s scale, and satisfaction [4,12] (a feeling or attitude generated when learners enjoy a certain activity or when their expectations or demands are satisfied during a learning process), it was evaluated via a questionnaire, which utilized a fivepoint Likert’s scale ranging from 1 for ‘‘extremely dissatisfied’’ to 5 for ‘‘extremely satisfied.’’ The scale was a modified version of that developed by Green and Taber [9] and Piccoli.

Learning was considered an internal process rather than an outcome. The content of the multimedia disclosure method for informed consent included ten topics dealing with it for patients receiving arthroscopy for knee arthritis. Its disclosure content was provided by a physician. The questionnaire is included as Appendix A (Table 1).

## 4.3. Participants

Subjects were patients and family representatives who, when registering at the hospital, were asked to participate in this experiment. There were 112 participants who were assigned to either an experimental or control group via block sampling. Only individuals who signed the surgical consent form were enrolled. Moreover, to avoid selection bias, case selection and exclusion criteria were predefined. First, subjects had to be over 18 years old, free of any visual or hearing impairment, and with unimpaired consciousness. Second, subjects had to be literate and able to communicate in either Mandarin or Taiwanese. Third, subjects had to be free of cognitive impairments, such as mental retardation or dementia. Fourth, subjects must not have undergone the same treatment previously. Fifth, participation had to be voluntary, subjects completed the questionnaire after research objectives and processes had been explained.

## 4.4. Procedure

After signing the experimental consent form assistance was provided to help patients obtain adequate rest and to avoid interference from other external factors.

Subjects were then assigned to one of the two groups (control versus experimental), and subjected to the following procedures:

(1) A 10-min pretest, in which we collected demographic data and information on learning motivation, and learning interest. Knowledge of degenerative knee arthritis was also tested.

(2) A 40-min disclosure in which the experimental group received multimedia consent disclosure information, while the control group received disclosure information from the same physicians or nurse practitioners.

(3) A 10-min post-test were administered; it assessed learning motivation, learning interest, remembering, comprehension, and satisfaction.

The experiment ended when subjects had completed this questionnaire. Subjects were given a small gift for participating.

## 4.5. Experiment manipulation

For the subject distribution, as we were unable to conduct an effective random distribution for the experimental and control groups, because individuals undergoing surgery may not have the same operation; however, the experimental group (n = 54) and control group (n = 53) contained a similar number of subjects. Additionally, block sampling was performed; in this the first block contained 5 patients, with patients 1, 3, and 5 assigned to the experimental group, while patients 2 and 4 were assigned to the control group and in the second block, the first patient was assigned to the control group and the second patient was assigned to the experimental group; this process continued until all subjects were distributed to one of the two groups.

In our sample, females outnumbered males; this is consistent with a World Health Organization report: more women have degenerative knee arthritis. Thus, our use of block distribution excluded errors arising from other factors such as gender, age, learning style, learning motivation, and educational level.

The multimedia method presented healthcare knowledge via animation before arthroscopy. A computer with speakers, an over-head projector, and a projection screen were used to present this information. The conventional method disclosed informed consent information orally with handouts and physician instructions. Specialists used graphic cards, and models to disclose the consent information. Disclosure time for each group was 40 min.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Demographics

In total, 112 questionnaires were collected. Five incomplete questionnaires were removed, thus the final sample consisted of 107 questionnaires. The experimental group had 12 males and 42 females, while the control group had 12 males and 41 females. Female subjects therefore were in the majority (78%). Subjects aged 41–50 and 51–60 were 65% of the study population. Eighteen subjects were aged <40 (approximately 17% of the study population). In terms of educational level, most subjects had completed high (vocational) school (29%), followed by junior high school (26%).

## 5.3. Hypothesis testing

Table 4 lists the mean and standard deviations for research variables obtained using the disclosure method. Before disclosure of informed consent information, the experimental group participants’ learning motivation, interest, remembering, perceived comprehension, and actual comprehension were similar to those of the control group indicating that the two groups shared similar prior knowledge and learning incentives. However, after disclosure of the informed consent information, the experimental group had higher learning motivation and interest, and learned more effectively than the control group.

In a covariance analysis, the effect of a covariate should be excluded to test the significance of independent effects. We used a MANCOVA to determine the relationship between disclosure methods and learning motivation and interest (see Table 5). The disclosure methods (multimedia or conventional) were the independent variables. ## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Effects of disclosure methods on psychological learning processes

The different disclosure methods significantly influenced learning motivation and learning interest. On the other hand, prior to this experiment, the two groups had similar learning motivation and learning interest. However, after participating in the experiment, the experiment, that group had higher learning motivation and interest than the control group.

In our experiment, disclosure information was presented using text, pictures, video, audio, and animation (multimedia) in a manner that attracted the attention of patients and family representatives. Furthermore, the animation was designed by professional art or multimedia designers. Thus, it seemed professional, having appealing colors, with actors playing the main roles in physician’s clothing, with their voices dubbed by medical professionals. All design details of disclosure content were determined by skilled instructional, multimedia, and audio/video designers who had analyzed the material content and made suitable presentations. For example, after arthroscopy surgery, the probability of an improved knee was stressed. Notably, the multimedia system presented relevant information via words and statistical plots. Furthermore, content presentation was flexible, comprising video, audio, text, plots, and animation as appropriate. Multimedia provided material that made learning fun, interesting, and attractive, thereby enhancing learner motivation and interest. When learning motivation and learning interest were strong, patients or their family representative explored relational knowledge about preoperative preparation, the operative process, and postoperative care.

In contrast, the control group was given informed consent information orally and also in a handout; these patients or their family representative were found to have difficulty in focusing, even though the disclosure time was only 40 min and the session was conducted by a single nurse practitioner, patients and family representatives became bored.

Most subjects (83.2%) were older than 40, and they found the multimedia instruction difficult to internalize. Thus, multimedia disclosure of preoperative informed consent information significantly enhanced learning motivation and learning interest. This finding was consistent with that obtained by Chang et al. [6]; that multimedia tools enhanced learning motivation and interest.

## 6.2. Effect of disclosure methods on learning outcomes

Our study found that multimedia disclosure of informed consent information enhanced remembering, comprehension, and satisfaction significantly more than the conventional method. Multimedia content was more easily accepted by patients, enabling them and their family representatives to understand content easily, thus promoting learner satisfaction.

With respect to the influence of disclosure methods on learning comprehension, we examined both actual and perceived comprehension. After disclosure, actual and perceived comprehension levels of the two groups were similar. They had significantly improved their actual comprehension, and their knowledge of arthroscopy and postoperative care had improved. However, the experimental group had significantly better comprehension than the control group. Nevertheless, after implementing the different disclosure methods, perceived comprehension of the experimental group exceeded that of the control group, including the material on post-operative rehabilitation and healthcare. Patients or their family representatives had an abstract understanding of the operation when this information was given in the conventional way.

Unlike our study, most previous studies only used video in their experiments, an approach that cannot be considered as multimedia. In our study, the disclosure information was presented using video, audio, and animation, increasing information clarity and helping overcome the shortcomings associated with conventional information disclosure. Through the multimedia presentation, patients and their family representatives had an enhanced understanding of postoperative care, such as the need to rest one’s feet at the correct angle, and proper use of aids in achieving this.

## 6.3. The effect of psychological learning processes on learning outcomes

We found that both learning motivation and learning interest were positively correlated o learning outcomes (remembering, comprehension, and satisfaction). Furthermore, learning motivation and learning interest significantly influenced perceived comprehension and satisfaction. Additionally, learning interest influenced learning outcomes more than learning motivation.

Previous studies demonstrated that learning motivation and interest were strongly correlated with learning outcome. Generally, high learning motivation and interest made learning easier, leading to superior outcomes. In clinical practice, it is difficult to communicate preoperative informed consent information clearly and efficiently. Moreover, its effectiveness in terms of remembering, comprehension, and satisfaction were better than those of the traditional method. Medical institutions should utilize technologymediated learning via multimedia to fulfill obligations and explain informed consent material, improve doctor–patient communication, reduce the number of medical malpractice suits, and increase patient satisfaction with medical treatment.

## 6.4. Research implications

Our study examined how different disclosure methods affected the psychological learning process and its outcomes. We found that learning motivation and interest were positively correlated with learning outcomes (remembering, comprehension, satisfaction), and that statistical results for perceived comprehension and satisfaction differed significantly.

For hospital managers, adopting a multimedia disclosure process does not increase costs or expenses significantly, but it enables efficient implementation of the informed consent process. A hospital can use multimedia disclosure of informed consent information to introduce preoperative, operative, postoperative care methods to satisfy patient learning demands, and promote learning outcomes, trust, and loyalty to the hospital. Multimedia preoperative disclosure of informed consent information promotes learning motivation and interest of patients

For multimedia system developers, we found that multimedia disclosure influenced learning motivation and interest. The multimedia method increased the amount of attention patients and their family representatives paid to disclosure content. Multimedia system developers should thus carefully analyze medical information from nurse practitioners, and then design appropriate picture, text, video, audio, and animation material based on multimedia, spatial contiguity, temporal continuity, and coherence principles. Additionally, the design of multimedia information should be human-centered and supplemented with technology. Finally, we suggest that designs of multimedia content should adopt problem-oriented design logic, as in the multimedia content presentation in our study. The presentation method should be based on simplicity, clarity, understandability, and demands of patients or their families.

Patients and their families typically have difficulty understanding medical information. Therefore, before explaining informed consent information using multimedia, patients and their families, after completing administrative procedures related to hospitalization, should be notified in advance of the time, importance, and purpose of the presentation. Moreover, a comfortable space should be provided.

## 7. Conclusions

We conducted field experiments in a regional and teaching hospital to provide evidence, based on the TML perspective, for multimedia disclosure of preoperative informed consent information. Furthermore, we explored how implementing different disclosure methods influenced psychological learning processes and learning outcomes. Careful experimental design yielded several important findings. (1) Different disclosure methods lead to significantly different learning motivation and learning interest. Moreover, multimedia generated better learning motivation and learning interest than the conventional method. (2) Different disclosure methods lead to significantly different learning outcomes. Multimedia disclosure of informed consent information was more effective than the conventional method in terms of remembering, comprehension, and satisfaction. (3) During the psychological learning processes, learning motivation and learning interest were positively correlated with learning outcomes (remembering, comprehension, and satisfaction), and correlations with comprehension and satisfaction were significant. Moreover, learning interest affected learning outcomes more than that of learning motivation.
