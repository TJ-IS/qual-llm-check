# Predicting personal information system adoption using an integrated diffusion model

Donghyun Kim <sup>a,</sup>\*, Tony Ammeter <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Business, Delta State University, United States

<sup>b</sup> School of Business Administration, University of Mississippi, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 3 October 2011 Received in revised form 27 November 2013 Accepted 26 February 2014 Available online 18 March 2014

Keywords: Innovation adoption Task-technology fit Mobile technologies

## A B S T R A C T

The purpose of this study is to develop a research model that integrates the relationship between innovation characteristics (innovation diffusion theory) and technology characteristics (task-technology fit model). With the research model, the current study investigates the adoption of the ‘‘personal information system,’’ a concept that we develop in the context of mobile technologies. In this paper, we performed the first quantitative test of the model by integrating the innovation diffusion and tasktechnology fit models. Further, we proposed a theoretical definition of ‘‘personal information systems’ by highlighting the differences in the types of tasks across individual users’ needs. To test our research model, 202 college students were surveyed. Partial least squares (PLS) structural equation modeling was employed to analyze our data, and these analyses provided empirical support for the proposed hypotheses. Quality, compatibility, cost, and relative advantage were found to be important indicators of the intention to adopt due to their impact on the intended communication, information, transaction, and entertainment tasks, while compatibility, relative advantage, and complexity had a direct impact on the intention to adopt.

## 1. Introduction

In the 21st century, many companies have made large investments to develop new and fast mobile communication devices, and mobile technologies are often touted as the next ‘‘killer application.’’ However, despite the investment and cuttingedge features that are available in mobile devices, most new mobile phone subscribers have adopted low-cost handsets instead of the latest mobile devices. For example, according to the U.S. Census Bureau, only 37% of cell phone users selected a smart phone in 2007; the Census Bureau forecasted this figure to increase by less than 10% in 2008. In this paper, we explore the reasons for these outcomes by adapting organizational-level innovation diffusion theory to the individual, or personal, level and develop an alternative conceptualization of mobile devices, that is, we conceptualize these devices as personal information systems. The research question that we seek to answer is ‘‘What characteristics of personal information systems affect their adoption as innovations?’’ The intended contribution is a more complete framework for investigating the adoption of personal information systems, such as hand-held mobile devices, by analyzing user experience and its impact on future usage and by exploring the fit between the intended tasks and the user’s knowledge regarding the characteristics of the technological innovation.

Our research model integrates the relationships between innovation characteristics in innovation diffusion theory and technology characteristics in the task-technology fit model to better investigate individuals’ adoption of mobile devices. We use adoption theories that were originally developed to explain the adoption of innovations by individuals in an organizational context. We argue that the concept of fit is an important factor to consider when explaining the process of innovation adoption.

## 1.1. Motivation for the study

Our study is motivated by three limitations of previous research on innovation adoption. First, scant research has paid attention to the process of mobile device adoption. Sarker and Wells [54] argue that the first condition of success for mobile commerce is the adoption of suitable mobile devices. They stress that it is important to understand the motivations and circumstances surrounding mobile device use and adoption from the consumers’ perspective. Previous studies have focused on the impact of mobile technology on customers’ adoption [14,22,23,27,28,31,53]. Similarly, a large body of mobile technology research has focused specifically on mobile commerce and not on the decisions to adopt mobile devices. We believe that studying the adoption of a ‘‘personal information system’’ is the key to studying the success of mobile commerce and mobile technologies.

Second, prior research has primarily considered mobile devices as a single-function system [14,23,27,31,54]. However, given the multifunctional nature of personal information systems, consumers likely perceive distinct values from different functions such as work-related applications compared with entertainment applications such as games or YouTube. We consider multiple tasks and their corresponding values that individuals derive from the fit between these tasks and the technology offered in the personal information system. In addition, IS literature has called for the reconceptualization of the systems usage construct in different contexts; system usage involves a user, a system, and a task [11]. Thus, we propose four tasks to analyze personal information system use: communication, information, transaction, and entertainment.

Third, previous research has applied innovation diffusion theory and task-technology fit, as developed for organizational information systems [6,48], to explore the processes of individuallevel information system adoption without adequately recognizing the level of adoption processes. As Wu and Lederer [64] note, investigating the different roles of the factors that affect adoption in different contexts by developing an integrated framework can help researchers understand the adoption process with innovations in a new context. This is important because we argue that personal information system adoption differs from organizational device adoption, chiefly in terms of the acquisition process. We develop a framework using the concept of the personal information system and use innovation diffusion theory and tasktechnology fit to explore the adoption and implementation of such systems.

In sum, the current study is motivated by the need to study (1) knowledge about customers’ cognitive perspective in the personal information system adoption and implementation process, (2) knowledge about the impact of multifunctionality on the adoption of the personal information system, and (3) knowledge about the research on personal information system adoption and the implementation processes in the context of an individual system. This paper is organized as follows. First, we review the characteristics of mobile technology and define the personal information system based on mobile technology characteristics and tasks. Then, we combine the innovation diffusion theory and task-technology fit models to explain personal information system adoption and develop hypotheses based on this integrated model. Finally, we report an empirical test of this model to address the intention to adopt a personal information system. We conclude with a discussion regarding the contributions of the model and our empirical test and make suggestions for future research.

## 2. ‘‘Personal information systems’’ and mobile technology

As indicated in the Introduction, we seek to define a ‘‘personal information system’’ to aid in our research into the adoption of mobile devices. Mobile devices have been identified by several names, such as the smart phone. We wish to distinguish the smart phone from other mobile devices on the grounds that a smart phone is an advanced information system that is used by a single user (unique to that device). The smart phone is a hybrid of a cell phone and a personal digital assistant. A smart phone often runs a mobile data services (MDS) application, which is a digital data service that can be accessed using a mobile device over a wide geographic area. Smart phones enable users to communicate with others, just as cell phones do, to exchange multiple messages, pictures, and e-mail, and to access the Internet. The essential features of these mobile devices are high levels of mobility, accessibility, personalization, and localizability.

## 2.1. Mobility

Mobility refers to the characteristic of a personal information system that allows users to accomplish the tasks that the system offers, unlimited by the users’ physical movements. Mobility consists of two dimensions: spatiality and temporality. Spatiality refers to the unlimited geographical movements of users, resources, and devices, and temporality includes more than the clock-time perspective. Temporality also refers to the ‘‘spaceless’’ nature of mobility, which enables users to reduce the information search time, conduct a transaction or information search unrestricted by location, and conveniently conduct an information search at any time. A small size, being light weight, and always-on operation are necessary for mobility [37] to enable users to carry a personal information system anytime and anywhere [23]. Therefore, users can engage in a wide range of other activities while conducting tasks with the personal information system.

## 2.2. Accessibility

Accessibility provides time-sensitive and real-time data to users [14,43]. Mobile technologies allow users to have access to timely information and services from any location at any time. This real-time information and these services allow users to make better decisions, thus enhancing the accuracy of information in context [43]. This accessibility also increases the value of personal information systems for information-gathering tasks and results in greater business opportunities. For example, some insurance companies report increasing their revenues by 17–21% after implementing remote access information systems [14].

## 2.3. Personalization

Personalization refers to devices, applications, and services that are customized to the owner’s preference and interests. Because personal information systems typically belong to only one user, the user has exclusive access to his/her platforms. Personalized services increase users’ loyalty and satisfaction and make them feel ‘‘closer’’ to personal information systems.

## 2.4. Localizability

Identifying the geographical position of a device (and, by implication, its user) is a distinctive characteristic provided by a personal information system. This gives service and content providers a chance to deliver localized information and services to users. Personal information systems, which are generally equipped with GPS or similar technology, can use location information to customize the information and service choices offered to the consumer [14,18].

## 2.5. Definition of personal information systems

The four characteristics of mobile technology are related to each other. When they are synthesized and coordinated, superior mobile services are provided to users. As such, the real-time connection and ‘‘everywhere’’ presence of mobile technology offers capabilities that are uniquely beneficial to each user. In addition, the advantages presented by the omnipresence of realtime knowledge, localized information, and continuous access to networks drive people to communicate with others through mobile devices. Thus, mobile technology-induced advantages have influenced daily life. Significant transformations in people’s lives occur through the use of mobile technology when the users communicate with others who have mobile devices. These situations from IT-induced change have been termed ‘‘technochanges’’ [8]. It is difficult for the users of modern technology to anticipate the effect of technochanges on their lives without recognizing the environmental shift in which users and technology interact. Thus, to discover the technochanges derived from mobile devices, we feel that it is important to explore personal information systems.

Given the lack of recognition regarding the information systems used by individuals, this paper presents, for the first time, a term for this sort of information system, ‘‘personal information system,’’ and a definition that enables researchers to explore another research area in the MIS discipline. In creating this definition, we are specifically referring to the type of personalization referred to by Adomavicius [1] as ‘‘consumer-centric’’ personalization, whereby an individual is the holder of a ‘‘personalization engine,’ that is, an artifact that has an understanding of the sets of data, services, or applications that define that user. In our context of mobile devices, this personalization engine is essentially encapsulated in the personal information system that is hosted on a mobile device.

Thus, we propose that a personal information system involves three elements: (1) a user, i.e., the subject using the personal information system, (2) a personalization engine, i.e., an artifact that has an understanding of the user, and (3) a task, i.e., the function being performed. A user is an individual person who uses information systems in his/her daily life. The personalization engine contains features that are distinct attributes that mobile technologies enable. Four features are identified in our study: mobility, accessibility, personalization, and localizability. A task is a goal-directed activity performed by a user [66]. In this study, we have categorized four tasks: communication, information, transaction, and entertainment. For our purposes, we assume that the features are designed to support the functions.

Thus, this study defines a personal information system as an information system that is equipped with mobility, accessibility, personalization, and localizability to support the communication, information, transaction, and entertainment tasks of an adopter. We distinguish this device from the other mobile devices to emphasize the multifunctional and system-like nature of the personal information system and so it can be evaluated as an ‘‘information system.’’ In addition, this definition clarifies the context of information systems. In contrast to previous definitions of phones, mobile devices, and smart phones, which focused on only their features such as Voice-Over-IP digital signals transmission, personal information management, wireless communications, satellite based transmissions, and text and multimedia messaging [13], our definition of personal information system implies that the systems is being used by an individual, is equipped with certain features, and is used at certain times. Accordingly, we suggest that personal information systems are designed for individual users’ daily tasks that are related to their own purposes. Finally, our definition of personal information systems aligns with Robey’s [51] argument that ‘‘a system that does not help people perform their jobs is not likely to be received favorably. . . (p. 537),’ by delineating a user, a personalization engine, and a task.

## 3. Theoretical foundations

In our study, we argue that an integration of the tasktechnology fit model with the innovation diffusion model can help us explain the individual-level adoption of personal information systems. Many studies have used the innovation diffusion model to examine the characteristics of innovations that lead to their adoption. Because one of this study’s research goals is to investigate the multifunctional nature of personal information systems, we explore the implications of fit between innovation characteristics and the tasks for which the personal information system is used–a theme supported by the tasktechnology fit model. Incorporating these two models, we propose that the fit between the characteristics of the technology (i.e., the innovation) and the tasks that the individual user envisions needing the technology for will drive adoption decisions. Before describing this relationship in detail, we first review the basic tenets of innovation diffusion theory and technology fit.

## 3.1. Innovation diffusion theory

Innovation diffusion theory describes the process by which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system [52]. The research on innovation diffusion theory falls into two streams: (1) research about the processes of innovation diffusion [6,48] and (2) research about the attributes of innovations [41,46,58]. In the second stream, five characteristics that affect the diffusion of an information technology (IT) innovation are defined: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, observability, and trialability [41,58]. Moore and Benbasat [41] empirically assessed the effect of these characteristics on information system adoption by measuring users’ perceptions of adopting an IT innovation. In the study, a relative advantage is defined as the degree to which an innovative information system is considered better than the legacy or existing technology. Compatibility refers to the degree to which an IT innovation is consistent with the existing values, needs, and experiences of potential adopters. Complexity refers to the difficulty in usage that the innovative information system would introduce. Observability refers to how easy it is to observe the results or output of the IT innovation. Trialability refers to the amount of experimentation that could be done with the innovative information system before adoption.

Innovation diffusion theory has been empirically applied in a variety of contexts to explain the diffusion of IT innovations, including the implementation of electronic data interchanges [46], the implementation of enterprise resource planning system [48], computer-aided software engineering [56], the acceptance of advanced mobile services [36], and a mobile ticketing service [39].

## 3.2. Task-technology fit

The concept of fit is not unique to the MIS field and has been used as an important building block for theory construction [59]. Vessey [60] argues that cognitive fit encourages the use of problem-solving processes to solve a specific task. In MIS research, the study of fit has focused on the effects of task and technology on task performance or information system performance [20,24,35,40]. Goodhue and Thompson [24] argue that task-technology fit results in performance impact, and they define task-technology fit as ‘‘the degree to which technology assists an individual in performing his or her portfolio of tasks (p. 216).’’ That is, task-technology fit is the correspondence between task requirements and the functionality of the technology. In the task-technology fit model, technology refers to ‘‘tools used by individuals in carrying out people’s tasks,’’ and tasks are defined as ‘‘the actions carried out by individual in turning into outputs (p. 216)’’ [24].

Task-technology fit has been examined as a predictor of the performance of group support systems [66], software engineering tools [21], mobile commerce in the insurance industry [32], knowledge management system [5], and the fit between mobile work and mobile work support functions [65].

## 3.3. The concept of ‘‘task’’ in task-technology fit in information systems

In the current study, task is defined as the behavior requirements for accomplishing stated goals using given mobile devices. In information systems research, work on ‘‘task’’ has been conducted mostly in the context of measuring system performance. Zigurs and Buckland [66] investigate group support systems’ effectiveness in the context of different tasks and find a task-effectiveness relationship. Applying task to mobile technology, four consistent categories of tasks are revealed [22,27]. Table 1 presents these four task categories: (1) communication, (2) information, (3) transaction, and (4) entertainment.

Communications are a basic feature of wireless technology and are mostly used to communicate with others. The task is undertaken with several forms: voice, short message service (SMS), multimedia message service (MMS), video call, and email. Information tasks provide, for example, several types of news such as traffic and weather. In addition, local information services and self-scheduling are informational tasks. Transaction tasks refer to real-payments through a mobile device. Entertainment tasks include games, ringtones, browsing the Internet for fun, and blogging.

## 4. Research model and hypotheses development

## 4.1. Research model

In the innovation diffusion model, adoption processes are accelerated when users are able to transfer their knowledge of the legacy system to the usage of the new (innovative) system innovation, which is also known as knowledge transfer through analogy [25]. An innovation is adopted more rapidly when the innovation is compatible with old technology and is less complex, resulting in a ‘‘fit’’ with the legacy system, wherein the transferred knowledge reduces the cognitive efforts needed to do a task [3] and leads to higher performance with the innovation [49].

These effects are strengthened in individual-level adoption because knowledge is an important trigger of the individual adoption process [6]. Thus, a fit between a legacy system and an innovative system enables users to more readily adopt the innovative system. We argue that the task-technology fit literature provides a useful conceptual framework within which to describe this legacy-innovative system fit and thus propose that integrating the task-technology fit model with the innovation diffusion model will help us explain the individual-level adoption of personal information systems, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

As a preview of the development of our model, we note that innovation diffusion research suggests that the most important innovation characteristics are perceived compatibility, perceived cost, perceived relative advantage, and perceived complexity. In our model, potential adopters conceptualize the focal innovation, in this case a personal information system, in these terms. A synthesis of innovation diffusion theory and task-technology fit suggests that we add perceived quality to this list of characteristics. Because the current study addresses the phase before adoption, when it is too early to assess actual usage or behavior, our dependent variable is the intention to adopt the personal information system.

In IS adoption research, it is important to note the differences between adopting a personal information system and adopting an organizational information system. An organizational information system is generally adopted using an acquisition-and-evaluation model, while a personal information system is adopted using an evaluation-and-acquisition model. The acquisition of an information system in an organization occurs via an adoption decision made by upper-level managers or ‘‘C-level’’ executives (i.e., CIO), ideally with recommendations from their technical staff and the advice of key end users. This process places the majority of end users in a more passive role, and an implicit assumption of both the innovation diffusion and task-technology fit models is that ‘‘adoption’’ has occurred once the information system is in the hands of the end user. Therefore, in the organizational adoption context, after the system is delivered, IS theories and models such as innovation diffusion theory and the task-technology fit model can be applied to assess the success and speed of success of adoption and the performance impact, along with how (much) the technology supports a task.

Unlike organizational-level acquisition, personal information system acquisition occurs through a user’s willingness to evaluate a technology and the corresponding task and self-evaluation of the innovation. Individual end users are far less likely to participate in organizational acquisition decisions, while their use (or non-use) of the innovation is deemed to be a measure of the ‘‘adoption’’ of the innovation. Once an innovation is acquired by an organization, the end user has far less flexibility in choosing to use it, while the individual users of the personal information system are more likely to participate in their adoption decision- making process by evaluating the innovations before adopting the innovations. Therefore, assessing individual-level adoption using the acquisition-and-evaluation (organizational) model is, at worst, potentially problematic or, at best, possibly inaccurate. Thus, the current study proposes a research model to investigate the adoption of a personal information system in the individual adoption context (the evaluation-and-acquisition model).

To develop our research model explaining the adoption of a personal information system, adjusting the task-technology fit model is a good starting point. The task-technology fit model investigates how (much) technologies lead to performance impacts in an organizational context. However, along with our research goal to develop a research model exploring the adoption of a personal information system and given the nature of the evaluation and acquisition model, this study focuses on the early stage of adoption. We thus suggest that assessing the intention for adoption is more suitable to our personal information systems research model compared with assessing performance impacts. We diagram this in Fig. 2.

The next step in the process of integrating the two models is to understand ‘‘fit’’ as it applies to our research question. Incorporating the task-technology fit literature, we propose that the fit between the characteristics of the technology (the innovation) and the tasks that the individual user envisions needing the technology for will drive adoption decisions. According to Goodhue and Thompson [24], task-technology fit is the degree to which a technology helps an end user perform a task or tasks. In addition, task-technology fit is the correspondence between task requirements and the functionality of the technology. Thus, in the context of individual-level personal information system adoption, we can understand task-technology fit as how (much) technology supports individuals’ task(s). This leads us to replace measuring the task-technology fit variables with examining the relationship between technology characteristics and task (Fig. 2). In other words, our research model investigates how (much) the innovation characteristics support (assist) the performance of a specific task.

## 4.2. Hypotheses development

4.2.1. Task as a mediator of the effect of innovation characteristics on intention to adopt

The overall perceived performance of and satisfaction with a product is often the result of a collection of the product’s attributes.

Oliver [45] explains attribute satisfaction as attribute experience. He defines attribute experience as ‘‘the consumer’s perception of a product or services feature’’ (p. 421) [45]. Attribute satisfaction is defined as ‘‘the consumer’s subjective satisfaction judgment resulting from observations of attribute performance’’ (p. 421). That is, a customer consumes various functions (attributes) of product or service based on experience and develops both positive and negative affect toward each function. Then, the customer evaluates his/her satisfaction with each attribute experience, and the attribute satisfaction is aggregated to overall satisfaction. In turn, overall satisfaction affects the intention to use the product [26].

Thus, we believe that the aggregate level of the intention to execute the four tasks identified in our model will influence the intention to adopt a personal information system. Chen and Nath [14] find that task characteristics affect attitudes toward smart phone adoption. Furthermore, we believe that the effects of the characteristics of the innovation, in addition to their direct effect on adoption, will have a unique effect on the adoption of the innovation through the intention to engage in each task; the intention to engage in a task thus acts as a partial mediator of the effect of the characteristic on the adoption likelihood. We thus have the following hypotheses:

## 4.2.2. Perceived quality

Within our context of the personal information system and utilizing the task-technology fit, quality refers to the perception that the information provided by a personal information system is current enough, appropriate, and at the right level to meet the requirements of an individual’s set of tasks [24,32]. Thus, this study defines perceived quality as the extent to which the information and services provided by the personal information system are perceived to be current, appropriate, and wide-ranging enough to meet a user’s needs.

Perceived quality has been examined as a direct predictor of the performance of organizational systems [24] and as an influence on the relationship between perceived quality and attitude toward Internet telephony through the perceived usefulness of Internet telephony and Internet TV [55]. In particular, system quality with respect to technical adequacy, delay, and privacy has been identified as an important factor in users’ attitude and intention to accept an online retailing service [2]. Similarly, the perceived quality of the technical adequacy of sending and receiving voice calls, video calls, SMS, and MMS, their delivery speed, and the security and privacy of communications would affect the intention to execute communication tasks. Accordingly, we propose that the perceived quality of a personal information system would influence the intention of executing communication tasks (H2b). In addition, previous research revealed the impact of information quality on systems use [19], on perceived net benefits in data warehousing [63], and on net benefit in an e-government environment [47].

Acknowledging the effect of information quality on systems use and benefits, we propose that the perceived quality of a personal information system would affect the intention of executing information tasks (H2c). Moreover, Well et al. [62] empirically test the impact of website quality on online purchasing decisions through perceived product quality. For transactions in a mobile environment, the quality of a personal information system is a critical element because the transaction is asymmetrically made and because additional asymmetries are often inherent to an ITmediated environment. We therefore suggest that perceived quality positively affects the intention of executing transaction tasks (H2d). Finally, perceived quality has been investigated as an antecedent of user satisfaction through entertainment in web-based customer support system [44] and of playfulness on user acceptance of online retailing [2]. Thus, we propose that perceived quality affects the intention of executing entertainment tasks (H2e).

Summarizing this section, we propose that perceived quality is positively related to the intention to use personal information systems because this intention is related to the intention to perform subsequent tasks with that personal information system:

H2. The perceived quality of the information and services provided by the personal information system is positively related to (a) the intention to adopt the personal information system and the intention of executing (b) communication tasks, (c) information tasks, (d) transaction tasks, and (e) entertainment tasks.

## 4.2.3. Perceived cost

In early innovation diffusion theory, only the absolute value (price) was considered a ‘‘cost factor’’ [58]. In later studies, cost included the perceived relative value, that is, the relative benefit against economic effort of using the innovation [41], resulting in the innovation being evaluated for both economic profitability and cognitive effort. The current study adopts both of these dimensions; perceived cost thus refers to the extent to which users evaluate monetary and cognitive effort in the adoption and use of the personal information system.

Researchers have attempted to identify the relationships between the cost attributes of innovations and their diffusion. Hong and Zhu [29] find that managers considering the adoption of e-commerce, for whom obstacles to adoption and system operating costs loom large, are reluctant to adopt e-commerce. In our study, to communicate with each other using their personal information systems, users continuously generate operating costs such as rates for voice calls, SMSs, and MMSs. Accordingly, we propose that perceived cost is negatively related to the intention of performing communication tasks (H3b). Moreover, one of the vital factors of adopting e-commerce is low search cost. Low search cost in an online environment offers users a consumer surplus by providing a variety of products and lower product price [9,10]. The implementation of innovations is a process of knowledge accumulation that consumes cognitive efforts and generates switching costs. Therefore, these perceived costs may increase perceptions of search cost. As a consequence, we propose that perceived costs have a negative effect on the intention of executing information tasks (H3c).

Another important factor that impacts the frequency of transaction tasks on mobile devices is security [39,61]. Cost increments may be incurred when protecting systems from malicious actions, such as installing anti-spyware software [33]. Users may perceive that improving security may require considerable effort. As a result, a greater perceived cost results in fewer users intending to execute transaction tasks on their personal information systems. Thus, we propose that the effect of perceived cost would be negatively related to intention of executing transaction tasks (H3d).

Finally, the negative impact of adoption cost on system adoption and implementation has held in variety of contexts, such as open systems attitudes of IT executives [12], the adoption of technology in households [7], information communication technology diffusion such as cell phones [4], and the intention to adopt anti-spyware software [33]. Entertainment tasks with personal information systems require adoption costs such as purchasing games and ringtones. Thus, we propose that an increase in adoption costs for entertainment tasks would lead to a decrease in intentions to perform entertainment tasks (H4e).

Summarizing this section, we propose that cost is negatively related to the intention to adopt personal information systems because this intention is related to the intention to perform subsequent tasks using that personal information system:

H3. The perceived cost of the personal information system is negatively related to (a) the intention to adopt the personal information system and the intention of executing (b) communication tasks, (c) information tasks, (d) transaction tasks, and (e) entertainment tasks.

## 4.2.4. Perceived compatibility

According to innovation diffusion theory, compatibility includes two dimensions: the values or norms of the adopters and the practices of the adopter. The first dimension refers to cognitive compatibility, or compatibility with what people feel or think about a technology, while the second refers to operational compatibility, or compatibility with what people do [58]. The compatibility of the task-technology fit includes only the second dimension of data compatibility, wherein compatibility occurs when data from different sources can be consolidated or compared without inconsistencies [24]. We argue for the inclusion of both dimensions because they would both be expected to play a critical role in the adoption decision for a personal information system. We adopt the definition of perceived compatibility as the degree to which personal information system are perceived as being consistent with the existing values, needs, and past experience of potential adopters.

Innovation diffusion scholars have identified the impact of compatibility on the intention to use on-line shopping by reducing the time to complete various tasks [61]. Similarly, positive relationships have been found between both organizational and technical compatibility and managers’ decisions to adopt a website application for their organization; this was found to be driven by reducing the effort required to modify organizational culture, norms, and technical infrastructure [5]. Acknowledging the effect of compatibility in reducing the effort to implement innovations, we predict that compatibility may be more likely to result in reducing the cognitive effort required to execute communication tasks. Therefore, we propose that compatibility is positively related to the intention of executing communication tasks (H4b). Furthermore, compatibility improves efficiency by saving time when searching for information by tracking users’ habits and preferences [61]. Taken together, we suggest that compatibility would positively affect the intention of executing information tasks on a personal information system (H4c).

In addition, the impact of compatibility has been identified in the adoption of mobile ticketing services [39] and banking services [34]. These findings suggest that compatibility would positively affect the intention of executing transaction tasks on personal information system (H4d). Compatibility is also an indicator of how well an innovation fits a potential adopter’s lifestyle, needs, and preferences. Similar views are shared by other researchers, for example, that compatibility positively influences the adoption of websites [61] and enterprise application integration in health-care organizations [30]. Thus, we propose that when a personal information system delivers entertainment tasks that are compatible with users’ lifestyle, needs, and preferences, users are more likely to adopt based on their perception of usage to execute entertainment tasks (H4e).

Summarizing this section, we propose that perceived compatibility is positively related to intention to use personal information systems because this intention is related to the intention to perform subsequent tasks using that personal information system:

H4. The perceived compatibility of the personal information system is positively related to (a) the intention to adopt the personal information system and the intention of executing (b) communication tasks, (c) information tasks, (d) transaction tasks, and (e) entertainment tasks.

## 4.2.5. Perceived relative advantage

Tornatzky and Klein [58] define relative advantage as ‘‘the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than the idea it supersedes.’’ However, ‘being better’ may be interpreted in several ways (i.e., economic profitability, social benefits, cognitive effort saved, or time saved). To eliminate confusion with the definition, we exclude economic profitability and include productivity, efficiency, and effectiveness. This is consistent with the tasktechnology fit notion that the use of the technology results in the more productive, efficient, and effective completion of tasks.

Relative advantage, as a strong indicator of innovation diffusion, affects users’ perceptions of adoption of innovations. Given its multifunctional and Internet-based characteristics, a personal information system provides users with diverse, productive, and efficient communication methods such as a better quality of voice and video call, faster delivery of SMS, MMS, and email, and

Internet-based communication applications. Each of these distinctive functions will contribute positively to users’ perceived benefits. Thus, we propose that relative advantage positively influence the intention of executing communication tasks (H5b).

Similarly, previous research has investigated enterprise application integration (EAI) adoption through the availability of the required information provided by EAI [30], organizational open systems adoption through the benefits of providing transparent data access [12], and behavioral intentions to use the web by providing informational convenience and trust [16]. These empirical studies highlight the role that information quality plays in the relationship between relative advantage and innovation adoption. Thus, we propose that relative advantage is positively related to the intention of executing information tasks (H5c).

In a related study, good website design, i.e., a design that is more convenient to use, provides users with greater benefit when they make a purchase on such a website [38]. Correspondingly, given the personalization, mobility, and accessibility characteristics of personal information systems, users may perceive advantages when the systems provide more convenient transaction methods than alternatives, such as making payments online using a non-personalized, non-mobile, and/or less easily accessed device (such as a home computer). We therefore suggest that relative advantage would lead to increased intentions of executing transaction tasks (H5d). Similarly, personal information systems offer the possibility for real-time entertainment that can also be location-based and time-sensitive. From this, we propose that potential adopters are likely to weigh the potential to engage in entertainment tasks with the adoption of their personal information system as a relative advantage (H5e).

Summarizing this section, we propose that perceived relative advantage is positively related to the intention to use personal information systems because this intention is related to the intention to perform subsequent tasks using that personal information system:

H5. The perceived relative advantage of the personal information system is positively related to (a) the intention to adopt the personal information system and the intention of executing (b) communication tasks, (c) information tasks, (d) transaction tasks, and (e) entertainment tasks.

## 4.2.6. Perceived complexity

In innovation diffusion research, complexity relates to the degree to which a personal information system is perceived as being difficult to use. Given their multifunctional nature, i.e., limited interfaces such as a small screen and a small number of keys (one key may play various roles in different tasks or contexts), personal information systems may indeed be perceived as difficult to use.

From previous research, complexity is considered an inhibitor to adoption [15]. Implementing innovations with high complexity generally requires a large amount of time and cognitive effort to complete various tasks [57]. This negatively affects users’ satisfaction and, in turn, the intention to adoption complex innovations. Acknowledging the effect of complexity in increasing the effort and time to implement the innovations, complexity may be perceived as likely to result in increasing the cognitive effort to execute communication tasks with a personal information system. Thus, we propose that complexity will be negatively related to the intention to execute communication tasks (H6b). Similar studies have found that complexity hinders a user’s intention to adopt websites because of perceptions of increased information search costs [42]. From this, we propose that complexity would lower the intention to perform information tasks with the personal information system (H6c).

Son and Benbasat [57] investigate the negative impact of complexity on the adoption of B2B e-marketplace because products that are high in complexity require the provision of large and involved descriptions. Similarly, transaction services with high complexity require more efforts to assimilate a large amount of information. Thus, we propose a negative relationship between complexity and the intention of executing transaction tasks (H6d) on a personal information system. Nadkarni and Gupta [42] identify a negative relationship between complexity and user satisfaction with websites. Their study argues that complexity inhibits website adoption because complexity lowers the fun, entertainment, and enjoyment value of a website. Acknowledging the negative effect of complexity on entertainment value, we propose that the perceived complexity of personal information systems would lower the intention to perform entertainment tasks with them (H6e).

Summarizing this section, we propose that perceived complex ity is negatively related to the intention to use personal information systems because this intention is related to the intention to perform subsequent tasks using that personal information system:

H6. The perceived complexity of the personal information system is negatively related to (a) the intention to adopt the personal information system and the intention of executing (b) communication tasks, (c) information tasks, (d) transaction tasks, and (e) entertainment tasks.

## 5. Methods and data analyses

## 5.1. Data and samples

Before distributing the questionnaire for the main study, a pilot test was conducted with a group of 35 university students who were similar to the respondents for our primary survey. In addition to collecting the survey data, we solicited feedback from them, including unclear wording and the definition of mobile devices. As a result, some questions were identified as difficult to answer due to the clarity, content, and/or appropriateness of the wording. Based on these comments, the questionnaire was revised. We achieved acceptable item analysis and reliability indices with both the pilot test and subsequent main study; we believe that this is a reasonable test of the internal and face validity of the survey instrument.

For the primary study, all respondents were university students in their sophomore, junior, or senior years. Students were recruited through a Management Information Systems course that students from a cross-section of general business majors were required to take. A total of 205 students were surveyed, and a total of 202 complete and valid surveys were received (98.5%). Thelarge response rate was likely due to the extra credit that was offered to students who completed the survey. The age of respondents varied from 19 to 40 years, and 87% were between the ages of 20 and 25. Thirty-seven percent of the respondents were female, and 54% indicated having more than one year of experience with smart phones.

## 6. Discussion

The key objective of the paper was to develop and test a research model to understand personal information system adoption. The model integrated the innovation diffusion and task-technology fit models. The model was supported by an empirical analysis of data collected from potential users, here, undergraduate students. The results of our PLS analyses demonstrate that the intention to engage in communication, information, and entertainment tasks affect the intention to adopt and that these tasks mediate to some extent the effect of the quality, compatibility, and relative advantage characteristics of adoption.

Concerning consumer practice, the findings of this study support the effect of compatibility on users’ implementation and adoption of personal information systems. Thus, users who perceived higher compatibility in an innovative personal information system with previous mobile devices for a variety of reasons (e.g., past experience, current situation), who have a significantly higher intention to engage in communication, information, and entertainment tasks, and who see a fit between the system and these tasks should be highly likely to adopt a personal information system.

The study also indicates that the intention to engage in transaction tasks is influenced by perceived quality, perceived cost, and perceived relative advantage but that these tasks do not seem to impact the intention to adopt. We also did not find a relationship between complexity and any of the task variables, indicating that these task variables are evaluated separately from any evaluation of how difficult a device is deemed to be to use. This finding may suggest that difficulty or, conversely, ease of use is a singularly important characteristic that trumps all other considerations. The questions that are not answered by our data present opportunities for future research.

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

This study makes several important contributions to IS research. First, integrating two of the most dominant models is a key contribution. This sheds light on the mechanisms through which personal information system characteristics and user tasks work together to affect adoption and implementation processes. Personal information systems are adopted and used by different users who presumably have different tasks in mind for ‘‘their’’ system. This further focuses research on the adoption of multifunctional devices through the use of multidimensional or system-level tools. Using our integrative model and the definition of the personal information system, researchers should be better able to conceptualize and model the adoption of such multidimensional and user-task specific systems.

Second, our research presents an exploratory attempt to define and conceptualize personal information systems and contributes in several ways. The core contribution of the research is that it provides a way for IS researchers to explicitly reconceptualize personal information systems. Although new information systems have long been identified and defined in IS research, past conceptualizations of them have remained implicit while lacking the recognition of a user, a feature, and a task. We believe that differentiating personal information systems from other mobile devices will open new areas to explore to understand a phenomenon that is related to an innovation in IS research.

Finally, the paper makes an important theoretical contribution by incorporating the construct of ‘‘intention to engage in tasks’’ as a mediator to explain the adoption of information systems. There has long been a lack of mediators between the characteristics of innovations and the intention to adopt them. By examining and incorporating users’ attitudes toward individual aspects of information systems to measure the users’ aggregated intention to adopt the information systems, this paper suggests that such indicators will provide very meaningful insights into how attitudes toward specific features of systems leads to behavioral intention to adopt the systems.

## 6.2. Practical implications

From a practitioner’s perspective, this study provides several meaningful insights for IS managers who control the development and implementation of personal information systems. First, our model can provide useful understandings for management to successfully distribute resources to develop a feature. For example, the finding of this study indicates that quality is the most dominant indicator of intention to engage in transaction tasks. This finding also indicates that a high level of appropriateness and credibility of information is more important than is ease of use.

Second, our research results indicate that IT managers could promote the adoption of personal information systems as multifunctional devices. While information systems are continuously becoming versatile and used in diverse aspects of life and business, these systems may have been developed without recognizing the usage characteristics of the target users. Practitioners should focus on increasing users’ intention to adopt information systems by examining how individual features support what they want to perform with their information systems. Our study will hopefully educate practitioners in terms of the importance of focusing on not only an aggregation of users’ intention to use systems but also an individual intention of adopting the systems by reflecting users’ attitude toward specific features.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

Though this study has offered some insights into personal information system usage, several limitations to our research must be acknowledged. First, the use of student subjects may limit the generalizability of our findings to individual IT usage. Undergraduate students are not completely representative of the entire population of individual users whose personal information systems usage intention and behavior we would like to model. However, we expect this problem to be minimal because the results reported in prior IT usage studies employing student subjects do not appear to be systematically different from those employing general users.

The undergraduate subjects in our study may be representative of the younger college-educated segment of end users. These students are most likely more digitally literate compared with adults. Hence, complexity may have been less an issue for this sample than it would have been for general users. With more complex or difficult systems, complexity may have had a greater impact on the intentions of executing tasks. In addition, these subjects were likely also more highly motivated to intend to perform entertainment tasks compared with the general population, which may have caused enjoyment to take on greater importance than it generally would. Further research on these variables and relationships in other settings will sharpen our understanding of their generalizability.

Second, our empirical test did not include all characteristics for personal information systems because a smart phone is used as a proxy for a personal information system. As we discussed above, a smart phone is the closest that we have to this technology.

Finally, although we developed and verified new constructs for the intention to engage tasks with good reliability and validity, the instrument development and validation steps might not have had an adequate sample size to provide a more rigorous test. Similarly, our lack of findings for some of the hypotheses may also have been due to the small sample size. More and larger-scale studies must therefore be conducted.

## 7. Conclusion

The goal of this paper was to define the personal information system and explore the antecedents of success in the adoption of such a system using an integrated innovation diffusion and tasktechnology fit model. We sought to explain the characteristics of mobile technology in the personal information system and the tasks supported by the personal information system and utilized both innovation diffusion theory and task-technology fit to more adequately model the process taken by an individual when deciding to adopt this unique and more and more ubiquitous device/system.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first empirical study to combine innovation diffusion theory and task-technology fit in such a model of personal-level adoption. This model contributes to research on both the adoption of personal information systems and the adoption of innovations in general. Similar work may be undertaken to identify task categories for other systems, and this model may be replicated using those task categories to more fruitfully research the systems. Finally, our definition of the personal information system might serve to more accurately describe devices that are becoming more multifunctional and defy other definitions that place them into specific product categories. As we have noted and leave as a final message, adopting a systems perspective has benefits when trying to investigate the antecedents and mechanisms for adoption decisions for that system.
