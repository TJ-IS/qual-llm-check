# An empirical investigation of Technology Readiness among medical staff based in Greek hospitals

Christos D. Melas<sup>1</sup>, Leonidas A. Zampetakis<sup>2,3</sup>, Anastasia Dimopoulou<sup>4</sup> and Vassilis S. Moustakis<sup>2,5</sup>

## Abstract

Technology readiness (TR) represents an individual’s mental readiness to accept new technologies. Although the TR scale has been used in many studies, its application in the healthcare context is limited. This paper focuses on identifying the TR profiles of medical staff and to model preference TR variations with respect to computer use, computer knowledge and computer feature demands. The study reports results from a nationwide study conducted in Greece, during a three-year period, which sampled responses from 604 physicians and nurses working in 14 Greek hospitals. Exploratory Structural Equation Modelling analysis is used in order to confirm the structure of the Technology Readiness Index. The results confirm the five groups of the TR taxonomy. Statistical differences were found between classes in information and communication technology (ICT) knowledge, ICT feature demands, hours of use per week as well as ICT use performance, but not in the general use of ICT. The results facilitate comprehension of the factors, which influence the use of ICT by medical staff and, in addition, they convey important policy and managerial implications. In conclusion, medical staff should be treated according to its TR taxonomy classes in order to expedite the acceptance and use of an ICT system. ## Introduction

The diffusion of information and communication technology (ICT) has attracted sizeable attention. Numerous articles have been published, focusing either on speci<sup>fi</sup>c system implementations or on organizational or individual dimensions, which are presumed to in<sup>fl</sup>uence system adoption. Nonetheless, ICT plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in healthcare service delivery and its effective use positively correlates with subsequent increased levels of service quality.

Coupled with effective ICT use and adoption in healthcare settings is the concept of technology readiness (TR), originally pioneered by Parasuraman (2000) and Parasuraman & Colby (2001). TR has emerged as a formal generic concept, leading to the formulation of a systematic protocol tool, the technology readiness index (TRI). TRI provides a valid statistical tool, able to classify individuals across a continuous tendency spectrum, from ‘technology pioneers’ (i.e., ‘technology ready’ individuals) to ‘technology gloomy’ persons.

The expanding role of information technology in healthcare delivery makes it imperative to understand the TR of the medical staff to use ICT (such as electronic medical records (EMRs), telemedicine, personal digital assistants, email and databases; Burke & Weill, 2009) which have been designed to improve clinical practice and patient care. Few studies have, however, addressed the TR of healthcare professionals.

In the literature there is a consensus that medical staff (especially doctors) differ from the general population of technology users with respect to technology acceptance. The level of specialization, training, work context and responsibility have, among other factors, been found to account for differences (Campbell et al, 2001; Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007; Yarbrough & Smith, 2007; Melas et al, 2011).

Agarwal et al (2010), in their research concerning the digital transformation of healthcare, examined a series of existing studies in health information technology (HIT) and speci<sup>fi</sup>ed several essential research areas in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of HIT that are important at the current stage of HIT adoption, such as: (1) HIT design, implementation and meaningful use, (2) measurement and quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation of HIT payoff and impact, and (3) extending the traditional realm of HIT.

Gagnon et al (2012) in their systematic review examined various factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the adoption of ICT by healthcare professionals. They concluded that ICT adoption is complex, multi-dimensional and in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a variety of factors at individual and organizational levels.

Decker et al (2012), in their research concerning the adoption of EMR systems by physicians, found that although trends in adoption of EMR systems across geographic regions converged from 2002 through 2011, adoption continued to lag for non-primary care specialists, physicians aged 55 and older, and physicians in small (1–2 providers) and physician-owned practices. In their study they concluded that federal policies should focus on encouraging adoption among non-primary care specialists, as well as addressing persistent gaps in the use of EMR systems by practice size, physician age and ownership status.

One of the most important federal policy interventions in the United States, created to stimulate and promote the adoption of HIT in healthcare, is the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act as part of the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009. The Act aims at addressing speci<sup>fi</sup>c barriers in HIT and EMRs acceptance and use by offering <sup>fi</sup>nancial bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to healthcare providers for demonstrating the meaningful use of EMRs and supporting technology (Blumenthal, 2009).

This article complements existing literature by reporting results based on a sample of 604 healthcare professionals working in 14 Greek hospitals. This is the largest professional speci<sup>fi</sup>c sample reported to date. Using statistically reliable sample responses, the article formulates the TR of healthcare staff. Furthermore, TR pro<sup>fi</sup>ling is examined in conjunction with the level of ICT use, the extent of ICT knowledge and skills and the demand for ICT functionality (ICT feature demand).

More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in the present research, ICT use is a generic term that records how many hours per week computers are used in general, how often computers are used and for what tasks (e.g. obtaining diagnostic or therapeutic advice and/or for communicating with colleagues) and self-reported ICT performance. ICT knowledge is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘how much’ clinicians perceived to know about ICT, and ICT feature demands refer to ‘how sophisticated’ a computer system must be before clinicians would be willing to use it (Cork et al, 1998).

Several widely recognized theoretical models of new technology adoption, such as the theory of planned behaviour (TPB) (Ajzen & Madden, 1986), the technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis, 1989) or the uni<sup>fi</sup>ed theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) (Venkatesh et al, 2003) are based on the general premise that utility beliefs (i.e. user’s evaluation of the usefulness of the technology), usability beliefs (i.e., user’s evaluation of the ease in order to apply the technology), social in<sup>fl</sup>uences (i.e. the perception that important others believe that the individual should use the technology) and facilitating conditions affect attitudes, which determine intention to use the technology. Previous research on technology acceptance suggests that the individual's personality may affect both the acceptance and the use (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007). TR model posits a person’s personality at the centre of his or her acceptance of technology. People with different traits hold different beliefs about various aspects of technology.

Because of the fact that TR refers to people's propensity to embrace and use new technologies, it is expected that TR pro<sup>fi</sup>les of medical staff will differ in terns of ICT use, ICT knowledge and ICT feature demands. Integrated use of statistical methods supports both the analysis and the results reported herein. Cluster analysis combined with analysis of variance (ANOVA) support the modelling of individual TR patterns and exploratory structural equation modelling (ESEM) is used both to explore and to model causal relationships between TR pro<sup>fi</sup>les and ICT use, ICT knowledge and ICT feature demands.

## Theoretical background

## Theories concerning technology adoption and acceptance

In the information systems literature two broad research paradigms have emerged in order to explain technology adoption and acceptance. The <sup>fi</sup>rst paradigm is system speci<sup>fi</sup>c, and focuses on how a technology’s attributes affect an individual’s perception of a technology. This in turn affects the usage of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c technology. The second paradigm focuses on latent personality dimensions to explain the use and acceptance of new technologies. In other words, an individual’s personality in<sup>fl</sup>uences the potential acceptance of technology in general. The TRI is related to this approach (Parasuraman, 2000).

Within the <sup>fi</sup>rst paradigm several theoretical models have been proposed and used such as the theory of reasoned action (TRA) (Fishbein & Ajen, 1975), the TAM (Davis, 1989) and the TPB (Ajzen & Madden, 1986). TAM, which identi<sup>fi</sup>es consumer beliefs about perceived usefulness and ease of use as the primary drivers of individuals’ attitudes toward new technologies, is considered as one of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential and most commonly used theoretical frameworks in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of technology acceptance and use (Melas et al, 2011). Nevertheless, mediocre results in predictive power have resulted in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant criticism of the TAM model (e.g., Venkatesh et al, 2003). This has triggered an effort to improve the predictive power of TAM through the consideration of additional explanatory variables.

Venkatesh et al (2003) presented a review and synthesis of eight theories/models of technology use. The TAM, the motivational model, the TPB, the combined TAM and TPB (C-TAM-TPB), the model of PC utilization, innovation diffusion theory, and social cognitive theory were all identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. The primary contribution of Venkatesh et al (2003) was the development of the UTAUT. UTAUT has four key constructs (i.e., performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and facilitating conditions) that in<sup>fl</sup>uence behavioural intention to use a technology and/or technology use. UTAUT has been found to explain about 70% of the variance in behavioural intention to use a technology and about 50% of the variance in technology use (Venkatesh et al, 2012). Recently, Venkatesh et al (2012) extended the UTAUT. Their UTAUT2 model incorporates three constructs into UTAUT namely, hedonic motivation, price value and habit. Compared with UTAUT, the extensions proposed in UTAUT2 produce a substantial improvement in the variance explained in behavioural intention and technology use (Venkatesh et al, 2012). Nevertheless, this approach of increasing the predictive power through the consideration of additional explanatory variables has led to increased complexity and lack of parsimony of the aforementioned frameworks.

Within the second framework, a person’s personality is at the centre of his or her acceptance of technology. According Fishbein’s & Ajen’s (1975) TRA, readiness is a mental state of attitude related to predispositions towards new situations or innovations. Positive or negative past experiences in<sup>fl</sup>uence the willingness of individuals to accept or to reject innovations. However, people may have simultaneously both positive and negative beliefs about technology-based products and services. For instance, Meuter et al (2003) suggested that 55% of Americans suffer from some degree of technophobia. In other words, many individuals avoid technology if they are not comfortable with, and not ready to use, the technology. In the same vein, Mick & Fournier (1998) focused on technology and concluded that con<sup>fl</sup>icting attitudes about technology may co-exist simultaneously. Thus, it seems that the meaning of technology for any person arises out of a process of contrast and similarity.

Rogers (2003) suggested an alternative framework, termed ‘Diffusion of Innovations Theory’ (DoI) as an attempt to associate readiness with attitude formation and individual adoption characteristics. Rogers focused on ‘diffusion’ and the ‘time it takes’ for an innovation or technology to become accepted. According to DoI, the decision to adopt an innovation is a function of <sup>fi</sup>ve distinctive innovation attributes, namely: Relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability. Furthermore, the theory led to a bell shaped curve within which individuals would fall into one of <sup>fi</sup>ve distinct classes, that is ‘innovators’, ‘early adopters’, ‘early majority’ ‘late majority’, and ‘laggards’. However, Rogers’s (2003) framework does not focus speci<sup>fi</sup>cally on technologically based innovations.

Based on Rogers’ DoI theory, Parasuraman (2000) and Parasuraman & Colby (2001) reinforced the importance of belief towards TR and investigated how experience contributes to belief and predisposition in attitude formation. TR refers to ‘people’s propensity to embrace and use new technologies to accomplish goals in home life and at work (Parasuraman, 2000, p. 308). It is a combination of positive and negative technology-related beliefs and will vary from one individual to the next. These beliefs can be categorized into four dimensions: optimism and innovativeness being the contributors that increase a person’s TR; the other two dimensions – discomfort and insecurity – are inhibitors that suppress TR. The TR construct explicitly acknowledges technology may trigger both positive and negative feelings (Mick & Fournier, 1998). However, Parasuraman (2000) suggests that one or the other of these feelings will exhibit relative dominance in the individual (see Appendix A).

Work by Parasuraman (2000) and Parasuraman & Colby (2001) resulted in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c index, the TRI, which supports the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of individuals into <sup>fi</sup>ve distinct types. Type formation is analogous to the classes identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Rogers, and is based on the various combinations of both positive (contributing) and negative (inhibiting) beliefs regarding technology.

Taylor et al (2002) and Tsikriktsis (2004) have con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed the validity and internal reliability of the TRI (Parasuraman, 2000) in diverse settings. The TR model has proven reliable through rigorous empirical tools, particularly in the business marketing domain, where research focuses on identifying segments of the market that are likely to adopt new technologies, such as online services (Massey et al, 2007), self-service technologies (Liljander et al, 2006) and online insurance (Taylor et al, 2002) among others. Caison et al (2008) modi<sup>fi</sup>ed the TRI construct in a study with nursing and medical students and made it more relevant for applications in a healthcare setting rather than for applications in consumer settings.

## Technology readiness in healthcare

Various factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence the physicians’ readiness towards computer acceptance. Campbell et al (2001) investigated the readiness of rural USA Missouri physicians to engage in telemedicine and found that many physicians felt that technology threatened their practice and that they resented the imposition of the new technology. Some, however, viewed the technology as an opportunity and enjoyed being connected via the project to a prominent university.

Yarbrough & Smith (2007) in their review reported that based on extant research on barriers to physician technology acceptance, it can be concluded that time/practicerelated issues, organizational issues, personal issues and system-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics in<sup>fl</sup>uence a physician’s acceptance of a new technology. Zhiping & Lopez (2008) found that the doctors’ perceived threat to professional autonomy is a salient outcome belief negatively affecting their acceptance of technology. Doctors may perceive technology as a threat to their professional autonomy, as technology may, to a certain degree, codify knowledge possessed by them. It seems plausible that medical staff may need to be convinced of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of technology in order to overcome perceived threats. Sykes et al (2011) found that physicians who were better connected to their peers – that is, other physicians - for advice on their work used EMR less than those who were less connected.

As already discussed, TR conceptualizes beliefs about technology and classi<sup>fi</sup>es individuals across a continuous tendency spectrum, from ‘technology pioneers’ (i.e., technology-ready individuals) to ‘technology gloomy’ (i.e., technology-unready individuals) persons. It is expected, for instance, that highly optimistic medical staff will use ICT technologies more frequently and try to use a variety of innovative functions as well as basic functions. Medical staff with high innovativeness levels will feel comfortable using technology and require little proof of the outcomes of the technology; being innovative means being experimental and having a tendency to try different things. Medical staff with a sense of insecurity are expected to be sceptical about new technologies and subsequently feel uncomfortable with them. Consequently, they become suspicious of new functions and reduce trials to accept and use them. Medical staff with high discomfort levels will use technology-based products and services less frequently than originally intended.

Prior research suggests distinct differences in the pro-<sup>fi</sup>les of technology adopters. Parasuraman (2000) and Parasuraman & Colby (2001) offer a typology of individuals, based on the various combinations of both positive (contributing) and negative (inhibiting) beliefs regarding technology. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, they describe <sup>fi</sup>ve types of technology adopters in the U.S., namely: (a) Explorers, (b) Pioneers, (c) Sceptics, (d) Paranoids and (e) Laggards. In terms of the segmentation pro<sup>fi</sup>le, prior studies have produced mixed results. Tsikriktsis (2004) in a U.K. study identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four segments with Paranoids excluded. Victorino et al (2009) in a study of U.S. hotel users pro<sup>fi</sup>led three segments: Innovators, Paranoids and Laggards. However, Massey et al (2007) successfully identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the <sup>fi</sup>ve segments found by Parasuraman & Colby (2001).

Although the medical staff’s professional training may differ from the training of technology users of other industries, there is no theoretical or empirical evidence supporting that data from medical staff produce TR segments that are different from the data of populations of other industries. Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>rst research hypothesis is:

## <sup>H1:</sup> Data from medical staff working in hospitals will yield the same five TR clusters found by Parasuraman and Colby.

Parasuraman’s (2000) model is also applied in technology acceptance research. The technology readiness and acceptance model (Lin et al, 2007) represents the latest contribution to explain how personality dimensions (assessed through TRI) may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the way people interact and use technology (assessed through TAM). Lin et al (2007) in their research regarding the integration of TR into TAM found that optimism about technology and innovativeness leads to higher perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness of e-service while insecurity and discomfort are negatively related to perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness. Lam et al (2008) in their study investigating the effects of the dimensions of TR on technology acceptance found a positive effect of optimism and negative effects of discomfort and insecurity concerning actual use of the Internet. Massey et al (2007) used the TR construct to segment consumers in the online <sup>fi</sup>eld based on their positive or negative attitude to technology. The aforementioned empirical studies show that the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed segments vary in their usage requirements and ease of use of new technologies. Thus, it is important to investigate empirically as to whether different TR groups develop different behaviours in terms of computer use. As different TR segments have different levels of optimism, innovativeness, discomfort and insecurity, then this is to be tested:

## <sup>H2a:</sup> TR segments of medical staff differ in terms of computer use.

In the same vein, positive predispositions towards ICT such as optimism and innovativeness may in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals to develop positive attitudes towards ICT knowledge and negative predispositions such as discomfort and insecurity may well result in negative attitudes towards ICT knowledge. ICT knowledge (i.e., how much medical staff know about computers) has been found to in<sup>fl</sup>uence intention to use health information systems indirectly, through perceived ease of use (e.g., Melas et al, 2011). Therefore, it makes sense to investigate empirically if TR segments differ in terms of computer knowledge; thus, the following hypothesis is to be tested:

## <sup>H2b:</sup> TR segments of medical staff differ in terms of computer knowledge.

Both TR and information systems research about people– technology interactions suggest that different individuals will have different technology needs due to their underlying beliefs. Lin & Hsieh (2006) described seven dimensions (functionality, enjoyment, security, assurance, design, convenience and customization) that constitute customer expectations of service quality within self-service technologies. The researchers have found that the TR construct had an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the perceived quality and adoption behaviour of the individual with regard to selfservice technologies (Lin & Hsieh, 2006). In the same vein, Zhu et al (2007) suggested that TR in<sup>fl</sup>uences the relationship between two design elements of self-service technologies, namely comparative information and interactivity and perceived control (i.e., the subjective assessment of control over a task in an environment).

Existing evidence suggests that not all medical staff use computer technology in the same way. For instance, in a study about hand-held clinical decision support tools in clinical settings, Yu et al (2007) found different patterns of use among the doctors studied. It is plausible that medical staffs’ preferences concerning the capabilities of medical computing systems (i.e., ICT feature demands) may vary according to their general tendency to approach or avoid technology innovations. User groups that hold high levels of TR drivers are willing to try new technology and more sophisticated things compared with user groups that hold high levels of TR inhibitors. Thus it makes sense to test empirically to see if TR segments differ in terms of ICT feature demands:

<sup>H2c:</sup> TR segments of medical staff differ in terms of computer feature demands.

## Overview of the study’s aims and hypotheses

The study aimed <sup>fi</sup>rstly to determine the number of different segments of healthcare professionals working in Greek hospitals, based on TR. Because TR is an individualspeci<sup>fi</sup>c and system independent construct as opposed to a system-speci<sup>fi</sup>c construct of usefulness and ease of use, investigating the readiness of healthcare providers towards general ICT system tools aiming towards supporting clinical tasks could be a starting point for understanding medical staff TR. Thus there is a need for validation and elaboration of the TR scale in a new context such as that of healthcare. It is expected that data from medical staff will produce the same TR segments, originally identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Parasuraman & Colby (2001). Secondly, because existing studies suggest that the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed TR segments vary in their usage requirements and ease of use of new technologies, it was aimed to test whether, and to what extent, TR segments of medical staff differ in terms of computer use, computer knowledge and computer feature demands.

Finally, we need to stress that results presented herein refer to relative young medical staff (under 55 years old). Although socio-demographic characteristics (e.g., age) are seldom considered as ICT adoption factor (Gagnon et al, 2012), recent research results suggest that the adoption of any ICT system in the healthcare during the 2002–2011 decade increased more for physicians aged 55 or younger compared with those older than 55 (Decker et al, 2012). However, considering the persuasive role of older medical staff within healthcare organization, we expect to some extent that our results re<sup>fl</sup>ect the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of older medical staff on younger medical staff; observed behaviours of older medical staff may in<sup>fl</sup>uence younger medical staff to emulate those behaviours.

## Materials and methods

## Sampling method

A preliminary questionnaire was designed and sent out for review to <sup>fi</sup>ve experts who had practical and academic experience with ICT in healthcare. This phase was used to clarify the wording, content and general layout of the survey instrument. Based on expert feedback, minor modi<sup>fi</sup>cations were made to the wording of the ICT use and ICT feature demand scales, leading to the improvement of the wording of the questionnaire. The latest questionnaire was used in the survey.

The survey was carried out via personal interviews, involving 1015 individuals (doctors and nurses) who were ICT users, from 14 main hospitals in four regions of Greece. Healthcare professionals (medical doctors or nursing staff) were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the personnel department of each hospital. An ‘ICT user’ is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a healthcare professional that uses a computer system to support his/ her daily clinical work. Complete lists of all individuals who use ICT applications were made available. The questionnaire was given to all individuals and then an interview was conducted.

Out of a total population of 1015 ICT users (doctors and nurses), 604 answered the questionnaire, marking a response rate of about 59.5%. ICT users were given the following explanation for the purposes of the study: ‘This is an effort to combine research into factors affecting ICT use and ICT feature demands in clinical settings. Your participation is not obligatory; you will answer a questionnaire without <sup>fi</sup>lling in anything that will identify you, or your department. The results will be used to better understand the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence computer use in healthcare’. In an effort to assess the possible impact of response bias, 40 randomly selected non-respondents were contacted directly by telephone and their input was requested. Twenty of them complied with the request. Comparison of respondents and non-respondents indicated that they did not differ with respect to any of the variables of interest in the present study.

The sample consisted of 364 males (60.3%) and 240 females (39.7%) with mean age 36.45 years (SD = 7.9 years). Average tenure in years was 5 (SD = 6.2 years). There were 534 doctors (88.4%) and 70 nurses (11.5%). The percentage of medical personnel was 88.4%. The speciality of a medical doctor was 35.6% surgery, 38.6% pathology, 7.5% laboratory specialties and 6.8% general medicine. Medical doctors reported a mean of 12.52 (SD = 10.8) hours of hands-on use of a computer per week. Nursing personnel reported a mean of 7.04 (SD = 9.9) hours of hands-on use of a computer per week. Average hours of hand-on use of computer per week was 11.88 (SD = 10.8) for the total sample.

## Measurement of constructs

All the constructs included in the analysis were based on multi-item scales whose psychometric properties are well established. Because participants were Greek-speaking, all the scales used were <sup>fi</sup>rst translated into Greek, following the procedure recommended by Brislin (1981). The speci-<sup>fi</sup>c measures used in the analysis, along with sample items of the relevant constructs, are outlined.

Technology readiness Thirty-six TRI items were used (Parasuraman, 2000). Items were split into four component scales: (a) optimism (10 items), (b) innovativeness (7 items), (c) discomfort (10 items) and (d) insecurity (9 items). The responses to all 36 items were made on 5-point Likert-type scales (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree).

TRI was originally designed to ascertain the readiness of consumers to adopt new commercial technologies. Following the example of Caison et al (2008), several minor modi<sup>fi</sup>cations were made to the original 36-item instrument. For instance, item 2 from the original optimism subscale ‘Products and services that use the newest technologies are much more convenient to use’ was presented as ‘New technologies are much more convenient to use’. Item 6 from the original innovativeness subscale ‘You enjoy the challenge of <sup>fi</sup>guring out high-tech gadgets’ was presented as ‘You enjoy the challenge of <sup>fi</sup>guring out new technology’, item 3 from the discomfort scale ‘There is no such thing as a manual for a high-tech product or service that is written in plain language’ was presented as ‘There is no such thing as a technology manual that is written in plain language’.

Preliminary results from exploratory factor analysis (EFA) of the 36 items comprising the TRI (see Appendix B) resulted in the extraction of four meaningful factors con-<sup>fi</sup>rming the original factor structure reported in previous studies (Parasuraman, 2000; Tsikriktsis, 2004). Cronbach’s alpha coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of internal reliability for the four components were: Optimism: 0.87; Innovativeness: 0.83; Discomfort: 0.75; Insecurity: 0.84. Coef<sup>fi</sup>cient alpha for all the 36 items of the TRI was 0.83.

The problems found, concerning the high cross-loadings of all four dimensions, as well as the lack of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of the four-factor structure of the TRI, arising in the CFA analysis reported also in many studies in the literature, were resolved (see Appendix D).

ICT use Ten items of the computer use scale developed by Cork et al (1998) were used to assess the ICT use attribute. This instrument has been widely used in healthcare informatics evaluation studies such as Richards et al (2005). Each item listed a speci<sup>fi</sup>c task undertaken along with <sup>fi</sup>ve options (1 - ‘never perform this task’ to 5 - ‘always use computer for this task’) for the respondent to indicate the relative frequency with which he or she personally uses a computer for this task. Sample items were ICT1 - ‘Obtaining advice on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c patient’s diagnosis or therapy’, ICT2 - ‘Documenting patient information (e.g., progress notes)’.

The scores for the ICT use factor were obtained by the CFA scores, which were generated using the Mplus (version 5.21) software (Muthén & Muthén, 2007).

ICT knowledge Five items of the computer knowledge (CW) scale as developed by Cork et al (1998) were adopted, in order to assess the computer knowledge attribute. For each item, using a 3-point response scale: 1 - I don’t understand the distinction at all, 2 - I have a general appreciation of the distinction but could not de<sup>fi</sup>ne it, 3 - I can de<sup>fi</sup>ne the distinction precisely. ICT feature demands Fifteen items of the ICT feature demands scale developed by Cork et al (1998) were used to assess the ICT feature demand attribute. Each item presented a feature or capability of a medical computing system. ## Statistical method considerations - analytical strategy Statistical method considerations - analytical strategy

All factor analyses were performed using the Mplus (version 5.21) software (Muthén & Muthén, 2007) and the robust weighted least square (WLSMV) estimator (Flora & Curran, 2004). Several statistics were employed in order to assess model <sup>fi</sup>tness: (a) RMSEA: 0 = an exact <sup>fi</sup>t, <0.05 = a close <sup>fi</sup>t, 0.05 0.08 = a fair <sup>fi</sup>t, 0.08 0.10 = a mediocre <sup>fi</sup>t, and > 0.10 = a poor <sup>fi</sup>t (Mplus also computes a 90% con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval around RMSEA); (b) CFI: best if above 0.9; (c) TLI: best if above 0.9; (d) Akaike information criterion (AIC). For model comparisons, smaller values in AIC represent a better <sup>fi</sup>t of the model.

To avoid problems associated with common method variance often found in cross-sectional survey research, several steps were taken described in Podsakoff et al (2003). Fornell & Larcker’s (1981) procedures were followed to evaluate the measurement model’s reliability and validity (see Appendix H).

Owing to the hierarchical structure of the present data (medical professionals based within 14 hospitals; hospitals nested in four geographic areas), the proportion of systematic between group variation for each latent variable in the model was estimated, following Muthén (1994) and Muthén & Satorra (1995). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the intraclass correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient (ICC) was estimated as well as the size of the design effect (DEFF). In practice, multilevel modelling may provide few bene<sup>fi</sup>ts when ICCs are less than 0.05. In addition, a design effect greater than two indicates that the clustering in the data needs to be taken into account during estimation (Muthén & Satorra, 1995).

In order to identify clusters of individuals that have similar values on TR dimensions, the latent pro<sup>fi</sup>le analysis (LPA) was employed. Model parameters were estimated using the Robust Maximum Likelihood (MLR) estimator as implemented in Mplus (Muthén, 2001). LPA postulates that the observed data are assumed to arise from a number of a priori unknown segments (latent classes) that are mixed in unknown proportions. The goal of LPA is to identify the smallest number of latent classes that adequately describe the associations among the TR beliefs.

To determine the number of latent classes that should be used, in LPA, several statistics were employed (Nylund et al, 2007): the AIC and sample-size-adjusted BIC (sBIC), where lower values of s-BIC and AIC are indicative of better model <sup>fi</sup>t. The entropy statistic was used to account for separation in the estimated posterior probabilities and to adjust for over-parameterization compared with a single segment model.

In the present research the LPA was conducted in an exploratory fashion. A single-class model was examined <sup>fi</sup>rst, and classes were added until no further improvements were observed. Finally, the bootstrap likelihood ratio test was used (BLRT; Nylund et al, 2007) to gain con<sup>fi</sup>dence concerning the correct number of classes. The BLRT compares the estimated model with a model with one class fewer than the estimated model. The P-value obtained in the test is an approximation of the probability that the data have been generated by the model with one less class; thus, a low P-value indicates that the model with one less class is rejected in favour of the estimated model.

Latent class pro<sup>fi</sup>les and external validation - Hypothesis 2a, 2b and 2c On the basis of the LPA <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the <sup>fi</sup>ve classes model external validity was examined; that is, the homogeneity and the distinctiveness of the classes in terms of gender, age, tenure, specialty, hours using a computer per week, ICT use and knowledge, ICT feature demands, self-reported ICT use performance was tested. The results indicate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between and within the TR classes with regard to external variables (Table 5).

To test the hypothesis (H2a, H2 and H2c) that TR segments of medical staff will differ in computer use, computer knowledge and computer feature demands, an ANOVA was conducted. The hypothesis was also tested for gender and specialty differences using the Kruskal–Wallis non-parametric test.

Table 5 presents ANOVA results: means, standard deviations and statistical comparisons among the <sup>fi</sup>ve TR classes. According to Table 5, the results show that there are statistical differences between classes in ICT knowledge, ICT feature demands, hours of use per week as well as ICT use performance, but not in the general use of ICT.− Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, classes differ in ICT knowledge, with Pioneers having the higher score (M = 0.47) and Paranoids having the lower score (M = 0.12).

In addition, the within-subjects analysis (with Games-Howell post-hoc comparisons), that compare the mean differences across classes, show as an example that Pioneers spend on average 19.28 h per week using a PC while Laggards only 5.93 h per week and Sceptics somewhere in between at 13.75 h. The between-subjects analysis shown in Table 5 illustrates that TR classes do, in fact, vary in terms of hours using PC per week, ICT knowledge and selfreported computer use performance.

## Discussion

According to the survey results, the TR pro<sup>fi</sup>les of the medical staff in clinical settings match the TR pro<sup>fi</sup>les to be found in the general population. The empirical results provide support for the existence of <sup>fi</sup>ve distinct groups, in line with Parasuraman & Colby’s (2001) taxonomy of TR. According to the results presented here, although classes differ in terms of hours using a computer per week, selfreported ICT performance and ICT knowledge, no differences were found however in terms of ICT use in clinical settings. A possible explanation for this could be the fact that ICT measures computer use in work<sup>fl</sup>ow and healthcare daily operations but not the general purpose use of ICT. This is in line with the theory of Mick & Fourier (1998) regarding paradoxical attitudes, when healthcare providers may use technologies in their daily lives but at the same time have concerns and raise resistance when healthcare technologies are to be used.

While several studies in the literature have examined the role that individual differences play in the adoption of new technologies, very few empirical studies have actually focused on the link between TR and computer use and knowledge, in general, and in clinical settings in particular.

TR classes hold different beliefs about technology; thus, differences were looked for in terms of ICT use, ICT knowledge and ICT feature demands. The empirical results presented in Table 5 provide support for differences in average hours of hand-on use of computer per week, ICT knowledge and self-reported computer performance, but not for ICT use and ICT feature demands. No differences were found between classes in terms of gender, age, tenure and specialty.

An important issue in the present study is the resolution of the problem of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of the four-factor structure of the TRI emerging in the CFA analysis, despite the wellde<sup>fi</sup>ned EFA structure, as well as the mediocre model <sup>fi</sup>t. These problems have been reported in the literature and they were also found in the present study. Using ESEM and following the recommendations of Polites et al (2012) and Wright et al (2012), the construct of the TR scale had an acceptable <sup>fi</sup>t and the second order of the TRI construct was deemed satisfactory.

A key <sup>fi</sup>nding of the present research is that although Parasuraman & Colby’s taxonomy (2001) applies to medical staff in clinical settings, the size of the clusters is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different compared with previous studies in the literature. The empirical results suggest the following cluster sizes: Explorers (3.5%), Pioneers (4.1%), Sceptics (84.9%), Paranoids (3.0%) and Laggards (4.5%) which are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from the U.S.A. pro<sup>fi</sup>le reported by Parasuraman & Colby (2001) ((a) Explorers (16%), (b) Pioneers (27%), (c) Sceptics (21%), (d) Paranoids (20%) and (e) Laggards (14%)) or the U.K. pro<sup>fi</sup>le reported by Tsikriktsis (2004) ((a) Explorers (27%), (b) Pioneers (32%), (c) Sceptics (20%), (d) Laggards (21%)). In the present sample Sceptics are almost 85% of the population.

A possible explanation for this could be the fact that the current data in question were based on a rather young sample of medical staff (M = 36.45 years, SD = 7.89 years), with almost 90% of the respondents being under 50 years old and with most of the participants being physicians (88.4%). Generally, physicians enjoy a high level of prestige and autonomy. However, relatively young physicians tend to be cognizant of the image they project as it relates to their professional status and their interactions within their social network (Yi et al, 2006). Considering that the Greek culture is characterized as uncertainty avoidant and collectivistic (Hofstede, 2001), that prefers to avoid, or feels threatened by ambiguous or risky situations and to conform to the norms compared with an individualistic society (i.e., U.S., or U.K. society) that is inclined to make individual choices and therefore seemingly more likely to be innovative and adopt new ideas, the present results may re<sup>fl</sup>ect these cultural differences. For a relative study examining cultural differences in the context of TR please, see the study of Elliott et al (2008).

The results reported here are in line with studies suggesting that medical staff in general are reluctant to give a positive response to the implementation of an IS that interferes with their traditional routines, with several barriers to technology acceptance actually being cited in the literature such as Bhattacherjee & Hikmet (2007) and Yarbrough & Smith (2007). It was found that the majority of medical staff is sceptical about technology. That is, that although they may be ‘evolutionary’ in their thinking, they are driven by a sense of practicality and need to be convinced of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of technology. Sceptics seem to hold both negative and positive beliefs about technology (see Figure 1). They support ICT use in clinical settings without feeling obliged to use it themselves. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is in line with recent theorizing and research results reported by van Offenbeek et al (2013). These authors proposed a two-factor framework and suggested that ambivalent (i.e., opposing forces existing simultaneously within a person) user behaviours, such the ones observed for the Sceptics in our sample, are common, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the fact that acceptance of technology and support/resistance of technology can be conceptualized as two distinct behavioural dimensions.

Based on the extant literature, each TR segment adopts various new technologies at different time intervals (i.e., Explorers before Pioneers, Pioneers before Sceptics, etc. (Parasuraman & Colby 2001; Tsikriktsis, 2004). A strategic programme for the introduction of new technologies in clinical staff should initially be targeted towards the Explorer group. Explorers are the <sup>fi</sup>rst ones who adopt new technologies. These individuals could subsequently serve as change agents in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the rest of the members of the internal social network (Rogers, 2003; Zampetakis & Moustakis, 2007). If a strategic programme is aimed, however, at the ‘Pioneers’ group, because of their high discomfort and insecurity, they will most probably not adopt new technology in the introductory stage.

The current <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that hospital and IT managers could use different approaches in order to categorize users, in order to improve new ICT adoption and use in clinical settings and in order to avoid mandatory use that might minimize practitioners’ believes about technology.

Managerial interventions should target to promote the adoption of a new ICT system tactically and to minimize problems associated with systems use. Furthermore, in light of van Offenbeek et al (2013) research, managers should also pay attention to ambivalent behaviours such as ‘use and resistance’ and ‘support and non-use’ during clinical information systems (CIS) implementation, as these represent independent dimensions and may require more differentiated interventions. This is particular important for the Sceptics which are the vast majority representing 84.9% of the population in the sample.

Moreover, developments such as evidence-based practices (EBPs) (Melas et al, 2012) should be considered, as they represent a strong commitment to the application of CIS to support: (a) assessment of information from knowledge sources, (b) analysis of medical data, and (c) diffusion of information to patients and colleagues (Georgiou, 2001). Practitioners should be aware of the TR classi<sup>fi</sup>cation schema. Not only because it helps them understand the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of TR in ICT acceptance, but also because their role as societal opinion leaders could assist in increasing the diffusion of new technologies into the healthcare system, and other segments of society, thus performing a meaningful public service.

## Study limitations and directions for future research

This research relied on data obtained from Greek hospitals with a relative small percentage of older medical staff in the sample (almost 90% of the respondents were under 50 years old). Future research should incorporate stuff from other cultures and validate the TR scale and segmentation pro<sup>fi</sup>le among mature medical staff (e.g., over 50 years of age). Future research should also incorporate multilevel designs, since the medical staff in question are located within directorates or departments. Moreover, future research should examine whether the results presented herein could be replicated in a sample of non-adopters, that is, a control group of medical staff that doesn't use any system. The results should be used with caution when considering a speci<sup>fi</sup>c IS, as ICT is considered as a general concept in the study. Future research should validate TR segments when a speci<sup>fi</sup>c IS is considered. Finally, as the data are cross-sectional in nature, alternative relationships might also exist; thus, future research should be longitudinal.

This study analyses the TR pro<sup>fi</sup>les of the medical staff in clinical settings and investigates their beliefs concerning technology, in terms of ICT use, current ICT knowledge and ICT feature demands. It reveals signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in terms of computer use and knowledge between the different TR segments, based upon a sample of medical staff. The study focuses on medical staff in clinical settings. Despite the widely acknowledged importance of TR in consumer research (Parasuraman & Grewal, 2000;

## Explanation of the four dimensions of beliefs

Optimism is a general dimension that captures speci<sup>fi</sup>c feelings, suggesting that ‘technology is good’. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it captures a positive view of technology and a belief that it offers people increased control, <sup>fl</sup>exibility and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in their lives. Innovativeness re<sup>fl</sup>ects the extent to which an individual believes that he or she is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to use new technology-based products or services. Discomfort represents the extent to which people believe that new technology-based products tend to be exclusive rather than inclusive of all kinds of people; individuals high in the discomfort component of TR believe technology is not really designed for use by ordinary people and is simply too complicated. Lastly, the insecurity dimension refers to technology distrust due to security or privacy reasons. The insecurity dimension focuses on speci<sup>fi</sup>c aspects of technology-based transactions, rather than on a lack of ease with technology in general.

Several high cross-loadings have been found for all four dimensions (see Appendix C). This is consistent with the EFA results presented in the studies of Parasuraman (2000, Table 3 - p. 315) and Tsikriktsis (2004, Appendix B - p. 50).

Furthermore, several past studies have reported problems with the four-factor structure of the TRI. For instance, the four proposed dimensions of TRI could not be con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in the study of Liljander et al (2006), in line with previous results reported by Taylor et al (2002). Thus, although TR seems to have an apparently well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned EFA structure, it cannot be represented adequately within a CFA approach. For instance, CFA results of TR presented by Parasuraman (2000, p. 314) (with four latent constructs representing the four TR subscales and the corresponding items for each subscale speci<sup>fi</sup>ed as manifest variables) indicated that <sup>fi</sup>t statistics were below prevailing standards of an acceptable model <sup>fi</sup>t (i.e., goodness-of-<sup>fi</sup>t index = 0.91; non-normed <sup>fi</sup>t index = 0.87; CFI = 0.88; root mean square residual = 0.05). In the present study, CFA results also resulted in mediocre model <sup>fi</sup>t: [ χ<sup>2</sup> (588, N = 604) = 1193.86, P<0.001; (RMSEA = 0.41 (90% CI: 0.038–0.045); (CFI = 0.880; (TLI = 0.790].

One possible explanation for the factor analytic <sup>fi</sup>ndings of TR to date is that the models previously examined in past studies are built on speci<sup>fi</sup>cations that are based on a ‘simple structure’ CFA, that is, they do not include crossfactor loadings. It has been argued that such an approach is unnecessarily restrictive and that cross-factor loadings are sometimes a necessary element of model testing (Asparouhov & Muthen, 2009; Marsh́ et al, 2009).

To overcome problems associated with the high crossloadings in the TR structure reported in previous research and which have also been found in the present study, the ESEM approach was used (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2009; Marsh et al, 2009). Like EFA, ESEM also permits the estimation of the factor loadings of all items in all factors, so that the problem of <sup>fi</sup>xing the cross-loadings to zero disappears. When the loading matrix of the population includes cross-loadings, ESEM recovers this matrix better than CFA does and also is not subject to its parameter estimation bias. As such, ESEM may be the most appropriate model for the TR. The results of the ESEM analyses indicated that the theoretical constructs used from the TR scale had an acceptable <sup>fi</sup>t: [ χ<sup>2</sup> (492, N = 604) = 941.29, P<0.001; RMSEA = 0.039 (90% CI: 0.035-0.043); CFI = 0.955; TLI = 0.914] (see Appendix B).

Finally, following the recommendations of Polites et al (2012) and Wright et al (2012), TRI was considered as a second-order construct with re<sup>fl</sup>ective dimensions; TRI is a concept that is manifested by its four dimensions (namely optimism, innovativeness, discomfort and insecurity). The latent variable scores have been used, which were produced from the ESEM analysis, for each of the TRI dimensions as indicators of the second-order TRI construct. The <sup>fi</sup>t of the second-order TRI construct was deemed satisfactory: $[ \chi ^ { 2 }$ (2, N = 604) = 236.37, P<0.001; RMSEA = 0.028 (90% CI: 0.018–0.026); CFI = 0.985; TLI = 0.971].

## Appendix H

Fornell & Larcker’s (1981) procedures were followed to evaluate the measurement model’s reliability and validity. This involves the examination of individual indicator reliabilities (squared standardized loadings), the reliabilities for each construct’s composite of measures (i.e. internal consistency reliability) as well the measures’ convergent (AVE) and discriminant validity (Fornell-Larcker’s criterion). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, each indicator’s reliability was considered, whereby each indicator’s absolute standardized loading should be higher than 0.5 (Hair et al, 1998). Composite reliability does not assume that all indicators are equally reliable (such as Cronbach’s alpha. Convergent validity is established if the AVE for each factor accounts for 0.50 or more of the total variance (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988). An AVE value of 0.50 and higher indicates that the latent variable explains more than half of its indicators’ variance. Moreover, convergent validity is demonstrated if all items load signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly on their respective construct (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988). For the assessment of discriminant validity, the Fornell–Larcker criterion was used (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). The Fornell– Larcker criterion is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed if the AVE of each latent construct is greater than the latent construct’s highest squared correlation with any other latent construct (see also Segars, 1997).

## Appendix I

## Assessment of common method bias

The basic assumption of Harman’s one-factor test is that if a substantial amount of common method variance exists in the data, either a single factor will emerge or one general factor will account for the majority of the covariance among the variables. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, all the self-reported variables in an EFA with principal axis factoring and varimax rotation were entered. Ten factors emerged with eigenvalues greater than 1 and 60.58% of the variance explained. No single factor was dominant with the <sup>fi</sup>rst factor accounting for 17.83% of the variance. To further establish discriminant validity, a measurement model, which constrained the correlation between the factors to unity, was estimated. The observed chi-square $( \chi ^ { 2 } )$ value of this restricted model was large: 418.96 (d.f. = 20). The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ difference of the restricted and freely estimated models is: 418.96–42.29 = 376.67 (d.f. = 1, P<0.001). This highly signi-<sup>fi</sup>cant difference suggests that the constructs are distinct and that their underlying scales exhibit the property of discriminant validity. Thus, no common method variance was detected in this study. In summary, results indicate that common method effects are not a likely contaminant of the results observed in this investigation; furthermore, results provide evidence of the discriminant validity of all scales.
