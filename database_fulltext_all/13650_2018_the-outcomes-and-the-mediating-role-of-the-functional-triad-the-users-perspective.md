# The outcomes and the mediating role of the functional triad: The users' perspective

## Abstract

B.J. Fogg's Functional Triad shows the manner in which computing technologies can persuade people by playing 3 different functional roles, namely, as tools, media, or social actors. However, the effects of user perceptions of these 3 functional roles are largely unknown. We advance Fogg's framework by developing a conceptual model to explain how a feature of a computing technology (ie, the trade‐off transparency feature of a recommendation agent [RA], which interactively demonstrates the trade‐offs among product attribute values) can result in certain outcomes by shaping the beliefs of individuals regarding the 3 functional roles. We examine the effects of the perceived Functional Triad on the following 3 outcomes: (1) persuading users to use an RA (intention to use), (2) persuading users to follow the advice of the RA (recommendation adherence), and (3) persuading users to recommend the RA to others (recommendation to friends). We conducted a laboratory experiment to manipulate 4 levels of trade‐off transparency, thereby creating an adequate amount of variations for the perceived Functional Triad. A total of 160 participants were recruited from a large university in North America. Although designers could control the technology design aspects, these designs may not accomplish the intended effects on users, who have their own perceptions. This study contributes to existing literature by simultaneously evaluating the 3 different outcomes of the Functional Triad from the perspective of users.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Although usability is an important design feature of an e‐commerce website, it is not enough to attract users to linge on a website or return to it (Kim & Fesenmaier, 2008; Schaffer, 2009). The next wave in website design is leveraging persuasive technologies (Oinas‐Kukkonen & Harjumaa, 2008, 2009; Schaffer, 2009). According to the fields of psy chology and communication, persuasion is the attempt to guide others toward the adoption of some behaviour and belief preferred by the persuader and to do so through a reasoning process (Reardon, 1981). Research has shown that the Internet has become one of the most persuasive media for marketers to influence consumers' behaviour (Anderson, 2006; Hwang, Gretzel, Xiang, & Fesenmaier, 2006; Kim & Fesenmaier, 2008; Tapscott & Williams, 2006). For example, marketers may leverage the Internet and online recommendation agent (RA) technologies to tailor specific products to meet each user's needs with the intent to persuade online users to accept their purchase recommenda tions (Doherty & Ellis‐Chadwick, 2010; Wang & Doong, 2010).

Online RA is one type of persuasive technology, which is an interactive computing system designed to influence people's beliefs and behaviours (Fogg, 2002). Fogg (1998, 2003) has provided a seminal conceptual framework that illustrates the different functional roles that a computing technology can play: as tools, media, or social actors. As tools, technologies can increase people's ability to perform a task more effectively (Johnson, Marakas, & Palmer, 2006, 2008; Marakas, Johnson, & Palmer, 2000). As media, technologies can use interactivity or simulation to create persuasive experiences (eg, exploring causal relationships). As social actors, technologies can exert their influences by playing anthropomorphic roles, such as coach or assistant (Johnson et al., 2006, 2008; Marakas et al., 2000). The Functional Triad summarizes the fundamental ways in which people can view or respond to persuasive technologies The Functional Triad accounts for the various perspectives and theories on persuasion prevalent since the days of Aristotle, while at the same time stressing the persuasive potential of computing technologies (Fogg, 2002).

The Functional Triad provides insights into the roles played by persuasive technologies. However, the Functiona Triad is designer‐ and system‐centred, as it projects the view that designers are omniscient (Davis, 2009; Johnson, 2004); that is, potential users are almost ignored in the design process of persuasive technologies (Davis, 2009). Thus, previous studies are silent on the effect of users' perceptions of the Functional Triad and the Triad's mediating roles between computing technology features and the outcomes of using them. However, studying users' perceptions is important in information systems (IS) literature (Davis, 1989; Hsu, Lee, & Straub, 2012; Iivari, Isomäki, & Pekkola, 2010; Wang, Schneider, & Valacich, 2015; Xu, Dinev, Smith, & Hart, 2011). For example, the technology acceptance model posits users' perceptions of ease of use and usefulness as being key factors driving IT usage (Davis, 1989). In addition, users' perceptions are also important in disciplines such as human‐computer interaction (Caroll, 2000, Xu et al., 2009, 2010; Xu, 2016; Argyris & Xu, 2016), design science (Brocke, Riedl, & Léger, 2011), requirement engineering (Shaw & Gaines, 1996), and usability engineering (Nielsen, 1993). Without understanding users' perceptions, we cannot know whether or not the design criteria considered as effective by designers will have their intended effect on users. Furthermore, without studying users' perceptions of these 3 functional roles, it is not clear whether designers should design a technology to be more like a tool, a medium, and/or as a social actor for the sake of persuasion

Surprisingly, to date, there has been no empirical study to investigate users' perceptions of the Functional Triad on IT adoption. However, there is a growing trend toward the use of persuasive technologies (Chatterjee & Price 2009; Jameson, 2013; Kraft, Drozd, & Olsen, 2009; Oinas‐Kukkonen & Harjumaa, 2008, 2009; Parmar, Keyson, & de Bont, 2009). This combination of circumstances makes the first objective of this study all the more important: to empirically examine the outcomes of the user‐held perceptions of these 3 functional roles

The second objective is to extend Fogg's (2003) conceptualization by positing the mediating effect of the perceived Eunctional Triad between computing technology features and outcomes. In this paper, the perceived Func tional Triad is proposed to refer to perceived tool functionality, perceived media functionality, and perceived socia actor functionality. To test the mediating effect of the perceived Functional Triad between technology features and outcomes, we present an illustrative example model using the trade‐off transparency (TOT) feature (ie, a feature that allows users to understand product attribute trade‐offs; Xu, Benbasat, & Cenfetelli, 2014) of an online RA. Online RAs recommend products for users and help users choose the “right fit” products (Ricci & Werthner, 2006–2007). Fogg gives the example of amazon.com; this firm attempts to persuade people to buy more products by providing recommendations and promotions based upon user preferences collected during their prior visits and combining this data with feedback from other consumers who ordered the same product. Similarly, an online RA interacts with users and attempts to persuade people to select the products that it recommends and implicitly persuades people and their friends to use the RA to select products. The TOT aspect of the RA interactively demonstrates the trade‐offs (eg, the faster processor of a laptop comes with a higher cost) among product attribute values (Xu et al., 2014), which is expected to enhance an RA's persuasion due to the TOT's various features.

We depart from previous studies by focusing on 3 types of outcomes to examine the efficacy of the perceived Functional Triad: (1) Persuading the users to accept the RA (intention to use the RA), (2) Persuading the users to accept the advice provided by the RA (adherence to the recommendation of the RA), a measure of user behaviours, and (3) Persuading the users to recommend the RA to others (recommendation to friends). Usage intention is fre quently used in IS literature as an outcome of IT utilization such as decision support systems (Kamis, Koufaris, & Stern 2008; Kamis, Stern, & Ladik, 2010), software applications (Chan & Teo, 2007), and e‐commerce websites (Cenfetelli & Schwarz, 2011). Recommendation to friends predicts customer loyalty and is instrumental in the company's profitable growth (Reichheld, 2003), the central purpose of persuasive design. While the intention to use the RA and recommend it to friends are subjective measures of outcomes, recommendation adherence represents an objective outcome.

To summarize, the 2 major research questions derived from the above discussion are as follows:

1. Does the perceived Functional Triad influence intention to use, recommendation adherence, and recommendation to friends?

2. Does the perceived Functional Triad mediate the effect of computing technology features on these outcomes?

By answering the above research questions, this study will make the following novel contributions. First, this study is the first of its kind to examine the impact of the perceived Functional Triad from the user's perspective. Second, it elucidates which aspects of the Functional Triad can significantly influence certain persuasion outcomes. Third, it investigates to what extent the perceived Functional Triad mediates the effect of an RA feature on the 3 persuasion outcomes.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews the literature and the theoretical foundation; Section 3 presents the hypotheses, Section 4 describes the study design and data analysis, and Section 5 conclude the paper by discussing the theoretical and practical implications of our research

## 2 | LITERATURE REVIEW AND THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS

First, we introduce Fogg's Functional Triad and point out that the Functional Triad is designer‐centred and does no consider users' perceptions of the tool, media, and social actor functional roles. Next, we define the Functional Triad by looking at it from the user's perspective. We then articulate the importance of the 3 outcome variables of the per ceived Functional Triad, and the relationship and differences among them.

## 2.1 | Fogg's Functional Triad and related literature

Fogg (1998, 2003) proposed that computing technologies can function in 3 fundamental ways: as tools, media, and social actors. The tool functionality is the first pillar of the Functional Triad. As a tool, the goal of persuasive technology is to improve task effectiveness by making activities more efficient to perform. As tools, computing technologies can influence and motivate people in specific ways (Fogg, 2002). For example, tools can provide information that enables better decisions (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007) and shapes a person's mental model (Fogg, 1998; Mutschler, 1990). In this aspect, the tool view of computing technologies is consistent with that in the IS literature. As a tool, technologies can be substitutes for labour and process information (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). In addition, technologies are th engineered artefact and are expected to do what its designers intend it to do (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001)

The second pillar of the Functional Triad is as media, whereby technology can provide compelling simulated experiences (Fogg, 2002). Technology as symbolic media uses symbols to convey information (eg, text, graphics, charts, and icons), or, as sensory media, it provides sensory information (eg, animation, audio, and video). Both media can influence people. In particular, technologies have distinctive capabilities to provide interactive experiences that moti vate and persuade. For instance, technologies can promote understanding of the cause/effect relationships, or moti vate people through a simulated experience.

The third functional pillar describes the role that computing technologies play as social actors. When people use interactive technologies, they often respond to it as though it were a living being (Fogg, 2002), which has been wel documented in the IS and marketing literature (eg, Al‐Natour & Benbasat, 2009; Qiu & Benbasat, 2009; Reeves & Nass, 1996). Fogg's social actor functionality is grounded on the “Computers as Social Actor” (CASA) paradigm (Reeves & Nass, 1996), which maintains that people attribute social characteristics to computing technology, particularly when it is perceived to possess features associated with human behaviour. Laboratory studies at Stanford Uni versity revealed that students who participated in these experiments responded to the computers much as the would respond to other human beings (Fogg, 1997). A computing technology can be considered a persuasive socia actor because it can establish social norms, act in accordance with social rules (eg, apologies), and form relationships with users (Fogg, 1998)

Although a computing technology or certain features of the technology can simultaneously play 3 roles in the Functional Triad, the roles that they play may not always be equal. A specific technology might play more of a role as a tool, but less so as a medium and social actor, or vice versa. Taking an arcade game as an example, it plays rela tively less role as a tool, but bigger roles as the other two (Fogg, 1998). In short, a computing technology could lie somewhere in the Functional Triad.

The Functional Triad has provided a useful framework for understanding the 3 roles that computing technologies can play to persuade people. Several studies have drawn on the Functional Triad as a conceptual framework to classify and evaluate persuasive technologies, eg, in motivating household energy conservation (Tan. 20o9) and a meta-anal: ysis on evaluating the persuasive technique used in promoting physical activity through the Internet (Zhu, 2007). In IS literature, the Eunctional Triad has been considered as “the first and so far most utilized conceptualization of persua: sive technology” (Oinas-Kukkonen & Hariumaa. 2009, p. 491), The Functional Triad has been applied to the develop ment of 28 design principles for persuasive system content and functionality (Oinas‐Kukkonen & Harjumaa, 2009), to explain why people interact with media and technology as with humans (Cugelman, Thelwall, & Dawes, 2009), to design a persuasive health information system to change users' social beliefs (Parmar et al., 2009), to identify persua: sive tactics utilized in e‐commerce Websites (Chu, Deng, & Chuang, 2014), and to develop design elements for fitness apps that can stimulate enhanced physical activity behaviour (Yoganathan & Kajanan, 2015). In particular, Cugelman et al. (2009) drew upon the Functional Triad to posit that models of human‐with‐human credibility (trust toward humans) can be applied to human‐with‐computer applications (trust toward computers). Considering a website as a computer application, they empirically found that the human credibility model better fit the data with the website than the computer credibility model, providing support to the social actor functionality of the Functional Triad.

Fogg's Functional Triad is congruent with literature about computing technologies (Johnson et al., 2006, 2008; Marakas et al., 2000). Johnson and his colleges found that people have preconceived beliefs about the social actor role and capabilities of computing technologies; interface design (eg, “social” interfaces) can affect how people form their beliefs about the social actor role of computing technologies. Users have the desire to associate social characteristics with a computing technology if it can respond to users based on that user's input (Marakas et al., 2000). Marakas et al. (2000) and Johnson et al. (2006, 2008) further theorized and empirically supported that users' perception of contro by the technology (eg, enforcing a set of behaviours) and knowledge and intelligence within the technology are features of social characteristics. Eor example, when interacting with a decision support system providing answers to specific decision‐making scenarios, users will perceive knowledge, purpose and/or intelligence within the technolog (Johnson et al., 2008). In summary, the perception of people regarding the social actor role of a computing technolog includes the dimensions of socialness, intelligence, and control (Johnson et al., 2008)

## 2.2 | Adaptation of Functional Triad to the users' perspective

However, Fogg's Functional Triad is a designer‐centred, system‐centred framework (Johnson, 2004) and is silent about users' perceptions. This is a limited view, as a designer's criteria might not necessarily be appreciated by users. Previous studies have demonstrated that designers often failed to predict the users' desires and preferences accurately (Fu, Jiang, Zhang, Zhang, & Gero, 2016; Kujala & Marjo, 2004) as designers' mental models about a product differ from that of the users' (Norman & Ortony, 2003). The reason is that designers use professional sense‐making instead of ordinary sense‐making that users use (Krippendorff 2005), and they might have different specific interests and goals (Nicolás, Aurisicchio, & PMA, 2013). As the potential discrepancy between designers' and users' goals is widely recognized, one of the important steps in system development is a usability test (Iivari et al., 2010). However, gathering users' perspectives is costly and time‐consuming, and requires rigorous research that is rarely implemented in real design projects (Fu et al., 2016).

Empirical studies confirmed the designer's and users' differences in both cognitive and conceptual ways. For example, Fu et al. (2016) compared the differences between users' and designers' perceptions of app interface design of smart household lighting and found that they differed in terms of visual appeal, interactivity, colour adjustment, and intuitiveness, among others. Feuerstack and Bertram (2015) revealed designers and users' different perspectives in the cognitive model and resulted visualization when using monitoring systems. Nicolás et al. (2013) found different preferences between users and designs for experiencing and eliciting emotions in product design. They explained that differences are the result of their different specific interests. Users would like to experience certain emotions from product design because they are pleasant, expected, and indicative of good product choice, but designers prefer cer tain emotions because they are of interest, appropriate to their designs, and those preference elicitations are per ceived as a responsibility. Kadoda (2000) discovered significant differences between designers and users in terms of the theorem proving assistants' features and usability. The difference is attributed to the possibility that the user is inexperienced with the tool, and/or not aware of the existence of some features. Johnson et al. (2006) rightl pointed out that users of computing technology may bring a variety of social norms and rules that they apply to their interactions with computing technology. As a result, computing technology has become associated with meanings and ideas not anticipated by their designers, and thus, empirical research about users' perception becomes importan (Johnson et al., 2008).

Even if designers and users share agreement in terms of the importance of the Functional Triad, the effects of th Functional Triad on different outcomes are still largely unknown. Therefore, we modify the Functional Triad from a designer's perspective to an end user's perspective.<sup>1</sup> We define perceived tool functionality as a user's perception that a target technology has the capability to improve the user's task effectiveness. We define perceived social actor func tionality as a user's perception that a target technology has human‐like features and/or capability to transmit informa tion so that users can establish a relationship with those technologies. Perceived media functionality is the perceive ability of a target technology that allows people to explore scenarios in an interactive way. One important type of computer simulation related to persuasive technologies is the simulated cause‐and‐effect scenario, which allows user to vary the inputs and observe the effects (Fogg, Cuellar, & Danielson, 2002; Hennessy & O'Shea, 1993). These effec tive cause‐and‐effect simulators can demonstrate the consequence of actions instantaneously (Alessi, 1991; de Jong, 1991; Hennessy & O'Shea, 1993). For example, a computer simulation can provide insight on how putting money aside on a regular basis (input) can lead to accumulated savings for retirement (output). Such computer simulations can influence user beliefs and behaviours by allowing people to explore causes and effects of situations interactively In addition, persuasive technologies providing people with interactive experiences can shape people's beliefs and behaviours (Fogg et al., 2002).

In the RA context, an RA should act beyond the productivity tool role to establish a social relationship with RA users during the recommendation process. More socially oriented interfaces will make the RA easier to use and understand, as well as more natural for users (Johnson et al, 2008), If an RA user feels that the interaction with the RA is similar to an actual social interaction, then the RA can enhance its effectiveness. Our arguments for this expanded view is based on the extensive work of Johnson et al. (2006, 2008), Nass and Moon (2000), Nass and Lee (2001), and Reeves and Nass (1996), which establish that users view their interactions with com: puters as interpersonal in nature and react to them as if they are interacting in social situations. Hence, users are expected to consider both their interpersonal interactions with RAs and their functioning as productivity tools in evaluating them. For example, Moon and Nass (1996) and Moon (2002) determined that people respond to computer personalities the same way they tend to respond to human personalities: Dominant participants were more attracted to computers that are endowed with a dominant personality characteristic, whereas submissive participants were more attracted to computers with a similar personality characteristic than a dissimilar one. Empirical evidence also indicates that people exhibit similarity‐attraction toward computer‐generated speech (Nass & Lee, 2001). These results are consistent with the similarity‐attraction theory observed in human‐tohuman interaction. Moon (2000) also revealed that consumers disclose more personal information when a computer follows the reciprocity principle of disclosing information about itself first. Nass and Moon (2000) observed through a series of experiments that participants would mindlessly apply social rules and expectations to computers. Particularly, users of computing technology have been shown to attribute gender types (Nass, Moon, & Green, 1997), ethnicity (Nass, Isbister, & Lee, 2000), voice (Nass & Brave, 2005; Nass & Lee, 2001), and physical representation (Nass, Moon, Fogg, Reeves, & Dryer, 1995) to technology. Applying these findings to the RA context, we may design different types of social characteristics that can be manifested by the RA. evaluate how users perceive these char: acteristics, and determine which can be used to improve the effectiveness of RAs and affect the perceptions and behaviour of users.

## 2.3 | Importance of the 3 outcome variables

To evaluate the efficacy of the perceived Functional Triad, we focus on the following 3 important outcome variables intention to use, recommendation adherence, and recommendation to friends. Intention to use refers to a person's perceived likelihood or subjective probability of an individual to use a technology or system in the near future (Venkatesh & Davis, 1996). It is an important consequent variable that has been heavily used in IS adoption research (Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003; Wang & Benbasat, 2009) and remains important in recent research (Venkatesh, Thong, Chan, & PJ, 2016). The behaviour in using an RA is essential to e‐commerce firms (Kamis et al., 2008), and intentions are immediate and strong predictors of actual behaviour (Ajzen, 2002). The current study provides researchers with a better understanding of the formation of an intention to use persuasive technologies by examining the key factors that affect the intention to use. Practitioners can better manage their online presence to enhance these key factors appropriately, thereby achieving a high level of RA usage behaviour.

Recommendation adherence is the extent to which a user's product choice matches products recommended by the RA. Recommendation adherence reflects a user's actual behaviour and thus represents an objective behavioura outcome. Firms invest in recommendation sources, including RAs, in enticing more customers to select and buy their recommended products. McKinsey revealed that 35% of what consumers purchase on amazon.com and 75% of what they watch on Netflix are based on product recommendations (MacKenzie, Meyer, & Noble, 2013). According to Forrester Research,<sup>2</sup> 76.4% of the top 500 Internet retailers feature product recommendations on their websites, which can increase their revenue by up to 300%. Recommendation adherence is an important driver of purchases. Thus. the influence of these factors on recommendation adherence warrants further investigation, In addition, the problem with using intention is that users' self‐reported purchase intentions do not perfectly predict their behaviour (Chandon, Morwitz, & Reinartz, 2005; Lim, Sia, Lee, & Benbasat, 2006; Morwitz, Steckel, & Gupta, 2007; Wright & MacRae, 2007). Some users are “non‐intenders” in that actual compliance with their stated intention is low (Bagozzi, Davis, & Warshaw, 1992; Wright & MacRae, 2007). Furthermore, intention to use and recommendation adherence are different in terms of the level of dependence on the RA for decision making. When customers intend to use an RA, they may or may not adhere to RA's recommendations. They might use an RA to reduce information overload, to reduce their search effort, or to confirm their decision making and check if they overlooked good products that the RA might recommend (Xiao & Benbasat. 2007). However, when users adhere to RA's recommendation, their intention to use RA should be high. We investigate these 2 outcome variables because we are interested in whether the 3 functional roles will be the same in influencing users' different levels of dependence on the RA.

The third outcome variable that we investigate is one's recommendation of the RA to friends. Recommendatior to friends is a key driver of vendor financial performance (Reichheld, 2003). It is more influential on the behaviour and decision making of other users compared with other marketer‐controlled sources, such as advertising (Roman & Cuestas. 20o8), Recommendation to friends is regarded as a prominent factor in the survival of online businesses because it can spread faster online than offline (Reichheld & Schefter, 2000). Recommendation to friends significantly affects technological innovation awareness, and users rely heavily on such recommendations to decide on their trials and use of technology (Gauri, Bhatnagar, & Rao, 2008; Gu, Park, & Konana, 2012; Reichheld & Schefter, 2000). Users might recommend the RA to their friends for a variety of reasons, which might be similar to those leading to intention to use an RA explained above. If users have high recommendation adherence to an RA (due to the reason of matching recommendation), they are more likely to recommend the RAs to their friends. However, intention to use an RA anc recommendation adherence do not necessarily mean that users will recommend the RA to their friends. This is because when users recommend something, they are putting their reputation on the line (Reichheld, 2003). They wil not take the risk unless they believe in the RA intensely. Thus, the high recommendation to friends usually implies a high intention to use an RA. However, it does not necessarily mean high recommendation adherence, as the reason for recommending the RA to friends may not be due to the matching recommendation generated by the RA but due to the reduction of information overload and search effort

## 3.1 | The effect of the TOT on the perceived Functional Triad

The TOT interactively demonstrates the trade-offs among product attribute values (eg, price ys faster processor of a laptop: large screen size ys heavier weight). Figure A1 illustrates an example of an RA with TOT. The value of each product attribute (with low values on the left side and high values on the right side) is represented by a horizonta scale with a slider. A user will be able to indicate the preferred level of a product attribute by clicking and dragging the slider to a certain spot on the bar. The placement of the slider on a given level of the attribute will lead to an imme diate, real‐time change in one or more values for other related attributes observable to the user through the move ments of other sliders. The number of attributes that shift automatically is a function of the degree of TOT that th RA is designed to have. The TOT integrated into the RA is expected to increase the persuasive capability of an RA, as it has been shown to elicit better input from users and accordingly recommend high‐quality products to the users (Xu et al., 2014). Furthermore, the interactive TOT feature supports exploring relationships among product attributes Such simulation experience can shape behaviour and persuade (Fogg, 2002)

We expect that TOT influences the perceived tool functionality. Trade‐off relationships among product attributes are important for understanding a product (Delquie, 2003; Frisch & Clemen, 1994). An RA with TOT is effective in informing users about the trade‐offs existing among product attribute values. As users gain a better understandin of product attribute values, they are more able to correct their original attribute value preferences. Subsequently, TOT prevents users from misspecifying their product preferences and encourages the more realistic input of attribute preferences to the RA. When more realistic attribute values are provided to the RA, the RA is more likely to recom mend a better set of products that fit a user's needs in an efficient manner; accordingly, perceived tool functionality o the RA should be higher. ## H1a : The trade‐off transparency feature positively influences perceived tool functionality.

We also expect that TOT influences perceived media functionality. Media functionality of a computing technol ogy enables users to explore cause‐and‐effect relationships (Fogg, 2002). For example, a website has media function ality if the website presents the before and after pictures of people who have lost weight (Oinas‐Kukkonen & Harjumaa, 2008). In our context, The TOT feature of the RA provides a simulation experience for users to observe immediately the link between the relationships among the product attribute values. When a user specifies the value o one attribute (eg, the hard drive of a laptop), the RA withTOT automatically adjusts the values of related product attri butes (eg, price). Thus, a user can understand how changing the value of a product attribute will have a causal effect on others. By exploring the cause‐and‐effect relationship among multiple pairs of attributes with TOT, a user's perceived media functionality will be higher. Accordingly, we propose the following:

H1b : The trade‐off transparency feature positively influences perceived media functionality.

We posit that TOT will also affect perceived social actor functionality. As reviewed in Section 2.2, Nass and his colleagues, through a series of experiment, has confirmed that computer users view their interactions with computer as social in nature and react to them as if they are interacting in social situations. According to this Computers as Social Actor” (CASA) paradigm (Reeves & Nass, 1996), users might perceive the RA as a social actor and perceive RA's certain features with social characteristics to have social actor functionality.

As noted in Section 2.1, features of social actor characteristics include responses based on users' input (Marakas et al., 2000), the perception of control by the technology, and perception of knowledge and intelligence within the technology (Johnson et al., 2006, 2008; Marakas et al., 2000). The TOT has these 3 social actor characteristics. First, the TOT responds to users' input. It vividly shows how the product attribute values are related to each other and can interactively respond to a user's attribute preference indication. When a user moves the slider related to one product attribute (ie, input aspect of TOT), it will lead to an immediate, real-time change in one or more values for other relatec attributes observable to the user through the movements of other sliders. Second, the TOT might create a perception of control. For example, if a user moves a slider to indicate a need for a “lighter” weight for a laptop computer, the “large” value for the screen size will automatically be directed to a “small" value, controlled by the TOT. Third, the TOT might be perceived to have knowledge. Its slider movement (Appendix A) is to reflect the product attribute relationships and is based on the knowledge (eg, a large hard drive with a high price and a large screen with a heavy weight) built in for the RA. In summary, the TOT uses its built‐in intelligence about the correlation among the product attribute values to respond to a user's attribute preference indication through controlling which values of a product attribute can reach, which has social actor characteristics (intelligence, control, and socialness) discussed in the literature (Johnson et al., 2008). Thus, an RA with such a TOT is expected to significantly enhance perceived social acto functionality compared to an RA without such a TOT, and we propose the following hypothesis:

H1c : The trade‐off transparency feature positively influences perceived social actor functionality

## 3.2 | Outcomes of the perceived Functional Triad

## 3.2.1 | Outcomes of perceived tool functionality

We posit that higher perceived tool functionality of an online RA leads to higher intention to use. The RA can effi ciently recommend a small consideration set to fit a user's product preferences from a large number of product alternatives available on a website (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007). RA improves the decision‐making effectiveness and makes the product selection process easier. If an RA can perform the tool functionality by effectively eliciting individual users product preferences, and can make recommendations fitting users' needs accordingly, users are more likely to continue to use the RA. In contrast, if the tool functionality of an online RA is perceived to be ineffective, users wil not likely use the RA. The direct and indirect effects of perceived tool functionality on usage intention has been supported in IS literature (eg, Kamis et al., 2008; Wang & Benbasat, 2009). In summary, RA can improve the perceived tool functionality for the following reasons: (1) lower information overload by narrowing a considering set, (2) decrease the amount of effort exerted by users in processing information by evaluating alternatives and arriving at a product selection, and (3) provide matching product recommendations (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007). All of these condi tions that result in high perceived tool functionality can increase user intention to utilize the RA. Thus, we propose

H2a : The higher the tool functionality of the RA is perceived to be, the higher the intention to use.

The key goal of an RA is to help users make a better choice decision through users' acceptance of the top‐ranked recommendations of the RA (Kramer, 2007). If the recommendations of an RA are adhered to, then the RA plays the role of the tool effectively and performs its job by recommending a proper product set. Among the reasons above (for H2a) regarding the effect of perceived tool functionality on intention to use, only the third reason on the capability o RAs to provide product recommendations that best match the preferences of users can lead to high recommendation adherence. If an RA recommends products that cannot fit users' needs, it is expected that the recommendations presented by the RAs will have little influence on users' product choices. Thus, the recommendation adherence will be low. Prior literature has suggested that if a buyer has fewer concerns about whether a product (or its characteristics) matches that buyer's needs, a seller's price premium increases (Hong & Pavlou, 2010). Similarly, we propose

## H2b : The higher the tool functionality of the RA is perceived to be, the higher the recommendation adherence.

Literature regarding the antecedents of positive recommendation to others includes the following: (1) user satis faction with product quality (Anderson, 1998), (2) the product offers the experience of emotional reactions (Gelb & Johnson. 1995). (3) users desire a “smart” image to others by proiecting themselves as intelligent consumers (Ander son, 1998; Sundaram, Kaushik, & Webster, 1998), and (4) users would like to help the company (Sundaram et al. 1998). We expect that a higher tool functionality perceived by users indicates the increase in the likelihood that the users will recommend the RA to their friends. When an RA confirms the prior expectations of users that an RA performs its tool functionality by helping users in lowering information overload, decreasing effort, and providing preference‐matching product recommendations, users will be satisfied with the performance of the RA, which agrees with the expectation–confirmation theory (Bhattacherjee, 2001). Satisfied users will be more likely to engage in the recom mendation of the RA to their friends (Anderson, 1998). Thus, we propose the following:

H2c : The higher the tool functionality of the RA is perceived to be, the higher the likelihood o recommending the RA to friends.

## 3.2.2 | Outcomes of perceived media functionality

When an individual perceives a computing technology to have media functionality, it means that the technology provides compelling experiences through simulations and enables the individual to explore cause‐and‐effect relationships interactively. If an RA has features that provide simulation experience, we expect that the intention to use the RA wil be high. This prediction can be derived from RepresentationTheory (Wand & Weber, 1990, 1995), which holds that an information system is made up of several structures that serve to represent some part of the world that a user under stands. As an example, an inventory system can represent the reality of the items in the warehouse, and a sales system can represent the status of a company's revenues. Representation Theory states that the purpose of an information system is to faithfully represent a real‐world domain (Weber, 1997, p. 73). The underlying assumption is that an individual uses systems because the systems provide a more informed basis for action (Burton‐Jones & Grange, 2013). However, a system can represent the real world in a faithful or unfaithful way. A system that more faithfully represents the real world will help users obtain their goals and lead to more effective use (Burton‐Jones & Grange, 2013). Applying Representation Theory, we derive that an RA that more effectively represents the domain will be more likely to be adopted and recommended by its user, and the RA's recommendations are more likely to be adhered to.

In the context of RAs, an important real-world domain that users need to understand is the value of relationships among the product attributes (Hedgcock & Rao, 2009), such as the relationship between the faster processor of a laptop and its price. The understanding of this domain product knowledge helps users provide realistic product prefer ences to an RA and make better purchasing decisions (eg, Delquie, 2003; Frisch & Clemen, 1994). As compared to an RA that lacks the media functionality, an RA that has effective media functionality would better represent the real‐world domain by allowing users to explore relationships among the product attribute values (Xu et al., 2014) Accordingly, users can leverage the representation to fulfil their goals, ie, understand the relationships among th values of product attributes. Prior research has found that better understanding of products through certain presen tation format can lead to greater intention to return to a website that provides that product presentation (Jiang & Benbasat, 2007b). Thus, perceived media functionality that enhances product understanding is expected to lead to higher intention to use the RA.

H3a : The higher the media functionality of the RA is perceived to be, the higher the intention to use.

When a user has a better understanding about the relationships among product attribute values, they can provide more realistic product preferences as input to the RA. For example, a user will not state many advanced features desired and a very low price as inputs to the RA. As a result, the RA can base users' realistic input and provide a better set of products to fit users' preferences (Xu et al., 2014). Accordingly, users will be more likely to adhere to the RA's recommendations. In contrast, when an infeasible combination of preferences was conveyed to the RA, the RA wil not be able to recommend matching products to fulfil users' requirements. Hence, recommendation adherence wil be low. Based on our discussion, we propose

H3b : The higher the media functionality of the RA is perceived to be, the higher the recommendation adherence.

Extant literature indicates that an interface with interactive and stimulating features positively affect the emotional feelings of users with the content presented (Parboteeah, Valacich, & Wells, 2009), which results in a positive affective feeling of users toward the interface. For example, positive emotional effects can be generated by interactive graphics (Cyr et al., 2009), frequently asked questions, and online guest books (Teo et al., 2003). An RA with a high level of media functionality vividly shows how the product attribute values are related to each other and can interac tively respond to the attribute preference indication of users. Consequently, the RA stimulates the sensory experience of users and subsequently results in positive emotional effects. As discussed for H2c, a key antecedent for recommending items to friends is the experience of emotional reactions from the offered product (Gelb & Johnson, 1995). Thus, the more an RA is perceived to have high media functionality, the higher the likelihood for users to recommend the RA to their friends. Thus, we propose

H3c : The higher the media functionality of the RA is perceived to be, the higher the likelihood of recommending the RA to friends.

## 3.2.3 | Outcomes of perceived social actor functionality

Perceived social actor functionality is an important criterion for online firms seeking to build relationships with users (Qiu & Benbasat, 2009). Adding features (eg, a personal greeting) that increase the social actor functionality of the website may increase users' beliefs that the firm is trying to provide good customer relationship management and thus increase purchase intentions (Gefen & Straub. 20o3). Customers desire technological attributes that portray a sense o personal, sociable, and sensitive human contact (Gefen & Straub, 2003; Lowry, Zhang, Zhou, & Fu, 2010). In contrast lower levels of social actor functionality may be more than a mere inconvenience for customers; it may actually preven customers from purchasing such services (Gefen & Straub, 2003). When a website serves as a communication interface between an online vendor and a customer, social actor functionality can positively influence attitude (Hassanein & Head, 2006, 2007), e-lovalty (Cyr, Hassanein, Head, & Ivanoy, 2007), and purchase intention (Gefen & Straub, 2003)

In the context of RAs, prior studies have demonstrated that users are inclined to attribute a number of human-like characteristics to an RA, which influences users' subsequent evaluations of these RAs. To illustrate, users of online RAs have been shown to attribute ethnicity (Nass et al., 2000), physical attractiveness (Holzwarth, Janiszewski, & Neumann, 2006), and personality types (Al‐Natour, Benbasat, & Cenfetelli, 2006) to these RAs. Accordingly, users perceiving an RA to have higher social actor functionality will have more positive evaluations of the RA, including satisfaction, favourable attitude, and purchase intention (Goel, Johnson, Junglas, & Ives, 2013; Holzwarth et al., 2006) Thus, we propose that perceived social actor functionality influences intention to use.

H4a : The higher the social actor functionality of the RA is perceived to be, the higher the intention to use.

We expect that perceived social actor functionality will positively influence recommendation adherence. Our reasoning is based on the literature regarding the positive effect of social cues on trust (Hess, Fuller, & Campbell, 2009; Komiak & Benbasat, 2004) and the positive effect of trust on purchase behaviour (Lim et al., 2006; Pavlou, Liang, & Xue, 2007). Computing technologies that convey more social cues may be viewed as more transparent, as more trust worthy, while fewer social cues conveyed could indicate the possibility of engagement in an untrustworthy activit (Gefen & Straub, 2003; Hess et al., 2009). The social cues of an RA provide trust‐building cues, which are expected to increase the users' reliance on RA's recommendations, just as trust served as a determinant of purchase behaviou in prior studies of trust (Lim et al., 2006; Pavlou et al., 2007).Therefore, we hypothesize

H4b : The higher the social actor functionality of the RA is perceived to be, the higher the recommendation adherence.

When users perceive an RA as a social actor, a social relationship may be established. When users feel connected to an RA, this relationship may engender the trust of users toward the RA (Gremler, Kevin, & Stephen, 2001). Prior research reveals that trust is associated with many pro‐firm‐related behaviours in empirical and theoretical research (Ganesan, 1994). As reviewed for H2c, helping a company is one of the reasons for recommending a product/service to others. Thus, we expect that trust relationships entail positive anticipations and result in advocacy behaviour, such as recommending the RA to friends. As previously mentioned, a reputation risk is involved for users to recommend an RA to friends (Reichheld, 2003). Therefore, only those users who trust the RA will be more likely to take the risk of recommending the RA to their friends. Thus, we propose the following

H4c : The higher the social actor functionality of the RA is perceived to be, the higher the likelihood of recommending the RA to friends.

## 3.3 | The mediating role of the perceived Functional Triad

We expect that the perceived Functional Triad mediates the effect of a persuasive technology on intention to use and recommendation to friends. To ground our hypotheses of the mediating effect of the perceived Functional Triad, we use the TOT feature (Xu et al., 2014) of an RA to illustrate a feature of a persuasive technology. Again, the main pur pose of introducing TOT is to demonstrate the mediating effect of the perceived Functional Triad between a technol ogy feature and the 3 outcomes.

To establish the mediating effect of the perceived Functional Triad between TOT and the 3 outcomes, 2 basic conditions have to be fulfilled: The perceived Functional Triad influences the 3 outcomes, and the TOT influences the perceived Functional Triad. Our discussion of hypotheses H2 through H4 already explains why we expect the perceived Functional Triad to have a direct effect on the 3 outcomes. In addition, we have hypothesized the effects of TOT on the perceived Functional Triad in hypothesis H1.

SinceTOT influences the perceived Functional Triad and the Functional Triad influences the 3 outcomes, we hav the confidence to speculate that the perceived Functional Triad mediates the effectiveness of TOT on the 3 outcomes. In the case of RAs. RAs are supposed to elicit the purchasing needs of users and then make product recom mendations that satisfy these preferences (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007). Thus, perceived roles of an RA that ca facilitate this RA goal are expected to improve intention to use, recommendation adherence, and recommendation to friends. TOT can improve perceptions of tool functionality (improving task effectiveness), perceptions of media functionality (allowing users to understand product attributes values through simulation), and perceptions of socia actor functionality (demonstrating control and knowledge), all of which have a positive influence on fulfilling the RA' goal in assisting users. Therefore, we propose that:

H5a : The effect of the TOT level of an RA on intention to use is fully mediated by the perceived Functional Triad.

H5b : The effect of the TOT level of an RA on recommendation adherence is fully mediated by the perceived Functional Triad.

H5c : The effect of the TOT level of an RA on recommendation to friends is fully mediated by the perceived Functional Triad.

## 4 | METHODOLOGY

A 4‐condition (RA with zero, low, moderate, or high level of TOT) between‐participants design was implemented to test the hypotheses. The participants' task was to choose a laptop computer.

## 4.1 | Manipulation of TOT

The TOT of an RA was utilized to create the range of variance that is possible when designing technologies with dif ferent levels of the perceived Functional Triad so that we can evaluate the effect of the perceived Functional Triad on the 3 outcomes. Four levels of TOT (zero TOT as a control group, low, moderate, and high TOT) were created by manipulating the number of trade‐off relationships revealed by the RA. Specifically, the RA revealed 0, 7, 15, and 23 unidirectional trade‐off relationships in zero, low, moderate, and high TOT treatments, respectively. In the case of a low level of TOT, the RA revealed the 7 trade‐off relationships between the 7 nonprice attributes and the price attribute. That is, whenever a user indicated her product preferences on each of the 7 nonprice attributes, the price automatically adjusted to reflect their underlying correlations, while the values of the rest of the nonprice attributes remained constant. RAs with a moderate level of TOT revealed 8 additional trade‐off relationships in addition to those specified in the low TOT condition, such as the correlation between screen size and weight of a laptop computer. An RA with a high level of TOT revealed an additional 8 trade‐off relationships, as well as those specified in the moderate TOT condition. One example is the relationship between the battery and the weight of a laptop computer. In the control condition (zero TOT), when a user indicates a certain value for a product attribute, the values of other attributes maintain constancy. However, participants were told in a written form that “When you indicate your preferences, please bear in mind that the better the computer component (eg, faster processor or larger hard disk), the more expensive it is; and, the larger the screen size, the heavier it is. Hence, be careful not to overestimate your needs.” A more detailed description of the manipulation of TOT can be found in Appendix B.

## 4.2 | Participants and procedures

A power analysis for a between‐participant design determined that 160 participants (40 participants in each group) would assure a sufficient statistical power of 0.80 to detect a medium effect size (f = .25) (Cohen, 1988). Incentives consisting of a minimum \$10 honorarium and an additional \$25 for the 20 best performers were provided to the participants. Participants were first required to fill in a questionnaire to record their demographic and control variables. Before participants were randomly assigned to the experimental groups (control, low, moderate, and high leve of TOT). they were trained to use the website to which they were assigned. In the experimental websites. they could indicate the product attribute preferences to the RA by dragging the slider on each attribute bar. After participants submitted their attribute preferences to the RA, the RA accordingly recommended a list of ranked products (ie, 50 laptop products). The 5 products that best matched the participant's preferences were presented on the first page of the website, with the rest of the 45 products displayed 5 products at a time on the subsequent pages. Participants could decide whether or not to follow the RA's advice and then select one of the 50 products to put in a shopping cart. The system was programmed to capture which product was put in the shopping cart so that we were able to capture the product choice decision made by the participants and determine their recommendation adherence. After that, they answered questions related to the model constructs, such as perceived TOT and perceived tool functionality.

Except those stated otherwise, all measures were based on 7‐point Likert scales ranging from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree”(7). Perceived tool functionality was measured with items representing the degree to which users believe the RA helps them improve the task effectiveness (Fogg, 2002). The measurements for perceived media functionality were based on Fogg's definition and description of the media functionality (eg, enabling users to experience simulated cause‐and‐effect). We adopted established scales for intention to use and recommendation to friends from prior literature. The measure of the recommendation to friends is the top‐ranking question found to be most effective across industries and the single mos reliable indicator of a company's ability to grow (Mithas, Ramasubbu, Krishnan, & Fornell, 2007; Reichheld, 2003; Yang, MM, Winer, Assael, & Chen, 2012). Use of single‐item measures as a dependent variable is valid and common when the item is unambiguous and is consistent with previous research (Bergkvist & Rossiter, 2007; Drolet & Morri son, 2001; Mithas et al., 2007).

Perceived social actor functionality was measured with the following items: 1 item of, “Interacting with the RA felt like interacting with an actual human being” and 4 items of “There was a sense of human contact/human warmth personalness/sociability when interacting with the RA.” The first item was directly derived from the social actor def inition conceptualized by Fogg (2003, p. 26), who stated, “When people use an interactive technology, they often respond to it as though it were a living being.” The other 4 items also follow the overall description by Fogg of th social actor functionality.

Although our measurement of perceived social actor functionality is highly similar to those for the construct of social presence, we must emphasize 2 points: (1) perceived social actor functionality is different from perceived socia presence, which is the extent to which a technology allows users to experience others as being psychologically pres ent (Fulk, Schmitz, & Power, 1987), which creates a feeling of human contact (Yoo & Alavi, 2001) and (2) our measure ments for the social actor functionality is slightly general and may not cover the multifaceted social actor role of RAs in the aspects of intelligence, control, and socialness as conceptualized by Johnson et al. (2008). In other words, the con ceptualization of Johnson et al. (2008) about the social role of computing technologies is more precise than that of social presence (ie, feeling of general human contact)

The present study primarily aims to examine the outcomes of user perceptions of the 3 general functionalities. We are less interested in the specific role that an RA can play within each of the 3 functionalities but on the response of users to the RA in its overall role (eg, role as a social actor). Given that we measured the general tool functionality o an RA rather than its specific tool roles, as well as measured the general media functionality of the RA rather than its specific media role, we evaluated how the participants respond to the RA in the general role of a social actor. Regard less of the specific social actor role an RA plays (eg, intelligence, control, or socialness), the RA creates a feeling of human contact. Thus, our items directly tap into the perceptions of users about the ability of the RA to convey a sens of human contact.

## 5.3 | Test of the research model

We analysed the structural model using partial least squares (PLS) structural equation modelling, a component‐based approach (Lohmöller, 1989). The PLS allows for simultaneous testing of the measurement model (the psychometric properties of the scales used to measure a variable) and the estimation of the structural model (the strength and direction of the relationship between the variables). We used the software SMART PLS 2.0 (Ringle, Wende, & Will, 2005 to conduct the analyses.

## 5.3.1 | Measurement model

Assessments of measurement models should examine (1) individual measurement item reliability, (2) internal consis tency, and (3) discriminant validity (Barclay, Higgins, & Thompson, 1995). To support individual item reliability, we examined the loadings of the individual measurement items on their intended constructs and compared these wit a recommended tolerance of 0.70 (Barclay et al., 1995; Chin, 1998). All of the measurement items met this threshold (Table 3). To show internal consistency of the constructs, we calculated composite reliability and Cronbach's alpha fo each construct. All met the recommended tolerances (>0.70, Fornell & Larcker, 1981) (Table 4). The diagonal elements in Table 4 represents the square roots of the average variance extracted of latent variables, while the off‐diagonal ele ments are the correlations between latent variables. For adequate discriminant validity, the square root of the average variance extracted of any latent variable should be greater than the correlation between this particular latent variable and other latent variables (Barclay et al., 1995). All construct pairs met this requirement. Moreover, as shown in Table 3, the loadings of a given construct's indicators are higher than the loadings of any other, and these same indi cators load more highly on their intended construct than on any other construct. This lends further support to discrim inant validity.

We then investigate the factor structure of the perceived Functional Triad to confirm whether they should b treated as 3 different roles as we approached in the study. The factor analysis indicates that the 3 factors emerged with each having an eigenvalue higher than 1.00. Thus, the 3 perceived functionalities are indeed separate ones.

To address the potential concern for common method bias, we performed 2 tests. First, we applied the Harman (1967) one‐factor extraction test. This test determines whether a single method factor explains most of the variance among the instrument variables (Podsakoff & Organ, 1986). If one contributes more than 50% of total variance, common method bias might exist (Chengalur‐Smith, Nevo, & Demertzoglou, 2010; Nov & Ye, 2008). Using a principa component analysis for all of the items of the 7 constructs (Figure 1) measured in the study, we found that the first factor accounted for only 37.03% of the total variance, less than 50% of the total variance; this indicates a lack of substantial common methods bias (Chengalur‐Smith et al., 2010; Nov & Ye, 2008). Second, we tested for multicollinearity among the perceived Functional Triad. To formally test for the presence of collinearity, we calculated the varianc inflation factor (VIF). The results indicated that all of the VIFs were lower than 2.0, with the highest VIF being 1.60 Tabachnick and Fidell (1996) and Thatcher and Perrewé (2002) suggest that when VIFs exceed 10, collinearity biases the result. Because the VIFs did not exceed 2, our analysis indicated that collinearity did not influence the results.

## 5.5 | Discussion

The results support the theorization that perceived tool functionality, perceived media functionality, and perceived social actor functionality are effective in increasing intention to use the RA. Furthermore, perceived media function ality and perceived social actor functionality are the only two that significantly influence recommendation to friends, while perceived media functionality is the only one that significantly influences recommendation adherence. The per ceived Functional Triad (perceptions of tool, media, and social actor functionalities) fully mediates the relationships between the TOT and the 3 outcomes.

Investigating these 3 perceived functionalities simultaneously in a single study, we see that, among the 3 outcomes, perceived tool functionality significantly influences only intention to use (the dominant conseguent variable used in the IS literature), but not the other 2 outcomes: recommendation adherence and recommendation to friends. Arguably, intention to use is a relatively weak form of outcome as compared to the other two. Intention to use does not necessarily mean users will actually return to the website to continue to use the RA (Chandon et al., 2005; Lim et al., 2006) nor they will follow the RA's advice and recommend it to friends. Thus, any of the 3 functionalities wil significantly increase the intention to return but not necessarily the other 2 outcomes. In contrast, recommendation adherence is a user's objective behaviour, and recommendation to friends is a strong and proven indicator of repeat purchase and profitable growth (Reichheld, 2003). Given that perceived tool functionality does not have significant effects on either recommendation adherence or recommendation to friends, it indicates that the utility of perceived tool functionality fades when it comes to these 2 outcomes. The findings also suggest that we should look bevond the perceived tool functionality to identify the determinants of recommendation adherence and recommendation to friends.

Perceived social actor functionality has significant effects on both intention to use and recommendation to friends, underscoring the emerging emphasis upon social relationships, as desired by online customers (Al‐Natou et al., 2006; Grewal, Krishnan, & Lindsey‐Mullikin, 2008; Schultze, 2003). Indeed, the human service component is the most crucial ingredient for attracting customers to a traditional physical retailer, while online businesses suffer inherent constraints, such as the absence of helpful retail personnel (Grewal et al., 2008). Thus, social actor functionality embedded in the RA will give customers a better experience and lead to greater persuasion. Perceived tool functionality was not determined to influence the recommendation to friends positively. The tool functionality of the RA (versus the other 2 functionalities) is likely to be expected by users and no longer excites them. One of the reasons for friend referrals is to appear well informed or “smart” (Sundaram et al., 1998). Recommending a regular RA to friends may not serve this purpose because their friends may already be aware of similar RAs with effective tool functionality.

Perceived media functionality is the only functionality that has a significant effect on all 3 outcomes. In this aspect, perceived media functionality is the most important functionality in the Functional Triad. It is likely that the media functionality of an RA essentially demonstrates the core purpose of an information system: The manner in which values of product attributes are related to each other in the real world. As summarized by Burton‐Jones and Grange (2013, p. 5), “The core purpose of all information systems is to help people understand the states of some real‐world systems that are relevant to them.”

The results support the assertion that online RAs with a higher degree of TOT are more effective in influencing users' perceptions of all 3 functional roles, as compared to RAs with a lower degree of TOT. Perceived media function ality is influenced the most by TOT becauseTOT provides a unique simulation experience for users to explore the precise relationship among product attribute values. In addition, TOT provides information on how product attribute values are related to one another. This can make users aware of potential value trade‐offs that they had not previously recognized, thus leading to better‐perceived tool functionality. In addition, as more interactive cues were provided in the RA with a high level of TOT, the RA triggered more sensory channels (Jiang & Benbasat, 2007a), leading to higher perceived social actor functionality. We found that TOT has the strongest positive effect on perceived media functionality, followed by perceived tool functionality and perceived social actor functionality. This is evident in the significant differences among the path coefficients in the model (0.45, 0.21, and 0.19, respectively).<sup>4</sup> These results sugges that not all computing technologies play the 3 functional roles simultaneously to the same degree. It is likely that technology may have a greater impact on certain perceived functionalities but have a weaker effect on others

Studying the effects of the 3 functionalities independently would be theoretically and practically significant. First, these 3 functionalities are distinct. User perceptions about the tool and social actor roles of a computing technology are not always related. A simple calculator (as a tool) is not necessarily perceived as a social actor. Given that the 3 functionalities each have different features, they may differ in their substantive implications. Thus, studying these 3 beliefs separately is important rather than lumping them into an overall triad construct for better theoretical understanding. Our approach is also supported by the empirical findings reported in Section 5.3.1. Second, explanations differ as to why each of the 3 functionalities is related to each of the 3 outcomes. For example, the effect of social acto functionality on recommendation to friends could be due to the trust of a user toward the RA, whereas the effect of medium functionality may be based on an emotional reaction. Third, the triad distinction is important because the 3 types of functionality may have different outcomes (eg, one may lead to intention to use, whereas the other may lead to recommendation adherence).

## 6 | CONTRIBUTIONS AND CONCLUSIONS

## 6.1 | Theoretical contributions

The results of the study make important theoretical contributions. First, grounded in the Functional Triad framework, we derived 3 constructs (perceptions of the tool, media, and social factor functionalities) as representations of the perceived Functional Triad, which serves as antecedents of persuasion outcomes. The 3 constructs representing the perceived Functional Triad are important in that they explain the underlying reasons of why certai features of computing technologies can lead to certain outcomes. Our findings also shed light on the important rol of the perceived Functional Triad in mediating the effect of technology features on 3 outcomes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate and empirically test the direct and mediating roles of the perceive Functional Triad.

The second and related contribution we offer is in our examination of 3 outcomes of the perceived Functiona Triad, such that we are able to more comprehensively evaluate the efficacy of the Functional Triad from the users perspective. Most prior research on persuasion focused only on one outcome, such as intention to use (Angst & Agarwal, 2009) or recommendation adherence (Bhattacherjee & Sanford, 2006). Similarly, prior research on RAs included only 1 or 2 conseguent variables, such as usage intention and/or choice behaviour (Tam & Ho. 2005: Wang & Benbasat, 2009). In this study, we combined 3 outcomes in one study, including recommendation to friends—the most influential question found by both practitioners and marketing scholars in predicting company growth and customer loyalty (Reichheld, 2003). As little IS research has included this variable, we also bridge the marketing and IS literature by examining the effects of the perceived Functional Triad on recommendation to friends. As noted, recommendation to friends is predicted by user satisfaction with the product, experience of emotional reactions, the willingness to helping the company, and the desire to appear intelligent. As we argued for hypothesis H2c, perceived tool functionality is expected to increase users' satisfaction only, but not necessarily affect user's emotional reactions, and increase their desire to help the company and project themselves as intelligent consumers. The nonsignificant effect of perceived tool functionality on recommendation to friends might suggest that user satisfaction is a relatively weaker predictor for recommendation to friends as compared to the other 2 predictors (ie, experience of emotional reactions and the willingness to helping the company) that, respectively, drive the significant effects of media and social actor functionalities on recommendation to friends (hypothe ses H3c and H4c).

Most importantly, this study offers contributions to the literature on persuasive technologies. The Functional Triad was proposed by Fogg as a classification of 3 basic ways that people view computing technologies, ie, persuasive technologies which can function as tools, media, or social actors. Surprisingly, no studies had empirically examined the impact of the perceptions of the Functional Triad. Theoretically, it was not clear whether these perceived functionalities can persuade or which functionality is more effective. We address this gap by adapting Fogg's Functional Triad Framework from the designer's perspective to the end‐users' perspective and empirically testing the impact of the 3 perceived functionalities together on 3 different outcomes. We found that perceived tool functionality did not influence adherence and recommendation to friends, although it is thought to be persuasive. Likewise, we did not find that perceived social actor functionality influences recommendation adherence.

At a micro level, we assessed the impact of the TOT feature on perceived tool functionality, perceived media functionality, and perceived social actor functionality. We demonstrated that with the TOT feature, users have a higher level of perceived tool functionality, perceived media functionality, and perceived social actor functionality. This study increases our understanding of how an RA with TOT can enhance its persuasive power by shaping users' perceptions of media functionality greatly, followed by both tool functionality and social actor functionality. This model can also serve as a framework to evaluate other features of computing technologies.

## 6.2 | Practical contributions

In addition to theoretical contributions, our results also have practical implications. First, the mediating role of the per ceived Functional Triad highlights that perceptions of the 3 functional roles play significant roles in persuading users to adopt computing technologies and recommend them to friends. Technology designers aiming to persuade peopl to change intentions are advised to pay particular attention to technology features that can shape the user's perceptions of these 3 functional roles

Second, regarding which functional roles are important for certain outcomes, extant research provided limited implication as designers were not sure whether they should design a technology to resemble a tool, medium, and or social actor. By combining our examination of the perceptions of 3 functional roles simultaneously into one study we offer the following suggestions to practitioners: (1) To improve recommendation adherence, we recommend practitioners pay particular attention to improving users' perceived media functionality, because it is the sole functionality that influences recommendation adherence; (2) to encourage recommendation to friends, practitioners should focus on improving users' perceived media functionality and perceived social actor functionality; (3) to increase usage inten tion, they are advised to pay equal attention to all 3 components in the perceived Functional Triad.

Next, the TOT feature enables users to better understand the product attribute value trade‐offs and provide appropriate attribute input to the RA. This enables the RA to provide better product recommendations, thus leading to better‐perceived tool functionality. The simulation experience enabled by the TOT improves the perceived media functionality. Also, the interactive interface of the RA with TOT enhances the shopping experience which leads to better perceived social actor functionality. Together, the TOT feature influences users' perceptions of the Functional Triad that subsequently motivates them to go to their websites to use RAs in product choice decisions. Overall, our study shows that TOT in an online RA for recommending laptop computers is valuable. Similarly, practitioners are advised to incorporate the TOT feature into online RAs for other product (eg, TVs and dishwashers).

## 6.3 | Limitations and future research

There are several limitations to this study, which in turn open avenues for future research. The first limitation is related to participants in our study evaluating the perceived Functional Triad in the early stage of their interaction with the technology feature. As users become more familiar with the feature, the model results might be different. Future research would benefit by examining the relative importance of 3 functional roles on post‐adoption perceptions and behaviours. Second, although the measures for the social actor functionality (ie, users perceiving a sense of sociability when interacting with the RA) were drawn from prior social actor studies (Hess et al., 2009; Qiu & Benbasat, 2009) that describe the social relationships between a recommendation technology (a social actor) and its users, the scale is slightly coarse, We call for future research to adopt a more detailed measure of social actor func: tionality (eg, Johnson et al., 2008). Third, the insignificant effect of perceived tool functionality on recommendation to friends is likely due to the fictitious nature of the experimental website; thus, participants have less reason to refer the RA of such website to their friends. This result may become significant in reality, and other significant effects would likely be stronger. Similarly, the actual purchase behaviour of the participants in using a real website may be different from their recommendation adherence behaviour that is collected from a laboratory experiment. Hence, our findings may not be universally generalizable, and future research will benefit by using a realistic website to verify if the same results will hold.

Another potential stream of research would be to use the proposed model as a framework to evaluate other types of technology features and to explore how the model could be augmented with other possible moderators. In addi tion, recommendation adherence is only affected by the media functionality, and recommendation to friends is onl affected by the media functionality and the social actor functionality. Future research will benefit from examining other antecedents of these 2 outcomes: recommendation adherence and recommendation to friends. Finally, as we examined only 3 outcomes of the perceived Functional Triad, other outcomes deserve further research to determine the effect of the perceived Functional Triad.

## 6.4 | Conclusions

This study addresses an important research gap in understanding how perceptions of the Functional Triad influence users' usage intention, recommendation adherence, and recommendation to friends. The findings indicate that the 3 functional roles have different effects on the 3 outcomes. Perceived media functionality is the most important, influencing all 3 outcomes. It is followed by perceived social actor functionality, which influences 2 of the 3 outcomes We also find support for the perceived Functional Triad's mediating role between computing technology features and the 3 outcomes. Our proposed model can be adopted to evaluate alternative features of other computing technolo gies in the future. We also advance the literature of persuasive technologies by adapting the model from the designer's perspective to the end‐users' perspective. The results not only contribute to the literature on information systems and persuasive technologies but also inform developers about the relative importance of the 3 perceive functionalities.

## Manipulation of trade‐off transparency

Four levels of trade‐off transparency (zero, low, medium, and high) were created by manipulating the number of tradeoff relationships revealed by the RA. Specifically, the RA revealed<sup>5</sup> 0, 7, 15, and 23 unidirectional trade‐off relationships in zero, low, medium, and high trade‐off transparency (TOT) treatments, respectively.

In the case of a low level of TOT (Appendix B Table 1), the RA revealed the 7 trade‐off relationships<sup>6</sup> between the 7 nonprice attributes and the price attribute. This manipulation is based on the notion that the most common form of a trade‐off in most marketplace settings is that between price and product quality (Hedgcock & Rao, 2009). Specifically, whenever a user indicates her product preferences on each of the 7 nonprice attributes, the price will automat ically adjust to reflect their underlying correlations,<sup>7</sup> while the values of the rest of the nonprice attributes remain constant. Note that the change in price does not lead to a change in other product attributes because of the very larg number of possible combinations of nonprice attributes

The RAs with a medium level of TOT (Table B2) revealed 8 additional trade‐off relationships in addition to those specified in the low TOT condition. One example, as noted earlier, is the correlationbetweenscreensize and weightof a laptop computer. Similar to the interpretation used withTable B1, Table B2 should be interpreted by looking at each column heading first, and then the intersected rows. For example, when the value of the hard drive attribute (column heading changes, the values of two other related attributes (price and processor) in the corresponding intersected rows change accordingly.

The RA with a high level of TOT (Table B3) revealed an additional 8 trade‐off relationships, as well as those specified in the medium TOT condition. One example is the relationship between the battery and the weight of a laptop computer. Similar to the other tables, Table B3 should be interpreted by looking at each column heading first and then the correspondent intersected rows. For example, when the value of the hard drive attribute (column heading changes, the values of 3 other related attributes (price, processor, and weight) in the intersected rows change accordingly.

## ADDITIONAL DATA ANALYSIS USING COVARIANCE‐BASED SEM

In addition to the structural model results reported in Section 5.3.2 using PLS (a component‐based approach), we employed IBM SPSS AMOS V24.0 software to perform covariance‐based structural equation modelling (SEM) for the effects of the perceived Eunctional Triad on the 3 outcomes. The parameter estimates. fit indices and observec residuals showed acceptable goodness of fit between our proposed main effects model and the empirical data: normalized chi‐square $( \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathsf { d f } = 1 . 7 0 3 )$ was less than the maximum recommended value of 3.0, comparative fit index (CFI = 0.969), and non‐normed fit index (NNFI = 0.960) were each more than the minimum threshold value of 0.90 (Bentler & Bonett, 1980; Hoyle, 1995). The root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) is 0.066. RMSEA should be below 0.10 (Browne & Cudeck, 1993), but has also been suggested to represent a very good fit if below the more restrictive threshold of 0.08. The GEL should be at or above 0.90 (Hoyle, 1995), while the AGEL should be at or above 0.80 (Chin & Todd, 1995; Segars & Grover, 1993). The GFI and AGFI were 0.901 and 0.852, exceeding the threshold requirements.
