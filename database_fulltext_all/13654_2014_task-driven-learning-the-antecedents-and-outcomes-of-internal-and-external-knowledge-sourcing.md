# Task-driven learning: The antecedents and outcomes of internal and external knowledge sourcing

Yinglei Wang <sup>a,</sup>\*, Peter H. Gray <sup>b,1</sup>, Darren B. Meister <sup>c,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Fred C. Manning School of Business, Acadia University, Wolfville, Nova Scotia B4P 2R6, Canada

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 8 September 2012 Received in revised form 7 July 2014 Accepted 29 August 2014 Available online 10 September 2014

Keywords: Knowledge sourcing Knowledge integration Learning Boundary spanning

## A B S T R A C T

The degree to which individuals leverage knowledge resources influences their effectiveness and may shape their organizations’ competitive advantage. We examine the ways in which tasks with different characteristics affect individuals’ use of internal and external knowledge and the outcomes of such behaviors. Our analysis reveals that interdependent and non-routine tasks drive internal knowledge sourcing, while complex tasks motivate external knowledge sourcing. Internal and external knowledge sourcing activities contribute to individuals’ cognitive adaptation and innovation, with a negative interaction between them, while cognitive replication benefits only from internal knowledge sourcing. These findings can help managers better satisfy individuals’ knowledge needs and achieve intended organizational outcomes.

## 1. Introduction

In the ongoing quest for competitive advantage, organizations have increasingly focused on knowledge as a strategic resource [2], which has led them to adopt a range of knowledge management practices, such as knowledge reuse [65], that are intended to help them compete more effectively. They often seek to manage knowledge through information technology (IT) enabled initiatives that enhance the availability of knowledge. Many firms implement electronic knowledge repositories so that individuals can access explicit knowledge easily [e.g., 34]. Others offer computer mediated communication channels (e.g., email, forums, and Skype) so that individuals can communicate with experts conveniently to acquire tacit knowledge [3]. Web 2.0 technologies are also gaining popularity, complementing formal knowledge repositories and directories and assisting individuals in their knowledge sourcing efforts [37,95]. Through such initiatives, the supply of knowledge in many organizations has progressed to such an extent that a broad spectrum of knowledge resources often compete for individuals’ attention and use [39].

While the supply of knowledge is an important component of knowledge management, how individuals make use of available knowledge resources is also key [35]. If knowledge can be thought of as justified beliefs and skills that enable effective action [72], then it is individuals’ behaviors in seeking out knowledge and taking action based on what they have learned that produces organizational benefits. Indeed, empirical studies support this connection between knowledge seeking behaviors and improved performance [36,59]. Managers who understand how employees use knowledge resources have a better ability to design policies that encourage individual knowledge sourcing behaviors in ways that are more likely to benefit their organizations. They can also prepare and allocate IT resources (e.g., investing in the needed systems and acquiring relevant IT products) more effectively so that individual knowledge sourcing behaviors are optimally supported.

To better elaborate this ‘‘demand perspective’’ on knowledge management, we seek to contrast how individuals leverage internal and external knowledge. Internal knowledge is the knowledge captured and stored by an individual’s organization and/or possessed by its members, and external knowledge is the knowledge possessed by outsiders (for instance, experts in other organizations) [68]. Research on knowledge sourcing has principally focused on internal knowledge sources; for instance, some researchers have examined what drives individuals to seek knowledge from internal electronic knowledge repositories [e.g., 34,40,47,65], while others have investigated the effects of using different types of internal knowledge sources on individual performance [e.g., 36]. However, there is very limited research on the use of external knowledge at the individual level, even though research at the group and firm levels has highlighted the importance of environmental scanning and absorbing knowledge from the external environment [e.g., 17,20]. Environmental scanning can help reduce strategic uncertainty and facilitate innovation [1], while importing knowledge and combining internal and external knowledge is an important method for achieving superior performance [70].

Research on boundary spanning has offered limited insights into this issue [see 67]. For instance, Tushman and Scanlan [86] explored the mechanisms by which information from outside the organization can be brought in and integrated through the communication behaviors of well-connected individuals. Marrone et al. [66] found that individuals who have higher levels of boundary spanning self-efficacy are more likely to import external information and knowledge and that such boundary spanning behaviors help their teams achieve superior performance. While these studies provide useful insights, most research in this stream considers those individuals who occupy boundary spanning roles, with a strong focus on innovation across a range of performance outcomes [e.g., 85]. There have been few inquiries into the ways in which ordinary knowledge workers leverage internal and external knowledge resources to accomplish various daily tasks [cf., 83], and as a result, there is little holistic understanding that simultaneously considers the antecedents and outcomes of internal and external knowledge sourcing. Nevertheless, it is important to develop such an understanding because individuals’ use of knowledge sources can directly affect their effectiveness in the workplace. Moreover, sourcing behaviors collectively affect knowledge integration, which has been found to be a key determinant of organizational performance [70,82]. Without this understanding, the ability of organizations to facilitate knowledge use and consequently enhance performance is inevitably compromised. Researchers have noticed this shortcoming and have called for more research on this important theme [4].

In this study, we adopt a job-related learning perspective to address this lack of research [e.g., 35,61]. The learning perspective has been applied in the context of knowledge sourcing and proven relevant. For instance, Kankanhalli et al. [46] found that individuals’ knowledge sourcing initiative is positively associated with their learning effectiveness. Building on prior research, we propose and test a model that integrates task demands, internal and external knowledge sourcing, and learning outcomes. Our purpose is to provide a fine-grained depiction of how individuals mobilize internal and external knowledge resources to cope with different tasks and achieve important learning outcomes in the workplace. The resources relevant to our research include IT enabled resources such as electronic knowledge repositories, the resources accessible via IT such as experts on a forum, and traditional resources such as personal connections and publications. In doing so, we contribute to the literature in two ways. First, we extend the research on individual level knowledge sourcing by considering external knowledge in addition to internal knowledge and examining their direct and interacting effects. Second, by simultaneously examining the antecedents and outcomes of internal and external knowledge sourcing, we elaborate the micro-foundations of how knowledge integration (typically examined at the group or firm level) unfolds at the individual level.

We begin by reviewing the research on individual knowledge sourcing, and introduce a framework of task-driven learning that leads to the development of several novel hypotheses. We then report on our empirical test of these hypotheses and conclude with a discussion of the implications of our findings for both academics and practitioners.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses

## 2.1. Literature review

Knowledge sourcing refers to individuals’ intentional actions taken to seek out and access others’ expertise, experience, insights, and opinions [35]. Rooted in the demand perspective of knowledge management, such individual-level behaviors are key to the success of knowledge management initiatives. Regardless of what an organization does to manage knowledge, benefits are only achievable when employees actively draw on knowledge resources to enhance their performance. While knowledge sourcing occurs in a number of ways, it often relies heavily on IT systems and tools in today’s organizations [59]. A range of studies have examined the antecedents of knowledge sourcing, and provide evidence of positive relationships between knowledge sourcing behaviors and individuals’ learning outcomes and performance [35,36,59]. Appendix 1 lists the relevant studies identified in our review of the literature with keywords such as knowledge sourcing and knowledge seeking, which we synthesize below.

Research on knowledge sourcing focuses mainly on seeking knowledge within the organization with a considerable focus on the adoption and use of electronic knowledge repositories [e.g., 8,34,47]. For instance, Bock et al. [8] found that collaborative norms facilitate individuals’ knowledge seeking from electronic knowledge repositories. Other researchers have explored the use of different types of knowledge sources. As an example, Gray and Meister [36] studied the effects of seeking knowledge from individual co-workers, groups of co-workers, and internal published materials. This focus on internal knowledge sources is consistent with the prevalent notion of sharing best practices and reusing existing knowledge within organizations [65].

Nevertheless, knowledge sourcing also occurs across organizational boundaries [e.g., 83]. Research at the group and firm levels has extensively discussed the importance and mechanisms of importing knowledge from external sources [e.g., 15,20]. Knowledge from third-party databases, other organizations, universities, and other external ties is often perceived as being new and inspiring and is thus considered to be more likely to improve both short-term and long-term performance if integrated properly [70,97]. The R&D literature has shown that organizations that seek knowledge from a variety of external sources are better able to foster innovation [55]. Because external knowledge sources represent a considerable portion of the valuable knowledge resources available to individuals [68,83], understanding individuals’ behaviors in this regard is an important step forward for the research on knowledge sourcing.

While the availability of knowledge resources has expanded considerably in recent years, the capacity of individuals to process knowledge remains, of course, relatively inflexible [39]. A theoretical model that distinguishes the systematic differences between internal and external knowledge sourcing may provide important insights into how individuals grapple with the challenge presented by abundant available sources. Considerable satisficing is likely to take place as employees estimate the significant effort that would be required to consult all available sources. Indeed, it is likely that employees apply heuristics and make trade-offs when using internal and external knowledge sources rather than approaching each new situation with the goal of conducting a complete search of all available sources [e.g., 96]. For example, anecdotal evidence suggests that employees often search Google for information or knowledge regardless of whether their organization has an internal – and potentially better – source available [34]. Moreover, because of the differences in the valuation and perceptions (e.g., image cost, ease of access) associated with alternate types of sources [68,91], individuals are likely to approach internal knowledge and external knowledge differently. Trade-offs and patterns in employee sourcing behavior may systematically influence knowledge integration at the firm level over time and may therefore even affect firm-level performance [15,70], as employees assume the roles of boundary spanners, bridging internal and external knowledge [e.g., 83]. Managers who understand these patterns and trade-offs can provide better guidance and support to help employees effectively source and integrate knowledge.

Despite its importance, research pertaining to individuals’ use of both internal and external knowledge sources is underdeveloped and fragmented. Prior studies have provided some insights into the choices made between internal and external knowledge and the respective effects of their use. In particular, researchers have found that individuals generally prefer external knowledge to internal knowledge because they perceive external knowledge to be more innovative and because they believe that importing knowledge from outside the organization improves their status with peers [68,69]. Other studies have tapped into the effects of internal and external knowledge on individuals’ performance and found that each tends to lead to different outcomes. For instance, Teigland and Wasko [82,83] found that external codified knowledge sources contribute to individuals’ creative performance but not efficient performance, while seeking internal knowledge improves their general performance. King and Lekse [53] revealed that accessing internally-based knowledge sources indirectly enhances managers’ reputation and status, while searching for external knowledge produces more direct managerial benefits. Nevertheless, there are many underdeveloped areas of research, including the drivers of the use of internal and external knowledge as supplementary resources [4] and their impacts on specific workrelated outcomes.

Our goal is to address this shortfall by hypothesizing and testing the motivators of individuals’ use of internal and external knowledge sources and the outcomes of each type of knowledge sourcing. We approach this task from a job-related learning perspective because knowledge sourcing is in essence learning from others in work settings [35]. Having roots in the adult learning literature, this perspective suggests that an important reason why individuals engage in learning is that their jobs motivate them to acquire knowledge, which leads to improvements in their cognitive structures [42]. Accordingly, we propose a theoretical model suggesting that different cognitive demands associated with jobs drive knowledge sourcing, which in turn produces learning outcomes [42,54,61]. While different knowledge sources may compete for individuals’ attention, internal and external knowledge sources may also supplement each other in helping individuals address a variety of tasks and contributing to improvements in their cognitive structures. ## 2.2. Job demands for learning and knowledge sourcing

Job demands for learning describe the extent to which jobs pressure individuals to acquire knowledge and skills to perform certain tasks [35,61]. When faced with demanding tasks, individuals engage in learning from various sources to satisfy the perceived knowledge shortfall that they face [35,54]. To provide a detailed depiction of how job demands for learning influence individuals’ use of internal and external knowledge sources, we discuss the effects of three generic characteristics, namely, task interdependence, task complexity, and task nonroutineness. These characteristics were selected based on a synthesis of prior research. In the IS literature, the most influential work on task characteristics is the study of task-technology fit [33], where nonroutineness and interdependence are proposed to be two key characteristics of tasks that affect information use; we therefore include them. In addition, in the research on information seeking, task complexity has been found to be a key factor [10,11,23]. Although information seeking is different from knowledge sourcing [35], task complexity may affect knowledge sourcing because of the close relationship between knowledge and information, and it is therefore also included. Other characteristics in the literature, such as task difficulty and uncertainty, usually overlap with at least one of these three characteristics. For instance, task uncertainty has two dimensions: complexity and volatility [7]. While the former dimension is directly reflected, the latter is closely associated with task nonroutineness (e.g., both pertinent to the amount of exceptions). Therefore, task interdependence, task complexity, and task nonroutineness appear to be representative of the key relevant characteristics identified in the literature.

## Task interdependence

Task interdependence is the extent to which an individual must exchange outputs with co-workers to accomplish a task [60]. When task interdependence is high, sharing knowledge with others is necessary because individuals must rely on the results of colleagues’ work to finish their own work while also taking into consideration its impact on others [47]. Highly interdependent tasks also often require the development of shared knowledge amongst those involved [22], which is often developed via internal knowledge sourcing [35]. Seeking knowledge from co-workers or internal knowledge repositories can help produce a shared frame of reference through which individuals can understand each other’s efforts and thereby enhance cooperation [71]. Individuals whose tasks are highly interdependent are therefore likely to demonstrate internal knowledge sourcing behaviors. However, because task-related interdependence is predominantly internal, we do not expect it to predict external knowledge sourcing. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Task complexity

Task complexity refers to the level of cognitive demand associated with a task [12]. A task involving multiple distinct elements and diverse information cues demands a large amount of cognitive resources and is thus viewed as being complex [80,93]. Given that individuals have limited cognitive resources [44], complex tasks may impose psychological strain on individuals, causing them to adopt coping strategies to regulate stress [30]. One such coping strategy is knowledge sourcing, which allows individuals to reduce their own cognitive efforts by drawing upon others’ justified cognitive structures and insights [35,80]. However, the aspects of work that may make it cognitively demanding are not inherently restricted to the organization itself (and thus may emanate from internal or external dimensions). Therefore, individuals are likely to seek both internal and external knowledge when handling complex tasks [see 23], when they believe that doing so would reduce their cognitive load and consequently their level of stress. This leads to the following hypotheses:

H2A. Task complexity is positively related to internal knowledge sourcing.

H2B. Task complexity is positively related to external knowledge sourcing.

However, the relative impacts of task complexity on internal knowledge sourcing and external knowledge sourcing may differ. Compared to internal knowledge, external knowledge is often perceived to be more valuable by individuals, as it is often scarcer and may appear to be less flawed because it has not been exposed to direct scrutiny [68]. As a result, individuals often have a preference for external knowledge [e.g., 69] and may believe that external knowledge is more effective than internal knowledge in solving complex problems. Accordingly, individuals facing complex tasks may be more prone to seek knowledge from external sources. This is consistent with empirical research that has shown that individuals are less likely to consult internal information sources when task complexity increases [11]. Therefore, we expect:

H2C. Task complexity is more strongly (positively) associated with external knowledge sourcing than with internal knowledge sourcing.

## Task nonroutineness

Task nonroutineness describes the extent to which a task is predictable and analyzable [24,63]. Faced with non-routine tasks, individuals may feel unsatisfied with their existing knowledge when there are no adequate references or answers readily available [48]. This dissatisfaction may trigger the need to seek additional information and knowledge so that individuals can identify, analyze and eventually accomplish these tasks [50]. Therefore, individuals are likely to seek more knowledge from both internal and external knowledge sources to fill the gap between existing knowledge and the knowledge required to accomplish non-routine tasks [15,83]. Moreover, from the perspective of information processing, as a result of the increased amount of exceptions that non-routine tasks entail, individuals not only need to acquire more information and knowledge but also have to exercise discretion more often [51]. This typically makes them engage in active thinking that requires ongoing efforts in collecting and sharing facts and insights [90]. Research has shown that when task nonroutineness is high individuals become more active in personal exchanges with others [88]. Consulting internal and external knowledge sources is therefore instrumental in completing non-routine tasks, as it assists the cognitive processing individuals evoke in response to those tasks [18]. Based on these arguments, we propose the following hypotheses:

H3A. Task nonroutineness is positively related to internal knowledge sourcing.

H3B. Task nonroutineness is positively related to external knowledge sourcing.

However, individuals may find external knowledge more appealing than internal knowledge when dealing with non-routine tasks. This pattern appears similar to individuals’ preferences for external sources when handling complex tasks, but it has a very different underlying rationale. Different from complex and interdependent tasks, which are typically comprehensible with clearly defined boundaries and structures, non-routine tasks present challenges that are not structured and systematized within organizational processes and thus often appear novel [24]. Some are only new to individuals in the organization but not to the industry or profession, while others are completely new [63]. For the former, individuals may prefer outside sources because they can offer valuable insights derived from experiences that are not available internally. For the latter, which demand innovative ideas, external knowledge sources have been well documented as superior in supplying and stimulating the creation of such ideas [e.g., 55]. Therefore, when faced with non-routine tasks, individuals are likely to give greater weight to external knowledge sources because they believe that these sources are more likely to contain the new inputs needed to comprehend the tasks and create solutions and in turn assist in performing the work on hand. Accordingly, we predict:

H3C. Task nonroutineness is more strongly (positively) associated with external knowledge sourcing than with internal knowledge sourcing.

## 2.3. Internal knowledge sourcing and learning outcomes

There have been a number of conceptualizations of the outcomes of knowledge sourcing. Kankanhalli et al. [46] examine the overall learning effectiveness related to knowledge sourcing. Teigland and Wasko [82] measure efficient and creative perfor mance as the outcomes of knowledge transfer among individuals. Gray and Meister [35] categorize the outcomes of knowledge sourcing into three categories, namely, replication, adaptation, and innovation. Similarly, Lin et al. [59] use cognitive replication, cognitive adaptation and cognitive innovation to represent the learning outcomes of knowledge sourcing. The learning perspective we adopt directs our attention to learning outcomes instead of performance directly [e.g., 46]. We use an approach similar to those of Gray and Meister [35] and Lin et al. [59] in this research, as collectively, the three constructs (cognitive replication, adaptation, and innovation) are comprehensive and able to cover the learning outcomes identified by other researchers while simultaneously providing more fine-grained dependent variables that help us tease out the different effects of internal and external knowledge sourcing. Below, we provide a set of hypotheses to advance the understanding of the effects of internal knowledge sourcing on these three types of learning outcomes.

As a learning outcome, cognitive replication is the extent to which an individual’s cognitive structures have changed in such a way that they align with existing (proven) organizational knowledge [35]. Knowledge management initiatives are often intended to reuse organizational knowledge so that knowledge workers do not need to ‘‘reinvent the wheel’’ when encountering similar tasks [65]. Consistent with this purpose, organizations often improve the availability of internal knowledge through knowledge repositories and knowledge networks [2,45]. Prior research has found that such knowledge resources can help individuals accomplish recurring tasks in two ways [36]. First, they provide direct references for individuals to draw upon; for example, a consultant can reuse a proposal from a prior project for a similar project. Second, they provide opportunities for individuals to discuss the issues at hand with others and learn from their experiences and expertise. Thus, seeking knowledge internally helps individuals replicate existing knowledge, improving their cognitive structures and consequently their performance because individuals can absorb and apply justified practices that are tailored to the organization [26]. Thus, we hypothesize:

H4A. Individuals’ levels of internal knowledge sourcing are positively associated with their levels of cognitive replication.

Cognitive adaptation reflects the extent to which an individual’s cognitive structures have evolved (incrementally) in response to changes such as new requirements within the organization [35]. When faced with changes that are not addressed by existing knowledge, individuals must adapt to complete their work. Internal knowledge sourcing can help them adapt to changes in several ways. First, knowledge from internal sources constitutes an important part of the knowledge basis upon which individuals draw to make sense of changes [25]. Seeking insights from internal sources helps individuals appreciate changes within their own contexts [36] – it may improve their understanding of the meanings and consequences of changes in relation to their work, colleagues and even physical settings, which is crucial to adaptation given its situated nature [87]. With such an understanding, individuals can better identify well-grounded approaches to changes and respond in an effective fashion. Second, because adaptation to change often involves a series of trials and errors [e.g., 64], consulting internal knowledge sources may serve as a feedback mechanism by which individuals can evaluate and adjust their adaptation according to others’ inputs, which leads to reasonable and acceptable alternatives, instills confidence, and therefore facilitates the evolution and transition of mental models [5]. Thus, we hypothesize:

H4B. Individuals’ levels of internal knowledge sourcing are positively associated with their levels of cognitive adaptation.

Cognitive innovation describes the extent to which an individual has developed novel ways of thinking and has experienced creative new ideas [35]. The effect of internal knowledge sourcing on cognitive innovation may take place through the creation of knowledge via the interaction among different types of knowledge from a variety of knowledge sources [72]. Seeking knowledge from different internal knowledge sources exposes individuals to diverse perspectives and makes them perceive situations differently [36]. This change stimulates a fusion of perspectives and results in new ways of thinking as well as new ideas [83]. Additionally, absorbing external innovations requires certain levels of internal knowledge processing capability [20]. Sourcing information and knowledge within the organization contributes to the development of such a capability in individuals, which in turn fosters the identification, selection and integration of new ideas and practices in the environment [56]. Overall, engaging in internal knowledge sourcing is likely to help individuals become more innovative, which suggests the following:

H4C. Individuals’ levels of internal knowledge sourcing are positively associated with their levels of cognitive innovation.

## 2.4. External knowledge sourcing and learning outcomes

The definition of cognitive replication suggests that it has an internal focus and is a result of knowledge propagation and reuse within the organization [see 65]. It serves to assist individuals in completing recurring work that does not demand novel additions from the outside [35]. In this case, external knowledge sourcing is unlikely to be relevant to cognitive replication because individuals can accomplish such work effectively by simply learning and applying established or justified practices within the organization [92]. Knowledge that is obtained from outside an organization is often novel and different from internally established best practices; externally sourced knowledge thus triggers change or the augmentation of existing knowledge structures. Even when best practices are involved, importing them from outside the organization is likely to lead to cognitive adaptation and/or cognitive innovation and not cognitive replication.

H5A. Individuals’ levels of external knowledge sourcing are not associated with their levels of cognitive replication.

External knowledge sourcing may affect cognitive adaptation by presenting new inputs to address changes above and beyond what is available from internal sources [21]. External knowledge is often preferred by individuals, as it is perceived to be more valuable and less threatening [68]. Learning from outsiders experiences enables individuals to perceive changes from perspectives other than their own, leading to a more comprehensive understanding of a situation and an expanded solution pool [32]. As a result, individuals are better able to draw causal inferences in the presence of changes and come up with viable responses, both contributing to the adaptation of their cognitive structures [78]. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H5B. Individuals’ levels of external knowledge sourcing are positively associated with their levels of cognitive adaptation.

The most discussed contribution of external knowledge is its effect on innovation [e.g., 15,20]. Although internal knowledge sources are able to provide some new ideas, external contacts, publications, communities, institutes and databases constitute a crucial source of novel ideas and inputs [55,89]. For instance, research has found that scientists in cutting-edge technology firms draw heavily upon researchers at universities for new ideas [58]. Similarly, Perry-Smith [74] found that outside ties may stimulate creativity, especially for individuals who are on the periphery of an internal social network. Because external knowledge stems from outsiders who may have very different cognitive structures, it can extend individuals’ orientations considerably and inspire them to approach situations from new angles [89]. Therefore, individuals who source knowledge externally are likely to be more innovative because they benefit from the new knowledge offered directly by external sources as well as the knowledge created as the result of the integration of diverse perspectives [20,72,83]. This brings us to the following hypothesis:

H5C. Individuals’ levels of external knowledge sourcing are positively associated with their levels of cognitive innovation.

## 2.5. The interaction between internal and external knowledge sourcing

Although both internal and external knowledge sources can be beneficial to learning, as discussed previously, individuals have limited time and cognitive resources that prevent them from thoroughly consulting all possible sources [39]. For example, when paying more attention to internal knowledge sources, they allocate less effort to understanding and incorporating knowledge from external sources. They have biases, perhaps a preference for outsiders [68] or, alternately, a susceptibility to the not-inventedhere syndrome [49]. The more they use one type of sources, the less they are willing to invest in the other type. As a result, external knowledge is often accessed sub-optimally, generating outcomes less than its full potential. In the same vein, greater external knowledge sourcing may dilute the resources spent on comprehending and utilizing internal knowledge [38]. Internal and external knowledge sources may present different and at times inconsistent approaches that undermine each other. When knowledge from one type of sources is being sought more, knowledge from the other type of sources may be doubted and challenged more often, which interferes with the realization of its value.

H6A. There is an interaction effect between internal knowledge sourcing and external knowledge sourcing on cognitive adaptation such that when one increases, the effect of the other on cognitive adaptation becomes weaker.

H6B. There is an interaction effect between internal knowledge sourcing and external knowledge sourcing on cognitive innovation such that when one increases, the effect of the other on cognitive innovation becomes weaker.

## 3. Methodology

Data to test these hypotheses were collected at a major international technology company. Below, we discuss the operationalization of the constructs, the data collection procedure, and data analysis.

## 3.1. Operationalization of constructs

Self-report questions were used to measure constructs in this research project. All of the questions were transformed into 7- point Likert scales anchored in ‘‘strongly disagree’’ and ‘‘strongly agree’’ whenever possible. Appendix 2 lists the questions used in the survey.

For new constructs such as external knowledge sourcing, measures were developed through a thorough process suggested by Churchill [19]. In addition to carrying out a literature review and consulting with fellow researchers, interviews were conducted to gain an understanding of the practices at the research site when developing the measures. Definitions of key concepts such as knowledge and knowledge sources were discussed with several managers and employees and were subsequently amended to improve their wording and clarity. The measures were then verified by conducting a pilot study using 30 employees.

Internal knowledge sourcing and external knowledge sourcing were measured by asking individuals to evaluate the frequency with which they turned to each type of sources. To assess learning outcomes, the measures in Gray and Meister [35] were used, which asked individuals to compare the levels of their capabilities and knowledge with those of a year ago. For instance, one item that measured cognitive innovation read, ‘‘I have been very innovative in my thinking in the last year.’

To gauge task complexity, the items developed by Campbell [12] and Wood [93] were used with minor modifications. Items derived from the work of Goodhue and Thompson [33] and Kettinger and Grover [52] were used to measure task interdependence and task nonroutineness. Learning orientation, a control variable, was measured by items based on Brett and Vandewalle’s work [9].

## 3.2. Data collection

Because knowledge sourcing is likely to be influenced by the tools used and the social environment in which it occurs [8,96], a single research site was intentionally chosen to control for these effects. The manufacturing engineering division of an international technology company sponsored this study and allowed us to interview and survey its employees. The employees in this division were engineers and technical managers who dealt with a variety of tasks and needed to update their knowledge constantly to keep up with their work. Accordingly, this company had established internal knowledge repositories and directories and provided employees with access to external publications, databases, experts and professional organizations to support knowledge sourcing. It thus served as an appropriate research site, as knowledge sourcing was both well appreciated and practiced.

Dillman’s [29] approach was followed during the data collection procedure. Two top managers initially sent out email invitations to participate in the electronic survey to 1009 employees, encouraging participation and assuring them of anonymity and voluntariness. They also followed up with two reminders 5 and 10 days after the initial invitation. Over a 14-day period, 417 employees completed the survey, producing a response rate of 41%. The average age of the respondents was 36 years, and the average tenure in the company was 6.3 years. Approximately onethird were female, 59% were front-line employees, and 75% were involved in technical work. Eighty-four percent were located in the United States, and the rest were distributed globally with no dominant second country.

We screened the responses before proceeding to data analysis. We removed the responses of the individuals whose job tenures were less than a year, because we measured learning outcomes by asking individuals to evaluate their cognitive structure improvements over a period of one year, and these individuals could not provide an appropriate assessment. We also dropped responses with many missing entries (25% and above). The screening process ultimately left us with 313 usable responses. The individuals in the sample reported fairly frequent use of various methods to source knowledge. For instance, on average, they would use email almost once a day (mean = 5.92; 9-point scale with 0 – ‘‘Never’’, 2 – ‘‘Once a month’’, 4 – ‘‘Once a week’’, 6 – ‘‘Once a day’’, and 8 – ‘‘Once an hour’’), the intranet several times a week (mean = 4.80), electronic discussion groups once every few months (mean = 1.6), and electronic repositories more than once a month (mean = 2.29) for knowledge sourcing.

## 3.3. Data analysis

We analyzed data using Partial Least Squares (PLS) analysis. PLS is an advanced structural equation modeling technique that tests the measurement model and theoretical model simultaneously [16]. The results of PLS analysis include indicators to assess reliability, convergent validity and discriminant validity for the measurement model as well as t-statistics of path coefficients for hypothesis testing. The computer program used was SmartPLS 2.0 M3 [77].

One item was trimmed from the task interdependence scale, as it referred to the behavior of individuals to consult other employees and thus could be conflated with internal knowledge sourcing. Two rules were followed in the examination of reliability. First, to accept an item, its loading on its underlying construct should be higher than 0.7, which implies that the item shares more variance with the construct than with error [13]. Second, the composite reliability of a construct should be higher than 0.7 [73]. As shown in Table 1 and Table 3, after dropping items that did not perform well, all constructs reached the benchmarks, demonstrating that the measures were reliable.

## 3.3.2. Theoretical model

We ran a bootstrapping analysis in PLS with the option of 500 subsamples of size 313 to test hypotheses [16]. Table 4 shows the standardized path coefficients and their significance levels. Fig. 2 presents the results, with solid lines indicating significant relationships and dotted lines showing non-significant relationships.

Among the antecedents of knowledge sourcing, task interdependence was positively related to internal knowledge sourcing, task complexity was positively related to external knowledge sourcing, and task nonroutineness was positively associated with internal knowledge sourcing, supporting H1A, H2B and H3A, respectively. Together with the control variables (i.e., learning orientation and demographic variables), they explained 15.3% of the variance in internal knowledge sourcing and 12.8% of the variance in external knowledge sourcing. Regarding the outcomes, internal knowledge sourcing was found to have positive effects on individuals’ learning outcomes regarding replication, adaptation and innovation; therefore, H4A, H4B, and H4C were supported. External knowledge sourcing was positively associated with individuals’ learning outcomes regarding adaptation and innovation, supporting H5B and H5C. In addition, consistent with H6A and H6B, there were negative and significant interaction effects between internal and external knowledge sourcing on cognitive adaptation and cognitive innovation. The overall model including the control variables explained 12.6%, 23.3% and 17% of the variance in replication, adaptation and innovation learning outcomes, respectively.

## 4. Discussion and Implications

Our goal was to provide a holistic view of the ways in which individuals leverage internal and external knowledge via a taskdriven learning model. The results support thirteen hypotheses and present interesting patterns of knowledge sourcing.

## 4.1. The role of tasks

Our findings suggest that internal knowledge sourcing is a mechanism by which individuals seek to enhance their ability to collaborate with coworkers. According to Cramton [22], a shared knowledge base is often key to effective collaboration. Our results demonstrate that internal knowledge sourcing is a means by which mutual knowledge is negotiated and integrated on an ongoing basis. That is, individuals turn to internal knowledge when performing interdependent tasks. This facilitates the building of mutual knowledge because individuals either acquire public knowledge shared by co-workers or seek knowledge from each other directly, reducing barriers to collaboration [e.g., 71].

The finding that individuals tend to use external knowledge but not internal knowledge when dealing with complex tasks concurs with the favoritism toward external knowledge found by Menon and Pfeffer [68]. According to Menon and Pfeffer [68], individuals prefer seeking knowledge from external resources over seeking knowledge from internal resources because they value external knowledge more than internal knowledge. In this vein, when they have complicated problems to solve, they would consult external knowledge sources, if possible. And if their knowledge needs are indeed met, there is no need to seek internal knowledge because this means extra and nonproductive effort and is inconsistent with the purpose of reducing cognitive load and stress caused by complex tasks.

We were therefore surprised to see an entirely different pattern regarding non-routine tasks. Contrary to what we hypothesized, task nonroutineness was not significantly related to external knowledge sourcing. A possible explanation may be that when employees face non-routine tasks, they may not understand enough about the tasks to know what knowledge they need to obtain. In this case, they need to seek inputs to understand the new tasks [e.g., 24]. Internal sources, especially internal experts, may be more effective in this sense-making process because they can provide situated interpretations, and communication is easier [62,83]. In addition to helping make sense of tasks, interactions with internal knowledge sources may also shed light on how to address them. The articulation of problems, the combination of explicit knowledge from knowledge repositories, and the internalization of knowledge sought all stimulate the creation of new knowledge that can be applied to non-routine tasks [72]. This may help explain why individuals seem to rely on internal knowledge sources when faced with non-routine tasks.

Knowledge of how tasks with different characteristics may affect knowledge sourcing provides a foundation on which managers can structure knowledge management practices and organize IT resources to better meet individuals’ knowledge needs. As suggested by Hansen and Hass [39], it is beneficial to be selective in providing knowledge resources because individuals have limited cognitive resources available for learning. Therefore, for individuals whose jobs are non-routine and/or dependent on others’ work, it might be beneficial to strengthen internal knowledge repositories and communication. Advanced knowledge management systems and electronic communication and collaboration tools (e.g., internal wikis and intranet) could be used to this end and are thus worth consideration. On the other hand, for those whose jobs are complex, initiatives that stress openness to external environments (e.g., inter-organizational networks and participation in professional development in the industry) may fit their needs more appropriately and thus deserve more managerial attention. Accordingly, IT applications that give them access to external codified knowledge (e.g., a portal to a collection of research reports) and allow them to be connected to the professional community (e.g., social media) may be helpful in this regard.

## 4.2. The direct and interaction effects of internal and external knowledge sourcing

On the outcome side, the results illustrate the respective benefits of internal and external knowledge sourcing as well as the interaction between the two types of knowledge sourcing. As expected, internal knowledge sourcing helps with the acquisition of established knowledge in the organization. Additionally, both types contribute to individuals’ adjustment of their underlying cognitive structures in light of change and are instrumental in generating novel ideas and approaches. Nevertheless, there are negative interaction effects - when one type becomes more active, the benefits of the other start to diminish. This observation is consistent with prior findings about individuals’ limited cognitive resources and may be a result of their preference for either type [e.g., 39,68].

Organizations can adjust their knowledge management strategies and IT investments based on these findings to achieve their intended outcomes. For instance, if the goal is to improve efficiency in recurring work, a strategy with the focus on knowledge sharing and reuse within the organization would be preferable, in which case investments in IT applications that facilitate these are likely to pay off. If the intended outcome is prepared individuals who help the organization survive within turbulent environments and/or creative individuals who contribute to organizational performance through innovation, initiatives that advocate the use of both internal and external knowledge resources rather than just one of them are more likely to be appropriate. Accordingly, investments in both internal-oriented and external-oriented technologies are needed. Moreover, organizations need to be mindful of the interaction effects between internal and external knowledge sourcing. They should take a cultivating approach and help individuals find the right balance that leads to maximal benefits in their own environments. Simply increasing the time available for knowledge sourcing will not necessarily lead to robust outcomes. Strategies must be carefully planned with an understanding of the interaction effects between internal and external knowledge sourcing. There are no simple shortcuts, and there are potential drawbacks. Another point to note is that these knowledge management strategies are neither static nor mutually exclusive – organizations can apply them to different contexts (e.g., functional groups) simultaneously and should align them with the overall organizational strategy on an ongoing basis [28].

Moreover, the results enrich our understanding of how individuals use internal and external knowledge by presenting an alternative view of the relationship between the two that stresses that they are ‘‘coopetitive’’ [cooperative and competitive, 84] rather than mutually exclusive in improving individuals’ overall performance. Prior research on knowledge valuation and choice to some extent assumes that knowledge from internal and external sources can provide similar utility even though they may present different approaches, and as a result, people simply choose between them [e.g., 68,69]. This is mostly valid given the ample supply of knowledge currently available on both sides [39]. However, this is not always the case. Evidence at the firm level suggests that internal and external knowledge sources could supplement each other [15]. Inspired by this notion, our research explores the potential coopetition between internal and external knowledge at the individual level and examines the role of tasks. The focus is on individuals’ overall knowledge use patterns across tasks; while it could be an either-or choice when individuals deal with a single task, they are likely to combine both types of knowledge resources to cope with the knowledge needs induced by diverse tasks [e.g., 10]. The results regarding the effects of tasks demonstrate that internal and external knowledge resources do indeed supplement each other in helping people address tasks with different characteristics. The cooperative and competitive relationship is more pronounced with the results related to the learning outcomes. On the one hand, both internal and external knowledge sources contribute to cognitive adaptation as well as cognitive innovation. satisfving different knowledge requirements and offering verification for each other when necessary. On the other hand, the negative interaction suggests they, at the same time, compete for individuals’ cognitive resources. Therefore, in addition to identifying a set of significant antecedents of internal and external knowledge sourcing, this study contributes to the literature by suggesting a new direction for future research in this area. For instance, it may be interesting for future research to explore the notion of a knowledge source portfolio.

Another important insight obtained from the results is that the role of a knowledge integrator seems to be spread across a broad range of individual knowledge workers and not a select few. From an organizational design perspective, many scholars have emphasized boundary roles in the integration of internal and external knowledge [e.g., 57,85]. Individuals occupying boundary-spanning positions are able to bridge internal and external knowledge because they have the necessary connections and competence [86]. However, this study presents a somewhat different picture – according to the descriptive statistics of external knowledge sourcing in Table 1, employees at the research site in general were involved in some levels of external knowledge sourcing. This suggests that many knowledge workers may be involved in boundary spanning regardless of their positions in the organization. In this case, it would be beneficial to consider the contributions and impacts of these knowledge workers in addition to those in boundary spanning roles, especially in an information age in which individuals have easy access to a vast array of intellectual resources through IT. Knowledge integration is likely to be a collective endeavor undertaken by all knowledge workers rather than by a few assigned agents.

This insight is crucial to managers who wish to foster knowledge integration, as it reveals a bottom-up (and mostly informal) mode of knowledge integration. Driven by the tasks at hand and empowered by IT, individuals seek knowledge from a variety of internal and external knowledge sources. This search can reinforce the knowledge integration initiated by formal boundary spanners or by the organization if they share the same pattern. However, when there is inconsistency between formal knowledge integration initiatives and individuals’ actual use of internal and external knowledge, the effectiveness of knowledge integration may be undermined. For instance, if everyone needs to access external knowledge and if this access is only granted to a few assigned boundary spanners (e.g., the research team at a consulting firm), it would not be surprising to observe how little external knowledge is actually integrated, let alone the burnout of boundary spanners [e.g., 79]. Therefore, instead of relying solely on formal boundary roles, organizations should pay attention to how average knowledge workers utilize internal and external knowledge resources and then apply appropriate managerial interventions.

## 4.3. Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, following the job related learning perspective, this study emphasizes an instrumental view of knowledge sourcing. Other useful views, such as the social image perspective [69], are not included. Many relevant factors such as norms and relational benefits [46,94] are not integrated into the research model. This may explain the relatively small variance explained by the model (mostly less than 10%, excluding the effects of control variables). While our model contributes to the literature by presenting a unique perspective, future research that develops an integrative model that considers a number of different perspectives can surely enhance the understanding of the phenomenon examined. Second, our data were collected at a single research site, limiting the ability to generalize the findings. This may be another factor that explains the relatively small variance explained - these values could be unique to our research site. This firm is a leader in its industry and it hires motivated employees who are eager to learn, which may downplay the role of tasks. These employees may have also established their own knowledge base and thus may not have needed to source as much knowledge for things they were familiar with, which may explain the relatively limited contribution of knowledge sourcing to some of the cognitive outcomes. The results for employees of other firms might not follow the same pattern. Our purpose was to control for the potential effects of environmental factors (e.g., management support, knowledge sharing norms, knowledge accessibly, etc.). Future research can test the proposed model in multiple settings to validate the findings and provide insights into the potential interactions between the model and environmental factors. Third, we employed only one data collection method, namely a self-report survey, in this study; therefore, the results are subject to common method bias. We conducted a Harmon onefactor test to assess the severity of this bias [76]. The results demonstrated no sign of severe common method bias. Nevertheless, future research that employs multiple methods can increase the confidence in the findings. Fourth, some of the constructs were measured by two items. Although the measures show good face validity, convergent validity, discriminant validity and nomological validity, scales with more items would certainly increase confidence in the results. Scale development is thus a potential direction for future research.

## 5. Conclusions

A key to the success of knowledge management is the effective use of available knowledge resources by individual knowledge workers [35]. In line with this demand perspective, we have proposed and tested a learning-based model to explore how individuals use internal and external knowledge in light of the strategic importance of knowledge integration. In general, we found that individuals use internal and external knowledge discriminatingly to cope with tasks of different characteristics and achieve different learning outcomes accordingly. Interdependent tasks drive internal knowledge sourcing, complex tasks motivate individuals to seek ‘shortcuts’ from external knowledge sources, and non-routine tasks draw individuals to internal knowledge sources for interpretation. Moreover, learning from internal knowledge sources helps individuals replicate existing knowledge in the organization, adapt their cognitive structures to the changing environment, and be innovative; learning from external knowledge sources assists in adaptation and the creation of innovative thinking and novel ideas. For researchers, the results provide insights into the antecedents and outcomes of internal and external knowledge sourcing, shedding light on how knowledge integration occurs at the individual level. For managers, this study provides guidelines to organize IT resources and facilitate knowledge use depending on task characteristics and expected outcomes.
