Research Paper

# Disruption as worldview change: A Kuhnian analysis of the digital music revolution

Kai Riemer and Robert B Johnston

Journal of Information Technology 1–21 © Association for Information Technology Trust 2019 Article reuse guidelines: sagepub.com/journals-permissions https://doi.org/10.1177/026839621983510DOI: 10.1177/0268396219835101 Journals.sagepub.com/jinf ⑤SAGE

## Abstract

Why is it that technology-enabled industry disruptions appear entirely inevitable with hindsight, yet practitioners in disrupted businesses typically struggle to detect and respond appropriately to disruption while it is unfolding? We term this surprising contradiction ‘interpretive discontinuity’ and use it to problematize the established understanding of disruption in the literature. We suggest that the contradiction at the heart of interpretive discontinuity holds an important key to what exactly changes during disruption and why. By juxtaposing an empirical case of disruption in the music industry with theoretical resources sensitive to the nature of radical change – Thomas Kuhn’s work in the unrelated field of scientific practice – we demonstrate that it is productive to understand disruption as a Kuhnian paradigm shift. We are then able to trace interpretive discontinuity to the gestalt switch in worldview that accompanies such a paradigm shift. This insight sheds new light on both what is actually ‘disruptive’ about disruption and also on the limitations of prior work theorizing disruption. Our work is important because it adds to the literature on disruptive innovation important yet overlooked conceptual tools in Kuhn’s work – the role of exemplars, the worldview aspect of a paradigm, and paradigm incommensurability

## Keywords

Disruption, innovation, Kuhn, paradigm shift music industry, mp3

## Introduction

I had no idea what would come next, and I met Karlheinz Brandenburg and he was talking about this great new thing, that would just be the coolest, you could play music on your phone, on your cellphone, and I remember thinking ‘who cares, I don’t need to play music on my phone’, I just did not see what the mp3, what the future was going to be, I did not see it coming. (Suzanne Vega, in: Chermayeff and Dupre, 2016)

Our kids, our grandkids will literally be baffled by the idea that at one point people owned music. (Moby, in: Chermayeff and Dupre, 2016)

When the music compression standard mp3 emerged in the late 1990s, its significance for the music industry was initially widely missed and dismissed both within the industry and in society more generally. The first quote above expresses this and carries particular significance because Suzanne Vega’s ‘Tom’s Diner’ famously was one of the songs that mp3 developer Karlheinz Brandenburg and his team used to improve their compression algorithm that later underpinned mp3 (Witt, 2015). Today, with hindsight, such scepticism seems foolish and we ask ‘How could people not have seen this?’. Importantly, the changes set off by mp3 have fundamentally altered the music industry, invalidating incumbent business models that were based on selling ‘records’ (LPs, cassettes, and CDs) and at the same time produced new market entrants, most notably Apple with its iPod and iTunes store, and streaming services such as Pandora or Spotify. Moreover, having become a normal and taken-for-granted part of life today, these changes in music consumption via download or streaming look entirely inevitable, while old practices of buying CDs are dying out and appear decidedly outdated to younger consumers, as expressed in the second quote.

In this article, we argue that the apparent contradiction between the two opening quotes – that the disruptive potential of technological innovations is frequently missed initially but appears self-evident with hindsight – is both an important characteristic of industry disruptions more generally and also provides a conceptual portal into understanding the nature of technology-enabled business disruption in a more nuanced way. We name this apparent contradiction the ‘temporal discontinuity in interpreting disruption’, or ‘interpretive discontinuity’ for short.

Interpretive discontinuity is important because both common sense and the approaches taken in the academic literature on technology-enabled innovation would suggest that the inherent logical necessity of the technological change that we see clearly in hindsight should also have been available to incumbents in the disrupted industry as the changes unfolded and could have been used by them to develop either more effective defensive tactics or to exploit the new technological developments themselves. In fact, as we will show, this assumption underpins typical attempts to theorize disruption and offer normative advice about ‘how to cope with technological disruption’. For the music industry, we now know this was not the case, and the result was that the commercial organization of the whole industry was disrupted, with many formerly lucrative incumbent businesses failing or being diminished in importance in the process. While we will evidence these outcomes for the music industry, we will argue that they are part of the disruption phenomenon more generally.

Thus, ‘interpretive discontinuity’ presents a surprise that suggests that a theoretically interesting contradiction resides within the ‘common wisdom’ on digital disruption. According to Alvesson and Kärreman (2007), surprises arise from unexamined assumptions; they provide an important opportunity to reveal and challenge such otherwise hidden assumptions of a field and, as a result, to develop new theory. In this article, we make use of ‘interpretive discontinuity’ in this way to surface and challenge certain widely accepted tacit assumptions underpinning both existing theorizing and common-sense understanding that give rise to this surprising phenomenon. By challenging these, we develop new theoretical insights about what changes actually take place during technology-enabled industry disruptions, beyond the well-researched role that technology affords in creating new products and changing production and consumption practices. Our research question then is ‘What does the phenomenon of interpretive discontinuity reveal about the nature of industry disruptions?’.

To address this question, we present a detailed empirical account of how mp3-enabled digital disruption unfolded in the music industry, drawn from extensive secondary sources. We then interrogate this with Kuhn’s (1962) welldeveloped theoretical account of revolutionary change in the largely unrelated field of scientific practice. Drawing parallels between scientific revolutions and industry disruptions allows us to explain how technology-enabled innovations induce discontinuous shifts, not only in how business is done, but more importantly, in how business is understood – how industry participants make sense of the technology, their industry, the value offered by their prod ucts, and what are valid forms of innovations to pursue. Specifically, we liken such shifts to Kuhn’s notion of paradigm shift and demonstrate that it is the incommensurability of the worldviews at the heart of two competing paradigms involved in such a shift that is responsible for the phenomenon of interpretive discontinuity.

These insights are important for two reasons. First, since the changes set off by radical innovations frequently threaten the viability of incumbent business models (Tushman and Anderson, 1986), a better understanding of why incumbents are frequently unable to deal with such innovations is warranted (Christensen et al., 2015; Markides, 2006), especially as incumbent decision-makers are often blamed in hindsight for such ‘failures’. Second, our insights into why unfolding disruptions are frequently invisible at the time puts in question the feasibility of theories predicting ex ante the disruptive potential of emerging innovations and their future development trajectories (cf. Govindarajan and Kopalle, 2006; Schmidt, 2004).

We contribute to the literature a set of theoretical propo sitions that outline an alternative understanding of technology-enabled business disruption, whereby disruption results from a change of worldview in the industry. We further contribute to practice a tentative list of tactics for how incumbents can approach an unfolding paradigm shift.

## Research approach

Our research objective is to surface assumptions underpinning the accepted narratives about digital disruption and to challenge these as a way of advancing theory in the area. To do this, we must employ a research approach explicitly suited to providing access to the normally unstated assumptions of a field. Alvesson and co-workers (Alvesson and Kärreman, 2007; Alvesson and Sköldberg, 2009) have developed such an approach under the rubric ‘problematization’ (Alvesson and Sandberg, 2011; Alvesson and Sandberg, 2013: 144). Their approach focuses on the opportunity that ‘surprising’ observations provide to uncover and challenge tacit assumptions because surprises arise from conflict between expectation (informed by implicit theory) and actuality (empirical experience). It should be noted that their approach makes use of empirical data and theory in a quite novel way, compared to the standard options (Eisenhardt, 1989) of theory building or theory testing.

First, they argue that researching surprising phenomena requires a more symmetrical approach to empirical and theoretical materials (Alvesson and Kärreman, 2007) than is usual. This is because, the theory-laden nature of ‘facts lies at the heart of the phenomenon of surprise (Alvesson and Kärreman, 2007: 1287). As a result, a researcher must engage in an iterated, dialectical interrogation (Alvesson and Sandberg, 2011: 252) of data with theory and theory with data, to question the supposed ‘facts’ of the field. This makes it possible to challenge and replace underlying assumptions (Alvesson and Sandberg, 2013) as a path to a new ‘way of seeing’ the phenomena of the field (Alvesson and Sandberg, 2014).

Second, the nature of the surprise also guides the choice of both empirical and theoretical materials. Empirical materials are chosen to elaborate and contextualize a particular instance of the surprise in detail; theoretical materials are chosen for their ability to provide deeper access to the nature of the surprise than is common in the field, and therefore, to undercut crucial unreflective ‘wisdoms’ of the field (Alvesson and Kärreman, 2007).

For this project, we applied these ideas as follows. We took ‘interpretive discontinuity’, as outlined in the introduction, as such a revelatory surprise. We chose the disruptive change wrought within the music retailing industry by the digital music compression standard mp3 as our source of empirical material because it clearly exhibits our surprising phenomenon and its history is extensively documented. To interrogate these empirical materials, we chose Kuhn’s theoretical account of scientific revolutions because his explanations of changes in understanding and worldview within scientific practice hold the key to explaining how the phenomenon of ‘interpretive discontinuity’ arises in business. We elaborate on this in the section ‘Theoretical material: Kuhn’s account of scientific revolutions’. Through an extended and iterated dialectical reinterpretation of both the data of the music industry case and our understanding of digital disruption, we were led to propose that disruption of the music industry by mp3 was not just a change in technological trajectory, but rather a change in worldview within the industry. Our article shows how this insight resolves ‘interpretive discontinuity’ and points to a richer understanding of digital disruption more generally.

Such an approach to research has been described as ‘assumption-challenging’ (Alvesson and Sandberg, 2013) rather than ‘gap-spotting’ (Alvesson and Sandberg, 2011), and as such, it needs to be communicated in a way consistent with this character. This is why the organization of the report is somewhat at odds with the ‘standard academic genre’ (Avital et al., 2017) associated with gap-spotting research. First, two cuts on evaluating existing literature are required: the first is devoted to documenting the surprise, how it is currently understood, and what the current literature leaves unexplained about it; the second is a reevaluation of the literature challenging certain foundational assumption in the light of our dialectic analysis, and so must await later developments in the article. Second, while the actual research was highly iterative, for clarity, the process is ‘cleaned-up’ and communicated as a single dialectic episode – literature analysis, case description, reference theory exposition, re-analysis of case through the reference theory, and synthesis of new domain theoretical insights.

## Literature

The literature on disruptive innovation is vast and spans multiple fields and disciplines. In this article, we concentrate on the phenomenon of disruptive innovation at the sector, industry and societal level – specifically how disruptions come about and what makes technological innovations disruptive. Consequently, works on organizational adoption of disruptive technologies at the intra-firm level (Carlo et al., 2014; Sherif et al., 2006) are considered out of scope. This also includes studies that discuss the impact of disruptive technologies on internal IT innovation (Lyytinen and Rose, 2003) and software development practices (Carlo et al., 2011), or how firms adapt to such technologies internally (Lyytinen et al., 2010).

Furthermore, we concentrate on those works that are relevant to our research question because they recognize certain aspects of the phenomenon of interpretive discontinuity and explicitly provide explanations for why incumbents miss or dismiss unfolding disruptions. In keeping with our research approach, we do not seek to spot gaps in this literature that our research might fill but rather to identify and challenge implicit assumptions underpinning these works. We begin with a brief outline of key terminology.

## Terminology

The term ‘disruption’ has been applied to a wide range of change-related phenomena in both academia and in public discourse, where it has lately been used as a synonym for change itself (Gobble, 2015). Such casual use brings the risk of losing the distinctive power of the term (Yglesias, 2013). It also ignores that truly disruptive change does not occur that often (DeTienne and Koberg, 2002). Furthermore, technology-enabled disruption has been theorized in a range of academic fields, such as new product development (Danneels, 2006), engineering and technology management (Augsdörfer et al., 2013a; Kirchhoff et al., 2002), information systems (Lucas and Goh, 2009), business management (Tushman and Anderson, 1986), and leadership (Christensen and Overdorf, 2000). Given the breadth of this literature, a lack of consistency in the use of key terms is unsurprising (Augsdörfer et al., 2013b; Garcia and Calantone, 2002) and requires that we are explicit about our own definitional choices.

In this work, we employ the following nomenclature (Garcia and Calantone, 2002): ‘transformational’ refers to the way in which novel technologies open up entirely new action possibilities for entrepreneurs and consumers; ‘radicalness’ refers to the degree to which such technologies result in new-to-the-world products and services; ‘(dis)continuity’ refers to the changes in the trajectories of markets or industries that result from the emergence of radical innovations; and ‘disruptiveness’ refers to the effects that certain kinds of innovations have on incumbent businesses. The relation between these terms in the literature is as follows.

‘Transformational technologies’ (Lucas and Goh, 2009) typically result from new scientific discoveries or engineering inventions and are thus based on a ‘new knowledge base’ (Hill and Rothaermel, 2003: 258). Such technologies disclose, initially through experimentation or tinkering, novel possibilities for human action. As a result, they provide opportunities for entirely new product and service designs. It is thus important to distinguish between ‘invention’, the creation of a new technology, and ‘innovation’, the exploitation and commercialization thereof (Hill and Rothaermel, 2003).

‘Radical innovations’ are new products that are based on transformational technologies (Danneels, 2004); they contrast with incremental innovations that originate from improvements to well-understood and already adopted technologies. As a result, radical innovations offer newto-the-world value (Slater et al., 2014). Yet, many radical innovations are never broadly adopted and thus fail to have any disruptive impact in their markets (Hill and Rothaermel, 2003).

At the industry level, radical innovations can result in discontinuities in the trajectories along which competition unfolds and products are developed (Bergek et al., 2013; Henfridsson and Yoo, 2014; Veryzer, 1998) – changes in the ‘rules of the game’ (Phillips et al., 2006) and ‘the performance metrics along which firms compete’ (Danneels, 2004: 249). Several of the dominant frameworks in the study of such ‘discontinuous change’ are inspired, either directly (Christensen and Rosenbloom, 1995; Dosi, 1982) or indirectly, by Kuhn’s notion of a paradigm, which underpins concepts such as dominant designs (Tushman and Anderson, 1986), dominant logics (Bettis and Prahalad, 1995), and technological regimes (Kemp, 1994; Nelson and Winter, 1982).<sup>1</sup> Common to all is that they theorize the effects of radical innovations as discontinuous shifts in ‘technological paradigms’, which break with established industry-wide ways of doing business and put the industry on a new development trajectory.

Finally, the term ‘disruption’ describes the effects that such discontinuities have for industry actors, most notably the threat that new market entrants bring to the viability of incumbent businesses (Garcia and Calantone, 2002). The theory most widely employed is the Theory of Disruptive Innovation (Bower and Christensen, 1995; Christensen, 1997), developed to explain how large incumbent businesses can be disrupted by much smaller new industry entrants. The theory stipulates that incumbent businesses are often displaced by new actors that employ transformative technologies with new performance attributes (Adner, 2002; Danneels, 2004; Garcia and Calantone, 2002; Yu and

Hang, 2009) that initially only attract niche customers because they are ‘worse, initially, as judged by the performance metrics that mainstream customers value’ (Christensen and Overdorf, 2000: 72). Over time however, the new products ‘improve at such a rapid rate that they can squarely address mainstream market needs in the future (Christensen et al., 2002: 961), thus disrupting the incumbent business.<sup>2</sup>

## Analysis of interpretive discontinuity in the literature

The struggles of incumbent businesses to deal with radical innovations as they emerge are widely documented in the literature (Sandström et al., 2009) and expressed in terms such as the ‘incumbent’s curse’ (Chandy and Tellis, 2000) or the ‘innovator’s dilemma’ (Christensen, 1997). However, a detailed analysis of the literature reveals that scholars have offered a wide range of different explanations for why incumbent businesses and their managers frequently fail to avert unfolding disruption to their businesses. Broadly, there are two groups of explanations (Table 1):

1. Explanations of why incumbents fail to notice or make sense of an emerging innovation: failure to recognize the disruptive potential of emerging innovations is variously attributed to limitations in the cognitive capabilities of incumbent executives such as: management errors (Ghemawat, 1991); outdated beliefs (Tripsas, 2009); ineptitude (Lucas and Goh, 2009); irrationality (Danneels, 2004); distraction of attention due to a focus on the core business (Christensen, 1997); or the complexity of evaluating competing, alternative innovations (Dosi, 1982).

2. Explanations of why incumbents fail to react to the unfolding threat: a number of authors argue that even though incumbents are frequently aware of the unfolding disruption they still fail to adjust due to: organizational inertia (Tripsas, 2009); resource constraints (Christensen and Rosenbloom, 1995) lack of capabilities (Karimi and Walter, 2015); pressures from external stakeholders such as investors; or the fact that it is not economical to make the shift in the short term for fear of cannibalizing existing products (Hill and Rothaermel, 2003), thus missing the opportunity to take on board the innovation (Chandy and Tellis, 2000).

In sum, the state of the literature appears unsatisfactory because, not only does no clear agreement exist about why incumbents fail to deal with emerging disruptions, but the explanations listed above (Table 1) are also contradictory: it remains unclear whether incumbents are, for whatever reason, unable to see the unfolding disruption or merely unable to react to it.

In the following, we do not seek to reconcile existing explanations of interpretive discontinuity or add to this list. Rather, we argue that the existing divergence in explanations indicates that a different approach altogether is warranted, one that problematizes the established understanding of industry disruption and ultimately reveals and challenges implicit assumptions that underlie this body of research. To do so, we must study carefully what happens during the process of disruption as it unfolds. To this end, we present a detailed empirical account of disruption in the music industry.

## Empirical material: disruption of the music industry by mp3

In this section, we give a preliminary account of disruption in the music industry set off by the release of the file compression algorithm mp3 and fuelled by both the emergence of the Internet and developments in mobile technology. This empirical material will provide a definite instance of technology-enabled disruption to interrogate with our theoretical materials in a later section.

Our account draws on extensive secondary literature in the form of books, films, and online materials on the history of music recording and sound (Chermayeff and Dupre, 2016; Katz, 2010; Millard, 2005; Milner, 2009), music as a business (Suisman, 2009), music consumption (Albright, 2015), the mp3 format (Sterne, 2012), Napster (Menn, 2003; The New York Times, 2014; Winter, 2013), piracy (Cummings, 2013; Kernfeld, 2011), and song sharing more specifically (Witt, 2015).

This case is by no means unique. Similar dynamics, including the phenomenon of interpretive discontinuity, can be found in other cases of radical innovation, such as the emergence of smartphones (McNish and Silcoff, 2015; Silcoff et al., 2013), digital photography (Larish, 2012; Lucas and Goh, 2009), or earlier ones such as the personal computer.

## Mp3 as a transformational technology

Mp3 is emblematic of the digital music revolution. Technically, mp3 refers to the standard by which music is converted into digital files used by digital music players (e.g. the iPod), smartphones (e.g. the iPhone), and streaming services (e.g. Spotify). Today, mp3 and subsequent variants (e.g. MPEG-4, Ogg, and AAC) underpin how music is normally consumed.

Mp3 was developed at the University of Hannover and the Fraunhofer Institute of Integrated Circuits in Germany and later standardized by the Moving Pictures Experts Group, MPEG (Milner, 2009). It originated from a decade of research on psychoacoustics, the study of human sound perception (Sterne, 2012). Based directly on these findings, mp3 sound compression works by dropping the parts of the full-spectrum digital sound format used on a CD that are inaudible to the human ear (Katz, 2010). However, to achieve a result that was acceptable to a human listener took years of experimentation, thousands of hours of laboratory testing using ‘difficult’ sound samples, such as Suzanne Vega’s Tom’s Diner and tweaking of the compression algorithm to create the final mp3 (for more, see Witt, 2015). The resultant file compression required about onetwelfth the storage of full-spectrum digital sound (Katz, 2010). The mp3 standard was officially published in 1993, and in 1994 the Fraunhofer Institute released its encoder I3enc. With the release of the WinPlay3 software in 1993, end-users were able to create their own mp3 files for the first time. This release coincided with the release of the Intel Pentium processor chips, which were powerful enough to enable home users to encode their own mp3 files by ‘ripping’ music from CDs (Millard, 2005).

## Online file sharing and the issue of piracy

Mp3 would not have had any significant impact without the rapid rise of the Internet in the years after its release, which enabled users to share both the software tools for creating and playing mp3 as well as the music itself compressed by mp3 to palatable size to accommodate the low Internet bandwidth available at the time. The year 1997 marked the launch of the website mp3.com that provided a list of the growing number of websites that shared mp3 files (Millard, 2005). At the same time, a growing number of secretive underground communities emerged operating through Internet Relay Chat (ICR) that were devoted to releasing music albums as mp3 files before their official release dates. These sites were fed by rogue individuals from inside the music industry supply chain who were responsible for the vast majority of pirated music shared online in the years to come (Witt, 2015).

But it was the launch in 1999 of the file sharing software Napster that enabled everyday computer users to easily share music directly from their own hard drives with a large group of anonymous others (Winter, 2013). Napster grew rapidly during the year 2000, from half a million to beyond 40 million users by the end of the year (Millard, 2005: 394). The music industry reacted to the success of Napster by opening legal action and launching an expensive media campaign branding the sharing of music as theft and piracy. However, while Napster was outlawed and shut down in July 2001, the proliferation of successor services such as Grokster, Kaazaa and Limewire, meant that online music sharing for free continued to grow (Witt, 2015).

## Digital music sales and streaming services

The rise in file sharing and the proliferation of mp3 enabled the creation of new products and services, radical innovations such as mobile mp3 players, online mp3 sales and later streaming services, as well as integration with home and in-car entertainment systems, which allowed users to play mp3 files on devices other than their computers. While the first mp3 player was launched in 1996, it was the launch of Apple’s iPod in October 2001 that brought awareness of mp3 players to the wider public. While sales in the first years were comparatively small, the launch of Apple’s online music store iTunes in 2003 spurred both the iPod and digital music sales (Lloyd, 2004).

At the time, a number of record companies and music labels had launched their own online music services.<sup>3</sup> While all of these failed quickly, it was the launch of Apple’s iTunes Music Store which for the first time demonstrated that mp3-based music downloads could be monetized. Apple CEO Steve Jobs had been able to create an integrated online music store by convincing the major labels to list their music catalogues in iTunes. In conjunction with the iPod, the iTunes Music Store formed a platform (Lusch and Nambisan, 2015) with an attractive offering and unprecedented ease of use for consumers. The iTunes Store offered single song downloads for 99 [US]cents; by March 2004, it had sold 50 million songs, and in 2010, it passed the 10 billion mark and became the world’s largest music retailer (Witt, 2015).

Further stages in this development were the emergence of competitors imitating the iTunes Store, by for example Amazon and Google, and the rise of online streaming services such as Pandora, Spotify, Rdio, Rhapsody, Apple Music, Amazon Prime, and Google Play, which offered unlimited access to their entire online music catalogues for a monthly fee of around US\$10 (Albright, 2015). Consumers were no longer limited to listening to music they had purchased. Instead, they could ‘stream’ music via mobile devices such as smartphones, car entertainment systems, or Internet-connected speaker systems, providing unlimited, everywhere access to music as a service.

These developments brought about a discontinuous shift in how the music industry operated, which disrupted incumbent businesses. As a transformational technology, mp3 set the music industry on a new trajectory, enabling entirely new ways of distributing and consuming music. Record companies that resisted and grappled with the changes for a long time and then attempted unsuccessfully to set up their own online distribution (Witt, 2015), ultimately had to live with the fact that music distribution had become dominated by new market entrants from the computer industry, such as Apple and Google. Notwithstanding a recent revival of vinyl as a boutique, retro format, record sales (LP, cassette, and CD) have all steadily declined after peaking in the year 2000, and record retailers such as Tower Records have famously been bankrupted in the process (Hanks, 2015). New start-up businesses such as Pandora and Spotify are now household names. In total, changes underpinned by mp3 have been widespread, profound, and irreversible.

We will return to this case after we introduce our theoretical material in the next section. We will then be able to bring out in more detail (section ‘Reinterpretation of the music revolution as a paradigm shift’) how the events presented where embedded in, and propelled by, the tacit practices and understanding of the actors involved.

## Theoretical material: Kuhn’s account of scientific revolutions

In this section, we introduce Thomas S. Kuhn’s seminal work in The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (henceforth ‘SSR’) (Kuhn, 1962). In the next section, we will use his work to revisit the music industry case and show that strong parallels exist, in both process and structure, between technology-enabled industry disruptions and scientific revolutions. In the section ‘Synthesis: disruption as worldview change’, we will use these parallels and conceptual tools drawn from Kuhn’s work to uncover precisely how interpretive discontinuity arises in the music industry case.

As we have pointed out in the methodology section, a problematization approach requires both the empirical and theoretical materials deployed to be selected for their relevance to the nature of the identified surprise. In our case, the surprising phenomenon has two significant characteristics: (1) understanding of the events of a disruption appears to depend on when in the history of the event that assessment is made, and (2) this change of understanding appears to be related to the discontinuous nature of disruptions.

A number of theoretical resources would potentially shed light on the historicity of understanding of events, including the hermeneutical notions of ‘prejudice’ from Gadamer (1989), ‘distantiation’ from Ricoeur (1981), ‘world’ as a totality of meaning from Heidegger (1962), or the notion of ‘Whig interpretation’ (Butterfield, 1965 [1931]) of the events of scientific discoveries from Latour and Woolgar (1986) or Pickering (1995). All these notions are also present in the works of Kuhn on the nature of scientific revolutions, as we will make clear in what follows. Kuhn’s work has the decisive advantage for our purposes of providing an explicit and well worked out account of the actual processes of revolutionary change, albeit in a different context.

The application of Kuhn’s work outside of the natural sciences requires justification, and previous such applications, such as in sociology, have been challenged (Bryant, 1975). By evidencing parallels between change in scientific fields and change in business sectors, we argue that both are underpinned at a deeper level by similar principles and structural similarities, even though the actual enterprises and their objects of interest are quite different.

## Paradigm

In SSR, Kuhn (1962) challenged the view accepted in his time that science presents a linear story of progress and accumulation of new insights through discoveries. Instead, he demonstrated that science tends to go through periods of stability referred to as ‘normal science’, interspersed with disruptive periods of ‘revolutionary science’ characterized by shifts in the underlying paradigm that organizes scientific practice.

Under normal circumstances, any scientific field engages in ‘normal science’ in which scientists advance the field using an on-going process of ‘puzzle-solving’ applied to its known problems, by employing methods and tools in ways that are commonly understood by members of the field. Kuhn refers to these common ways of doing science, underpinned by a shared understanding, as a paradigm – ‘that thing which allows scientists to go about solving the puzzles they continually generate’ (Eckberg and Hill, 1979: 929). Novice scientists are inducted into a paradigm both through textbooks and by practical apprenticeship.

In his original work, ‘paradigm’ had a rather broad meaning (Masterman, 1970) referring at the same time to the explicit theories, procedures, tools, methods, and activities employed by the field, as well as to the tacit skills, beliefs, goals, and how the world makes sense to the scientist as the basis for normal science. Kuhn (1977) later distinguished two notions, one broad, and one more specific. The broad notion refers to ‘all the shared commitments of a scientific group’ (Kuhn, 1977: 294), which Kuhn came to refer to as the disciplinary matrix. In essence, this usage of paradigm refers to the totality of scientific practices that a group shares and which distinguishes it as a particular group. The specific notion of paradigm refers to the ‘exemplars [which] are concrete problem solutions, accepted by the group as, in a quite usual sense, paradigmatic’ (Kuhn, 1977: 298). Kuhn argued that it is through exposure to exemplars that an important skill of distinction-making is learnt, which allows the novice then to see ‘the situations that confront him as a scientist in the same “gestalt” as other members of his specialists’ group’ (Kuhn, 1970: 189) – a skill that cannot be captured by explicit rules. Thus, a paradigm governs at once how the world shows up for the scientists and how to respond.

In the innovation literature (e.g. Dosi, 1982), the notion of paradigm refers mainly to shared activities and technological commitments. However, for Kuhn, it is the meaning-giving aspect of paradigms – the shared ‘ways of seeing’ (Masterman, 1970) and making sense of their world through a common worldview – that allows scientists to know what is worthy of attention and what counts as a relevant problem. This aspect of a paradigm has largely been missed in this literature but is the key to explaining the phenomenon of interpretive discontinuity.

## How scientific revolutions unfold

Kuhn (1962) demonstrated that revolutionary changes in scientific fields unfold in four distinct stages, which we now briefly introduce.

Normal science. When a scientific field operates effectively, its scientists will be guided by a paradigm to identify pertinent problems to solve and to see how to approach them. As such, normal progress in the field happens in an incremental fashion because ‘normal science does not aim at novelties of fact or theory and, when successful, finds none’ (Kuhn, 1962: 52). There are always certain discrepancies – empirical findings that run counter to theoretical expectations of the field. However, during such periods of normal science where what can be ‘seen’ is governed by a paradigm, discrepancies will always show up as either simply another puzzle to be solved using paradigmatic methods or as irrelevancies unworthy of attention. Such discrepancies cannot a priori be recognized as genuine anomalies that may eventually overthrow the paradigm. In fact, it is only later that some may take on significance as ‘anomalies’.

Emergence of anomalies and crisis. Certain nagging puzzles and unexpected outcomes of experiments may persistently resist solution within the paradigm and begin to appear as anomalies that challenge the concrete situations, experi ments, and paradigmatic problems of the field (Eckberg and Hill, 1979). The initial response, as always, is guided by the paradigm but might involve stretching its rules and tweaking its methods in search of success. Success may come, and an incremental modification or re-evaluation of the paradigm ensues. But if the anomaly fails to be tamed in this way, a full-blown crisis occurs, where it begins to appear that nature has ‘violated’ the paradigm-induced expectations that govern normal science. Dealing with this crisis soon becomes the all-consuming task for the field and increasingly desperate remedies are proposed and tried. Often the deepest assumptions of the paradigm, which are normally veiled by the tacit way that exemplars provide meaning, become an explicit topic for reflection. Insecurity follows as the sense tacitly provided by the paradigm is undercut, and more explicit and unfamiliar sense-making activity and controversy places new demands on the working scientist.

Crisis resolution and paradigm shift. Out of the ferment created by crisis come new techniques and theories that differ in many respects from those of the dominant paradigm. Often these originate at the fringe of the field where the anomaly may have already been recognized as such for some time. Often this recognition and possible solution occurs to a younger generation who are not so invested in the paradigm and its exemplars. Progress is heralded when new theory and new techniques are successful in accounting for the anomaly. However, it is only when new theories begin competing with existing theories, and this success is recognized by scientists in the dominant paradigm, that a ‘paradigm shift’ begins to unfold. The new paradigm-breaking solutions become an exemplar for further work which, if it continues to tame other anomalies, will crystallize into a new paradigm providing a new disciplinary matrix – new tools, methods, procedures, and activities – but also new exemplars governing how the world is understood, what counts as a relevant problem, and ultimately, the self-understanding and identities of the scientists involved.

It may take some time for many in the field to accept these new activities as valid science, especially the ‘old timers’ who continue to use their cherished ‘tried and true exemplars to judge the matter. For them it will take a ‘gestalt switch’ in worldview to see the new paradigm as a successor to the old. While such a revolution is a source of excitement to those advocating change, for those still committed to the old paradigm, it is experienced as an existential crisis, and failure to account for anomalies is often seen as a failure of the scientist and not of the theory. As a result, some quit the field altogether. Such a paradigm shift is always transformative, not cumulative, because paradigms cannot be reconciled – the rejection of a paradigm requires not only the rejection of practical aspects of the old way of ‘doing science’ but of its fundamental assumptions and ways of making sense of the world. Thus, a ‘paradigm shift amounts to a ‘reconstruction of the field from new fundamentals’ (Kuhn, 1962, 85).

Post-revolutionary normalization. Once the paradigm shift has occurred a new phase of normal science is entered. Initially many aspects of the new scientific field are still being changed to fit the new understanding of the world, textbooks are being rewritten, new methods, and tools are being developed. Most significantly however, the shift in paradigms has transformed the scientific field fundamentally: ‘It is rather as if the professional community had been suddenly transported to another planet where familiar objects are seen in a different light and joined by unfamiliar ones as well’ (Kuhn, 1970: 111). In other words, ‘a scientific revolution results in a more or less total transformation of the scientists’ conception of the world such that their very data, the facts of their science, are transformed by the paradigm shift’ (Urry, 1973: 464).

Moreover, a form of historical reconstruction takes place, in which the new system of meaning is applied retrospectively also, to make sense of the past as having led up linearly to the present (Latour and Woolgar, 1986; Pickering, 1984): now the revolution shows up as an inevitable correction of mistaken beliefs, even though it was experienced as radical rather than inevitable at the time. Conceptual aspects of the prior paradigm typically become integrated into the new one, but in the process, they will change their meaning: ‘Though an out-of-date theory can always be viewed as a special case of its up-to-date successor, it must be transformed for the purpose. And the transformation can be undertaken only with the advantages of hindsight, the explicit guidance of the more recent theory’ (Kuhn, 1970: 102f).

## Paradigm incommensurability

Kuhn further argued that ‘the normal-scientific tradition that emerges from a scientific revolution is not only incompatible but often actually incommensurable with that which has gone before’ (Kuhn, 1970: 103) because ‘scientific knowledge claims are necessarily evaluated from a moving, historically-situated, Archimedean platform’ (Kuhn, 1990: 6). Importantly, a paradigm shift brings with it not merely changes to how science is practised, but more fundamentally, changes to meanings of old concepts and a ‘conviction that the [new] way of thinking is more worthy than its rivals’ (Malone, 1993: 91): for subscribers to the new paradigm ‘their arguments for their way of practicing research depend upon different senses of what it is significant to accomplish, what facts and theories it is important to take account of, what tools and methods are legitimate to use’ (Rouse, 1981: 279). Kuhn goes so far as saying that ‘the proponents of competing paradigms practice their trades in different worlds’ (Kuhn, 1962: 149), where ‘world’ refers to the socially constructed world of the scientific practices (Hoyningen-Huene, 1990; Rouse, 1981), a world that is always ‘already perceptually and conceptually subdivided in a certain way’ (Kuhn, 1962: 129).

Paradigm incommensurability thus refers to the inability of people in these different ‘scientific worlds’ to ‘make complete contact with each other’s viewpoints’ (Kuhn, 1962: 147). As such, incommensurability does not mean that people cannot talk across paradigms but that they make sense of the world in very different ways, so that what is relevant, important, and appropriate differs for each: members of different paradigms ‘can perfectly well understand what others do, and why they do any one particular thing in the context of their whole way of doing things, without in any way grasping why they would want to do things that way’ (Rouse, 1981: 280).

In addition, because paradigms underpin the meaning of core concepts in this way, there can never be a panoptic ‘view from nowhere’ (Nagel, 1986) from which to make independent judgements about paradigms. Literally, incommensurability means the inability to measure on a common yardstick. In essence, there is no paradigm-neutral ground, free from any pre-understanding of the world that would allow evaluating and deciding between paradigms: ‘the competition between paradigms is not the sort of battle that can be resolved by proofs’ (Kuhn, 1962: 147) but has to play out historically over time.

## Reinterpretation of the music revolution as a paradigm shift

Using the theoretical concepts and distinctions provided by Kuhn’s work, we now re-interpret the music industry case. We do not claim that disruption is the same as a scientific revolution, or that Kuhn’s theory applies directly to business. Rather, we demonstrate that both are underpinned by the same principles. First, we demonstrate that a parallel exists between how the music industry disruption unfolded and Kuhn’s account of how scientific revolutions unfold. Next, we provide detailed evidence that industry disruptions are equally underpinned by a Kuhnian paradigm shift by comparing the nature of key events in the music industry disruption with equivalent events in a scientific revolution. Finally, we provide evidence that such a shift comes with a change in worldview in industry disruptions also.

## Re-telling the music industry case using Kuhn’s four phases

We now use Kuhn’s four phases of revolutionary change in science and his notions of paradigm and paradigm shift to re-tell the story of disruption in the music industry presented in the section ‘Empirical material: disruption of the music industry by mp3’ to show that industry disruptions strongly resemble scientific revolutions in structure.

## Business as usual: the ‘record’ industry

In the late 1990s, on the strength of the CD boom, the recording industry enjoyed the most profitable years in its history. (…) Americans were spending more money on recorded music than ever before. Profit margins were expanding as well, as efficiency gains in compact disc manufacturing brought the per-unit cost of goods below a dollar – a saving that was not passed on to the customer, who was charged [US]\$16.98 retail. (Witt, 2015: 79)

In the late 1990s, both the music industry and music consumption were paradigmatically organized around the ‘record’ as its exemplar, music inscribed on a physical medium which can be sold to customers who replay it using a generic playing device. The production, distribution, and sale of records underpinned the industry: activities, tools, machinery, supply chain structure, company strategies, incentive structures, and job titles in the industry all intersected with the record. Accordingly, music production companies at the time were known simply as ‘record companies’ (Witt, 2015). Moreover, the ‘record’ as its core product defined how the industry engaged with and viewed its customers. Correspondingly, purchasing, curating, and displaying one’s personal record collection were normal activities for many people, even to the extent that it was supported by specialized record-holding furniture.

In the decades prior, evolution of the industry had occurred incrementally and exclusively within this paradigm, by improving its products, processes and business models. For example, music had come to be packaged in different ways, as singles, albums, ‘various artists’ compilations, and distributed successively on a variety of different physical media, most notably vinyl, cartridge, cassette, and then the CD. Importantly, neither the switch from analogue to digital recording, which had profound implications for how music was created (Millard, 2005; Milner, 2009), nor any of the subsequent innovations to physical media formats had challenged the paradigm and central status of the ‘record’ in music distribution and consumption in signifi cant ways. To the contrary because digital recording had lowered production cost the industry was able to increase its profit margins significantly until the mid 1990s (Millard, 2005). Thus, the CD re-affirmed the existing record paradigm.

Mp3 emerged outside of the music industry. It originated from academic research carried out in a university context and was not specifically aimed at music distribution or retailing (Sterne, 2012) with music being only one particular ‘hard case’ in a more general quest for improving sound compression. With the release of the I3enc encoder, mp3 made its way into computer hacking communities where people began tinkering with new software algo rithms to create digital music files from CDs. Spurred by its own exemplars and ideals of freedom of information and ‘an ethic based on sharing rather than charging’ (Millard, 2005: 390), this community embraced ‘ripping’ (so called because it allowed ‘freeing’ music from its physical medium), storage on computer hard discs, self-production of music CDs (‘burning’), and sharing via computer networks such as the Internet. However, at this stage the tech nology did not register with the music industry in any significant way: it was simply not relevant as an aspect of ‘record production’. It is only in hindsight that this early part of the mp3 story gains relevance as the development of a radical music industry innovation.

Anomalous competition and crisis: Napster and free music download

Before Napster he had regarded the mp3 as an annoyance, if he ever thought of it at all. He was a music guy, not a technology guy, and for a long time he stubbornly refused to acknowledge it might impact his industry. His job was to make hits, and when he looked at the mp3, he didn’t see how it could help him do that. (Witt, 2015: 117f, describing Doug Morris, CEO of Universal Music Group)

With Napster, ‘the utopian idea of free music graduated from the computer hacker subculture and became mainstream’ (Millard, 2005: 395). The success of Napster and the fact that it began engaging a significant customer group (young people) focused attention in the record industry on mp3 for the first time (Winter, 2013). At the same time Napster was clearly an anomalous form of competition, a disturbance of the natural order because it did not ‘play by the rules’ (The New York Times, 2014). Against the existing paradigm, mp3 and Napster were anomalous because giving away music for free did not make sense to an industry organized around selling records.

The way Napster provided its users with ‘an unprecedented wide universe of musical understanding and enjoyment’ (Kernfeld, 2011: 221) and allowed discovering new music on the hard drives of users globally, could not be understood as a legitimate value by actors in the music industry. Against the established paradigm, Napster was ‘simple thievery, conducted on an unprecedented scale (Witt, 2015: 118) and part of an existing music piracy phenomenon<sup>4</sup> (Cummings, 2013) that had already previously threatened the established order (Winter, 2013; Witt, 2015). Accordingly, the industry response was guided by its established paradigm; in mass media campaigns, it emphasized the aspect of ‘free music’ which supposedly threatened the idea of legitimate music production and ownership (The New York Times, 2014); it engaged in strong legal action to have Napster shut down (Kernfeld, 2011); and it tweaked its core product, the CD, to include ever more elaborate technical means to prevent the ripping of CDs (Cummings, 2013).

In addition, many industry insiders and customers were also publicly dismissive of mp3 itself: after all, mp3 as a lossy compression format ‘had to’ offer inferior sound quality, despite what psychoacoustics researchers postulated (Sterne, 2012). It was at odds with the industry’s quest for ever higher fidelity and sound quality (Milner, 2009). Moreover, mp3-based music required a computer which was cumbersome, creating mp3 collections on computers was not as satisfying as owning a well-curated CD collection, and mp3s could initially not be used in the car. Instead, the music industry in 1999 introduced its own innovation – the Super Audio CD (SACD) – a new and improved version of the compact disc that offered longer album play times and better sound quality.

However, neither its achievement in having Napster outlawed in 2001 (Kernfeld, 2011), its hacking tactics in undermining file sharing by flooding the services with bogus mp3 files (Katz, 2010; Kernfeld, 2011), nor any of its technical innovations – anti-ripping measures were deeply unpopular with paying CD customers, and the SACD failed to reach any significant market share (Milner, 2009) – allowed the industry to tame the music sharing phenomenon. On the contrary, the music industry found itself in a period of fullblown and all-consuming crisis; CD sales fell sharply in the period after 2001, music sharing via a range of new peer-topeer platforms continued unabated, further spurred by the emergence of the first portable mp3 players, and a search for new mp3-based distribution models ensued. In addition, record companies were already unpopular with the public for their ‘exploitation’ of ownership rights, given the high prices charged for CDs and the fact that they had been found to collude with retailers over CD prices (Witt, 2015). In essence, cornerstones of the established paradigm became openly and widely scrutinized for the first time.

## Industry response and paradigm shift: Apple’s iPod and iTunes

Apart from the legitimacy that downloading from iTunes offered, and the question of whether the general public actually cared about that legitimacy, the service provided a reliability that the world of uncontrolled and unauthorized song sharing could not match. (Cummings, 2013: 210)

Even though the industry was in crisis, incumbent record companies were ‘making only halfhearted efforts to assimilate song sharing into a legitimate business model’ (Kernfeld, 2011: 208). Instead, the industry displayed a ‘guildlike resistance to the technology’ (Witt, 2015: 91) because ‘it was married to the compact disc, in sickness and in health’ (Witt, 2015: 51). As a consequence, it was left to industry outsiders to experiment with new ways of retailing, distributing, and consuming music. In the end, it was Apple and Steve Jobs, who was known to strongly disapprove of file sharing himself (Witt, 2015: 132), who came up with a successful digital music retail business model. Built around ease of access, reliability, simple pricing, and the integration of online music download with its iPod music player, Apple’s iTunes Music Store proved for the first time that digital music retailing could be done successfully.

Given their economic success and rapid growth in the years after 2003, iTunes and the iPod became new exemplars for music retailing and consumption, respectively. Subsequently a new paradigm crystallized around these exemplars, setting off new innovations that led to further developments, including music streaming through services like Pandora or Spotify. Digital music thus quickly made inroads into wider society, becoming part of public popculture. And while the new paradigm emphasized new aspects of music distribution and consumption, such as ease of access and mobile use, it incorporated existing aspects, such as attention to ownership and royalties, in new ways. For example, the iTunes Store initially utilized digital rights management (DRM) as a concession to the record companies to prevent users from sharing purchased mp3s. However, DRM was quickly wound back and finally discontinued in 2009 (Witt, 2015).

While the new digital business model now openly competed for customers against the traditional CD-based model, for those incumbents still committed to the old paradigm mp3 broke with their understanding of what was important about and counted as a music product and thus did not make sense as a positive force of change in the industry.

Moreover, with the paradigm shift unfolding, the record industry lost its exclusive access to established distribution channels that had previously provided their gatekeeper position. New actors could now enter from adjacent fields because with digital music production and mp3 encoding, ‘the entire industry could be run off a laptop’ (Witt, 2015: 228). Not surprisingly, this change was experienced as an existential crisis by many in the industry (Cummings, 2013): businesses (such as Tower Records (Hanks, 2015)) failed, and people quit or lost their jobs.

## Post-revolutionary normalization: music as a service

In 2013, amid an upbeat economic picture, the recording industry’s total revenues once again declined, to their lowest level in three decades. Consumer research showed that new Spotify subscribers stopped pirating more or less completely. They also stopped buying albums. (Witt, 2015: 261)

Over time, digital music production and consumption via download and streaming became normalized. What happened in society more broadly was a gestalt switch in people’s understanding of the role of music in everyday life. It was the paradigm shift, and with it, the change in the meaning and role of music itself, which made visible both what the new value proposition was and what was the basis of the old model: ‘instant access anywhere and anytime underpinned the new model (via download and mobile use), whereas the old model was organized around ‘ownership’ and the sale of recorded music as intellectual property (via the ‘record’). Whereas earlier technology, such as the Walkman or other portable playing devices had merely reaffirmed and incrementally built on the record paradigm, digital download and streaming presented a genuine transformation in the availability and use of music. This new ‘music as a service’ paradigm is what anyone who newly joined this market as a customer would now encounter.

With the new paradigm now largely taken-for-granted, activities that were ‘normal’ under the former paradigm now looked decidedly ‘old world’. While distribution of music on CD still existed and vinyl was making a comeback (Sarpong et al., 2016), the physical ‘record’ became ‘an object of ritual and nostalgia’ (Katz, 2010: 192) associated with fringe identities such as the ‘audiophile’, ‘club DJ’, or simply ‘what old people do’. At the same time, record company executives were being blamed in hindsight for missing the threat of mp3 and for reacting ineptly by sinking large sums of money into their own online ventures ‘in a world [they] didn’t really understand’ (Witt, 2015: 118).

Finally, the industry’s past trajectory was rendered intelligible in a new way: ‘disruptive innovation’ became valorized in a retro-fitted story, as both resulting from the inspired inventions of a few individuals and as an inevitable progression towards better technology. For example, the Fraunhofer institute described mp3 ‘as a historic inevitability’, ‘relying as it did on superior German technology’ (Witt, 2015: 128). In industry, folklore stories of great invention and disruption by individuals, such as Karlheinz Brandburg (mp3), Shawn Fanning (Napster), or Steve Job (iPod) arose, while the story of the technology became retold as one of the inevitable progresses – a natural progression from analogue media (LP and cassette) to digital recording (CD) to fully digitized distribution (via mp3 and streaming). As a result, with hindsight, the messy and inherently social real-time process of crisis and response that led to the paradigm shift became largely covered up by this reconstructive story.

## Interpretive discontinuity illuminated and resolved

We have demonstrated that a Kuhnian paradigm shift underlies the unfolding events of a technology-enabled industry disruption and that such a shift is always accompanied by a gestalt switch in worldview that alters how participants make sense of their industry. This finally allows us to illuminate how interpretive discontinuity comes about, which involves giving a careful account of which paradigm provides the sense any observer makes of the events involved in the process of industry disruption, and the progress of the paradigm shift at each particular time.

To begin, it is important to recall a number of key features of how a paradigm shift unfolds. First, any shift in paradigm always occurs in response to a crisis because it is only under such special circumstances that the most takenfor-granted assumptions of a paradigm become accessible to, and challengeable by, actors in the field. Second, the new paradigm does not form independently and in isolation. Rather, it emerges as a genuine shift of the existing paradigm by amalgamating both radical innovations and aspects of the established paradigm. Third, any competition between old and new paradigms can only take place after the new paradigm forms as a resolution of the crisis. Finally, this competition phase (what Masterman (1970: 74) terms ‘dual-paradigm science’) is rather brief, as the new paradigm eventually subsumes and re-articulates remaining aspects of the old one.

To illuminate interpretive discontinuity, we first have to explain why the disruption of an industry by a radical innovation appears inevitable with hindsight. As part of the normalization that follows a paradigm shift, the disruption becomes part of a retrospective and selective re-telling of the history of the industry using the meanings and point of view – the worldview – provided by the new paradigm.<sup>5</sup> Crucially, such a re-telling systematically leaves out any events and human choices that appear not immediately relevant to the eventual outcome, thus reconstructing the disruption as the direct result of entirely rational management choices among precisely those technology options that are now known to be those that were relevant to the eventual outcome. The many unforced human choices and false leads – based on what made sense on the worldview of the time and that are crucial to how history actually unfolds – are thus deleted (Pickering, 1984) along with the possibility that the eventual outcome could have been quite different. This selective deletion of genuine situated human agency from the disruption story can thus be traced to the incommensurability of the old and new worldviews. The result is that our current technological world comes to be understood as a fully rational, deterministic – and therefore inevitable – outcome of prior generations of technology.

We now turn to what incumbents, faced with this supposedly ‘inevitable’ disruption, could have actually seen and known in real time while it was unfolding. Because this changes as the industry crisis is being resolved, it is useful to distinguish the following two stages early in the process of paradigm shift:

1. Before the resolution of the crisis: At this stage in the process, the new paradigm has yet to form. While the industry is in crisis, in response to an emerging new technology, no new business model has as yet been articulated that would be recognizable as a legitimate resolution to the crisis by members of the industry. Importantly, the formation of a new paradigm will depend on innovations that by necessity take place outside of the established paradigm, often in other unrelated industries guided by their own distinct paradigms and worldviews. These events are thus largely beyond the incumbents’ horizon of relevance and understanding. Moreover, the formation of the new paradigm will also depend on many unrelated events and human choices that have yet to occur, so the future implications of current events are unclear. Hence, while it appears with hindsight as if the incumbents ‘missed’ the innovation, in fact there was nothing for them to ‘see’ at this stage.

2. Once the new paradigm forms: At this stage in the process, old and new paradigms compete for a while, but since these paradigms are incommensurable, incumbents are in no position to fully understand what is significant about the emerging new paradigm. The more subscribed to ‘business-asusual’ they are, the harder it is for them to understand what is significant about the new paradigm. And even though with hindsight, it appears as if incumbents should have been able to evaluate and decide between competing alternatives and thus realize that the new way of doing business is superior, it is important to note that any such notion of ‘superior’ is already a judgement against the success criteria of the new paradigm and not those of the worldview of actors following the old paradigm. Hence, while it appears with hindsight as if the incumbents simply ‘dismissed’ the innovation without due consideration of its usefulness, in fact at the time they were not actually in a position to fully make sense of it as a useful innovation.

We conclude that the phenomenon of interpretive discontinuity is a product of hindsight with its point of view from the new paradigm. It arises from glossing over the differences in visibility and understanding that exist at various stages of the process of paradigm shift. Even the notions of ‘incumbents’ and ‘radical innovation’ used to formulate the contradiction depend for their meaning on the wisdom of hindsight – knowing that a group of ‘incumbent’ actors were ‘disrupted’ by what turned out to be a ‘radical innovation precisely because it brought about this disruption. Following Kuhn (1962: 149), we could say that interpretive discontinuity arises because incumbent participants and later observers inhabit different worlds, but this difference in their worldviews is normally deleted by the myopia (rather than the wisdom) of hindsight: the ‘foresight’ of participants and the ‘hindsight’ of later commentators are different and incommensurable. This insight thus resolves the contradiction inherent in the phenomenon of interpretive discontinuity.

## Revisiting the literature to challenge fundamental assumptions

We now return to the literature on radical innovation and industry disruption. Our earlier review of the literature in the section ‘Literature’ showed that previous works offer a wide range of, at times contradictory, explanations for why incumbents fail to see and/or react to emerging disruptions. This led us to problematize how the literature understands and approaches the phenomenon of interpretive discontinuity at a fundamental level. Indeed, our subsequent analysis of the music industry case, drawing parallels to Kuhn’s account of scientific revolutions, revealed that the contradiction inherent in the phenomenon of interpretive discontinuity is resolved only once disruption is understood to be underpinned by a shift in paradigm, accompanied by a gestalt switch in worldview as it unfolds.

This insight further reveals that the search for explanations for incumbent failure in the literature rests on the implicit assumption that radical innovations, as they emerge at the time, are in principle unproblematically available for inspection by industry participants and that incumbents should thus be able to see and react to them. It is this assumption that renders interpretive discontinuity a puzzle to be solved by this literature in the first place.

But this assumption is merely an expression of a widely held, yet largely unacknowledged, epistemological position that not only underpins theorizing of disruption but also common-sense thinking more broadly. This position assumes an objective world ‘out there’ that is always in principle available for perception, analysis, and ‘knowing’ by human actors. On this position, any ‘failures’ to do so will be assumed to reside in the individual actor, their cognitions, or their circumstances impeding the acquisition of ‘correct’ knowledge of the world. These assumptions are directly reflected in the kinds of explanations summarized in Table 1.

Such an epistemological position does not have a place for the sense-giving nature of paradigm exemplars as the ground that renders the world intelligible in particular local ways against the current paradigm and that can change during a disruption. The widespread tacit acceptance of this epistemological stance was one of the reasons that Kuhn’s work met opposition when first published (Masterman, 1970). It is not surprising then that existing studies that do employ the notion of paradigm shift to explain disruption tend to take this epistemological position, leading to a different understanding of paradigm from that advocated by Kuhn.

Kuhn’s work was introduced to the wider innovation literature by Dosi (1982) who used the distinction between normal science and scientific revolutions to make an analogous distinction between periods of continuous and discontinuous industry change, arguing that ‘continuous changes are often related to progress along a technological trajectory defined by a technological paradigm, while discontinuities are associated with the emergence of a new paradigm’ (Dosi 1982: 147). Subsequently, these distinctions have been taken up, often implicitly, by a number of other authors in theorizing disruptive innovation (e.g.

DeTienne and Koberg, 2002; Garcia and Calantone, 2002; Kemp, 1994; Nelson and Winter, 1982; Tushman and Anderson, 1986), including by Christensen in his widely cited theory of disruptive innovation (Christensen, 1997; Christensen and Rosenbloom, 1995). Some authors later referred to paradigms as ‘regimes’ (Kemp et al., 1998) or ‘logics’ (Bettis and Prahalad, 1995), yet only very few draw on Kuhn’s work directly (e.g. Garud and Rappa, 1994) beyond Dosi’s initial work.

Dosi (1982) defines a paradigm ‘as an “outlook”, a set of procedures, a definition of the “relevant” problems and of the specific knowledge related to their solution’ (p. 148). Following this definition, disruption begins with the emergence of one or more new technological paradigms, each with its own projected technological development trajectory. Disruption then is said to occur when the majority of customers in an industry choose to adopt the products that are based on a new technological paradigm, and abandon the ones of the old, thus setting the industry on a new trajectory and leaving behind incumbents who fail to make the shift. The task of industry actors during periods of discontinuous change is then understood to be to evaluate, compare, and choose between alternative technological paradigms (p. 155). On this view, the task of theorizing disruption is then to identify particular attributes of technological paradigms and to predict which associated development trajectory is likely to succeed, making it essentially into a multi-criterion optimization problem under uncertainty.

While on the surface, the above account of a paradigm shift may appear similar to Kuhn’s, it ignores the sensegiving function of paradigms, particularly the role of exemplars, and its consequences. Most notably, for Dosi (1982), a paradigm appears to be like any other entity available in the world to be perceived, evaluated, and compared by industry actors, whereas for Kuhn, paradigms are the very ground on which actors, both practitioners and theorists, tacitly come to see their world. From the latter follows Kuhn’s notion of paradigm incommensurability and his insight that there can never be a paradigm-neutral panoptic viewpoint from which to compare and evaluate paradigms objectively. Importantly, Dosi (1982) rejected Kuhn’s insights into paradigm incommensurability and its implications explicitly, calling them ‘extreme’ (see footnote on p. 154). Dosi (1982) and those building on his work thus preserve the established epistemological assumptions underpinning the field, and consequently miss the insight that a genuine Kuhnian paradigm shift brings to understanding disruption.

Even those accounts that recognize that established routines under an existing paradigm might render incumbents ‘unable to evaluate (or perceive as noise) data about new technological paradigms’ (Garud and Rappa, 1994: 348) still implicitly draw on epistemological assumptions that deviate markedly from Kuhn’s original work. For Kuhn a paradigm does not act merely as an individual ‘filter’ that prevents access to information about a competing paradigm: rather the paradigm provides a perspective, or worldview, to those enrolled in it, and thus the very ground that determines what could count as valuable information or relevant facts in the first place, prior to any explicit cognitive evaluation of them.

## Implications for theory: what makes radical innovations disruptive?

By revisiting the existing literature on disruption in light of Kuhn’s analysis of the sense-making role of paradigm, we have argued above that the extant literature has missed what is disruptive about radical innovations. Technologyenabled radical innovations are not disruptive simply because they result in a displacement of incumbent businesses through new forms of competition, but because the worldview change that accompanies a shift in paradigm invalidates the very basis on which incumbents understand their practices, their products and their value proposition – the very basis on which they previously understood how to fend off competition. This change in worldview challenges both our common-sense understanding of disruption and the feasibility of theorizing disruption in a conventional way. We capture these challenges in three propositions, each having implications for theorizing disruption:

1. Disruption results from a change in worldview: it is the discontinuous change in worldview that always accompanies a paradigm shift that renders changes enabled by radical innovations disruptive for incumbent businesses. This is because they find themselves confronted with a ‘world’ they no longer understand and in which established practices they tacitly rely on are no longer useful. This implies that any valid attempt to theorize disruption must proceed from an epistemological position that can provide an account of the worldview change that underlies disruption. This however does not hold for most prior theory, including the influential theory of disruptive innovation (Christensen, 1997).

2. Disruption as worldview change is unpredictable: when a paradigm shift is complete, it brings with it a new worldview that is incommensurable with and thus breaks in important ways from the understanding that the old paradigm provided. This implies that theory attempting to predict the outcome of disruption from conditions obtained prior to the paradigm shift is not feasible. Given the importance of worldview changes to social action, and the consequent under-determination of disruption, the best we can achieve is to try to theorize the nature of technology-enabled industry crises and how incumbents might cope with them tactically.

3. Disruption becomes retold in hindsight: It is the process of normalization, as the endpoint of a paradigm shift, that deletes from historical accounts the open, active, collective, and under-determined nature of disruption as it unfolds in real time, and then retells the process as a heroic story that attributes disruption to individual key actors or to attributes of particular technologies as causal agents of disruptive events. The implication is that the reconstructed chain of events and causation articulated in such retrospective accounts cannot be relied upon uncritically as data for theory development or for prescribing normative action in future industry disruptions. We note, however, that careful historical analysis of past events using contemporary sources can transcend this limitation, as illustrated by Kuhn’s work. We claim validity for our own metatheorizing on this basis.

## Practical implications: what then can incumbents do?

Our analysis challenges the very possibility of providing normative advice to actors that assumes that they can have a panoptic view of events as they unfold. Given this, what should we expect incumbent actors to do when an industry is thrown into crisis as a harbinger of potential disruption? Again we can turn to Kuhn (1962: 77–91) to understand how incumbents typically act in the face of a paradigm shift.

According to Kuhn, incumbents do not simply reject old exemplars because anomalies are encountered. The exemplars of the old paradigm are what anchor the incumbents’ ability to collectively solve problems as they arise, so they are not abandoned simply on the basis of the accumulating evidence of their inadequacy. Kuhn emphasizes that paradigm change, when it eventually occurs, is always embracing a definite new approach, not rejecting the tried and trusted old one. Accordingly, incumbents will normally stretch the old exemplars by experimenting with new ways of interpreting and applying them. These deviations will become more extreme as the crisis bites. On the other hand, increasingly doing things that are not routine and which lead to unexpected results, will allow and encourage more explicit reflection on core concepts and commitment of the field. An important contribution to this comes from novice practitioners who are not yet so invested in the paradigm and its exemplars. The result is that the hold of the old paradigm on both how to act and how to understand is weakened, paving the way for eventually acknowledging and embracing the new one. While the anchoring role of paradigms in normal periods ensures continuity, weakening their hold is a necessary condition for paradigm shift.

Thus, on a Kuhnian account incumbents can cope with the crisis at the heart of disruption through an active, collective, and largely myopic sense-making process (Weick et al., 2005). While they cannot be expected to see and evaluate their situation in a detached way when they are occupied with living it, there are certain ways in which incumbents can become more attuned to emerging radical innovations and loosen the grip of the established paradigm during crises:

Understand paradigm shift: By understanding disruption as a shift in worldview, incumbent actors will be better prepared in principle to be more open to having their understanding challenged when the industry plunges into crisis.

Scout the fringes: Incumbents should proactively be involved with innovation at the fringes of the industry because a new paradigm will always form by incorporating technologies or business principles from outside of the established paradigm. Active engagement with what might normally be judged as ‘nonsense’ or ‘crazy’ ideas will allow incumbents to become sensitized to potential sources of disruption.<sup>6</sup>

Be experimental: By creating ventures or business divisions that are allowed to ‘break the rules’ and experiment with new technologies, incumbents gain important inside understanding of the transformative potential of new technologies. It is important that such ventures are independently budgeted so they do not have to justify decisions according to the rules of the established paradigm.

Give novices a voice: During crisis, incumbents should actively involve novices to the industry in company decisions as they are not yet fully beholden to the established paradigm and will thus be better able to see existing group-think (Reimers et al., 2013) and imagine alternative ways of doing business that incorporate emerging new technologies.

This list is by no means complete and is intended as only an initial set of ideas for further studying successful tactics for coping with technology-related industry crises in real time.

## Conclusion

We began by focusing on an apparent contradiction in the way technology-enabled industry disruption appears entirely inevitable with hindsight and yet practitioners in disrupted businesses struggle to detect and respond appropriately to it while it is unfolding. We termed this surprising observation ‘interpretive discontinuity’ and suggested that it might hold the key to what exactly changes during disruption and why. By juxtaposing an empirical case of disruption in the music industry with theoretical resources sensitive to the nature of radical change, Kuhn’s work in the unrelated field of scientific practice, we demonstrated that industry disruptions, like scientific revolutions, are equally underpinned by a Kuhnian paradigm shift. We were thus able to trace interpretive discontinuity to the gestalt switch in worldview that accompanies such a paradigm shift. We then showed that this insight sheds new light on both what is disruptive about radical technology-enabled change and the limitations of prior work theorizing disruption.

Our work contributes to the body of literature on disruptive innovation a more complete deployment of Kuhn’s notion of paradigm. This is novel because, while previous work has employed ‘paradigms’ and ‘paradigm shift’ to distinguish periods of business-as-usual from revolutionary change, it has overlooked the worldview dimension of a paradigm that underpins how business within an industry is understood. We further contribute a detailed account of the temporal process of disruption when understood as a Kuhnian paradigm shift, outlining what exactly changes during the process of disruption, and when. In doing so, we add to the literature a detailed case study of digital disruption of the music retail industry that illustrates the actual moves and countermoves of actors and demonstrates the open, active, under-determined nature of disruption, as well as the role of human agency in the process. Finally, we contribute an important distinction between the global reconstructive view of disruption in hindsight, and the local, myopic understanding available to actors at the time when change unfolds.

Our work reveals that what is actually disruptive about radical technology-enabled change is not just the new technology itself, or the changes it brings to how business is done, but the discontinuous worldview change that these bring about. This insight is significant because it shows that anyone commenting on disruption with hindsight will normally view disruption from a different ‘world’ to the actors who experienced it in real time. This puts into question both the prevalent media narrative around disruption and existing ways of theorizing disruptive innovation, both of which put undue emphasis on the role of technology at the expense of human agency and fail to recognize the limitations imposed by changes in worldview on the ability of incumbents to recognize and respond to disruption as it unfolds. Our work thus challenges attempts to predict future disruption from past data and any normative advice to actors that assumes that they can have a panoptic view of events as they unfold. We have presented this alternative understanding of disruption in the form of three theoretical propositions.

The practical implication of this is the need to develop a new basis for providing normative advice to practitioners when a technology-related crisis begins to become apparent in their industry, which does not assume that the full-blown disruption that may result can be predicted or detected at that time and used as a basis to prescribe action. To this end, our work points to the need to balance the positive and negative roles that paradigms play in organizing industry activity: on one hand, paradigms ensure continuity, stability, and effective coordination of routines during business-as-usual; on the other hand, they tend to blind incumbents to potential threats and opportunities during periods of crisis. We have presented a tentative list of tactics for incumbents to cope with innovation-induced industry crises as they unfold. However, further research is required to examine in more detail what incumbents can do to actively loosen the grip of the existing paradigm in order to prepare for and engage in the sense-making process that is at the heart of a paradigm shift, beyond engaging in typical defensive moves that the current paradigm affords.

## Author biographies

Kai Riemer is Professor at the University of Sydney Business School. His research interest is in Disruptive Technologies, Enterprise Social Media, Virtual Work, Collaborative Technologies, and the Philosophy of Technology. He has published in leading IS journals such as MIS Quarterly, Journal of Information Technology, and European Journal of IS.

Robert B Johnston BSc, DipEd, MSc, PhD, is Professor of Business Information Systems at the University of Sydney Business School and has published in leading academic journals including Information Systems Research, Management Science, and MIS Quarterly. Prior to becoming an academic, he spent 13 years as an IT practitioner in Australia.
