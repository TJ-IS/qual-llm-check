# Impact of ATM intensity on cost efficiency: An empirical evaluation in Taiwan

## A R T I C L E I N F O

## A B S T R A C T

Although the use of ATMs in the U.S. is approaching saturation, ATM deployment is still increasing in other parts of the world, such as Australia, China, Canada, Germany, and the United Kingdom. The relationship between IT investment and firm performance has been extensively studied, but few researchers have examined the impact of self-service technology (SST) in general and ATMs in particular on a firm’s cost efficiency. Given the growing importance of SSTs in the banking industry, it is thus surprising that few studies have examined the impact of SST on banking cost efficiency. We therefore empirically examined the cost efficiency effects on ATMs.

Several prior studies tried to determine the value of ATM investments, These, however, failed to provide actual ATMs investment data that measured the intensity of its deployment and, as a result, used a dummy variable to indicate whether or not the ATM investment was made. This practice has made it impossible to assess the true value of ATM investment. To improve this imperfect measure, we utilized actual ATM numbers and bank operating and financial data to measure the extent of ATM investment. Our results suggested that ATM investments were associated with positive cost efficiency in the banking industry. To this end, we added new insights into cost effects of SST in general and ATM in particular. - 2009 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

Self-service technology (SST) allows people to complete transactions (services) at their discretion (i.e., decide when, where and how to do them). One of the popular examples of SSTs is the ATM in the banking industry. Employing SST helps banks streamline the teller processing function, reduce the teller’s workload and hence reduce operating costs by eliminating overlapping and manual steps, such as signature capture and form scanning. SST in general has long been advocated as a way to reduce operating costs in the banking industry. Surprisingly, empirical evidence on the cost impact of SST in generally and ATM in particular has been limited. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the few that provide any empirical evidence of the impacts of ATM on cost efficiency. Moreover, no analysis of the effects of ATMs on bank efficiency has been published for the greater China market (including Hong Kong and Taiwan). Our study analyzed the cost impact of ATMs in the Taiwanese banking industry, an important segment of the greater China market.

ATMs were initially only available at bank locations, though, by the early 1980s, shared ATM networks had been established in the U.S. and ATMs were introduced at retail locations (e.g., supermarkets and shopping malls). They appeared to provide competitive advantage, but it diminished as more banks adopted the technology and ATMs became a competitive necessity—forced to install them or risk loss of business. However, the replacement of human labor by automation certainly improved the traditional process.

ATM deployment in Taiwan has recently accelerated. By the end of 2005, the number of ATMs in Taiwan was 24,000, representing an average of approximately 1000 ATMs per million people, a significant increase from 150 ATMs per million people in 1991. Currently, their main functions include cash deposit and withdraw, transfer, cash advance, bill payment, and account balance checking: somewhat less than those offered in the U.S. and Japan.

The Taiwanese banking industry was strictly controlled by the government during early development. This control began to ease in 1991, when the Ministry of Finance (MOF) made a regulatory change and permitted the establishment of new commercial banks. The rapid increase in the number of commercial banks caused an increase in competitive pressure and thus improving operating efficiency became a necessity for survival. Since the ATM is one of the major IT investments in the banking industry, we empirically explored their impact on banks’ cost efficiency. We collected rich data on ATM use: in an attempt to measure their effect on banks’ cost and asset management efficiency and provide convincing evidence to show the ROI of IT in the banking industry.

## 2. Impact of ATM intensity on banks’ cost efficiency

IT investment can encourage complementary organizational capital investment, such as business process reengineering, structural transformation, and improvement in supplier and customer relations. For such reasons, productivity due to IT investment has received considerable attention [2,8,21]. Some researchers concluded that IT investment contributes positively to firm productivity [7,20,23] but others [16] found that it did not. This phenomenon was termed the productivity paradox.

Several studies emphasized and attempted to identify whether or not IT investment could improve a firm’s cost efficiency. Kudyba and Diwan [17] grouped U.S. industries into three levels of IT investment. The ranking depended on capital and labor inputs, including the ratios of IT to total capital, IT capital to sales, IT budget to sales, and IT employees to total employees. They also found that the banking industry ranked first in terms of IT investment. Additionally, many studies [3,4,10,24] further confirmed that, in the banking industry, IT investment can increase the cost efficiency.

Holden and El-Bannany [14] investigated whether IT investment affects bank profitability in the UK. The results showed that the number of ATM installed by a bank had a positive impact on bank profitability. The value of ATMs was in improving the teller’s productivity, increasing branch service value, and expanding the branch deposit market. A study of Haynes and Thompson [13] confirmed that ATM investment significantly influenced productivity, improved processing speed, and expanded additional service value by rendering a 24-h service.

We examined the effect of ATMs on banks’ cost efficiency using real ATM data and hypothesized that:

## H1. ATM intensity is positively related to bank cost efficiency.

We focused on the relationship between ATM investments and banks’ cost efficiency and the impact of five control variables: first, firm size affects new IT adoption [1]. If it is optimal, higher operating efficiency will occur by reducing operating cost. Papadopoulos [22] proposed that larger banks were more cost effective than smaller ones. We therefore hypothesized:

## H2. Bank size has positive impact on banks’ cost efficiency.

Second, branch establishment costs increase bank operating costs, which include essential equipment, personnel, and miscellaneous expenses. Banks with more established branches in urban and rural areas have a strong need to satisfy different types of customers in different markets. For branches located in urban areas, the bank will install more ATMs to provide convenient and efficient customer service, while branches located in rural areas will probably require fewer ATMs. Corrocher [5] assessed the effects of the intensity of branching onto the adoption of innovative IT applications, such as Internet banking. The results suggested that banks perceive innovative IT applications as a substitute for their existing branch networks. We therefore hypothesized:

## H3. Branch number does not influence banks’ cost efficiency.

Third, several cost efficiency studies examined the effects of salary on cost efficiency [6]. This may have been because salary level is one essential factor in measuring banks’ cost efficiency. From the labor productivity perspective, firms are generally willing to pay higher salaries to more skilled employees. However, most tellers perform simple services, such as cashing checks and processing deposits. To this end, the empirical results of Kwan [19] demonstrated that banks utilize more labor in some countries not because labor costs are cheaper, but because labor appears to be more productive in the banking industry. Therefore, labor costs are considered to be less important than labor productivity issue in developing banking operating strategies. From an operating costs perspective (salary costs are part of operating costs), it is reasonable to expect that higher salary costs will result in cost inefficiencies. We thus hypothesized:

## H4. Bank salary level is negatively related to cost efficiency.

Fourth, some studies included the variable of non-performing loans as a means to measure efficiency. Drake and Hall [9] observed that in controlling non-performing loans, the exogenous effect on banks was very important, since problem loans potentially impacted the measurement scale and technical efficiency results. We thus hypothesized:

H5. A high ratio of non-performing loans reduces bank cost efficiency.

Finally, Hao et al. [12] used age to capture the difference in efficiency between established banks and newcomers. In their study, the concept of ‘‘learning by doing’’ suggested that established banks had already developed effective operating strategies to improve their operating efficiency. However, Kulasekaran and Shaffer [18] found that the cost efficiency of the newer credit card banks exceeded that of older ones. Jemric and Vujcic [15] also reported that new banks were more efficient than old ones, but these studies contain no conclusive results. We thus hypothesized:

H6. There are no significant differences in cost efficiency between established banks and newcomers.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Variables

Operationalizations of all variables are summarized in Table 1.

## 3.1.1. Dependent variables

Two cost efficiency measures used in this study were operating cost rate (CR1) and asset management cost rate (CR2), which were computed as follows:

$$
\mathrm{CR1} = \frac {\mathrm{OE}}{\mathrm{TR}}
$$

$$
\mathrm{CR2} = \frac {\mathrm{OE}}{\mathrm{TA}}
$$

The higher the CR1 measure, the lower the cost efficiency of operating activities. For example, the mean CR1 for the sample banks was 0.240. That is, on average, the sample banks spend NT\$24 in operating costs to generate NT\$100 in revenue. Meanwhile, the asset management cost rate (CR2) measures asset management efficiency. Similar to the CR1 measure, the higher the CR2 measure, the lower the asset management efficiency. The mean value of CR2 was 0.014, indicating that the sample banks spend an average of NT\$1.4 to manage NT\$100 of assets.

## 3.1.2. Independent variables

The main independent variable in this study was the automated teller machine intensity (ATMI). Since one of the main reasons for banks to invest in ATMs is to reduce the branch operating costs in general and labor costs in particular, we measured the extent to which banks use ATMs in terms of their total labor utilization. The ATMI variable is thus computed as

$$
\mathrm{ATMI} = \frac {\mathrm{NATM}}{\mathrm{NE}}
$$

## 3.1.3. Control variables

The control variables used included bank size (BS), branch size (BRS), salary level (SL), non-performing loan ratio (NPLR), and a new bank dummy (NBD). These control variables were computed as

$$
\mathrm{BS} = \log (\mathrm{TA})
$$

$$
\mathrm{BRS} = \frac {\mathrm{TA}}{\mathrm{NB}}
$$

$$
\mathrm{SL} = \frac {\mathrm{PE}}{\mathrm{NE}}
$$

$$
\mathrm{NPLR} = \frac {\mathrm{NPL}}{\mathrm{TL}}
$$

## NBD ¼ 1 if the bank is a newcomer ðestablished since 1991Þ; and 0 otherwise

When a firm is operating at an optimal scale, its operating efficiency, due to economies of scale, will be maximized and consequently operating costs will be minimized. Since the operating scale affects the banks’ operating costs and cost efficiency, our study included bank size in its estimation model. Further, if a bank has more branches it will need additional support resources to maintain the branch’s operating costs and this will affect the overall banks’ cost efficiency. Thus, branch size must be included in the model.

IT can process a large number of transactions efficiently and provide valuable information to managers in a timely fashion. Therefore, banks’ labor costs can be replaced by IT capital investment per substitution theory. To this end, salary level affects banks’ operating efficiency and should be included in the model.

To measure bank efficiency, non-performing loans are also an important variable, due to their impact on banks’ scale and technical efficiency; prior studies have indicated the importance of controlling its exogenous impact.

Operating stability and management attitude are affected by the age of the bank. Recently established banks may face severe pressure to recover their start-up costs, leaving few resources for investment in IT. Meanwhile, older banks have a long operating history and their financial situation tends to be relatively stable. Consequently, older banks may have sufficient funds to invest and enhance their operational status. However, newer banks have few or no legacy systems to upgrade or maintain. In addition, newer banks may have a higher level of acceptance/ recognition of new IT technology. As a result, the bank’s age was included in the model.

## 3.2. The statistical estimation model

The statistical model derived from the theoretical model presented in Fig. 1 is

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { Model }: & \mathrm{CR} _ {i y} \\ & = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \mathrm{ATMI} _ {i y} + \alpha_ {2} \mathrm{BS} _ {i y} + \alpha_ {3} \mathrm{BRS} _ {i y} + \alpha_ {4} \mathrm{SL} _ {i y} + \alpha_ {5} \mathrm{NPLR} _ {i y} \\ & + \alpha_ {6} \mathrm{NBD} _ {i y} + \varepsilon_ {i y} \end{array}
$$

Since ATMI is believed to be positively associated with cost efficiency, the sign of $\alpha _ { 1 }$ is expected to be negative. Because of economies of scale, larger firms can spread their fixed costs and thus have cost advantage over smaller firms. Therefore, the sign of $\alpha _ { 2 }$ is assumed to be negative. Additionally, banks with more established branches must invest in more supportive resources and thus have greater operating costs. Moreover, branches operating in rural areas may have low operating efficiency if their operating costs exceed revenues generated. From another perspective, more established branches with ATMs installed at various locations may provide increased customer access, and thus may increase the operating revenue. Due to conflicting arguments, we did not predict the sign of $\alpha _ { 3 } .$ As for $\mathtt { S L }$ , since the job of tellers mainly involves routine work, banks’ cost efficiency reduces for an increase in tellers’ salary. The sign of $\alpha _ { 4 }$ was thus expected to be positive. Since non-performing loans required more resources to process than performing loans, the sign of $\alpha _ { 5 }$ was expected to be positive. Finally, because the difference of cost efficiency between the older banks and newcomers was hard to justify, we made no predictions regarding the sign of $\alpha _ { 6 }$

## 4. The experiment

## 4.1. Data

According to the Central Bank of the Republic of China (Taiwan), its banking sector is composed of eight categories: domestic banks, Taiwan local branches of foreign banks, investment and trust companies, credit cooperative associations, credit departments of farmers’ and fishermen’s associations, bill finance companies, and security finance companies. We only examined Taiwanese domestic banks.

Our data was obtained from two main sources. The number of ATMs were obtained from a survey by Financial Supervisory Commission of the Bureau of Monetary Affairs, Executive Yuan in Taiwan. Based on this ATM investment data, the actual ATM investment operating situation of the Taiwanese banking industry was measured. Second, related bank financial and operating data were obtained from the annual reports of banks and the report of the Central Bank of the Republic of China [25]. Our study investigated 35 banks in Taiwan, during the period from 1992 to 2001. Each observation consisted of the specific bank data for a 1-year period. Three-hundred and twenty-two initial observations were obtained. Further, any observations with missing data were eliminated. Thus, after selection, the sample contained 264 observations covering a 10- year period.

## 4.2. Sample statistics

Table 2 summarizes the descriptive statistics of data used in our study. The revenue for the 264 observations ranged from 66.177 to 0.831 billion NT dollars with a mean of 16.178 billion NT dollars.

Operating expenses ranged between 16.574 and 0.265 billion NT dollars with a mean of 3.692 billion NT dollars. Additionally, the average number of ATMs and employees were 145 and 1846, respectively. Regarding ATM investment intensity, the average ATM to employee ratio was 0.074.

## 4.3. Empirical results

Before performing the regression analysis, we first examined the correlations between independent variables. Table 3 reveals that some variables have higher correlations. For example, the Spearman correlation coefficient between BS and SL was 0.672, and the Pearson correlation coefficient between BRS and SL was 0.633. To examine colinearity, VIF testing can detect whether or not colinearity exists (no colinearity exists if VIF is below 10). Table 4 shows that the VIF values of the six independent variables range from 1.434 to 3.534 and it can be concluded that no colinearity exists between these variables.

Further, there is a significant result for six independent variables in terms of operating cost ratio, the one exception being branch size. For the asset management cost ratio, the ATM intensity, bank size, and salary level were also significant. Even the results of BRS, NPLR, and NBD in CR2 were insignificant; thus, the conclusions were not affected.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. ATM intensity

A higher ATMI indicates a greater substitution effect onto the labor force. The coefficient of the ATMI was statistically significant in measuring operating cost efficiency. ATMs may overcome the restrictions of traditional branch offices such as limited hours, finite banking sites, lower productivity, and slow processing speed. Thus, banks with a considerable human capital are facing pressures to improve their operating efficiency in today’s competitive environment. Therefore, ATMs are generally acknowledged to be one of the most direct methods of labor substitution and operating efficiency enhancement.

The coefficient of ATMI to CR2 is significant. Higher ATM intensity means banks have installed more ATMs implying considerable installation costs (sunk costs) as well as the variable costs associated with processing transactions. The prevalence of ATM utilization, the growing number of installations and the development of multiple-function ATMs all indicated a high rate of utilization of bank assets. Consequently, the significant negative results from our study demonstrated that banks have used ATMs to achieve good asset management efficiency. Thus, the empirical results of ATM intensity confirm the hypothesis that ATM intensity positively impacts banks’ cost efficiency.

## 5.2. Bank size

The model also allows us to examine the effect of bank size on operating efficiency and asset management efficiency, whose coefficients were 0.039 and 0.002, respectively. Large banks generally have cost advantages and higher operating cost efficiency than small banks. Also, larger banks generally have sufficient capital and usually have detailed asset management plans and gradually modify them to remedy any weaknesses encountered during their operation. Therefore, it can be concluded that bank scale positively influences banks’ asset management efficiency. Thus, banks’ cost efficiency and asset management efficiency improve with increasing bank size.

## 5.3. Branch size

Table 4 shows the effects of branch size on bank operating efficiency and asset management efficiency are all insignificant. Branch establishment requires a bank to invest considerable sunk costs and to have a sufficient labor force to maintain branch operation, which considerably increases the total fixed costs. As banks establish more branches, they must invest more resources in operating equipment and hire more employees to serve their customers. However, one of the comparative advantages of branch marketing is that it provides more personalized customer service. Additionally, the profitability of branches located in urban areas differs from those in rural areas. Branches in rural areas have fewer customers and lower daily transaction volumes than those in urban areas, and thus contribute less to overall bank profitability. Our study concluded that branch size did not significantly influence bank operating and asset management efficiency.

## 5.4. Salary level

From the regression results, the coefficients of salary level are 0.15 and 0.002 for the operating efficiency ratio and the asset management ratio, respectively. These results indicate that high salary levels are negatively related to operating and asset management efficiency. High salary levels indicate high human capital, and banks with significant human capital resources may be left with insufficient resources for automation and thus may have a lower operating efficiency. From the human resource perspective, companies are willing to pay more to higher skilled employees. The work of tellers, however, is mostly repetitive and simple. Therefore, employee wage rates do influence banks’ operating efficiency. Banks should reconsider their labor force strategy. For simple and routine tasks that can only generate limited benefits, they can increase automatic services to substitute for traditional labor, and hire more educated employees to operate high added value tasks, such as personal financial services.

## 5.5. Non-performing loan ratio

Problem loans are controlled because they are considered an exogenous influence, derived from unfortunate external events. Banks must pay more attention to processing, monitoring, and managing non-performing loans than to performing loans. If a bank’s non-performing loan ratio exceeds that of other banks, loan quality may also be affected. According to Girardone et al. [11], the non-performing loan ratio is generally positively related to a bank’s inefficiency. From the regression results, the coefficient was 0.157, which is significantly positive at a 0.10 level, confirming that non-performing loans negatively affected the bank’s operating efficiency. However, the effect on asset management efficiency was not significant. That is, non-performing loans probably only have an effect on operating costs and have no influence or direct effect on asset management.

## 5.6. New bank dummy

Surprisingly, the empirical results showed that the coefficients of NBD with respect to CR1 and CR2 were 0.031 and 0.001, respectively. The coefficient of NBD for measuring operating cost efficiency was significant at 0.01, indicating that the operating efficiency of older banks was superior to that of newer ones. Generally, newer banks have a shorter establishment history, and this may result in more operational instability and inefficiency than is found in older banks. New banks should reconsider their operational strategy and investment plans. If newer banks can enhance their operating efficiency, they can certainly generate greater operating benefits by reducing redundant or unnecessary operating costs. By doing so, newer banks may thus better compete with older, well developed banks. Large banks generally have a considerable advantage in their capital assets over those of small banks with relatively limited capital assets. Our study thus concluded that asset management efficiency was more correlated with firm size than with its age.

## 6. Conclusion

To retain a competitive edge, all banks must adopt and use technologies effectively and efficiently. By deploying SST, bank customers can complete transactions and related services themselves without requiring assistance from bank employees. Banks must be more aggressive in pursuing SST to lower their operating costs to be efficient. Even though all banks have now adopted ATM technology, the ATM intensity varies from bank to bank and thus quite different cost efficiency effects occur.

Given the increasing importance of the greater China market in this global world, our study is timely; it provides valuable empirical evidence to both academics and practitioners in the banking industry. To study the impact of ATM on cost efficiency in Taiwan, we assembled a unique data set, including the actual number of ATM deployed and related operating & financial data. Our empirical results confirmed that ATM intensity is positively related to banks’ cost efficiencies.

Analyzing IT effect on cost efficiency can allow firms and IT investors to make sound decisions. Moreover, IT providers can better plan and more accurately predict ATM development and usage by studying the relationships between IT investment and firm performance. Finally, our results can provide managerial and marketing insight to IT professionals and users who seek to investigate the value of IT investment in the Greater China marketplace.

This study has, however, some limitations. First, the actual savings in operating costs from investing in IT remain uncertain due to the confidential nature of the banks’ financial information. Second, not all banks in Taiwan were included. The variance in operating strategies between banks that focus on personal finance markets from those that focus on the corporate market may also have affected our results. Finally, due to limited data availability, some bank attributes could not be included in our model.
