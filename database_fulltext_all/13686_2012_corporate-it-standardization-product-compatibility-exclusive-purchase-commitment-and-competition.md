## 1. Introduction

Global information technology (IT) purchase is estimated to reach \$1,690 billion in 2011 (Bartels et al. 2011). When companies purchase IT products, such as hardware, software, and telecommunication equipment, for their employees, departments, or divisions, whether to standardize on one product or to allow the users, be it individual employees, departments, or divisions, to select their own products is an important decision for IT leaders to make (Westarp et al. 1999, Trotter 2009, Curran 2010). For example, should a company standardize its employees’ laptops on Windows machines or allow its employees to choose between Windows machines and Macs? Should a company standardize the smartphones and productivity software used by its employees on RIM phones and Microsoft Office or allow its employees to make their own choices? Should a company standardize its databases, servers, enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, and private branch exchange (PBX) systems across different departments or divisions on Oracle,

Intel-based servers, SAP, and Cisco or let each department or division be its own decision maker?

Standardization is defined as the primary objective by the CIO group of Siemens, a multinational enterprise with more than 400,000 employees (Weitzel et al. 2006). Standardization within the corporation is commonly observed in practice. For example, United-Health standardized its desktop management across the whole company (Swanborg 2009); U.S. Bank standardized on IBM’s Lotus collaboration software for all of its employees (Lai 2009); Dow Chemical, which sells 3,100 products, ran solely on SAP and had its employees all use the same laptop, voice over IP (VoIP) phone, and smartphone (Levinson 2008); and a multinational company surveyed by Ovum standardized on a single IP PBX vendor internationally (Trotter 2009). On the other hand, it is also not uncommon to observe that multiple types of IT products are used within the same organization. For example, although IT executives are placing greater focus on standardizing on a single business intelligence (BI)

platform, most companies have separate BI solutions running simultaneously (Gile 2005). A survey conducted by Westarp et al. (1999) in Germany and the United States shows that companies vary significantly in the number of products used for operating systems, database systems, office communication, and other software categories. An interesting question to ask is when IT standardization can be an optimal strategy for a company (Lyytinen and King 2006).

Standardization has both merits and limitations. Many IT products are subject to network effects in that a user’s utility of adopting a product increases with the number of others using the same or a compatible product. Depending on the compatibility, having a single or multiple types of products in place affects the network benefits received by the users and so their productivity (Westarp et al. 1999). Maintaining more than one type of product also involves additional staff support and cost (Liang 2003). These considerations suggest the benefits of standardization. On the other hand, users may have heterogeneous preferences over different products because of their expertise and experience with the products in the past, whereas standardization compromises users’ preferences for variety (Orlov 2008). Although the practitioner literature identifies the tradeoff between standardization and variety mostly from the users’ perspective, it ignores the effects of standardization on sellers’ price competition and their incentives to agree on making their products compatible, which calls for a comprehensive analysis on the optimality of corporate IT standardization that takes both the users’ and the sellers’ strategies into account. This study aims to fill this gap.

In this paper, we study a company’s optimal strategy of purchasing IT products for its employees, departments, or divisions when facing competitive sellers with both vertical and horizontal product differentiations and sellers’ strategic decisions on compatibility investments. Our model can be applied to both IT procurement for individual employees and IT procurement for a large number of departments and divisions within a large corporation. For the ease of exposition, we use mostly the term “employees” in the model and discussion in this paper, but it should be noted that “employees” could be interpreted as individual employees, departments, or divisions. Three important research questions are addressed in this study.

First, a critical decision for a company to make is whether to commit to exclusive purchase from a single seller to enforce standardization. To make such a decision, a tradeoff has to be evaluated by the employer aiming to maximize the total benefit to its employees minus maintenance cost. On the one hand, by consolidating demand and committing to buy from a single seller, the employer may induce intense price competition among sellers and enforce standardization. Standardized products require lower maintenance cost and ensure product compatibility, which guarantees maximum network benefits. On the other hand, when employees have heterogeneous preferences toward the sellers or specialize in different products, some employees may end up buying a less preferred product if the employer decides to purchase from one seller only. Moreover, the potential to induce intensive price competition through exclusive purchase is constrained by whether the competing products are compatible and the relative competitive advantages of the sellers. Thus, our first research question is under what conditions is it optimal for an employer to commit to exclusive purchase when network effects and standardization needs are present?

We set out to address this research question by analyzing a stylized IT product procurement model with network effects based on a set of standard demand and supply assumptions. Specifically, we model two competing sellers facing a buyer company whose employees are potential users of the sellers’ products. Sellers are differentiated both horizontally and vertically, and each seller can endogenously decide whether to make its product compatible with the competitor’s product. Products are compatible only if both sellers agree to cooperate and invest in compatibility. An employee’s utility of adopting either product is a combination of the product’s standalone value, which is independent of other employees’ consumption, and the network benefit, which increases with the number of employees using the same or a compatible product. The employees are heterogeneous in their preferences toward the sellers on product’s standalone value. The employer incurs additional cost if it has to maintain products from two sellers instead of one and its objective is to maximize the total benefit to its employees (and thus their productivity) minus maintenance cost.

We show that product compatibility and the relative competitive advantages of the sellers in terms of cost and quality differences have significant impacts on the optimality of the exclusive purchase commitment made by the employer. Interestingly, we find that the employer is less likely to commit to exclusive purchase to enforce standardization when products are incompatible and this likelihood decreases as the strength of network effects increases. This result arises because sellers compete more aggressively under nonexclusive purchase when products are incompatible because any price cut not only has a direct price effect on demand but also has indirect network effects as a result of the increased demand. The stronger the network effects, the more intense the competition. Therefore, the employer feels less of a need to use exclusive purchase commitment to encourage competition when products are incompatible and network effects are strong. Another interesting finding is that regardless of compatibility, exclusive purchase commitment is more likely to be optimal for the employer if the two sellers are less vertically differentiated and/or more horizontally differentiated. This is because the advantage of exclusive purchase commitment in inducing price competition among sellers diminishes when the asymmetry in the sellers’ competitive advantages grows, but it becomes especially useful when sellers are highly horizontally differentiated because in this case the employees under nonexclusive purchase have to pay high prices.

Second, our findings on the impacts of product compatibility and sellers’ competitive advantages on the employer’s choice of exclusive purchase commitment lead to the next research question to be addressed in the paper: when is it optimal for sellers to cooperate and invest in product compatibility? Our results suggest that sellers share common interest in compatibility investment only if the difference in their competitive advantages is not too big or too small. If one seller is significantly advantaged, it can win over all the employees when products are incompatible, which further provides dominant network benefit and thus higher price and profit. Therefore, a significantly advantaged seller will not be willing to share the exclusive network benefit with its competitor by investing in compatibility. If two sellers are similar in their competitive advantages, the disadvantaged seller has no incentive to invest because under compatibility, the employer will commit to exclusive purchase, leaving zero profit to the disadvantaged seller. Taking these together, only if the two sellers are moderately differentiated in their competitive advantages are they both willing to cooperate and make their products compatible.

Third, in reality, other than direct investments made by the sellers to accomplish compatibility, various converters exist that can provide partial compatibility between IT products. For example, ESF Database Migration Toolkit can transfer data between various database management systems. Ozgrid provides a number of converters for database management systems, spreadsheets, office applications, programming software, etc. MacLinkPlus Deluxe enables Mac users to communicate and exchange files with the Windows world. Converters are usually helpful in exchanging files and transferring data but are not able to fulfill all the functionalities. Therefore, they may provide only partial compatibility between these products (Farrell and Saloner 1992, Gandal 1995). An interesting question, which is our third main research question, is how does the availability of third party converters affect sellers’ incentives to cooperate and invest in full compatibility?

To answer this question, we extend our basic model to allow the employer to purchase converters from third party providers to achieve partial compatibility between these products. Our analysis shows that the presence of third party converters may induce more collaboration among sellers to invest in full compatibility, especially when the difference in their competitive advantages is large. Without converters, a significantly advantaged seller is not willing to cooperate in compatibility investment because of its dominant advantage under incompatibility. The availability of converters, however, forces it to share the network benefit at least partially with the disadvantaged seller, which reduces the advantaged seller’s ability to charge a high price, making the incompatible scenario less attractive to it. Therefore, converters can benefit buyers by providing partial compatibility directly in some cases, and in other cases their presence can even encourage compatibility investment from the sellers.

## 2. Literature Review

Our research contributes to three streams of literature: research on standardization and product compatibility, research on corporate procurement, and research on group buying.

Studies in the standardization and product compatibility literature primarily address the role of network effects, where one user’s utility of consuming a product depends on the number of other users consuming the same or a compatible product (Besen and Farrell 1994). Adopting a product used by many others can not only facilitate frictionless communication and information exchange but also benefit from a broad knowledge repository or a large pool of complementary products and services (Lee and Mendelson 2007, Liu et al. 2011). Empirical studies have found evidence of significant impacts of network effects on the adoption of many IT products, including word processing software (Chakravarty et al. 2006), spreadsheet software (Gandal 1995, Brynjolfsson and Kemerer 1996), database management systems (Gandal 1995, Harhoff and Moch 1997), ATM systems (Kauffman et al. 2000), Web server software (Gallaugher and Wang 2002), and open-standard interorganizational systems (Zhu et al. 2006). Chen and Forman (2006) further show that despite the presence of open standards in the market for switches and routers, vendors can influence the speed of new IT adoption by manipulating compatibility of their products with rival products as well as complementary products.

The focus of analytic studies in this literature is mostly on the impacts of network effects on market competition and sellers’ compatibility investment. Katz and Shapiro (1985) suggest that network effects give rise to demand-side economics of scale, which affects the number of sellers surviving in a homogeneous-good market, and that sellers with good reputations tend to be against compatibility whereas the opposite is true for sellers with weak reputations. Similarly, Farrell and Saloner (1992) argue that for horizontally differentiated products, a dominant seller has incentive to make conversion between incompatible products costly, further reducing market efficiency. Baake and Boom (2001) further examine a model of vertical product differentiation and find that although the high quality seller prefers incompatibility, the low quality seller can induce the high quality seller to agree on compatibility by choosing its quality appropriately. Whereas these studies suggest that the advantaged seller is usually the one against compatibility, we show that the disadvantaged seller may also prefer incompatibility given the buyer company’s strategic choice of exclusive purchase commitment to enforce standardization within the corporation and that both sellers agree on compatibility only if their differentiation on the vertical dimension is in the middle range. Lee and Mendelson (2007) suggest that IT product buyers should be cautious in promoting product compatibility among sellers because product compatibility reduces price competition when sellers compete head to head. We further show that product compatibility can be beneficial to buyers, however, if one seller is significantly advantaged in the vertical dimension. Liu et al. (2011) examine firms’ incentive to provide conversion technologies to achieve compatibility in IT markets that exhibit network effects in the context of sequential competition. In contrast, we examine how firms’ compatibility decisions are influenced by the existence of third party converters and buyers’ purchasing strategies under simultaneous competition.

Among the existing research, few studies look at the role of product compatibility and standardization within a corporation. Hanseth and Braa (2001) discuss the challenges faced by an organization in its process of standardization when balancing between setting up standards and satisfying needs of various divisions. Weitzel et al. (2006) point out that standardization is a key CIO challenge to many multinational enterprises, but solution strategies for using and dispersing standards within enterprises are scarce. We seek to fill this gap by examining the optimality of corporate IT standardization under different market conditions and its consequent impact on sellers’ compatibility investment. To our best knowledge, no previous research has examined the role of corporate IT standardization in this context. By taking into consideration the corporate IT standardization decision, we obtain interesting results new to the literature.

The extant corporate procurement literature (see the review by Yano and Gilbert 2004) focuses on optimal procurement decisions from competing suppliers to minimize production cost. In contrast, we examine how employers may manage procurement for products to be used by individual employees (or a large number of departments and divisions within a corporation) with heterogeneous preferences in order to maximize the total benefit to their employees, taking into account nonstandardization cost. The employermanaged procurement for employees is an important type of corporate procurement that is understudied.

Our research is also closely related to the literature on group buying and the resultant buyer power. Kauffman and Wang (2002) discuss the effectiveness of various business models of online group buying by analyzing a series of cases. Anand and Aron (2003) show that a group buying mechanism enables a firm to price discriminate when demand is heterogeneous. In a sense, corporate IT procurement for employees is a special type of group buying where the group formation is predetermined and the overall procurement strategy is decided by the employer who cares about the product benefit to the employees as a whole instead of maximizing each individual employee’s welfare. It has been shown in the group buying literature that an increase in buyer size can increase a buyer’s bargaining power (Chae and Heidhues 2004, Chen 2003, Chipty and Snyder 1999, Dobson and Waterson 1997, Horn and Wolinsky 1988, Inderst 2007, Stole and Zwiebel 1996, von Ungern-Sternberg 1996) and buyer size is most valuable when there is more than one seller (Ellison and Snyder 2010; Snyder 1996, 1998). Chen and Li (2008) and Dana (2009) further show that commitment to exclusive purchase may benefit a buyer group regardless of its size because it induces intense price competition among sellers, given that sellers are not too differentiated vertically. These papers, however, do not examine the role of product compatibility and network effects in group purchase or sellers’ strategic decisions to invest in mutual compatibility.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we present a basic model of an employer representing its employees to purchase from competing sellers offering potentially incompatible IT products. The basic model is analyzed in §4 where we investigate the optimal decision of the employer regarding exclusive purchase commitment and the sellers’ decisions on compatibility investment. We then extend the basic model in §5 to examine the impact of third party converters on sellers’ compatibility decisions. Finally, we conclude the paper in §6 and discuss the limitations and future research directions.

## 3. Basic Model

Consider an employer (the buyer) purchasing for its employees in an IT product market. There are two competing sellers, Seller 1 and Seller 2, in the market with marginal production costs $c _ { 1 } = c$ and $c _ { 2 } = c + \Delta c ,$ respectively, where $\Delta c$ can be either positive or negative. Each employee is assumed to consume at most one unit of the product sold by either Seller 1 or Seller 2. The size of the employees group is normalized to 1.

The gross benefit that an employee receives from adopting either product is the combination of the employee’s standalone valuation of the product and the network benefit that the employee receives from the network of employees adopting the same or a compatible product. Employees have heterogeneous standalone valuations towards the sellers. We assume that employees are uniformly distributed along a unit Hotelling line (Hotelling 1929) in the preference space with Seller 1 located at 0 and Seller 2 located at 1. We further denote $V _ { 1 } = V$ and $V _ { 2 } = V +$ $\Delta V ,$ where $\Delta V$ can be either positive or negative. We assume that $V _ { j }$ is sufficiently large so that all of the employees will purchase from the product category.<sup>2</sup> Our model assumptions follow the standard demand setup used in literature to model competition between firms with both horizontal and vertical differentiations (Brekke et al. 2006, Che and Gale 1997, Desai 2001, Iyer 1998, Ma and Burgess 1993, Wolinsky 1997)

in the presence of network effects in the product category (Katz and Shapiro 1985, Baake and Boom 2001, Lee and Mendelson 2007).

The employer maximizes its objective function, $W = Z - s I _ { s } .$ , which is the total surplus of its employees subtracting the IT maintenance cost.<sup>3</sup> Here, $\dot { Z } = \Sigma u _ { i }$ is the total surplus of its individual employees who are indexed by $i , s$ is the extra cost that the employer incurs if it has to maintain IT products from two sellers instead of one, $I _ { s } = 1$ if the employer purchases from both sellers, and $I _ { s } = 0$ if it purchases only from one seller. Without loss of generality, we normalize the cost of maintaining IT products from one seller to zero. The parameter, $s ,$ captures the idea that employers usually prefer standardization within their corporations to save on IT support and maintenance costs.

The employer in our model represents its employees to make purchases as a group and uses competitive bidding to solicit offers from the sellers. Competitive bidding is a procurement mechanism adopted ubiquitously by companies purchasing products for their employees (Ewerhart and Fieseler 2003). The employer may commit to buy from one seller exclusively or commit to allow employees to choose their own products. To commit to exclusive buying from one seller, the employer can run a total price auction, in which each seller’s bid comes in the format of a total price for all of the employees stated in the contract. The employer then chooses the seller whose offering leads to higher total surplus to the employees. If the employer prefers leaving the choice of product to each individual employee, it can solicit offers from the sellers in the format of a unit price of their products, with a commitment that price offers will be presented to the employees, who will make their own purchases.

The employer’s commitment to purchase exclusively or the commitment to allow employees to make their own purchases can both be explicitly stated in the contract so that the commitment is financially or legally binding. Moreover, the exclusive purchase commitment can be credible because once the total price auction is announced and bids are submitted, the employer cannot revert to nonexclusive purchase without a new auction. The employer will commit to exclusive purchase commitment only if such a commitment leads to higher surplus. Therefore, it is to the employer’s benefit to be able to make a credible commitment. In the nonexclusive purchase case, other than the financial and legal enforceability of the commitment, employees usually make purchases at various points in time, also making it difficult for the employer to reverse the decision later $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ to force employees to purchase from a single seller later); and the sellers may detect the deviation from nonexclusive purchase agreement from the employees who purchase early and adjust their pricing for the following purchase requests from other employees. To further assess the sensitivity of our results to the credibility of nonexclusive purchase commitment, we also conduct a robustness analysis in which we allow the employer to break its commitment and buy exclusively under the nonexclusive purchase prices. We find that our propositions and corollaries all hold qualitatively.<sup>4</sup>

## 6. Conclusion

Corporate IT standardization is a key issue for IT managers. From the users’ perspective, standardization saves on IT administrative costs and ensures product compatibility but sacrifices users’ preferences for variety. From the competition point of view, standardizing IT purchase, by committing to purchase from a single seller exclusively, also affects sellers’ price competition, with its effects varying under different market conditions. Although these issues have been discussed in the practitioner literature and academic research separately, this study provides a comprehensive analysis incorporating all of the above factors to examine the optimality of IT standardization within a corporation and its consequent impact on sellers’ incentives to invest in compatibility.

Our results suggest that the optimality of exclusive purchase commitment is critically dependent on the product compatibility, the relative competitive advantages, and the degree of horizontal differentiation between the sellers. Interestingly, standardizing IT purchase is more likely to be optimal when the sellers’ products are compatible, when the sellers are symmetric in their marginal costs and/or product quality, or when the sellers are more horizontally differentiated. We further investigate sellers’ incentives to cooperate in compatibility investment in this context. Sellers with different leverages in competition vary in their incentives to invest in mutual compatibility. Although the existing literature suggests that advantaged sellers are usually the ones against compatibility (Katz and Shapiro 1985, Farrell and Saloner 1992, Baake and Boom 2001, Chen and Forman 2006), we find that the prospect of standardization in IT purchase may also suppress the disadvantaged sellers’ incentive to invest in compatibility. Sellers share a common interest in compatibility investment only if the difference in their competitive advantages is moderate. We also find that the existence of third party converters that provide partial compatibility between the competing products can encourage more collaboration among sellers on compatibility investment. Lastly, a disadvantaged seller may find it optimal to increase rather than to reduce its gap with the competitor in order to induce the employer to purchase nonexclusively or the competitor to collaborate on compatibility investment.

Our results offer important managerial implications to the practice of IT procurement and IT standardization within a corporation as well as IT vendors’ compatibility investments. Our results suggest that when sellers are asymmetric in their competitive advantages, employers may want to allow the flexibility of nonexclusive purchase from multiple sellers in order to encourage price competition even though it may incur additional maintenance cost because of nonstandardization. If this competitive discrepancy is sufficiently high, standardization may take place naturally even without precommitment to exclusive purchase. Companies may benefit from consolidating purchasing quantity when buying IT products (e.g., basic switches and routers) that conform to open standards, but not necessarily when buying those with enhanced features that some vendors extend to open standards to create incompatibilities as shown by Chen and Forman (2006). For employers, searching for converters or sponsoring the investment of those can be beneficial when the quality of the competing products varies significantly, because converters can not only increase network benefit but also potentially induce more investment collaborations on compatibility among sellers.

Our analysis is intended to be largely prescriptive rather than descriptive, and the drivers behind our model predictions are likely to be highly context specific, making broad empirical study challenging. However, the factors that we identify giving rise to variation in players’ optimal strategies do have their roots in practice. According to a survey conducted by Westarp et al. (1999) with 352 of the largest enterprises in Germany and the United States, IT managers of most companies recognize the problem of incompatibility within a corporation and consider it as an important driver behind standardization. At the same time, they also recognize the heterogeneous functional needs of different users and departments. Costs, including both administrative costs and purchase prices, have also been frequently quoted as important motives behind the standardization efforts (e.g., Liang 2003). Whereas the conventional wisdom values standardization mostly from users’ perspective, similar to Lee and Mendelson (2007) who incorporate competition effects when examining the benefits of compatibility, we stress the effects of standardizing IT purchase on the intensity of competition. Our results suggest that standardizing

IT purchase may have positive or negative effects on seller competition contingent on market conditions, including product compatibility, product differentiation, and seller asymmetry.

The Westarp et al. (1999) survey also provides some support to our results. Their survey finds that the diversity of software solutions varies significantly across companies. The percentage of companies standardizing their software is less than 10% for operating systems but close to or more than 50% for business software, email programs, and office communication software. Considering that operating systems are usually incompatible and Microsoft significantly dominates the market, its lowest standardization ratio seems to be consistent with our results that exclusive purchase is less likely to be optimal when products are incompatible or sellers are asymmetric in their competitive advantages.

The discussion in the mobility industry that companies can negotiate better prices by dealing with a single provider in the competitive telecom service market (commented by Carrie MacGillivray at IDC) also echoes our result that exclusive purchase is more beneficial when facing sellers who are in similar competitive positions. In accordance with the discussion, Yankee Group standardized its cellular service around one telecom provider and one device in 2009 and attributed the savings mostly to the result of bulk purchasing with a single vendor.<sup>10</sup>

There is also anecdotal evidence that firms invest in mutual compatibility in the presence of converters. For example, although the converters between Windows and Mac documents are widely available (e.g., MacLink), Microsoft and Apple cooperated to improve compatibility between Windows and Mac in a number of adventures. In 1997, Microsoft committed to develop Microsoft Office for the Mac platform. In 2003, Apple developed a version of its iTune software that runs on Windows machines. In 2006, Apple opened its gate to offer its users a simple way to run the Microsoft Windows operating system on Mac (Markoff 2006).

Incompatibility across different mobile operating systems is also a problem frequently highlighted these days (Johnson 2010, Rosenberg 2010). Damaka recently unveiled a mobile multiprotocol messaging and video collaboration platform that enables video, voice, and text communication on any smartphone over any network. It would be interesting to see how the development of platform-independent applications (analogous to converters in our model) affects the incentives of the mobile phone manufacturers to collaborate and transform the competitive landscape of the mobile phone industry. There has been discussion on whether BlackBerry will and should open up its BlackBerry Messenger to Apple’s iPhone and Android smartphones (Bennington 2011).

There are several limitations in our study, which call for future research. First, in our model, price offers are initiated by sellers by engaging in competitive bidding. Future research may examine alternative price generating mechanisms such as the employer making take-it-or-leave-it offers to sellers or the employer and sellers engaging in bilateral negotiations.

Second, our results are derived in the context of a single employer facing two competing sellers. The employer can be considered as a representative one for a set of employers contributing significantly to the sellers’ revenue or an employer purchasing customized IT products tailored to its special needs. Our model also can be enriched to capture some of the effects beyond the current context. For example, we can interpret the cost parameters $c _ { 1 }$ and $c _ { 2 }$ as opportunity costs to capture the sellers’ considerations beyond the current setting. Alternatively, we can assume that attracting an additional buyer from this employer has a network benefit of b to a seller beyond the current setting (e.g., either in another market or at a later period). Then the seller’s profit function can be modified as $d ^ { * } ( p - c ) + d ^ { * } b _ { * }$ , which can be further simplified to $d ^ { * } ( p - ( c - b ) )$ . Thus, our model can still apply by replacing c with c − b. Although these approaches can capture the dynamics beyond the current setting to some extent, they are not perfect. Future research can further examine whether our results can be applied to multi-period and multi-employer settings.

Third, in this paper, we focus on the employees (or a large number of departments or divisions within a corporation) who are interested in the product category, and we focus on the product markets that are reasonably competitive. That is, sellers are not monopolies in their own segments without directly competing with each other, and all of the employees considered in the model receive positive utility in equilibrium. Although we feel this reasonably reflects the reality in our context, how our model behaves when the assumption of full market coverage is relaxed can be examined in future studies. We expect that our result that the employer is less likely to commit to exclusive purchase when products are incompatible than when products are compatible will still hold because exclusive purchase is never optimal when the market is not fully covered (as long as s is not prohibitively large) and the market is less likely to be fully covered when products are incompatible.

Finally, it will be interesting to see the emergence of empirical work examining the strategic decisions of corporate IT standardization and its impact on collaborative compatibility investment.
